# Ch 15: Clinical Ethics & Legislative Frameworks

This chapter covers the two pillars an examiner expects a clinical-psychology entrant to know cold: the statutory law governing mental health care in India (the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 and the RPwD Act 2016) and the professional ethics codes (RCI and APA) that bind a practising clinical psychologist. Expect direct one-mark factual questions on section numbers, percentages, and named bodies, plus applied vignettes (a client threatens a third party, a minor discloses abuse) where you must pick the ethically correct action. Read this so that both the "name the section" and the "what would you do" styles are covered.

## 1. The Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA), 2017

The **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** received Presidential assent in April 2017 and came into force on **29 May 2018**. It **repealed the Mental Health Act, 1987**, which itself had replaced the colonial **Indian Lunacy Act, 1912**. The driving force behind the new Act was India's ratification of the **United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD)** in 2007; the law shifts the entire framework from a custodial, institution-centred model to a **rights-based, patient-autonomy model**. Its stated object is to "provide for mental healthcare and services for persons with mental illness and to protect, promote and fulfil the rights of such persons."

A crucial conceptual move is the **definition of mental illness** in Section 2(s): "a substantial disorder of thinking, mood, perception, orientation or memory that grossly impairs judgment, behaviour, capacity to recognise reality or ability to meet the ordinary demands of life." Critically, the Act **excludes mental retardation (intellectual disability)** from this definition, and it explicitly states that mental illness shall be determined by **nationally or internationally accepted medical standards (such as ICD)** and never on the basis of political, economic, social status, religion, community, or membership of a cultural group.

The Act also enshrines the **right to access mental healthcare**, the **right to community living**, the **right to protection from cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment**, the **right to confidentiality**, the **right to legal aid**, and the **right to make a complaint**. It mandates that the government provide mental health services as part of public health coverage and that **insurance providers cover mental illness on the same basis as physical illness** (parity).

### 1.1 Advance Directive

An **advance directive** is a written statement made by a person specifying, in advance, **how they wish to be cared for and treated (and how they wish NOT to be treated) for a mental illness**, to be used during a future episode in which they lack the capacity to make decisions. This is a landmark feature directly importing the principle of **self-determination**. Any person who is an adult and has the capacity to make mental-healthcare decisions may make an advance directive; it does not require the person to have a mental illness at the time of making it. The directive must be **registered with the Mental Health Review Board**. It can be **amended, cancelled, or revoked** by the person at any time while they have capacity. Importantly, an advance directive may be **reviewed, altered, modified, or cancelled by the Mental Health Review Board** if a medical professional, nominated representative, or carer applies and the Board finds the directive was made without adequate understanding or is contrary to other provisions of the Act.

### 1.2 Nominated Representative

Every person has the right to **appoint a nominated representative** in writing. The **nominated representative (NR)** is the individual legally empowered to take mental-healthcare and treatment decisions on the person's behalf when the person lacks decision-making capacity, and to receive information, support the person in exercising rights, and access medical records. If the person has **not appointed** an NR, the Act lays out an order of default priority: the **advance directive's named representative**, then a **relative**, then a **carer**, then a **suitable person appointed by the Board**, and ultimately the **Director of the Department of Social Welfare or his designated representative**. For a minor, the NR is generally the **legal guardian**. The NR must always act in a way that **respects the wishes, dignity, and best interests of the person**, giving "due regard" to the person's own preferences.

### 1.3 Central and State Mental Health Authorities

The Act creates a two-tier administrative structure.

- The **Central Mental Health Authority (CMHA)** is the national regulatory body. Its functions include **registering all mental health establishments** under the central government, **maintaining a national register** of such establishments, developing **quality and service-provision norms**, and **maintaining a register of clinical psychologists, mental health nurses, and psychiatric social workers**. It advises the central government on policy.
- The **State Mental Health Authority (SMHA)** mirrors these functions at the state level: it registers and supervises mental health establishments in the state, maintains the **state register**, and ensures the rights of persons with mental illness are protected.

Both authorities are responsible for **registration of mental health establishments**, which is mandatory: no establishment may run without being registered, and minimum standards (staffing, infrastructure, hygiene) must be met.

### 1.4 Mental Health Review Boards (MHRB)

The **Mental Health Review Board (MHRB)** is a **quasi-judicial body** constituted by the State Authority at the district or regional level. It is the heart of the Act's rights-protection machinery. Its functions include: **registering, reviewing, altering, or cancelling advance directives**; **appointing nominated representatives**; **adjudicating complaints** regarding deficiencies in care and services; and crucially, **reviewing the admission of persons admitted for more than 30 days (supported admission)** to guard against arbitrary or prolonged detention. A person, their NR, or a representative may **appeal to the MHRB** against decisions of a medical officer or psychiatrist. Appeals against the Board's own decision go to the **High Court**.

### 1.5 Section 115: Decriminalisation of Suicide

This is among the most heavily tested provisions. **Section 115(1)** states: "Notwithstanding anything contained in Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code, any person who attempts to commit suicide shall be **presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress** and shall not be tried and punished under the said Code." **Section 115(2)** places a duty on the appropriate government to provide **care, treatment, and rehabilitation** to a person having severe stress who attempted suicide, to **reduce the risk of recurrence**. The practical effect is the **effective decriminalisation of attempted suicide**: the act is reframed as a mental-health and humanitarian concern rather than a crime. Remember the framing precisely for the exam: the presumption is of **"severe stress"** (a rebuttable presumption), not automatically of "mental illness."

### 1.6 Prohibited and Regulated Treatments

The Act bans or tightly restricts certain practices that were historically abused.

- **Unmodified ECT is prohibited.** **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** may be administered **only with the use of muscle relaxants and anaesthesia (i.e. modified ECT)**. Unmodified (direct) ECT, given without anaesthesia, is banned outright.
- **ECT for minors** requires the **informed consent of the guardian and prior permission of the Mental Health Review Board**.
- **Sterilisation** of men or women is **prohibited as a treatment for mental illness**; it may never be performed on the ground that the person has a mental illness.
- **Psychosurgery** requires **informed consent of the person and approval of the MHRB**.
- The Act also prohibits **chaining in any manner**, restricts the use of seclusion and physical restraint to exceptional circumstances (only to prevent imminent harm, on a psychiatrist's instruction, with documentation), and prohibits putting a person in solitary or forced detention.

## 2. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016

The **Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016** came into force on **19 April 2017** and **repealed the Persons with Disabilities (PWD) Act, 1995**. It was enacted to give effect to India's UNCRPD obligations. It **expanded the list of recognised disabilities from 7 to 21 conditions**.

For clinical psychology, the key inclusion is that the Act recognises **mental illness, intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and several chronic neurological conditions (such as multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease)** as disabilities. This is significant because it brings psychological and neurodevelopmental conditions within the protective ambit of disability law for the first time.

The Act defines a **"person with disability"** as someone with long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairment which, in interaction with barriers, hinders full and effective participation in society. It then defines a **"person with benchmark disability"** as a person with **not less than 40% of a specified disability** where the disability has not been defined in measurable terms, or as certified by the certifying authority. The **>= 40% threshold** is the magic number to memorise: it is the eligibility cut-off for benefits such as **reservation in higher education (5%) and government jobs (4%)**, and various concessions. The Act mandates **reasonable accommodation**, **accessibility**, **non-discrimination**, and **legal capacity on an equal basis with others**, and provides for **guardianship that is limited and supportive rather than plenary**.

## 3. Professional Ethics (RCI and APA)

Indian clinical psychologists are bound by the **Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI)** framework, which draws heavily on the **American Psychological Association (APA) Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct**. The APA code rests on five aspirational principles: **(A) Beneficence and Non-maleficence; (B) Fidelity and Responsibility; (C) Integrity; (D) Justice; and (E) Respect for People's Rights and Dignity.**

### 3.1 Informed Consent

**Informed consent** is the process by which a client voluntarily agrees to assessment or treatment after being given adequate information. Three elements must all be present: **(1) capacity / competence** (the person can understand and reason about the decision), **(2) disclosure of adequate information** (nature and purpose of the service, risks and benefits, alternatives, confidentiality limits, fees), and **(3) voluntariness** (free from coercion). Consent must be **documented** and is **ongoing**, not a one-time signature. For **minors or those who lack capacity**, consent is given by a parent or guardian, but the clinician still seeks the person's **assent** (their agreement at their level of understanding).

### 3.2 Confidentiality and Its Limits

**Confidentiality** is the clinician's duty to protect information disclosed in the professional relationship. It is **not absolute**. The exam tests the recognised exceptions where a clinician **may or must break confidentiality**:

| Exception | Trigger |
|---|---|
| **Harm to self** | Imminent risk of suicide or serious self-harm |
| **Harm to others** | Serious, foreseeable threat to an identifiable third party (Duty to Warn) |
| **Child abuse / elder abuse** | Mandatory reporting of suspected abuse or neglect |
| **Court order / subpoena** | Legally compelled disclosure by a court |
| **Client consent** | The client waives confidentiality in writing |

The guiding rule is **disclose the minimum necessary** to address the specific risk, and where possible inform the client beforehand.

### 3.3 Duty to Warn, The Tarasoff Rule

The **duty to warn (and protect)** originates from **Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1976)**. The facts: Prosenjit Poddar, a patient, told his therapist he intended to kill **Tatiana Tarasoff**; the therapist did not warn her, and Poddar later killed her. The California Supreme Court held that **"the protective privilege ends where the public peril begins"**, establishing that a therapist who determines (or reasonably should determine) that a client poses a serious danger of violence to a foreseeable victim has a **legal duty to take reasonable steps to protect that intended victim**, which may include **warning the victim, notifying the police, or initiating involuntary hospitalisation**. The case evolved from an initial **duty to warn (1974)** into a broader **duty to protect (1976)**. The required conditions are a **serious threat of physical violence** against a **reasonably identifiable victim**. This is the single most cited ethics-law case in entrance exams.

### 3.4 Dual Relationships and Professional Boundaries

A **dual (or multiple) relationship** exists when a clinician occupies a second role with a client in addition to the therapeutic one (e.g. also being the client's friend, business partner, employer, teacher, or relative). These are discouraged because they **impair objectivity, risk exploitation, and create conflicts of interest**. The clearest violation is a **sexual relationship with a current client**, which is absolutely prohibited; the APA also bars sexual relationships with a **former client for at least two years** after therapy ends, and even then only in the most unusual circumstances. **Boundary crossings** (a minor, sometimes benign deviation) are distinguished from **boundary violations** (harmful, exploitative breaches). The principle is to **avoid relationships that could reasonably be expected to impair the clinician's competence, objectivity, or effectiveness, or risk harm to the client**.

### 3.5 Cultural Competence

**Cultural competence** is the clinician's ability to deliver assessment and treatment in a way that is sensitive to, and effective across, the client's **cultural, linguistic, religious, ethnic, gender, sexual-orientation, and socioeconomic background**. The APA's **Justice** and **Respect for Rights and Dignity** principles require psychologists to be **aware of their own biases**, to recognise the **limits of their competence with diverse populations**, to use **culturally valid assessment tools**, and to avoid imposing the dominant culture's norms. In the Indian context this includes sensitivity to **idioms of distress, family-centred decision making, and stigma**, and using validated, language-appropriate instruments.

### 3.6 Tele-therapy (Telepsychology) Ethics

**Tele-therapy** (telepsychology / telemental health) delivers services remotely via video, phone, or text. Indian practice is guided by the **Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (2020)** and the **Telepsychiatry Operational Guidelines (NIMHANS, 2020)**. Key ethical requirements: **verify the client's identity and location** at the outset (so that an emergency referral is possible); **obtain specific informed consent for the remote modality**, including its risks and the technology's limits; ensure **data security, encryption, and privacy** of the platform; have an **emergency / crisis protocol** with local contacts because the clinician cannot physically intervene; and practise only **within the jurisdiction and licence** in which the clinician is registered. The clinician must also assess whether tele-delivery is **clinically appropriate** for that client and condition.

## 4. RCI Registration and the Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR)

The **Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI)** is a **statutory body established under the Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992** (which became fully operational in 1993), under the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment. Its mandate is to **regulate and monitor the training of rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists)**, standardise syllabi, and maintain a register of qualified professionals so as to protect the public from **unqualified and quack practitioners**.

To practise as a **clinical psychologist in India you must be registered with the RCI**, which requires a recognised qualification such as an **M.Phil in Clinical Psychology** (the traditional RCI-recognised licensing qualification) from an RCI-approved institution. Practising without RCI registration is an **offence** under the Act.

The **Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR)** is the **official register maintained by the RCI of all qualified and registered rehabilitation professionals and personnel**. Once you complete an approved programme and apply, your name is entered in the CRR and you receive an **RCI registration number (CRR number)**. Registration is **renewable** (currently on a periodic, typically five-year, basis, with continuing rehabilitation education requirements). The CRR is, in effect, the proof of legal eligibility to practise; only those whose names appear in it may use the protected title and provide clinical services.

## High-Yield Recap

- **MHCA 2017**: assented 2017, in force **29 May 2018**; repealed Mental Health Act **1987**; rights-based, UNCRPD-driven. Mental illness definition **excludes intellectual disability**.
- **Advance directive**: written, future-care wishes; registered with and reviewable by the **Mental Health Review Board**.
- **Nominated representative (NR)**: decision-maker when capacity is lost; default order ends with **Director, Dept. of Social Welfare**.
- **CMHA (national) and SMHA (state)**: register establishments, maintain registers of clinical psychologists / psychiatric social workers / nurses.
- **Mental Health Review Board**: quasi-judicial; handles advance directives, appoints NRs, hears complaints, reviews admissions over **30 days**; appeals go to the **High Court**.
- **Section 115**: attempted suicide → **presumed severe stress (rebuttable)**, not punished under IPC 309; government must provide care and rehabilitation. **Effective decriminalisation**.
- **Banned/regulated**: **unmodified ECT prohibited** (only modified, with anaesthesia + muscle relaxant); ECT on minors needs guardian consent + MHRB; **sterilisation for mental illness prohibited**; psychosurgery needs consent + MHRB; chaining banned.
- **RPwD Act 2016**: in force **19 April 2017**; repealed PWD Act 1995; disabilities **7 → 21**; includes **mental illness, intellectual disability, SLD, ASD, neurological conditions**.
- **Benchmark disability = >= 40%**; reservation 5% in higher education, 4% in government jobs.
- **Informed consent** = capacity + adequate disclosure + voluntariness; minors give **assent**, guardians give consent.
- **Confidentiality limits**: harm to self, harm to others, child/elder abuse, court order, client waiver; disclose **minimum necessary**.
- **Tarasoff (1976)**: "protective privilege ends where public peril begins"; **duty to warn/protect** a reasonably identifiable victim from a serious threat of violence.
- **Dual relationships**: avoid; sexual contact with current client absolutely barred; former client barred at least **2 years**. Boundary crossing vs boundary violation.
- **Cultural competence** and **tele-therapy ethics** (identity/location verification, modality-specific consent, encryption, crisis protocol, jurisdiction).
- **RCI**: statutory body under the **RCI Act 1992**; regulates rehabilitation professionals; M.Phil Clinical Psychology is the licensing route; practising unregistered is an offence.
- **Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR)**: RCI's official register of qualified professionals; entry = legal eligibility to practise; periodically renewable.
