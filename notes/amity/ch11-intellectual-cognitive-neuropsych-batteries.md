# Ch 11: Intellectual, Cognitive & Neuropsychological Batteries

This chapter covers the major standardized instruments used to measure intelligence, cognition, and brain function: the Wechsler scales, the Stanford-Binet, the Indian adaptations (Bhatia's Battery, Seguin Form Board), and the three classic neuropsychological batteries (Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska, NIMHANS). These tests are heavily examined because the RCI clinical syllabus treats psychometric assessment as a core clinical skill, and NIMHANS-pattern papers love subtest names, index structures, and the specific cognitive domain each test taps.

## The Wechsler Scales (WAIS-IV / WISC-V)

David **Wechsler** developed his scales in deliberate opposition to the single global mental-age model of the early Binet. He defined intelligence as "the global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment." His scales therefore yield not just one number but a profile of distinct ability domains. The **WAIS-IV** (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale, 4th edition, 2008) is used for ages 16 to 90, and the **WISC-V** (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, 5th edition, 2014) is for ages 6 to 16; the **WPPSI** covers preschoolers. All Wechsler scales use a **deviation IQ** with a mean of **100** and a standard deviation of **15**, replacing the old ratio IQ (MA/CA × 100). Subtest **scaled scores** have a mean of **10** and an SD of **3**.

### FSIQ and the Four Index Scores

The **Full Scale IQ (FSIQ)** is the single most comprehensive estimate of general intelligence (g), derived from the core subtests. Beneath it sit four **index scores**, each capturing a narrower domain. (Note: WISC-V split the old Perceptual Reasoning into separate Visual Spatial and Fluid Reasoning indices, giving it five indices; WAIS-IV retains the four below.)

| Index | Full name | What it measures | Core subtests (WAIS-IV) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VCI** | Verbal Comprehension Index | Acquired verbal knowledge, verbal reasoning, concept formation | Similarities, Vocabulary, Information |
| **PRI** | Perceptual Reasoning Index | Non-verbal/fluid reasoning, spatial processing, visual-motor integration | Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, Visual Puzzles |
| **WMI** | Working Memory Index | Attention, concentration, mental manipulation of information | Digit Span, Arithmetic |
| **PSI** | Processing Speed Index | Speed of visual scanning and graphomotor processing | Symbol Search, Coding |

**Verbal Comprehension (VCI)** subtests: **Similarities** asks how two concepts are alike (for example, "How are an apple and a banana alike?"), tapping abstract verbal reasoning and concept formation. **Vocabulary** asks the examinee to define words and is one of the best single indicators of general intelligence and a strong "hold" subtest (resistant to brain damage and aging). **Information** taps the breadth of general factual knowledge acquired from the environment, reflecting crystallized intelligence and long-term retrieval.

**Perceptual Reasoning (PRI)** subtests: **Block Design** requires reproducing a two-dimensional geometric pattern using red-and-white blocks; it is timed and is highly sensitive to right-hemisphere and parietal damage and to visuoconstructive deficits. **Matrix Reasoning** presents an incomplete visual matrix and asks the examinee to choose the piece that completes the pattern, measuring fluid intelligence with minimal verbal or motor demand. **Visual Puzzles** asks which three pieces could combine to make a target shape, tapping mental rotation and spatial analysis without a motor component.

**Working Memory (WMI)** subtests: **Digit Span** has three conditions: Forward (repeat digits in order, a measure of attention span), Backward (repeat in reverse, requiring mental manipulation), and Sequencing (reorder into ascending sequence). **Arithmetic** presents orally administered word problems solved without paper, taxing working memory plus quantitative reasoning.

**Processing Speed (PSI)** subtests: **Symbol Search** requires scanning a row to decide whether a target symbol appears, under time pressure. **Coding** (called Digit Symbol-Coding) requires rapidly pairing symbols with digits using a key; it is a sensitive but non-specific indicator of brain dysfunction, attention, and motor speed.

### GAI vs CPI

Because the WMI and PSI subtests are highly sensitive to attention, anxiety, learning disorders, and neurological disruption, Wechsler clinicians often compute two alternative composites. The **General Ability Index (GAI)** is derived from only the VCI and PRI subtests (verbal comprehension plus perceptual reasoning); it estimates intellectual ability while minimizing the influence of working memory and processing speed. The **Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI)** is derived from the WMI and PSI subtests and reflects the efficiency with which information is processed. A large **GAI greater than CPI** discrepancy is clinically meaningful: it suggests strong reasoning ability undercut by inefficient working memory or slow processing speed, a pattern seen in ADHD, specific learning disorders, and the early stages of some neurological conditions.

## Stanford-Binet, Fifth Edition (SB5)

The Stanford-Binet traces to **Alfred Binet** and **Theodore Simon** (1905 Paris scale) and was adapted at Stanford by **Lewis Terman** (1916), which introduced the term **IQ** (Intelligence Quotient) into American testing. The current **SB5** (Roid, 2003) covers ages 2 to 85+, uses a deviation IQ with mean **100** and SD **15**, and is notable for its **routing**: an initial routing subtest sets the starting difficulty so that testing begins near the examinee's ability level, then proceeds to a basal (lowest level all passed) and ceiling (level at which items are failed).

The SB5 is organized around **five factors**, each assessed in both a **verbal** and a **nonverbal** domain (giving a 5 × 2 structure), which yields a **Full Scale IQ**, a **Verbal IQ**, and a **Nonverbal IQ**.

| SB5 factor | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Fluid Reasoning** | Novel problem-solving, inductive and deductive reasoning, not reliant on prior learning |
| **Knowledge** | Accumulated general information and crystallized learning (vocabulary, facts) |
| **Quantitative Reasoning** | Numerical concepts and problem-solving with quantitative relationships |
| **Visual-Spatial Processing** | Analyzing spatial patterns, orientation, and part-whole relationships |
| **Working Memory** | Storing and transforming information held briefly in mind |

The five-factor model is explicitly grounded in the **Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC)** theory of cognitive abilities, which makes the SB5 a frequent comparison point with the Wechsler in exam questions about theory-driven test design.

## Indian Adaptations

### Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests of Intelligence

**C. M. Bhatia** (1955) created this performance battery specifically for the **Indian population**, where literacy and language barriers made verbal Western tests unfair. Its great advantage is that it is largely **non-verbal** and can be administered to **illiterate** and **rural** examinees. The standard age range is roughly **11 to 16** years (with extension formulae for adults). It comprises **five subtests**:

1. **Kohs Block Design Test**: reproducing colored designs with cubes, measuring visuomotor coordination and analytic-synthetic ability (adapted from Samuel Kohs's original).
2. **Alexander's Pass-Along Test**: sliding colored blocks along a tray to match a target arrangement in the fewest moves, measuring spatial planning and problem-solving.
3. **Pattern Drawing Test**: reproducing patterns by drawing without lifting the pencil or retracing a line, measuring visuomotor control and persistence.
4. **Immediate Memory Test**: recall of digits forward and backward, measuring short-term/working memory span.
5. **Picture Construction Test**: assembling cut pieces into a meaningful picture, measuring synthetic ability and perceptual organization.

Scores convert to a mental age and then to an IQ. Bhatia's Battery remains widely used in Indian clinical and educational settings and is a near-certain exam item because of its India-specific origin.

### Seguin Form Board Test (SFBT)

Developed by **Edouard Seguin**, the Seguin Form Board is a simple performance test in which the examinee fits ten differently shaped wooden blocks into matching recesses on a board as quickly as possible. The score is based on the **time taken** across (typically three) trials, with the **best/shortest time** used to estimate mental age and IQ. It chiefly measures **eye-hand coordination, visual-motor speed, and shape discrimination**. It is widely used in India for young children (roughly ages 3 to 7) and for the rapid screening of **intellectual disability**, and is valued because it requires no language at all.

## Neuropsychological Batteries

Neuropsychological tests aim to infer the integrity of specific brain systems from performance on standardized cognitive tasks, linking deficits to localization (which region) and lateralization (which hemisphere).

### Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery (HRB)

Begun by **Ward Halstead** and extended by **Ralph Reitan**, the HRB is a fixed (standardized) battery whose scores combine into an **Impairment Index** (the proportion of tests falling in the brain-damaged range) used to detect the presence, location, and severity of brain dysfunction. Its core tests:

- **Category Test**: the examinee infers an organizing principle behind sets of stimuli from feedback; a sensitive measure of **abstraction, concept formation, and executive reasoning** (frontal lobe).
- **Tactual Performance Test (TPT)**: blindfolded, the examinee places blocks into a form board using each hand and both hands, then draws the board from memory; measures **tactile/kinesthetic perception, motor speed, spatial memory, and incidental learning**.
- **Seashore Rhythm Test**: discriminating whether pairs of rhythmic beats are the same or different; measures **sustained attention and non-verbal auditory perception**.
- **Speech Sounds Perception Test**: matching spoken nonsense syllables to written choices; measures **auditory-verbal perception and attention** (left-hemisphere/language-linked).
- **Finger Tapping Test (Finger Oscillation)**: tapping a key as fast as possible with the index finger of each hand; a pure **motor speed** measure whose left-right comparison helps **lateralize** a lesion (the hand contralateral to a damaged hemisphere is slower).

The HRB is also typically paired with the Trail Making Test, grip strength, and an aphasia screen.

### Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (LNNB)

Based on the qualitative clinical theories of **Alexander Luria** and standardized by **Charles Golden**, the LNNB is shorter than the HRB and yields scores on **11 clinical scales** (sometimes listed as 14 with additional/summary scales). The eleven clinical scales are: **Motor functions, Rhythm (acoustic-motor), Tactile functions, Visual functions, Receptive speech, Expressive speech, Writing, Reading, Arithmetic, Memory, and Intellectual processes**. Each item is scored 0 (normal), 1 (borderline), or 2 (abnormal), and scale elevations above a critical level flag dysfunction. The LNNB bridges Luria's flexible, hypothesis-testing approach with the psychometric rigor of a standardized battery.

### NIMHANS Neuropsychological Battery

Developed at the **National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru**, this battery is normed on the Indian population and is the most exam-relevant for NIMHANS-pattern papers. It is organized by **cognitive domain**, with each domain probed by named tests:

- **Mental Status Examination (MSE) / focal signs**: an initial screen of orientation, level of consciousness, and focal neurological signs (for example, apraxia, agnosia, aphasia) to flag gross deficits before detailed testing.
- **Attention**: the **Color Cancellation Test** (crossing out target colored stimuli scattered among distractors, measuring focused/selective visual attention and visual scanning) and the **Digit Vigilance Test** (scanning rows of digits to cancel target digits within a time limit, measuring sustained attention).
- **Executive functions**: the **N-Back Test** (responding when a stimulus matches the one presented N steps earlier, measuring working memory and updating); the **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST)**, where the examinee sorts cards by a rule that changes without warning, with **perseverative errors** (continuing to sort by the old, no-longer-correct rule) being the hallmark index of **frontal/prefrontal dysfunction and cognitive inflexibility**; the **Tower of London**, which measures planning and sequencing by requiring the fewest moves to match a target bead arrangement; and the **Stroop Test**, where naming the ink color of color-words that spell a different color produces interference, measuring **response inhibition and selective attention** (the Stroop effect).
- **Memory and learning**: the **Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT)**, in which a 15-word list is read over repeated trials to measure immediate span, learning across trials, interference, and delayed recall; tests of **visual retention** (for example, reproducing geometric designs from memory, as in the Benton Visual Retention Test); and comprehensive memory scales such as the **Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS)** and the Indian **PGI Memory Scale** (developed at PGIMER Chandigarh by Pershad and Wig, with subtests covering remote/recent memory, mental balance, attention, delayed and immediate recall, retention, recognition, and so on).

## Psychometric Foundations: Reliability, Validity, Standardization, Norms

These four properties determine whether any of the above scores can be trusted, and are frequently asked in their own right.

- **Reliability** is the **consistency** of measurement. Key types: **test-retest** (stability over time), **internal consistency** (Cronbach's alpha, split-half), **inter-rater** (agreement between scorers, vital for the qualitative judgments in neuropsych testing), and **parallel-forms** (equivalence of alternate versions). Major Wechsler and SB5 reliabilities typically exceed **0.90** for the full scale.
- **Validity** is whether the test **measures what it claims** to. Key types: **content** (items represent the domain), **criterion** (correlation with an outcome, split into concurrent and predictive), and **construct** (the test behaves as the underlying theory predicts, supported by convergent and discriminant evidence). For neuropsych tests, **ecological validity** (whether scores predict real-world functioning) is an added concern.
- **Standardization** means administering and scoring the test under **uniform, fixed procedures** so that every examinee's experience is comparable; deviations (extra prompting, untimed administration) invalidate normative comparison.
- **Norms** are the **reference distribution** of scores from a large, representative **standardization sample** against which an individual is compared. This is exactly why Indian adaptations (Bhatia, NIMHANS battery, PGI Memory Scale) matter: a test normed on a Western sample can misclassify Indian examinees, so culturally appropriate, locally normed instruments are essential for fair and valid clinical decisions.

## High-Yield Recap

- **Wechsler**: deviation IQ, mean **100**, SD **15**; subtest scaled scores mean **10**, SD **3**. WAIS-IV ages 16-90; WISC-V ages 6-16.
- **Four WAIS-IV indices**: **VCI** (Similarities, Vocabulary, Information), **PRI** (Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, Visual Puzzles), **WMI** (Digit Span, Arithmetic), **PSI** (Symbol Search, Coding).
- **GAI** = VCI + PRI (ability minus efficiency); **CPI** = WMI + PSI (processing efficiency). GAI > CPI suggests ADHD/SLD pattern.
- **SB5**: five factors = **Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing, Working Memory**; each verbal + nonverbal; grounded in **CHC theory**; uses routing/basal/ceiling.
- **Stanford-Binet history**: Binet-Simon (1905) → Terman at Stanford (1916, coined "IQ").
- **Bhatia's Battery** (C. M. Bhatia, 1955, India, non-verbal): **Kohs Block Design, Alexander Pass-Along, Pattern Drawing, Immediate Memory, Picture Construction**.
- **Seguin Form Board**: ten shapes into recesses; scored by **best time**; for young children / ID screening; non-verbal.
- **Halstead-Reitan**: **Category Test** (abstraction), **Tactual Performance**, **Seashore Rhythm** (auditory attention), **Speech Sounds Perception**, **Finger Tapping** (motor speed, lateralization); yields **Impairment Index**.
- **Luria-Nebraska** (Luria → Golden): **11 clinical scales** (Motor, Rhythm, Tactile, Visual, Receptive Speech, Expressive Speech, Writing, Reading, Arithmetic, Memory, Intellectual Processes); items scored 0/1/2.
- **NIMHANS battery** by domain: Attention (**Color Cancellation, Digit Vigilance**); Executive (**N-Back, WCST perseverative errors, Tower of London, Stroop**); Memory (**RAVLT, visual retention, WMS / PGI Memory Scale**).
- **WCST perseverative errors** = key marker of **frontal lobe dysfunction**. **Stroop** = response inhibition / selective attention.
- **Psychometrics**: reliability (test-retest, internal consistency, inter-rater, parallel-forms) = consistency; validity (content, criterion, construct) = measures what it claims; standardization = uniform procedure; norms = representative reference sample (why Indian adaptations matter).
