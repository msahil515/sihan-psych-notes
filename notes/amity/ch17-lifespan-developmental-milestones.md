# Ch 17: Lifespan Developmental Milestones

This chapter covers the five "must-know" developmental frameworks that show up on almost every RCI / NIMHANS-pattern paper: Piaget's cognitive stages, Vygotsky's sociocultural theory, Erikson's eight psychosocial stages, Kohlberg's moral development (with Gilligan's critique), and attachment theory (Bowlby and Ainsworth). For each, know the stage names, the age bands, the defining phenomena, and the key figures, because items are usually matching ("which stage shows X?") or attribution ("who proposed Y?").

## Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development

Jean Piaget was a Swiss biologist and epistemologist who studied how children **construct** knowledge. He saw the child as a "little scientist" actively building mental structures, not a passive recipient. His theory is **constructivist** and **stage-based** (discontinuous, invariant sequence, universal).

### Core mechanisms: schemas, assimilation, accommodation, equilibration

A **schema** (plural schemata) is an organized mental structure or "unit" of knowledge, a pattern of action or thought used to make sense of experience (e.g., a "grasping schema" or a "dog schema"). Development proceeds by modifying schemas through two complementary processes:

- **Assimilation**: interpreting new information by fitting it into an *existing* schema. A child who calls every four-legged animal "doggie" is assimilating cats and horses into the dog schema. No structural change happens; the world is bent to fit the mind.
- **Accommodation**: *changing* an existing schema (or creating a new one) when new information will not fit. When the child learns "that is a cat, not a doggie," the dog schema is modified and a new cat schema forms. Here the mind is bent to fit the world.

**Equilibration** is the master process that drives development: the child seeks a balance (**equilibrium**) between assimilation and accommodation. When existing schemas cannot handle new input, the child experiences **disequilibrium** (cognitive conflict), and the resulting effort to restore balance forces schemas to reorganize, pushing the child to the next stage. **Organization** (combining schemas into higher-order systems) and **adaptation** (assimilation + accommodation together) are Piaget's two innate "functional invariants."

### The four stages

| Stage | Age (approx.) | Defining achievements and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensorimotor** | 0–2 yrs | Knows world through senses and motor action; develops **object permanence** |
| **Preoperational** | 2–7 yrs | Symbolic thought, language; but **egocentrism**, **animism**, **centration**, lacks **conservation** |
| **Concrete operational** | 7–11 yrs | Logical operations on concrete objects: **conservation**, **reversibility**, **transitivity**, **seriation**, **classification** |
| **Formal operational** | 11+ yrs | **Abstract** and **hypothetico-deductive** reasoning, systematic problem solving |

**Sensorimotor stage (0–2 years).** Intelligence is expressed through sensory and motor activity rather than symbols. The crowning achievement is **object permanence**: the understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen, heard, or touched. Piaget tested this by hiding a toy under a cloth; before about 8 months the infant does not search ("out of sight, out of mind"), but later the infant lifts the cloth. (Piaget subdivided this stage into six substages, ending with the beginning of mental representation.)

**Preoperational stage (2–7 years).** The child can now use symbols (words, images, pretend play) but thinking is "preoperational," meaning it lacks logical operations. Key limitations:

- **Egocentrism**: inability to take another person's perspective; the child assumes others see, feel, and know what they do. Piaget's classic **three-mountains task** demonstrated this: a child seated at a model of three mountains is asked what a doll seated on the opposite side "sees," and the preoperational child picks their *own* view.
- **Animism**: attributing life, feelings, and intentions to inanimate objects ("the sun is angry," "the table is naughty for hurting me").
- **Centration**: focusing on one salient dimension of a situation while ignoring others (e.g., only the height of liquid, not the width).
- **Lack of conservation**: failure to grasp that quantity stays the same despite changes in appearance. In the classic liquid task, water poured from a short wide glass into a tall thin glass is judged as "more" because the child centers on height.
- Other markers: **irreversibility** (cannot mentally reverse a sequence) and a focus on appearance over reality.

**Concrete operational stage (7–11 years).** The child acquires **operations** (internalized, reversible mental actions) but only for *concrete*, tangible content. Key gains:

- **Conservation**: understanding that quantity (number, mass, volume, length) is invariant despite changes in form. Conservation of number appears earliest, then mass and length, then volume (this staggered onset is called **horizontal décalage**).
- **Reversibility**: the ability to mentally undo an action (e.g., the water can be poured back).
- **Transitivity**: logical inference of relations, e.g., if A > B and B > C, then A > C.
- **Seriation**: arranging items in order along a quantitative dimension (e.g., sticks shortest to longest).
- **Classification** and **class inclusion**: grouping objects and understanding that a subclass is included in a larger class (e.g., there are more "animals" than "dogs").
- **Decentration**: attending to multiple dimensions at once, overcoming centration and egocentrism.

**Formal operational stage (11 years onward).** Reasoning becomes **abstract** and detached from concrete reality. Hallmarks:

- **Hypothetico-deductive reasoning**: generating hypotheses and systematically testing them, as in Piaget's **pendulum problem** where the adolescent isolates variables (length, weight, push) one at a time.
- Reasoning about possibilities, abstractions, ideals, and the future; understanding metaphor and propositional logic.
- Adolescent egocentrism re-emerges in a new form (Elkind's "imaginary audience" and "personal fable"), though that is an elaboration, not Piaget's own term.

**Exam relevance and critiques**: Piaget is criticized for *underestimating* children (with simpler tasks, object permanence and perspective-taking appear earlier), for fuzzy stage boundaries, for cultural and schooling bias, and because not all adults reach formal operations. Compare with the **neo-Piagetians** and **information-processing** views.

## Vygotsky's Sociocultural Theory

Lev Vygotsky, a Russian psychologist, argued that cognitive development is fundamentally **social** and **cultural**: higher mental functions originate in interaction with more knowledgeable others and are mediated by **cultural tools**, above all *language*. Where Piaget said development drives learning, Vygotsky said **learning leads development**.

- **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)**: the gap between what a learner can do *independently* (actual developmental level) and what they can do *with guidance* from a more competent person (potential level). Learning is most effective inside the ZPD, just beyond current independent ability. "What a child can do with assistance today she will be able to do by herself tomorrow."
- **Scaffolding** (a term coined by Wood, Bruner, and Ross, not Vygotsky himself, but tightly linked to the ZPD): the temporary, adjustable support a tutor provides, gradually withdrawn as competence grows, like the scaffolding removed once a building stands. The **More Knowledgeable Other (MKO)** can be a parent, teacher, or even a more capable peer.
- **Private speech to inner speech**: Vygotsky observed children talking aloud to themselves while solving problems (**private speech** / egocentric speech). Unlike Piaget (who saw such speech as immature and non-social), Vygotsky argued it is *self-guiding* and that it gradually becomes silent **inner speech**, the basis of verbal thought and self-regulation. The progression is: social (external) speech → private (egocentric) speech → inner speech.

**Exam relevance**: contrast Piaget (individual constructivist, biologically driven stages, child as solitary scientist) with Vygotsky (social constructivist, culture and language central, child as apprentice). Reciprocal teaching and guided participation are applied extensions.

## Erikson's Eight Psychosocial Stages

Erik Erikson extended Freud's psychosexual theory across the *whole lifespan* and emphasized **psychosocial** (social/ego) conflicts rather than sexual ones. Each stage presents a **crisis** (a turning point) framed as a tension between two outcomes. Successful resolution yields a **virtue** (basic strength); failure leaves a lasting deficit. The stages are **epigenetic** (unfold in a fixed order, each building on the last).

| # | Stage (conflict) | Age | Virtue / strength | Core question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Trust vs. Mistrust** | 0–1 yr (infancy) | **Hope** | Can I trust the world? |
| 2 | **Autonomy vs. Shame and Doubt** | 1–3 yrs (toddler) | **Will** | Can I do things myself? |
| 3 | **Initiative vs. Guilt** | 3–6 yrs (preschool) | **Purpose** | Is it okay to act and plan? |
| 4 | **Industry vs. Inferiority** | 6–12 yrs (school age) | **Competence** | Can I make it in the world of skills? |
| 5 | **Identity vs. Role Confusion** | 12–18 yrs (adolescence) | **Fidelity** | Who am I? |
| 6 | **Intimacy vs. Isolation** | young adulthood | **Love** | Can I share life with another? |
| 7 | **Generativity vs. Stagnation** | middle adulthood | **Care** | Can I make my life count? |
| 8 | **Integrity vs. Despair** | late adulthood | **Wisdom** | Was my life meaningful? |

Brief mechanism per stage: **(1)** consistent, responsive caregiving builds basic trust and the virtue of hope; neglect breeds mistrust. **(2)** Toilet training and self-feeding let the toddler exercise autonomy; over-control or shaming produces shame and self-doubt. **(3)** The child initiates play, questions, and goals; excessive criticism of this assertiveness produces guilt. **(4)** School demands productivity and skill; success yields a sense of competence, repeated failure breeds inferiority. **(5)** Adolescents experiment with roles to form a coherent **identity**; failure leaves **role confusion** (Erikson coined the term *identity crisis*). **(6)** Young adults seek committed, intimate relationships; fear of fusion or rejection leads to isolation. **(7)** Adults express **generativity** by guiding the next generation (parenting, mentoring, productive work); its absence is self-absorbed stagnation. **(8)** In old age, reflecting on a life lived well yields ego integrity and wisdom; regret yields despair.

**Exam relevance**: memorize the conflict–virtue–age triad. Identity vs. role confusion (adolescence) and the *identity crisis* concept are the highest-yield items.

## Kohlberg's Theory of Moral Development

Lawrence Kohlberg studied moral *reasoning* (the justification, not the choice) using moral dilemmas, most famously the **Heinz dilemma**: a man named Heinz cannot afford an overpriced drug to save his dying wife and considers stealing it. Kohlberg coded *why* people said Heinz should or should not steal. He proposed **three levels**, each with **two stages** (six total), an invariant, universal sequence.

| Level | Stage | Basis of moral judgment |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-conventional** (rules are external; self-interest) | **1. Obedience and Punishment** | Avoid punishment; "stealing is wrong because you get caught" |
| | **2. Instrumental / Individualism and Exchange** | Self-interest, "what's in it for me," simple reciprocity ("you scratch my back") |
| **Conventional** (conformity to social rules and approval) | **3. Good Boy / Nice Girl (interpersonal)** | Seeking approval, being seen as good, living up to others' expectations |
| | **4. Law and Order (social system)** | Upholding laws, authority, and duty to maintain social order |
| **Post-conventional** (abstract principles above law) | **5. Social Contract** | Laws are social agreements that can be changed for the greater good; recognizes rights |
| | **6. Universal Ethical Principles** | Self-chosen universal principles (justice, dignity); conscience above written law |

Key facts: most children are **pre-conventional**; most adolescents and adults reason at the **conventional** level (especially stage 4); **post-conventional** reasoning is relatively rare, and stage 6 is so rare Kohlberg later treated it as more theoretical than empirical.

**Gilligan's ethics of care critique**: Carol Gilligan (a student/colleague of Kohlberg) argued his theory was **gender-biased** because it was built on all-male samples and ranked an abstract, justice-based "**ethic of justice**" (more typical of how men were socialized) above a relationship- and compassion-based "**ethic of care**" (more typical of how women were socialized). Under Kohlberg's scheme women often scored at stage 3, which Gilligan argued reflected a *different but equally valid* moral orientation, not inferior moral development. Her work appears in *In a Different Voice* (1982). **Exam relevance**: pair "ethics of care / gender bias critique" with Gilligan, and "ethics of justice / six stages / Heinz dilemma" with Kohlberg.

## Attachment Theory

### Bowlby: the evolutionary basis and internal working models

John Bowlby proposed that **attachment** (the enduring emotional bond between infant and caregiver) is a biologically programmed, evolved system that promotes survival by keeping the infant close to a protector. He drew on ethology (Lorenz's imprinting) and emphasized a **secure base** from which the infant explores and a **safe haven** to return to under threat. Bowlby argued for a sensitive period for attachment formation and warned that prolonged disruption causes lasting harm (his **maternal deprivation hypothesis**). The infant builds an **internal working model (IWM)**: a mental representation of the self, the attachment figure, and relationships ("Am I worthy of care? Are others reliable?") that guides expectations in later relationships across the lifespan.

### Ainsworth: the Strange Situation and attachment classifications

Mary Ainsworth operationalized attachment with the **Strange Situation**, a structured 20-minute laboratory procedure of eight episodes involving separations from and reunions with the caregiver, plus a stranger, designed for infants around 12–18 months. Classification rests chiefly on the infant's behavior at **reunion**. She identified three patterns; Main and Solomon later added a fourth.

| Pattern | Behavior in Strange Situation | Caregiving correlate |
|---|---|---|
| **Secure** (Type B) | Uses parent as secure base; distressed at separation; *actively seeks and is comforted* at reunion, then resumes play | Sensitive, responsive caregiving |
| **Insecure-avoidant** (Type A) | Little distress at separation; *avoids or ignores* parent at reunion; treats stranger similarly | Unresponsive, rejecting, or intrusive care |
| **Insecure-resistant / ambivalent** (Type C) | Very distressed at separation; at reunion *seeks contact yet resists/angry*, hard to soothe, ambivalent | Inconsistent caregiving |
| **Disorganized / disoriented** (Type D, **Main & Solomon, 1986**) | Contradictory, confused, frozen, or fearful behavior with no coherent strategy | Frightening/frightened caregiving, abuse, unresolved trauma/loss |

Approximate distribution in low-risk samples: secure ~60–65%, avoidant ~20%, resistant ~10–15%, disorganized ~5–15% (higher in maltreatment samples). **Disorganized attachment** is the strongest infant predictor of later psychopathology, which makes it clinically high-yield.

**Exam relevance**: link **Bowlby** = theory/IWM/secure base, **Ainsworth** = Strange Situation + three original types, **Main & Solomon** = disorganized (Type D). Do not confuse Ainsworth (attachment) with Harlow (the rhesus monkey wire-vs-cloth "contact comfort" studies, which independently support attachment over feeding-based bonds).

## High-Yield Recap

- **Piaget mechanisms**: assimilation (fit world to schema), accommodation (change schema to fit world), equilibration (drive to balance the two via disequilibrium).
- **Piaget stages**: Sensorimotor 0–2 (object permanence) → Preoperational 2–7 (egocentrism/three-mountains, animism, centration, no conservation) → Concrete operational 7–11 (conservation, reversibility, transitivity, seriation, classification) → Formal operational 11+ (abstract, hypothetico-deductive, pendulum task).
- **Vygotsky**: ZPD = gap between independent and assisted performance; scaffolding (Wood/Bruner/Ross) = fading support; MKO; speech path = social → private (egocentric) → inner speech; learning leads development.
- **Erikson 8 stages → virtues**: Trust/Mistrust→Hope; Autonomy/Shame→Will; Initiative/Guilt→Purpose; Industry/Inferiority→Competence; Identity/Role Confusion→Fidelity; Intimacy/Isolation→Love; Generativity/Stagnation→Care; Integrity/Despair→Wisdom. Coined "identity crisis."
- **Kohlberg**: Heinz dilemma; Pre-conventional (1 obedience/punishment, 2 instrumental/exchange), Conventional (3 good boy/nice girl, 4 law and order), Post-conventional (5 social contract, 6 universal ethical principles).
- **Gilligan**: gender-bias critique; ethics of **care** vs. Kohlberg's ethics of **justice**; *In a Different Voice* (1982).
- **Bowlby**: evolutionary attachment, secure base/safe haven, internal working models, maternal deprivation.
- **Ainsworth Strange Situation**: Secure (B), Insecure-avoidant (A), Insecure-resistant/ambivalent (C); **disorganized (D)** added by **Main & Solomon**. Classification judged at reunion; secure ~60–65%.
