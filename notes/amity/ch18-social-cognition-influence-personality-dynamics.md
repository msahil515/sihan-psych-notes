# Ch 18: Social Cognition, Influence & Personality Dynamics

This chapter covers how people explain behaviour (attribution), how thoughts and behaviours are kept consistent (dissonance), how groups and individuals bend behaviour (conformity, obedience, compliance, group processes), and the major trait and social-learning theories of personality. These topics are heavily sampled on RCI and NIMHANS-pattern papers, usually as "name the experiment / name the bias / match the theorist" items, so precise figures, criteria, and the names attached to each effect are what win marks.

## 1. Attribution: How We Explain Behaviour

**Attribution** is the cognitive process of assigning a cause to a behaviour or event, our own or someone else's. It is the core of **social cognition** (how we perceive, interpret, and store information about the social world).

### Heider's Naive Psychology: Internal vs External

**Fritz Heider** (1958, *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations*) is the father of attribution theory. He argued ordinary people act as "naive scientists" who seek the causes of behaviour. He drew the fundamental distinction:

- **Internal (dispositional) attribution:** the cause is located inside the person, such as their personality, ability, effort, mood, or attitude. ("He failed because he is lazy.")
- **External (situational) attribution:** the cause is located in the environment or circumstances, such as luck, task difficulty, social pressure, or the weather. ("He failed because the exam was unfair.")

Heider noted a built-in human bias to over-attribute behaviour to internal dispositions because the actor is the most perceptually salient feature of a scene (we see the person, not the invisible pressures on them).

### Kelley's Covariation Model

**Harold Kelley** (1967) formalised attribution into the **covariation principle**: we attribute an effect to the cause with which it covaries (co-occurs) across observations. To decide whether a behaviour is internal or external, the perceiver weighs three kinds of information:

| Dimension | Question it answers | Example (Person laughs at a comedian) |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Do other people behave the same way toward this stimulus? | Does everyone laugh at this comedian? |
| **Distinctiveness** | Does this person behave this way only toward this stimulus, or toward many? | Does this person laugh at every comedian, or only this one? |
| **Consistency** | Does this person behave this way toward this stimulus across time and situations? | Does the person laugh at this comedian every time? |

The classic combinations to memorise:

- **High consensus + High distinctiveness + High consistency = External attribution** (the comedian really is funny; everyone laughs, this person only laughs at this comedian, and always does).
- **Low consensus + Low distinctiveness + High consistency = Internal attribution** (this person laughs at everything and no one else laughs; the cause is the person).

When **consistency is low**, attributions become unstable and people fall back on discounting or special circumstances.

### Attributional Biases and Errors

- **Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE):** also called **correspondence bias** (term coined by **Lee Ross**, 1977). The pervasive tendency to overestimate dispositional (internal) causes and underestimate situational (external) causes when explaining the behaviour of OTHERS. Classic demonstration: Jones and Harris (1967) quiz-show / essay study, where observers attributed a pro-Castro essay to the writer's true attitude even when told the writer was assigned the position by coin flip.
- **Actor-Observer Bias (Jones & Nisbett, 1971):** we attribute OUR OWN behaviour to situational causes but OTHERS' behaviour to dispositional causes. ("I tripped because the floor was wet; he tripped because he is clumsy.") This happens because of differing perceptual focus (the actor sees the situation, the observer sees the actor) and differing information (we know our own past variability).
- **Self-Serving Bias:** we take internal credit for our successes (ability, effort) but make external attributions for our failures (bad luck, unfair task). It protects and enhances **self-esteem**. It can reverse into a self-effacing bias in some collectivist cultures or in depression.
- **Just-World Hypothesis (Melvin Lerner, 1965):** the belief that the world is fair and people get what they deserve and deserve what they get. It leads to **blaming the victim** (assuming rape, poverty, or illness victims must have done something to deserve it), because admitting random misfortune threatens the comforting belief that one is safe if one behaves well.

## 2. Cognitive Dissonance: Festinger

**Leon Festinger** (1957) proposed **cognitive dissonance theory**: holding two psychologically inconsistent cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, or awareness of one's behaviour) produces an uncomfortable state of tension (**dissonance**), which the person is motivated to reduce. Reduction routes are: change a cognition, add new consonant cognitions, reduce the importance of the dissonant cognition, or change the behaviour.

**The $1 / $20 forced-compliance study (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959):** participants did an extremely boring task (turning pegs), then were paid either **$1 or $20** to tell a waiting "next participant" that the task was fun and interesting. Later, when rating how enjoyable the task really was, the **$1 group rated the task as significantly more enjoyable** than the $20 group. The counter-intuitive result is the key: the $20 group had a large external justification ("I lied for the money"), so they felt little dissonance. The $1 group had insufficient external justification, so they reduced the dissonance between "I said it was fun" and "it was boring" by genuinely convincing themselves the task was enjoyable. This is the principle of **insufficient justification** and shows that attitudes follow behaviour when external incentive is small.

## 3. Social Influence

### Asch Conformity Studies

**Solomon Asch** (1951) studied **conformity** (changing behaviour or belief to match a group). Participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard line, an obvious perceptual task, while seated among confederates who unanimously gave the wrong answer. About **75% of participants conformed to at least one wrong answer**, and on roughly **one-third (about 33%) of critical trials** participants conformed. Key moderators: conformity rose with group size up to about **3-4 members** then plateaued, and conformity **dropped sharply (to about a quarter of its level) when even one ally** also dissented (breaking unanimity).

Two motives for conformity (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955):

- **Informational social influence:** conforming because we believe others have accurate information, especially in ambiguous situations; produces genuine private acceptance (internalisation).
- **Normative social influence:** conforming to gain approval and avoid rejection, even when we privately disagree; produces public compliance without private change.

### Milgram Obedience Studies

**Stanley Milgram** (1963, Yale) studied **obedience** (following direct orders from an authority figure). Participants ("teachers") were told to deliver escalating electric shocks (labelled **15 to 450 volts**, in 15-volt steps) to a "learner" (confederate) for wrong answers. No real shocks were given. Astonishingly, **65% of participants went all the way to the maximum 450 volts** despite the learner's screams and pleas. Factors that changed obedience:

- **Proximity of the victim:** obedience fell as the learner became closer; when the teacher had to physically place the learner's hand on a shock plate, obedience dropped to about 30%.
- **Proximity / presence of the authority:** obedience was highest when the experimenter was in the room; giving orders by telephone cut obedience to about 21%.
- **Prestige / legitimacy of authority:** obedience fell (to about 48%) when the study moved from prestigious Yale to a run-down office building in Bridgeport.
- **Presence of disobedient peers:** seeing other "teachers" refuse dramatically reduced obedience (to about 10%).

The studies illustrate the **agentic state** (the person sees themselves as an instrument carrying out another's wishes and so disowns responsibility).

### Compliance Techniques

**Compliance** is a change in behaviour in response to a direct request. Three classic sequential techniques:

- **Foot-in-the-door (FITD):** start with a small request that is easily granted, then follow with the larger target request. Granting the small request shifts self-perception ("I am the kind of person who helps"), so the large request is more likely accepted (Freedman & Fraser, 1966).
- **Door-in-the-face (DITF):** start with a large, unreasonable request that will be refused, then retreat to the smaller target request. Works through **reciprocal concessions** (the requester appears to compromise, so the target feels obliged to reciprocate) and perceptual contrast (Cialdini, 1975).
- **Low-balling:** secure agreement to a deal, then raise the cost or reveal hidden conditions after commitment. Because the person has already committed, they tend to honour the now-worse deal (common in car sales).

### Social Facilitation vs Social Inhibition

**Social facilitation** (Triplett, 1898; theorised by **Zajonc**, 1965): the mere presence of others **improves** performance on **simple or well-learned (dominant) tasks**. **Social inhibition** is the flip side: presence of others **impairs** performance on **complex or novel tasks**. Zajonc's explanation: the presence of others raises physiological **arousal**, and arousal strengthens the **dominant response**, which is correct for easy tasks but wrong for hard ones.

### Social Loafing

**Social loafing** (Ringelmann effect; Latané, Williams & Harkins, 1979): individuals exert **less effort** when working in a group on a pooled task than when working alone, because individual contributions are not identifiable and responsibility is diffused. It is reduced by making contributions identifiable, increasing task importance, or building group cohesion. (Note the contrast: social facilitation involves individual evaluation; loafing involves merged, unidentifiable output.)

### Groupthink

**Groupthink** (Irving Janis, 1972): a mode of thinking in highly cohesive, insulated groups where the desire for **unanimity and harmony overrides realistic appraisal** of alternatives, producing poor decisions. Symptoms include illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalisation, belief in the group's morality, stereotyping of out-groups, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters, and **mindguards** (members who shield the group from contrary information). Classic case studies: Bay of Pigs invasion, Challenger disaster. Prevented by encouraging dissent, appointing a **devil's advocate**, and inviting outside opinion.

### Group Polarization

**Group polarization:** group discussion tends to **strengthen (make more extreme) the members' initial leanings** rather than moderate them. A group leaning slightly risky becomes riskier (the **risky shift**); a group leaning cautious becomes more cautious. Driven by persuasive-arguments (hearing new supportive arguments) and social-comparison (wanting to align with the valued group direction) mechanisms.

### Bystander Effect

**Bystander effect** (Latané & Darley, after the 1964 Kitty Genovese case): the more bystanders present, the **less likely** any one person is to help an emergency victim. Two mechanisms:

- **Diffusion of responsibility:** each bystander assumes someone else will (or should) act, so personal responsibility is divided among all present.
- **Pluralistic ignorance:** in an ambiguous situation each bystander looks to others for cues; seeing others stay calm, each wrongly concludes there is no emergency.

Darley and Latané's five-step decision model: (1) notice the event, (2) interpret it as an emergency, (3) assume responsibility, (4) know how to help, (5) decide to help. Failure at any step stops helping.

### Lewin's Leadership Styles

**Kurt Lewin**, with Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White (1939), ran classic experiments on boys' activity groups led by adults trained to use three distinct styles. The study is foundational for both social psychology and organizational behaviour, and the three styles are a recurring entrance-exam item.

- **Autocratic (authoritarian):** the leader makes all decisions alone, dictates tasks and methods, and stays aloof from the group. It produces high quantity of work but only while the leader is present, plus more hostility, aggression, and dependence. Work stops or collapses when the leader leaves.
- **Democratic (participative):** the leader involves the group in decisions, discusses options, and lets members choose methods. It yields slightly lower quantity but higher quality, more originality, stronger motivation, and group cohesion that continues even when the leader steps away. Lewin judged this the most effective style overall.
- **Laissez-faire (delegative):** the leader gives complete freedom and minimal guidance, supplying materials but not directing. It produces the least work, the lowest quality, and the most frustration and disorganization, because the group lacks structure.

Lewin is also the source of **field theory** (behaviour is a function of the person and the environment, B = f(P, E)), the concept of **life space**, and the **three-stage model of change (unfreeze, change/move, refreeze)**, all of which are separately testable.

## 4. Personality Theory

### Allport: Trait Theory

**Gordon Allport** (1937) founded modern trait theory and distinguished three levels of dispositions:

- **Cardinal traits:** a single, dominant trait that organises a person's entire life (rare; e.g., narcissism, Machiavellianism). Most people have none.
- **Central traits:** the 5 to 10 general characteristics that form the core of personality and that we use to describe someone (honest, kind, anxious).
- **Secondary traits:** specific, situation-bound preferences and attitudes that appear only in particular circumstances (e.g., gets impatient in queues).

Allport also coined the **functional autonomy** concept (a motive can become independent of its original cause).

### Cattell: Surface vs Source Traits, 16PF

**Raymond Cattell** used **factor analysis** to reduce thousands of trait words to underlying factors. He distinguished:

- **Surface traits:** clusters of behaviours that appear to go together (the visible, correlated surface).
- **Source traits:** the underlying, stable factors that actually cause and organise the surface traits; these are the real building blocks of personality.

He identified **16 source traits**, measured by the **16PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire)**. Cattell also distinguished **fluid intelligence** (raw reasoning ability) from **crystallised intelligence** (acquired knowledge).

### Eysenck: PEN Model and Biological Basis

**Hans Eysenck** proposed a hierarchical model with very few, biologically based superfactors (dimensions). His final model is **PEN**:

- **Psychoticism (P):** tough-mindedness, aggression, impulsivity, coldness, non-conformity (high) vs warmth and empathy (low). Linked to higher testosterone and lower serotonin.
- **Extraversion (E):** sociable, outgoing, sensation-seeking (high) vs introverted and reserved (low).
- **Neuroticism (N):** emotional instability, anxiety, moodiness (high) vs emotional stability and calm (low).

Eysenck's key biological claim links Extraversion to **cortical arousal via the ascending reticular activating system (ARAS)**. **Introverts have chronically HIGHER baseline cortical arousal**, so they avoid further stimulation (they are already near optimum); **extraverts have LOWER baseline arousal**, so they seek out stimulation to reach an optimal level. Neuroticism was linked to the **limbic system / autonomic nervous system reactivity** (visceral brain). This biological grounding is a favourite exam point.

### Costa & McCrae: Five-Factor Model (OCEAN)

**Paul Costa and Robert McCrae** developed the **Five-Factor Model (FFM)**, the dominant modern trait taxonomy, measured by the **NEO-PI-R**. The five factors, mnemonic **OCEAN** (or CANOE):

- **O – Openness to Experience:** imaginative, curious, unconventional vs conventional and concrete.
- **C – Conscientiousness:** organised, disciplined, dependable, goal-directed vs careless and impulsive.
- **E – Extraversion:** sociable, assertive, energetic vs reserved.
- **A – Agreeableness:** trusting, cooperative, warm vs antagonistic and suspicious.
- **N – Neuroticism:** anxious, emotionally reactive vs calm and stable.

The model is supported cross-culturally and considered largely heritable and stable across adulthood.

### Bandura: Reciprocal Determinism and Self-Efficacy

**Albert Bandura** (social-cognitive theory) rejected pure trait and pure behaviourist views. His central concept is **reciprocal determinism (triadic reciprocal causation)**: the person (cognition, beliefs), the behaviour, and the environment all **continuously and mutually influence one another** in three-way interaction (none is primary).

**Self-efficacy** is a person's belief in their own capability to execute the actions needed to handle a specific situation. High self-efficacy predicts persistence, effort, and resilience. Its four sources are: (1) **mastery experiences** (past successes, the strongest source), (2) **vicarious experiences** (watching similar others succeed), (3) **verbal/social persuasion** (encouragement), and (4) **emotional/physiological states** (interpreting arousal as readiness vs anxiety).

### Rotter: Locus of Control

**Julian Rotter** (social learning theory) introduced **locus of control**, a generalised expectancy about where the control over one's outcomes lies:

- **Internal locus of control:** belief that outcomes result from one's own actions, effort, and abilities. Linked to better coping, achievement, and health behaviour.
- **External locus of control:** belief that outcomes are controlled by luck, fate, chance, or powerful others. Linked to passivity and, in excess, to learned helplessness.

Rotter's broader theory uses **expectancy** (subjective probability of a reinforcement) and **reinforcement value** to predict behaviour potential.

## High-Yield Recap

- **Heider:** founder of attribution; internal (dispositional) vs external (situational) causes.
- **Kelley covariation:** consensus, distinctiveness, consistency. High-high-high = external; low-low-high = internal.
- **FAE / correspondence bias (Ross):** overweight disposition for OTHERS. **Actor-observer (Jones & Nisbett):** situational for self, dispositional for others. **Self-serving bias:** credit success internally, blame failure externally. **Just-world (Lerner):** victim-blaming.
- **Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) $1/$20:** $1 group rated boring task as MORE enjoyable (insufficient justification).
- **Asch:** ~75% conformed at least once, ~33% of critical trials; one ally collapses conformity. Informational (ambiguity, real belief) vs normative (approval) influence.
- **Milgram:** 65% reached 450 V; obedience falls with victim proximity, lower authority prestige (Bridgeport ~48%), phone orders (~21%), rebel peers (~10%). Agentic state.
- **Compliance:** foot-in-the-door (small then large), door-in-the-face (large then small, reciprocal concession), low-balling (commit then raise cost).
- **Social facilitation (Zajonc/arousal):** presence helps simple/dominant tasks, hurts complex tasks. **Social loafing (Ringelmann/Latané):** less effort in pooled, unidentifiable group work.
- **Groupthink (Janis):** cohesive insulated group, harmony over appraisal; mindguards, devil's advocate as cure. **Group polarization:** discussion makes initial leaning more extreme (risky shift).
- **Bystander effect (Latané & Darley, Kitty Genovese):** diffusion of responsibility + pluralistic ignorance; 5-step decision model.
- **Allport:** cardinal (rare, dominant), central (5-10 core), secondary (situational) traits.
- **Cattell:** surface vs source traits; 16 source traits, 16PF; fluid vs crystallised intelligence.
- **Eysenck PEN:** Psychoticism, Extraversion, Neuroticism. Introverts have HIGHER cortical arousal (ARAS); neuroticism tied to limbic/ANS reactivity.
- **Costa & McCrae FFM (OCEAN), NEO-PI-R:** Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism.
- **Bandura:** reciprocal determinism (person-behaviour-environment); self-efficacy with 4 sources (mastery strongest).
- **Rotter:** locus of control, internal vs external; expectancy x reinforcement value.
