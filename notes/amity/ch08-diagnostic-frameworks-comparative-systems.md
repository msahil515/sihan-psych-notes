# Ch 8: Diagnostic Frameworks & Comparative Systems

This chapter explains how mental disorders are classified, how the two dominant manuals (DSM-5-TR and ICD-11) are built and how they differ, the major theoretical paradigms used to explain why disorders arise, and the clinical reasoning that turns a presentation into a diagnosis (clinical significance, differential diagnosis, rule-outs, comorbidity). Entrance exams routinely test the architecture differences between the manuals, the ICD-11 personality-model change, and the abolition of the multiaxial system, so this is high-yield.

## 1. Why Classify? The Purpose of a Nosology

**Nosology** is the branch of medicine concerned with the classification of diseases. A psychiatric classification system serves several functions: it provides a **common language** so clinicians worldwide describe the same patient the same way (reliability), it groups conditions so that prognosis and treatment can be inferred, it enables **epidemiology** (counting cases for research and public health), and it structures billing/administration. The two cardinal properties demanded of any classification are **reliability** (different clinicians reach the same diagnosis for the same patient) and **validity** (the diagnosis corresponds to a real, distinct entity with a characteristic course, biology, and treatment response). Modern manuals deliberately prioritized reliability (via explicit operational criteria) sometimes at the cost of validity, which remains the central scientific criticism of categorical psychiatry.

## 2. The Two Major Systems: DSM-5-TR and ICD-11

The **DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders)** is published by the **American Psychiatric Association (APA)**. The current edition is **DSM-5-TR (Text Revision, 2022)**, which updated text, prevalence figures, and ICD coding while leaving most criteria unchanged from **DSM-5 (2013)**. The **ICD (International Classification of Diseases)** is published by the **World Health Organization (WHO)**; the current edition is **ICD-11**, adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2019 and in force from **1 January 2022**. ICD-11 is the global standard and is used for official health statistics in member states including India; its mental-disorders section is supported by the **CDDR (Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements)**, the successor to ICD-10's "Blue Book."

### Categorical vs Dimensional Classification

This is the single most tested conceptual axis. A **categorical model** treats a disorder as a discrete kind that a person either has or does not have, defined by meeting a threshold number of criteria (for example, 5 of 9 symptoms for major depressive disorder). It mirrors the medical disease model and is administratively simple, but it imposes artificial cut-offs on what are really continuous traits, struggles with the boundary between normality and disorder, and generates high **comorbidity** (people meeting criteria for several disorders at once) and heavy use of "not otherwise specified" leftover categories.

A **dimensional model** rates symptoms or traits along a continuum of severity rather than present/absent. It captures subthreshold cases, tracks change over time, and reflects the reality that traits like anxiety or impulsivity are normally distributed in the population. Its drawbacks are clinical clumsiness (numbers are harder to communicate than a label) and lack of natural treatment thresholds. Both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 remain **predominantly categorical** but have introduced **dimensional elements**: severity specifiers, cross-cutting symptom measures, and (most importantly) the new trait-based personality systems described below.

### Architecture: How the Two Manuals Are Organized

| Feature | DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022) | ICD-11 (WHO, 2022) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Mental disorders only | All diseases; mental disorders are one chapter (Ch 06) |
| Primary use | Clinical/research, mainly US | Global morbidity/mortality statistics, all member states |
| Diagnostic format | Explicit polythetic criteria (X of Y symptoms) | Prototype/"essential features" descriptions in the CDDR, fewer rigid counts |
| Coding | Uses ICD-10-CM / ICD-11 codes | Alphanumeric codes (e.g., 6A70 for single-episode depression) |
| Personality disorders | Retains 10 categorical types (Section II) + Alternative Model (Section III) | Abolished categorical types; single dimension of severity + trait qualifiers |
| Axes | Multiaxial system abolished (since DSM-5) | Never used a formal multiaxial system |

**Polythetic** criteria (DSM's approach) mean no single symptom is necessary; any sufficient subset from a larger list qualifies, which is why two people with the "same" diagnosis can share almost no symptoms. ICD-11 instead gives **essential (required) features** plus a clinical prototype, intended to fit how clinicians actually think and to work better in low-resource settings.

### Key ICD-11 Changes (high-yield)

ICD-11 introduced several structural changes the exams target directly:

1. **Acute stress reaction moved out of the disorders block.** In ICD-11, **acute stress reaction** is reclassified as a *normal reaction* and placed under "factors influencing health status" (Chapter 24, the "Z-code-like" QE-code section), NOT among mental disorders. A new grouping, **Disorders Specifically Associated with Stress**, was created to hold **PTSD**, the novel **Complex PTSD (CPTSD)**, **prolonged grief disorder**, **adjustment disorder**, and **reactive attachment / disinhibited social engagement disorders**. (Contrast DSM-5-TR, which keeps **Acute Stress Disorder** as a diagnosable disorder lasting 3 days to 1 month within the Trauma- and Stressor-Related chapter.)

2. **Complex PTSD** is a genuinely new ICD-11 diagnosis: core PTSD features (re-experiencing, avoidance, sense of current threat) PLUS three **Disturbances in Self-Organization (DSO)**: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbed relationships, typically following prolonged/repeated trauma.

3. **Dimensional personality model** (see below) replaces the ten categorical personality types entirely.

4. **Gaming disorder** added as a recognized behavioral addiction; **gender incongruence** moved out of mental disorders into a sexual-health chapter (depathologizing); **bipolar** and other groupings reorganized; **catatonia** elevated to an independent grouping.

### The ICD-11 Dimensional Trait-Qualifier Personality Model

ICD-11 **abolished the categorical personality disorder types** (no more separate "borderline," "narcissistic," etc. as official categories). Diagnosis now proceeds in two steps. **Step 1: global severity.** The clinician first decides whether personality functioning is impaired and rates it as **Personality Difficulty** (subthreshold), **Mild**, **Moderate**, or **Severe** Personality Disorder, based on impairment in **self functioning** (identity, self-worth, self-direction) and **interpersonal functioning** (capacity for close, mutually satisfying relationships). Severity, not type, is the strongest predictor of outcome, which is the rationale for putting it first.

**Step 2: trait qualifiers.** The clinician then optionally specifies which **prominent trait domains** color the presentation. There are five: **Negative Affectivity**, **Detachment**, **Dissociality** (antagonism/self-centeredness), **Disinhibition**, and **Anankastia** (rigid perfectionism/constraint). A single permitted **Borderline pattern qualifier** was retained largely to preserve continuity with the large evidence base on borderline personality and DBT. Note the symmetry: DSM-5-TR's **Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD, Section III)** is conceptually similar but uses **Level of Personality Functioning (Criterion A)** plus **25 maladaptive trait facets organized under 5 domains** (Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Antagonism, Disinhibition, Psychoticism) as Criterion B. The five ICD/DSM domains map closely except DSM has **Psychoticism** while ICD-11 has **Anankastia** instead.

## 3. Etiological Paradigms: Why Disorders Arise

A "paradigm" is a set of basic assumptions about the nature and causes of a phenomenon. No single paradigm fully explains psychopathology, which is why modern clinical psychology is integrative.

### Molecular Genetics and Behavior Genetics

This paradigm asks how much of a disorder is inherited and through which genes. **Heritability (h²)** is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population attributable to genetic variance; it is a population statistic, not a fixed personal percentage. Key methods: **family studies** (does the disorder run in families?), **twin studies** (comparing **monozygotic (MZ, identical, ~100% shared genes)** versus **dizygotic (DZ, fraternal, ~50% shared)** concordance; greater MZ concordance implicates genes), and **adoption studies** (separating genes from rearing environment). **Linkage analysis** tracks whether a disorder co-segregates within families with a genetic marker of known chromosomal location, localizing risk genes; **association studies / GWAS (genome-wide association studies)** scan the whole genome across many unrelated people for common variants. Representative heritability figures the exams cite: **schizophrenia ~80%**, **bipolar disorder ~70-85%**, **autism ~80%**, **major depression ~35-40%**. The modern consensus is **polygenic**: most disorders arise from many genes of small effect interacting with environment, not a single "gene for X."

### Neuroscience / Neurofunctional Circuitry

This paradigm locates disorders in brain structure, neurotransmitter systems, and functional circuits. Classic neurotransmitter hypotheses include the **dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia** (excess mesolimbic dopamine activity for positive symptoms; hypoactivity in mesocortical pathways for negative symptoms), the **monoamine/serotonin-norepinephrine hypothesis of depression**, and **GABA** involvement in anxiety. Circuit-level accounts emphasize the **HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis** in stress and depression (elevated cortisol, dexamethasone non-suppression), the **amygdala** in fear/threat processing (hyperactive in anxiety and PTSD), the **prefrontal cortex** in executive control and top-down regulation, and **fronto-striatal (cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical, CSTC) loops** in OCD. Neuroimaging tools that test these (fMRI, PET, structural MRI) underlie the move toward dimensional, circuit-based frameworks such as the NIMH **RDoC (Research Domain Criteria)**, which deliberately ignores DSM categories in favor of dimensions like Negative Valence and Cognitive Systems.

### Psychodynamic Paradigm: Structural and Object-Relational

Rooted in **Freud**, the **structural model** divides the mind into the **id** (unconscious instinctual drives, pleasure principle), the **ego** (reality-testing executive, reality principle, deploys **defense mechanisms**), and the **superego** (internalized moral standards). Symptoms are read as compromise formations expressing unconscious conflict, and **anxiety** signals threatened breakthrough of repressed material, managed by defenses (repression, projection, displacement, sublimation, reaction formation). **Object-relations theory** (Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Mahler) shifted the focus from drives to **internalized representations of self and others ("objects")** formed in early relationships; pathology reflects distorted or split internal objects (e.g., **splitting** into all-good/all-bad in borderline organization, per **Kernberg**). This paradigm directly informs the severity-and-functioning logic now embedded in the ICD-11/DSM personality models.

### Cognitive-Behavioral Paradigm: Schemas and Conditional Assumptions

The **behavioral** strand explains disorders through **classical conditioning** (Pavlov; phobias as conditioned fear, per Watson's Little Albert), **operant conditioning** (Skinner; behavior shaped by reinforcement/punishment), and **observational learning** (Bandura). The **cognitive** strand, associated with **Aaron Beck** and **Albert Ellis**, holds that disturbed emotion follows from disturbed thinking. Beck's model is hierarchical: deep **core beliefs / schemas** (absolute beliefs about self, world, future, e.g., "I am unlovable") generate **dysfunctional / conditional assumptions** ("If I am not perfect, I will be rejected"), which under stress produce **automatic thoughts** and **cognitive distortions** (catastrophizing, overgeneralization, dichotomous thinking, mental filter). Beck's **negative cognitive triad** (negative views of self, world, and future) is the signature of depression. Ellis's **REBT** uses the **ABC model**: an Activating event, **irrational Beliefs**, and the emotional/behavioral Consequence, with therapy targeting the irrational beliefs.

### Diathesis-Stress and the Integrative Model

The **diathesis-stress model** is the dominant integrative framework: a **diathesis** (a predisposition or vulnerability, which may be genetic, biological, cognitive, or personality-based) interacts with **stress** (environmental triggers, life events). A disorder appears only when stress exceeds the threshold set by the person's diathesis; high diathesis needs little stress, low diathesis needs much. This explains why not everyone with a vulnerability becomes ill and not everyone under stress does either. A refinement is the **differential susceptibility / "orchid and dandelion" hypothesis**, where the same sensitivity gene that worsens outcomes under adversity yields *better*-than-average outcomes under enriched conditions. The overarching framework taught today is the **biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977)**, which integrates biological, psychological, and social levels of causation, and is the conceptual parent of the multiaxial idea discussed next.

## 4. The Multiaxial System and Its Abolition

DSM-III (1980) through **DSM-IV-TR (2000)** used a **multiaxial system** to force clinicians to consider the whole person across five separate axes:

| Axis | What it recorded |
|---|---|
| Axis I | Clinical disorders (e.g., depression, schizophrenia, anxiety) and other conditions needing clinical attention |
| Axis II | Personality disorders and intellectual disability (mental retardation) |
| Axis III | General medical conditions relevant to the mental disorder |
| Axis IV | Psychosocial and environmental problems (stressors) |
| Axis V | **GAF (Global Assessment of Functioning)**, a 0-100 score of overall functioning |

**DSM-5 (2013) abolished the multiaxial system.** Axes I, II, and III were merged into a single combined list of diagnoses (removing the artificial and stigmatizing wall between "clinical" and "personality" disorders), Axis IV was replaced by selected **ICD Z-codes** for psychosocial/contextual factors, and the **GAF (Axis V) was dropped** because of poor reliability, with the **WHODAS 2.0 (WHO Disability Assessment Schedule)** recommended as a dimensional disability measure instead. ICD-11 similarly uses no formal multiaxial structure. Exam point: the move away from multiaxial diagnosis reflects the broader shift toward integrated, dimensional, less hierarchical classification.

## 5. The Logic of Diagnosis

### The Clinical Significance Criterion

Nearly every DSM disorder includes a **clinical significance criterion**: the symptoms must cause **clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning**. This is the principal gatekeeper separating disorder from normal variation, eccentricity, or expected reactions. A person can have symptoms (e.g., mild compulsive checking) yet not have a disorder if there is no distress or functional impairment. ICD-11 builds the same idea into its "essential features," requiring that the pattern is not better accounted for by normal cultural or developmental variation.

### Differential Diagnosis and Rule-Outs

**Differential diagnosis** is the systematic process of distinguishing the correct diagnosis from other conditions that could produce a similar presentation. The standard sequence: (1) **rule out malingering and factitious presentation**; (2) **rule out substance/medication-induced** etiology; (3) **rule out a disorder due to another medical condition** (e.g., hypothyroidism mimicking depression, temporal lobe epilepsy mimicking psychosis); (4) determine the **specific primary disorder**; (5) consider **adjustment disorder**; and (6) establish the **boundary with no mental disorder**. A **"rule-out" (R/O)** is a provisional notation meaning a diagnosis is being considered but not yet confirmed pending further information. DSM provides **exclusion criteria** ("not better explained by..." and "not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance or another medical condition") precisely to enforce this hierarchy. A **diagnostic hierarchy** principle also operates: more pervasive disorders (e.g., schizophrenia) can preempt less pervasive ones whose symptoms they fully explain.

### Comorbidity

**Comorbidity** is the co-occurrence of two or more disorders in the same person. It can be **concurrent** (at the same time) or **lifetime/successive** (across the lifespan). High comorbidity rates (e.g., depression with anxiety, ADHD with conduct disorder, substance use across the board) are partly real and partly an **artifact of categorical splitting**, where one underlying dimension is carved into several "separate" labeled disorders. Recognizing comorbidity matters clinically because it worsens prognosis, complicates treatment, and raises suicide risk; transdiagnostic and dimensional models (RDoC, HiTOP, the **p-factor** of general psychopathology) were developed partly to explain why comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception.

## High-Yield Recap

- **DSM-5-TR** = APA, 2022, text revision; mental disorders only; explicit **polythetic** criteria. **ICD-11** = WHO, in force **1 Jan 2022**; all diseases (mental = Chapter 06); **prototype/essential-features** approach via the **CDDR**.
- Both manuals are **predominantly categorical** with added **dimensional** elements (severity specifiers, cross-cutting measures, trait-based personality models).
- **ICD-11 acute stress reaction** is reclassified as a **normal reaction** (NOT a disorder); DSM-5-TR keeps **Acute Stress Disorder** (3 days to 1 month). ICD-11 created **Disorders Specifically Associated with Stress** and the new diagnoses **Complex PTSD** and **prolonged grief disorder**.
- **ICD-11 personality model**: abolished categorical types; **Step 1 = global severity** (Personality Difficulty / Mild / Moderate / Severe) based on self + interpersonal functioning; **Step 2 = five trait qualifiers** (Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, **Anankastia**) plus a retained **Borderline pattern**.
- **DSM AMPD (Section III)**: Criterion A = Level of Personality Functioning; Criterion B = **25 facets / 5 domains** (Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Antagonism, Disinhibition, **Psychoticism**). ICD uses Anankastia where DSM uses Psychoticism.
- **Heritability (h²)** is a population variance statistic. Twin: **MZ ~100%** vs **DZ ~50%** shared genes. **Linkage** localizes genes by co-segregation; **GWAS** scans common variants. Schizophrenia h² ~80%, bipolar ~70-85%, MDD ~35-40%.
- **Neuro**: dopamine hypothesis (schizophrenia), monoamine hypothesis (depression), HPA axis/cortisol, amygdala (fear), CSTC loops (OCD); **RDoC** is NIMH's dimensional circuit framework.
- **Psychodynamic**: id/ego/superego (structural); **object relations** (Klein, Winnicott, Kernberg's splitting). **CBT**: schemas/core beliefs → conditional assumptions → automatic thoughts; Beck's **negative cognitive triad**; Ellis's **ABC / REBT**.
- **Diathesis-stress** = vulnerability × stress threshold; parent framework = **biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977)**.
- **Multiaxial system (DSM-III to DSM-IV-TR)**: Axis I clinical, Axis II personality + ID, Axis III medical, Axis IV psychosocial, Axis V **GAF (0-100)**. **DSM-5 abolished it**; GAF replaced by **WHODAS 2.0**. ICD-11 uses no multiaxial structure.
- **Clinical significance criterion** = distress or functional impairment, the gatekeeper for disorder vs normality.
- **Differential diagnosis order**: malingering/factitious → substance-induced → another medical condition → primary disorder → adjustment disorder → no disorder. **R/O** = provisional. **Comorbidity** = co-occurrence; inflated by categorical splitting; motivates transdiagnostic/**p-factor** models.
