# Ch 10: Psychopathology II, Dissociative/Somatic, Eating/Sleep, Personality & Neurodevelopmental Disorders

This chapter covers the second major block of clinical disorders examined under the RCI / NIMHANS pattern: dissociative disorders, somatic symptom and related disorders, feeding and eating disorders, sleep-wake disorders, the ten personality disorders across three clusters, and the neurodevelopmental disorders. These are heavily tested because they hinge on precise diagnostic criteria, exact durations, and sharp differential distinctions (for example conversion versus factitious versus malingering, or ODD versus conduct disorder). Master the numbers and the discriminating features and you can answer almost any MCQ on these topics.

## 1. Dissociative Disorders

**Dissociation** is a disruption of, and discontinuity in, the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, and behaviour. The unifying theme is a breakdown in the usually seamless sense of "I am one continuous self." Dissociation is conceptualized as a defensive process, often linked to **severe trauma** (especially early, repeated childhood abuse), where parts of experience are walled off from awareness.

### 1.1 Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID)

Formerly called **multiple personality disorder**, DID is the most severe dissociative condition. The core criterion is the presence of **two or more distinct personality states** (also called identities or "alters"), each with its own relatively enduring pattern of perceiving, relating to, and thinking about the self and environment. There is a marked **discontinuity in sense of self and agency**, accompanied by alterations in affect, behaviour, consciousness, memory, perception, cognition, and sensory-motor functioning. A second essential feature is **recurrent gaps in the recall of everyday events, personal information, or traumatic events** that are too extensive to be ordinary forgetting (that is, dissociative **amnesia** between states). The symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment, are not part of a culturally accepted practice (for example trance in a religious ritual), and are not due to substances or another medical condition. DID is strongly associated with childhood trauma; the **post-traumatic model** holds that the disorder develops when a child facing overwhelming abuse "compartmentalizes" experience. In children, the symptoms must not be confused with imaginary playmates or fantasy play.

### 1.2 Dissociative Amnesia

**Dissociative amnesia** is an inability to recall important **autobiographical information**, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. The memory is not lost (the information is encoded) but is rendered inaccessible, which distinguishes it from organic amnesias. Forms / patterns to know exactly:

- **Localized amnesia**: failure to recall events during a circumscribed period of time (for example all events of the day a trauma occurred). This is the most common form.
- **Selective amnesia**: the person can recall some, but not all, of the events during a circumscribed period.
- **Generalized amnesia**: complete loss of memory for one's life history, including identity. This is rare and dramatic.
- **Systematized amnesia**: loss of memory for a specific category of information (for example all memories relating to one person).
- **Continuous amnesia**: forgetting each new event as it occurs.

The key **specifier** is **dissociative fugue**: amnesia accompanied by apparently purposeful **travel or bewildered wandering**, often with confusion about identity or assumption of a new identity. Fugue is now coded as a specifier of dissociative amnesia rather than a separate disorder.

### 1.3 Depersonalization / Derealization Disorder

This is defined by persistent or recurrent experiences of **depersonalization**, **derealization**, or both. **Depersonalization** is a feeling of unreality, detachment, or being an outside observer of one's own thoughts, feelings, sensations, body, or actions (feeling like a robot, or that one's body is foreign). **Derealization** is a feeling of unreality or detachment from one's surroundings (the world seems dreamlike, foggy, lifeless, or visually distorted). The single most exam-critical point: during these experiences **reality testing remains intact**. The person knows the experience is subjective and not actually real, which separates this disorder from psychosis. The symptoms cause significant distress or impairment and are not due to substances or another disorder.

## 2. Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders

These disorders share a prominent focus on **bodily (somatic) symptoms** or on health concerns, associated with significant distress and functional impairment. The modern (DSM-5) approach emphasizes the **positive psychological features** (excessive thoughts, feelings, behaviours) rather than merely the absence of a medical explanation.

### 2.1 Somatic Symptom Disorder

Characterized by **one or more somatic symptoms** that are distressing or disrupt daily life, plus **excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviours** related to those symptoms (disproportionate and persistent thoughts about seriousness, persistently high anxiety about health, or excessive time and energy devoted to symptoms). The symptomatic state is **persistent**, typically lasting **more than 6 months**, although any one symptom may not be continuously present. Importantly, the somatic symptom may or may not have a demonstrable medical cause; the diagnosis no longer requires that symptoms be medically unexplained.

### 2.2 Illness Anxiety Disorder

Formerly close to the old concept of **hypochondriasis**. Here the central feature is **preoccupation with having or acquiring a serious illness**, but **somatic symptoms are absent or only mild**. The person experiences high anxiety about health and is easily alarmed about personal health status, performing excessive health-related behaviours (repeatedly checking the body) or showing maladaptive avoidance (avoiding doctors and hospitals). The preoccupation has been present for at least **6 months**, though the specific feared illness may change.

### 2.3 Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder, FND)

Conversion disorder involves **one or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function** (for example weakness or paralysis, abnormal movements, swallowing problems, speech difficulty, seizures, anaesthesia, blindness). The defining diagnostic feature is **clinical incompatibility**: there is evidence of **incompatibility between the symptom and recognized neurological or medical conditions** (for example **Hoover's sign** in functional leg weakness, or normal EEG during a "seizure"). The symptom cannot be better explained by another medical or mental disorder. A classic associated (but not required) feature is **la belle indifference**, a surprising lack of concern about a dramatic symptom.

### 2.4 Factitious Disorder versus Malingering

This distinction is a perennial exam favourite and turns on **motivation**.

| Feature | Factitious Disorder | Malingering |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | Falsified / induced deliberately | Falsified / exaggerated deliberately |
| Motivation | To assume the **sick role** (internal, psychological gain) | **External / secondary gain** (money, drugs, avoiding work, evading law) |
| Status | A mental disorder | **Not** a mental disorder (a "condition that may be a focus of clinical attention") |
| Example | A nurse injecting herself to be hospitalized | Faking back pain for a disability payout |

**Factitious disorder imposed on another** (formerly Munchausen syndrome by proxy) is the deliberate falsification of illness in another person (often a child) by a caregiver, who is the one diagnosed. Note **secondary gain** is the technical term for the external incentive that defines malingering, in contrast to **primary gain** (the internal psychological relief, classically attributed to conversion).

## 3. Feeding and Eating Disorders

### 3.1 Anorexia Nervosa

Defined by **restriction of energy intake** leading to a **significantly low body weight** in the context of age, sex, and health; an **intense fear of gaining weight** or becoming fat (or persistent behaviour interfering with weight gain); and a **disturbance in the way body weight or shape is experienced** (distorted body image) or undue influence of weight on self-evaluation, or denial of the seriousness of low weight. Two subtypes:

- **Restricting type**: weight loss achieved mainly through dieting, fasting, and excessive exercise, with no recurrent binge-purge behaviour in the last 3 months.
- **Binge-eating / purging type**: recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging (self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics).

Medical sequelae include **amenorrhoea**, **lanugo** (fine body hair), bradycardia, electrolyte disturbance, and osteoporosis. It has one of the **highest mortality rates** of any psychiatric disorder.

### 3.2 Bulimia Nervosa

Characterized by recurrent episodes of **binge eating** (eating an unusually large amount within a discrete period with a sense of loss of control) followed by recurrent **inappropriate compensatory behaviours** to prevent weight gain (self-induced vomiting, laxatives, fasting, excessive exercise). The exact frequency criterion: binge eating and compensatory behaviours both occur, **on average, at least once a week for 3 months**. Self-evaluation is unduly influenced by body shape and weight. Unlike anorexia, individuals are typically of **normal weight or overweight**. A classic sign is **Russell's sign** (calluses on the knuckles from self-induced vomiting), plus dental erosion and parotid gland swelling.

### 3.3 Binge-Eating Disorder

Recurrent episodes of binge eating with marked distress, but **without** the regular compensatory behaviours of bulimia. Episodes are associated with features such as eating rapidly, eating until uncomfortably full, eating when not hungry, eating alone out of embarrassment, and feeling disgusted or guilty afterward. The threshold is binge eating **at least once a week for 3 months**. It is the most common eating disorder and is strongly associated with obesity.

## 4. Sleep-Wake Disorders

### 4.1 Insomnia Disorder

Dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, with difficulty **initiating** sleep, **maintaining** sleep (frequent awakenings), or **early-morning awakening** with inability to return to sleep. The frequency and duration criteria to memorize: the difficulty occurs **at least 3 nights per week** and is present for **at least 3 months**, despite adequate opportunity for sleep, and causes significant daytime distress or impairment.

### 4.2 Narcolepsy

Recurrent, irresistible **lapses into sleep** occurring within the same day, at least three times per week over the past 3 months. The hallmark associated feature is **cataplexy** (brief, sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone, usually triggered by strong emotion such as laughter, with retained consciousness). The biological mechanism is a **deficiency of hypocretin (orexin)**, a hypothalamic neuropeptide; narcolepsy with cataplexy is associated with low CSF hypocretin levels, thought to reflect autoimmune loss of hypocretin-producing neurons. Other features include **sleep paralysis**, **hypnagogic / hypnopompic hallucinations**, and intrusion of **REM** sleep at sleep onset (REM latency abnormalities seen on the multiple sleep latency test).

## 5. Personality Disorders

A **personality disorder** is an enduring, **inflexible and pervasive pattern** of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from cultural expectation, manifests in at least two of cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning, or impulse control, is stable and of long duration with onset traceable to **adolescence or early adulthood**, and leads to distress or impairment. They are grouped into three clusters.

### 5.1 Cluster A (Odd / Eccentric)

- **Paranoid PD**: pervasive **distrust and suspiciousness** of others, reading hidden malevolent meanings into benign events, bearing grudges, doubting loyalty of friends.
- **Schizoid PD**: **detachment from social relationships** and a restricted range of emotional expression; the person is a genuine "loner" who neither desires nor enjoys close relationships and appears indifferent to praise or criticism.
- **Schizotypal PD**: acute discomfort with close relationships plus **cognitive or perceptual distortions and eccentricities** (ideas of reference, magical thinking, odd speech, unusual perceptual experiences). It lies on the schizophrenia spectrum.

### 5.2 Cluster B (Dramatic / Emotional / Erratic)

- **Antisocial PD**: pervasive disregard for and violation of the rights of others. Two age rules are heavily tested: the individual must be at least **18 years old**, and there must be evidence of **conduct disorder with onset before age 15**. Features include deceitfulness, impulsivity, aggressiveness, reckless disregard for safety, irresponsibility, and **lack of remorse**.
- **Borderline PD**: a pervasive pattern of **instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image, and affect**, with marked impulsivity. Classic features include frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, identity disturbance, recurrent suicidal/self-mutilating behaviour, chronic emptiness, and **splitting** (a primitive defence of viewing people as all-good or all-bad, alternating between idealization and devaluation).
- **Histrionic PD**: excessive **emotionality and attention-seeking**, discomfort when not the centre of attention, theatrical and exaggerated emotional expression, suggestibility, and considering relationships more intimate than they are.
- **Narcissistic PD**: pervasive **grandiosity**, need for admiration, and lack of empathy; sense of entitlement, fantasies of unlimited success, exploitation of others, and envy.

### 5.3 Cluster C (Anxious / Fearful)

- **Avoidant PD**: social inhibition, feelings of **inadequacy**, and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation; avoids occupational activities involving interpersonal contact, but (unlike schizoid) **wants** relationships and is held back by fear of rejection.
- **Dependent PD**: pervasive and excessive **need to be taken care of**, leading to submissive, clinging behaviour and fears of separation; difficulty making everyday decisions without reassurance.
- **Obsessive-Compulsive PD (OCPD)**: preoccupation with **orderliness, perfectionism, and control** at the expense of flexibility and efficiency. Critically distinguished from **OCD**: OCPD is **ego-syntonic** (the person sees their rigid standards as correct, no true obsessions or compulsions), whereas OCD is **ego-dystonic** (intrusive obsessions and ritualistic compulsions the person resents).

## 6. Neurodevelopmental Disorders

These onset in the developmental period and produce impairments of personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning.

### 6.1 Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)

Requires **two** deficits with onset during the developmental period: (1) deficits in **intellectual functions** (reasoning, problem solving, abstract thinking, learning), confirmed by clinical assessment and standardized intelligence testing, and (2) deficits in **adaptive functioning** that fail to meet developmental and sociocultural standards across the conceptual, social, and practical domains. The modern emphasis is on **adaptive functioning** (which determines severity: mild, moderate, severe, profound), not solely the IQ score, although IQ is roughly **below 70** (about two standard deviations below the mean of 100).

### 6.2 Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Defined by **two core domains**, both required. First, persistent deficits in **social communication and social interaction** across contexts: deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, in nonverbal communicative behaviours, and in developing/maintaining/understanding relationships. Second, **restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities** (stereotyped movements, insistence on sameness and routines, highly restricted fixated interests, and hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input). Symptoms are present in the **early developmental period**. Severity is rated by level of support needed (Level 1, 2, 3).

### 6.3 Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

A persistent pattern of **inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity** that interferes with functioning. The exact criteria to memorize: at least **6 symptoms** (of inattention and/or of hyperactivity-impulsivity) for those up to age 16, or **5 symptoms** for those aged 17 and older, persisting for at least **6 months**. Several symptoms must be present **before age 12**, and symptoms must occur in **two or more settings** (for example home and school). Presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined.

### 6.4 Oppositional Defiant Disorder versus Conduct Disorder

Both are disruptive behaviour disorders, but they differ in severity and the nature of the behaviour. **ODD** is a pattern of angry/irritable mood, argumentative/defiant behaviour, and vindictiveness, lasting at least 6 months. **Conduct disorder** is a repetitive pattern of violating the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate norms, organized into **four symptom clusters**.

| Feature | Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD) | Conduct Disorder (CD) |
|---|---|---|
| Core nature | Defiance, hostility, irritability toward authority | Violation of others' rights and major social norms |
| Severity | Less severe; rights of others not seriously violated | More severe; serious rule and rights violations |
| Symptom clusters | Angry/irritable mood; argumentative/defiant; vindictiveness | (1) **Aggression to people and animals**, (2) **destruction of property**, (3) **deceitfulness or theft**, (4) **serious violations of rules** |
| Relation | Can precede CD | A precursor of antisocial PD (when onset before 15) |

## High-Yield Recap

- **DID**: 2 or more distinct personality states + recurrent amnesia for everyday/personal events; linked to childhood trauma.
- **Dissociative amnesia** types: localized (most common), selective, generalized, systematized, continuous. **Fugue** = purposeful travel/wandering, now a specifier.
- **Depersonalization/derealization**: reality testing stays **intact** (key vs psychosis).
- **Somatic symptom disorder**: distressing somatic symptoms + excessive thoughts/feelings/behaviours, typically **>6 months**.
- **Illness anxiety**: preoccupation with illness, somatic symptoms absent/mild, **≥6 months**.
- **Conversion (FND)**: altered motor/sensory function with **clinical incompatibility** (e.g. Hoover's sign); la belle indifference.
- **Factitious** = motivated by the **sick role** (internal); **malingering** = **secondary/external gain** (not a disorder).
- **Anorexia**: restricting vs binge-purge subtypes; intense fear of weight gain, distorted body image, low weight; highest mortality.
- **Bulimia**: binge + compensatory behaviour, **≥1x/week for 3 months**, usually normal weight; Russell's sign.
- **Binge-eating disorder**: binges without compensation, **≥1x/week for 3 months**.
- **Insomnia disorder**: difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep, **≥3 nights/week for 3 months**.
- **Narcolepsy**: sleep attacks + **cataplexy**; low **hypocretin (orexin)**; sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, REM intrusion.
- **Cluster A**: paranoid (distrust), schizoid (detached loner), schizotypal (odd + perceptual distortions).
- **Cluster B**: antisocial (conduct disorder before 15, must be ≥18, no remorse), borderline (instability + **splitting**), histrionic (attention-seeking), narcissistic (grandiosity, lacks empathy).
- **Cluster C**: avoidant (wants relationships but fears rejection), dependent (needs to be cared for), OCPD (perfectionism, **ego-syntonic**, vs ego-dystonic OCD).
- **Intellectual disability**: deficits in **intellectual + adaptive** functioning; IQ roughly <70; severity set by adaptive functioning.
- **ASD**: social communication deficits + restricted/repetitive behaviours; early developmental onset.
- **ADHD**: **≥6 symptoms** (≥5 if ≥17 yrs), **≥6 months**, onset before **age 12**, in **≥2 settings**.
- **ODD vs CD**: ODD = defiance/irritability (rights not violated); CD = 4 clusters (aggression to people/animals, property destruction, deceit/theft, serious rule violations); CD before 15 feeds antisocial PD.
