# Ch 16: Cognitive Gating, Memory, Learning & Perceptual Processes

This chapter covers the three pillars that the entrance loves to test together: how information is stored (memory architectures), how behaviour and knowledge are acquired (learning theories), and how the mind filters and organizes incoming sensation (perception and attention). Master the named models, their exact stages, the schedules of reinforcement, and the signal detection formulas, because these are reliably the highest-frequency objective questions in the cognitive section.

## 1. Memory Architectures

Memory is conventionally split into three processes (**encoding**, the conversion of sensory input into a usable representation; **storage**, the maintenance of that representation over time; and **retrieval**, the recovery of stored information into awareness). The models below describe the structures across which these processes operate.

### 1.1 Atkinson-Shiffrin Multi-Store Model (1968)

Also called the **modal model** because it became the standard ("modal") view. It proposes three structurally distinct stores through which information flows serially.

- **Sensory memory (sensory register):** Holds a complete but extremely brief replica of incoming sensation. It is modality-specific. The visual store is **iconic memory** (capacity essentially the whole visual field, decay roughly 0.25 to 0.5 seconds; demonstrated by **Sperling's 1960 partial-report** experiments, which showed that subjects could report any cued row of a briefly flashed grid, proving the whole array was momentarily available). The auditory store is **echoic memory** (decay roughly 2 to 4 seconds, longer than iconic, which is why we can "replay" the last few words someone said). Attention selects items from sensory memory for transfer onward.
- **Short-term store (STS):** Limited capacity and limited duration. Capacity is famously **7 plus or minus 2 chunks** (Miller, 1956, "The Magical Number Seven"); a **chunk** is a meaningful unit, so chunking dramatically expands functional capacity. Duration without rehearsal is roughly **15 to 30 seconds** (Peterson and Peterson, 1959, using the Brown-Peterson distractor task with backward counting to block rehearsal). **Maintenance rehearsal** keeps items in STS; the more elaborate processing is what moves them onward.
- **Long-term store (LTS):** Effectively unlimited capacity and potentially permanent duration. Information enters LTS primarily through rehearsal and elaboration. Forgetting from LTS is attributed mainly to retrieval failure and interference rather than decay.

A key supporting finding is the **serial position effect** in free recall: the **primacy effect** (better recall of early list items, attributed to more rehearsal transferring them to LTS) and the **recency effect** (better recall of the last items, attributed to their still being in STS). A delayed recall task with a distractor abolishes recency but not primacy, supporting two separate stores.

### 1.2 Baddeley and Hitch Working Memory Model (1974)

This model replaced the passive single STS with an active, multi-component **working memory** system that both stores and manipulates information.

| Component | Function | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Central executive** | Attentional controller; allocates resources, switches tasks, coordinates the subsystems | Modality-free, limited capacity, the "boss"; does not store information itself |
| **Phonological loop** | Holds verbal and acoustic information | Two parts: the **phonological store** ("inner ear", holds sound traces ~2 seconds) and the **articulatory rehearsal process** ("inner voice", refreshes the trace) |
| **Visuospatial sketchpad** | Holds visual and spatial information | The "inner eye"; supports navigation and mental imagery |
| **Episodic buffer** | Integrates information from the loop, sketchpad, and LTM into unified, time-ordered episodes | Added by Baddeley in 2000; limited capacity, multimodal binding store |

Classic supporting effects for the phonological loop: the **word-length effect** (lists of short words are recalled better than long words, because long words take longer to rehearse) and the **phonological similarity effect** (similar-sounding items are confused). **Articulatory suppression** (repeating "the, the, the") blocks the loop and abolishes these effects.

### 1.3 Tulving's Long-Term Memory Taxonomy

Endel Tulving divided LTM into two broad systems by whether the knowledge can be consciously declared.

- **Declarative (explicit) memory:** Consciously accessible, "knowing that". Subdivided into **episodic memory** (memory for specific personally experienced events tagged with time and place, e.g. your last birthday; supports mental time travel and autonoetic consciousness) and **semantic memory** (general factual knowledge and concepts independent of when learned, e.g. "Delhi is the capital of India").
- **Non-declarative (implicit) memory:** Expressed through performance, "knowing how", not consciously retrieved. Includes **procedural memory** (skills and habits such as cycling or typing), **priming**, **classical conditioning**, and **non-associative learning**. Procedural memory is notably spared in amnesic patients (e.g. patient **H.M.**, whose bilateral medial temporal lobe / hippocampal removal abolished the formation of new declarative memories but left him able to learn new motor skills like mirror-drawing).

### 1.4 Forgetting

- **Ebbinghaus forgetting curve (1885):** Using nonsense syllables on himself, Hermann Ebbinghaus showed forgetting is **rapid at first and then levels off** (roughly exponential decay). He also described **savings** (relearning is faster than original learning) and the spacing effect.
- **Interference theory:** **Proactive interference (PI)** is when old learning disrupts new (you keep dialing your old phone number). **Retroactive interference (RI)** is when new learning disrupts old (learning Spanish makes the French you learned earlier harder to recall). Mnemonic: the "pro" old material acts forward in time, the "retro" new material acts backward.
- **Decay theory:** Memory traces fade with disuse over time; more accepted for sensory and short-term memory than for LTM.
- **Retrieval failure / cue-dependent forgetting:** The information is present but inaccessible without the right cue (the tip-of-the-tongue state). **Encoding specificity** (Tulving) and **state-dependent / context-dependent memory** illustrate this.
- **Zeigarnik effect:** Bluma Zeigarnik (a student of Kurt Lewin) found that **interrupted or incomplete tasks are remembered better than completed ones**, because an unfinished task maintains a state of task-specific tension that keeps it active in memory.

## 2. Learning

**Learning** is a relatively permanent change in behaviour or behaviour potential resulting from experience (excluding maturation, fatigue, or drugs).

### 2.1 Classical (Pavlovian) Conditioning

**Ivan Pavlov** discovered it studying salivation in dogs. An initially neutral stimulus, when repeatedly paired with a stimulus that already evokes a reflex, comes to evoke that reflex itself.

- **Unconditioned stimulus (US):** Food (naturally triggers salivation).
- **Unconditioned response (UR):** Salivation to food (unlearned).
- **Conditioned stimulus (CS):** Bell (originally neutral).
- **Conditioned response (CR):** Salivation to the bell (learned).

Core phenomena:

- **Acquisition:** The CR strengthens as CS-US pairings continue; learning is fastest when the CS slightly **precedes** the US (delay/forward conditioning) by a short interval.
- **Extinction:** Repeated presentation of the CS without the US weakens and eventually eliminates the CR.
- **Spontaneous recovery:** After extinction and a rest period, the CR reappears (weakly) when the CS is presented, showing extinction suppresses rather than erases the association.
- **Generalization:** Stimuli similar to the CS also elicit the CR (a dog conditioned to one tone responds to similar tones).
- **Discrimination:** Learning to respond to the CS but not to similar stimuli.
- **Higher-order (second-order) conditioning:** An established CS can itself condition a new neutral stimulus (pair a light with the already-conditioned bell, and the light alone elicits salivation).

**John B. Watson** extended this to humans with the **Little Albert** experiment (1920), conditioning an infant to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise, with fear generalizing to other furry objects. Watson founded **behaviourism**.

- **Rescorla-Wagner model (1972):** A formal account stating that learning happens only when events are **surprising**. The change in associative strength is delta-V = alpha × beta × (lambda minus V-sum), where lambda is the maximum associative strength the US can support, V-sum is the combined current strength of all cues present, and alpha and beta are salience/learning-rate parameters. The term **(lambda minus V-sum)** is the **prediction error** or "surprise": large when the outcome is unexpected, near zero when fully predicted (so learning stops).
- **Blocking (Kamin):** If cue A already fully predicts the US, adding cue B in compound (AB to US) produces little conditioning to B, because there is no prediction error left for B to absorb. This is the signature prediction of Rescorla-Wagner.
- **Overshadowing:** When two cues are presented together from the start, the **more salient** cue captures most of the associative strength, weakening conditioning to the less salient cue.
- **Garcia effect (conditioned taste aversion):** John Garcia showed animals form a strong aversion to a **taste** paired with later nausea even with a **long delay** (hours) between taste and illness, and even after a single trial. It is **biologically prepared** (tastes associate readily with illness but not with shock; lights/sounds associate with shock but not illness), challenging the idea that any CS-US pairing conditions equally.

### 2.2 Operant (Instrumental) Conditioning

Behaviour is shaped by its consequences.

- **Thorndike's Law of Effect:** Using puzzle boxes with cats, Edward Thorndike found that responses followed by **satisfying** consequences are strengthened ("stamped in") and those followed by **annoying** consequences are weakened. Learning was gradual trial-and-error, not insight.
- **B.F. Skinner** formalized operant conditioning using the **Skinner box (operant chamber)**. Key terms:

| Consequence | Adds or removes | Effect on behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive reinforcement** | Adds a pleasant stimulus | Increases behaviour |
| **Negative reinforcement** | Removes an aversive stimulus | Increases behaviour |
| **Positive punishment** | Adds an aversive stimulus | Decreases behaviour |
| **Negative punishment (omission)** | Removes a pleasant stimulus | Decreases behaviour |

Note: negative reinforcement is **not** punishment; it strengthens behaviour by relief. Related concepts: **shaping** (reinforcing successive approximations to a target behaviour), **primary** vs **secondary (conditioned) reinforcers** (food vs money), and the **Premack principle** (a high-frequency behaviour can reinforce a low-frequency one).

**Schedules of reinforcement:**

| Schedule | Definition | Response pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed Ratio (FR)** | Reinforce after a set number of responses | High rate, short post-reinforcement pause |
| **Variable Ratio (VR)** | Reinforce after an unpredictable, averaged number of responses | **Highest, steadiest rate; most resistant to extinction** (gambling/slot machines) |
| **Fixed Interval (FI)** | Reinforce first response after a set time | "Scalloped" pattern; responding accelerates near the interval's end |
| **Variable Interval (VI)** | Reinforce first response after an unpredictable, averaged time | Slow, steady, consistent responding |

Exam keys: **continuous reinforcement** produces fastest acquisition but fastest extinction; **VR** produces the highest response rate and the greatest resistance to extinction. The slower, persistent extinction following intermittent reinforcement is the **partial reinforcement extinction effect**.

### 2.3 Cognitive Learning

These theories argue that learning involves internal mental representations, not just stimulus-response bonds.

- **Edward Tolman:** Demonstrated **latent learning** (learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to display it). Rats allowed to explore a maze unrewarded later solved it as fast as continuously-rewarded rats once a reward was introduced, showing they had built a **cognitive map** (an internal spatial representation). This is "purposive behaviourism".
- **Wolfgang Kohler (Gestalt):** Studied **insight learning** in chimpanzees (notably Sultan), who solved problems (stacking boxes, joining sticks to reach a banana) suddenly and completely after a pause, the "**aha**" experience, rather than by gradual trial-and-error. Insight implies perceptual restructuring of the problem.
- **Albert Bandura, social (observational) learning:** People learn by watching models, without direct reinforcement. The famous **Bobo doll** studies showed children imitating aggression they had observed. Modeling proceeds through four steps (mnemonic **ARRM**): **Attention** (notice the model), **Retention** (remember it), **Reproduction/Production** (physically able to reproduce it), and **Motivation** (a reason to perform, often via **vicarious reinforcement**, seeing the model rewarded). Bandura's later work emphasized **reciprocal determinism** (person, behaviour, and environment mutually influence each other) and **self-efficacy**.

## 3. Perception and Attention

### 3.1 Signal Detection Theory (SDT)

SDT separates **sensitivity** (genuine perceptual discriminability) from **decision bias** (willingness to say "yes"). On each trial a signal is present or absent and the observer responds yes or no, giving a 2x2 outcome matrix:

| | Observer says "Signal" | Observer says "No signal" |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal present** | **Hit** | **Miss** |
| **Signal absent** | **False alarm** | **Correct rejection** |

- **d-prime (d'):** The **sensitivity** index, the separation between the noise and signal-plus-noise distributions in standard deviation units. Higher d' means the signal is easier to detect; it is independent of the observer's criterion.
- **Beta (criterion):** The **response bias** or decision threshold. A **conservative** (high) criterion yields few false alarms but more misses (saying "no" when unsure); a **liberal** (low) criterion yields more hits but more false alarms. Payoffs and base rates shift beta without changing d'. Clinical relevance: a radiologist who must not miss a tumor adopts a liberal criterion (accepting more false alarms).

### 3.2 Gestalt Principles of Perceptual Organization

The Gestalt school ("the whole is different from the sum of its parts") described laws by which we group elements into wholes. The overarching principle is **Pragnanz** (the law of good form / simplicity: we perceive the simplest, most stable, most regular organization possible).

- **Proximity:** Elements near each other are grouped together.
- **Similarity:** Elements that look alike (colour, shape, size) are grouped.
- **Continuity (good continuation):** We perceive smooth, continuous contours rather than abrupt changes.
- **Closure:** We mentally fill gaps to perceive complete, closed figures.
- **Common fate:** Elements moving together in the same direction are grouped (a flock of birds).
- **Figure-ground:** We segregate a scene into a foreground figure and a background (illustrated by reversible figures like the Rubin vase).

### 3.3 Depth Cues

We reconstruct three-dimensional depth from a two-dimensional retinal image.

- **Monocular cues** (available to one eye, used by artists, hence "pictorial"): **relative size**, **interposition/overlap**, **linear perspective** (converging parallel lines), **texture gradient**, **aerial/atmospheric perspective** (distant objects hazier), **relative height**, **light and shadow**, and the motion-based **motion parallax** (near objects appear to move faster than far ones as you move). **Accommodation** (lens-shape change) is an oculomotor monocular cue effective at close range.
- **Binocular cues** (require both eyes): **retinal (binocular) disparity** (each eye gets a slightly different image; the greater the disparity the closer the object; the basis of stereopsis and 3-D viewing) and **convergence** (the inward turning of the eyes for near objects, the kinaesthetic feedback signalling distance, effective within about 10 metres).

### 3.4 Models of Attention (Selective Attention)

These models debate **where** in processing the filter for unattended information sits.

- **Broadbent's filter model (1958), early selection:** Information enters a sensory buffer, then a **filter selects one channel based on physical characteristics** (e.g. which ear, voice pitch) early, before meaning is analyzed. Unattended channels are blocked entirely. Supported by **dichotic listening / shadowing** tasks. Problem: it cannot explain the **cocktail party effect** (hearing your own name on an unattended channel), which requires some meaning analysis of the rejected channel.
- **Treisman's attenuation model (1964):** The filter does not block but **attenuates (turns down)** the unattended channel rather than eliminating it. High-priority or low-threshold words (your name, "fire") can still break through the attenuated signal. This is a "leaky filter" still operating relatively early.
- **Deutsch-Norman late-selection model (1963/1968):** **All** incoming information is fully processed for meaning, and selection occurs **late**, just before response/awareness, based on relevance. Attention determines what reaches consciousness and memory, not what gets perceived.

The modern consensus is flexible: selection can be early or late depending on perceptual load (Lavie's **load theory**), reconciling the debate.

## High-Yield Recap

- **Atkinson-Shiffrin:** sensory (iconic ~0.25 to 0.5 s; echoic ~2 to 4 s), STS (**7 plus or minus 2 chunks**, ~15 to 30 s), unlimited LTS. Sperling = iconic; Peterson and Peterson = STS duration; serial position = primacy (LTS) + recency (STS).
- **Baddeley-Hitch working memory:** central executive (controller), phonological loop (store + articulatory rehearsal), visuospatial sketchpad, **episodic buffer** (added 2000). Word-length and phonological-similarity effects support the loop.
- **Tulving:** declarative (episodic + semantic) vs non-declarative (procedural, priming, conditioning). H.M. lost declarative, kept procedural.
- **Forgetting:** Ebbinghaus curve = rapid then leveling; **PI = old hurts new**, **RI = new hurts old**; **Zeigarnik = incomplete tasks remembered better**.
- **Classical:** US/UR/CS/CR; acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination, higher-order. Watson = Little Albert.
- **Rescorla-Wagner:** delta-V proportional to **(lambda minus V-sum)** = prediction error/surprise; explains **blocking**; **overshadowing** = salient cue wins; **Garcia effect** = one-trial, long-delay, biologically-prepared taste aversion.
- **Operant:** Thorndike Law of Effect; Skinner. Negative reinforcement increases behaviour (relief), not punishment. **VR = highest rate and most extinction-resistant.** FI = scalloped.
- **Cognitive learning:** Tolman = latent learning + cognitive maps; Kohler = insight ("aha"); Bandura = observational learning, steps **Attention, Retention, Production, Motivation**, Bobo doll, vicarious reinforcement, self-efficacy.
- **SDT:** hit / miss / false alarm / correct rejection; **d' = sensitivity** (criterion-free), **beta = bias** (shifted by payoffs/base rates).
- **Gestalt:** proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, common fate, figure-ground, under **Pragnanz** (simplicity).
- **Depth:** monocular (relative size, interposition, linear perspective, texture gradient, aerial perspective, motion parallax, accommodation) vs binocular (**retinal disparity**, **convergence**).
- **Attention:** **Broadbent = early selection** (physical filter, blocks); **Treisman = attenuation** (leaky filter, name breaks through); **Deutsch-Norman = late selection** (full meaning analysis, then select).
