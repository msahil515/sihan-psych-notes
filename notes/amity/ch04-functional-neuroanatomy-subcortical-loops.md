# Ch 4: Functional Neuroanatomy & Subcortical Systems Loops

This chapter builds the physical map of the brain you will be tested on: how the cortex is layered, how Brodmann numbered its functional patches, how the limbic Papez circuit and amygdala wire emotion and memory, and how the basal ganglia run two opposing loops that decide whether a movement happens. Entrance papers love these because they convert a single anatomical fact into a clinical inference (a lesion at point X produces deficit Y), so learn each structure together with what its damage causes.

## 1. Cortical Cytoarchitecture: The Six-Layered Neocortex

The **neocortex** (also called **isocortex** or **neopallium**) is the evolutionarily newest cortex and makes up about 90 percent of the human cerebral cortex. Its defining feature is **six horizontal layers** (laminae) of cell bodies, numbered I (outermost, at the pial surface) to VI (innermost, against the white matter). This contrasts with **allocortex** (the older three-layered cortex of the hippocampus and olfactory areas). The study of these layers is called **cytoarchitecture**, and Korbinian Brodmann's 1909 map was built entirely by looking at how these layers differed in thickness from region to region.

The six layers, from surface inward:

| Layer | Name | Principal cells | Functional role |
|-------|------|-----------------|-----------------|
| I | Molecular (plexiform) | Few neurons, mostly dendrites and axons | Horizontal connections, apical dendrite tufts |
| II | External granular | Small pyramidal and granule (stellate) cells | Corticocortical (association) connections |
| III | External pyramidal | Small to medium pyramidal cells | Main source of corticocortical output fibers |
| IV | Internal granular | Densely packed stellate (granule) cells | **Receives thalamocortical INPUT** |
| V | Internal pyramidal | Large pyramidal cells (incl. **Betz cells**) | Main subcortical OUTPUT (to basal ganglia, brainstem, spinal cord) |
| VI | Multiform (polymorphic) | Spindle and pyramidal cells | Output back to the thalamus (corticothalamic) |

Two layers carry the most exam weight. **Layer IV (internal granular layer)** is the chief recipient of sensory information arriving from the thalamus; it is therefore thickest in primary sensory cortices (the **granular cortex** of visual, auditory and somatosensory areas) and almost absent in primary motor cortex, which is called **agranular cortex** for that reason. **Layer V (internal pyramidal layer)** holds the largest output neurons. In the primary motor cortex (BA4) these include the **Betz cells**, giant pyramidal neurons (cell bodies up to 100 micrometers) named after Vladimir Betz; their axons form a substantial part of the **corticospinal (pyramidal) tract** that drives voluntary movement. A useful mnemonic: **"4 takes in, 5 sends out"** (Layer 4 = thalamic input, Layer 5 = motor/subcortical output).

## 2. Brodmann Areas: The Functional Address Map

Brodmann divided each hemisphere into roughly **52 areas** based on cytoarchitecture, and clinicians later mapped functions onto them. You must know the high-yield ones cold.

### Sensory and motor strip
- **BA 1, 2, 3, Primary somatosensory cortex (S1).** Located on the **postcentral gyrus** of the parietal lobe. It receives touch, pressure, vibration and proprioception from the contralateral body via the thalamic VPL/VPM nuclei. The body is mapped here as the **sensory homunculus** (face and hand grossly over-represented). Order within the strip from the central sulcus backward is 3, then 1, then 2.
- **BA 4, Primary motor cortex (M1).** The **precentral gyrus**, immediately anterior to the central sulcus. Houses the **Betz cells** and the **motor homunculus**; it executes voluntary contralateral movement. Damage causes contralateral spastic paresis.
- **BA 6, Premotor cortex and Supplementary Motor Area (SMA).** Anterior to BA4. It plans and sequences movement, prepares posture, and is active in internally generated (self-initiated) actions. SMA lesions cause apraxia and difficulty initiating movement.
- **BA 8, Frontal eye fields (FEF).** Controls voluntary conjugate (saccadic) eye movements toward the contralateral side. A destructive lesion makes the eyes deviate toward the side of the lesion.

### Prefrontal cortex (executive brain)
- **BA 9 to 12, Prefrontal cortex.** This is the substrate of executive function, personality and judgment. Three functional subdivisions are tested:
  - **Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, ~BA 9 and 46):** working memory, planning, cognitive flexibility, abstract reasoning. Damage produces the **dysexecutive syndrome** (poor planning, perseveration, impaired set-shifting).
  - **Ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC, ~BA 10/11/12 medial):** emotional regulation, value-based and social decision-making. Classic case: **Phineas Gage**, whose VMPFC damage left intellect intact but wrecked judgment and social conduct.
  - **Orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, ~BA 11/12):** impulse control, reward valuation, inhibition of socially inappropriate behavior. Damage causes disinhibition, impulsivity and **pseudopsychopathic** behavior.

### Visual cortex
- **BA 17, Primary visual cortex (V1, striate cortex).** On the **calcarine sulcus** of the occipital lobe. It is "striate" because of the visible **stria of Gennari** (a heavily myelinated band in layer IV). It is the first cortical destination of vision; damage causes contralateral homonymous hemianopia or cortical blindness.
- **BA 18, 19, Extrastriate / visual association cortex (V2 to V5).** These process motion, color, depth and form. From here arise two streams: the **dorsal "where/how" stream** (occipital to parietal, spatial location and visually guided action) and the **ventral "what" stream** (occipital to temporal, object and face recognition). Ventral-stream damage causes **visual agnosia**; dorsal-stream damage can cause optic ataxia and simultanagnosia.

### Language and higher association cortex (usually left hemisphere)
- **BA 22, Wernicke's area.** Posterior **superior temporal gyrus**. Comprehension of spoken and written language. Damage causes **Wernicke's (receptive/fluent) aphasia**: speech is fluent but meaningless ("word salad") with poor comprehension.
- **BA 44, 45, Broca's area.** **Inferior frontal gyrus** (pars opercularis = 44, pars triangularis = 45). Speech production and grammar. Damage causes **Broca's (expressive/non-fluent) aphasia**: effortful, telegraphic speech with relatively preserved comprehension; awareness of the deficit causes frustration.
- **BA 39, Angular gyrus** (inferior parietal lobule). Reading, writing, and cross-modal integration. Damage contributes to **Gerstmann syndrome** (agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, left-right disorientation) and to alexia.
- **BA 40, Supramarginal gyrus** (inferior parietal lobule). Phonological processing, the storage component of verbal working memory, and tool use; lesions contribute to **conduction aphasia** (impaired repetition with intact comprehension and fluency, due to arcuate fasciculus involvement) and ideomotor apraxia.
- **BA 41, 42, Primary auditory cortex (A1).** On **Heschl's gyrus** of the superior temporal lobe. Receives input from the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus. Tonotopically organized (different pitches map to different positions).

## 3. The Papez Circuit: The Anatomy of Emotion and Episodic Memory

In 1937 James Papez proposed a closed loop of structures that he believed carried emotion; it was later folded into the broader **limbic system** by Paul MacLean. Whatever its exact role in feeling, the **Papez circuit** is now understood as central to the consolidation of **episodic memory**, and exams test its exact sequence. Memorize the loop in order:

**Hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → mammillothalamic tract → anterior thalamic nucleus → cingulate gyrus → entorhinal cortex → back to hippocampus.**

Step by step:
1. The **hippocampus** (in the medial temporal lobe) is the origin and the structure most associated with forming new declarative memories.
2. Its output travels through the **fornix**, a C-shaped white-matter tract, to the **mammillary bodies** of the posterior hypothalamus.
3. From the mammillary bodies the **mammillothalamic tract** (also called the bundle of Vicq d'Azyr) projects to the **anterior nucleus of the thalamus**.
4. The anterior thalamus projects to the **cingulate gyrus** (the limbic cortex arching over the corpus callosum), thought to add the conscious, experiential "color" of emotion.
5. The cingulate projects to the **entorhinal cortex** (the gateway region of the parahippocampal gyrus), which feeds back into the hippocampus, closing the loop.

**Clinical relevance:** damage anywhere along this circuit produces amnesia. **Korsakoff syndrome** (thiamine/vitamin B1 deficiency, classically in chronic alcoholism) destroys the **mammillary bodies** and dorsomedial thalamus, producing severe anterograde amnesia and **confabulation**. Bilateral hippocampal damage (the patient **H.M.**, who had his medial temporal lobes resected for epilepsy) produces dense anterograde amnesia with preserved procedural learning, the single most cited case in memory neuroscience.

## 4. The Amygdala: Threat Detection and Emotional Salience

The **amygdala** is an almond-shaped cluster of nuclei in the anterior medial temporal lobe, just rostral to the hippocampus. It is the brain's salience and fear detector and is essential for **fear conditioning**. For the exam, know its input/output organization in terms of three nuclear groups.

| Nuclear group | Role | Connections |
|---------------|------|-------------|
| **Lateral nucleus** | Sensory **INPUT** gateway | Receives info from thalamus and sensory cortex |
| **Basolateral complex (BLA)** | Associative processing | Integrates sensory and contextual (hippocampal) input; links stimulus to emotional value |
| **Central nucleus** | **OUTPUT** hub | Projects to hypothalamus and brainstem to generate the fear response |

The flow is: sensory stimulus → **lateral / basolateral nuclei** (input and association) → **central nucleus** (output) → effector targets. Two output targets are high-yield:
- Projection to the **hypothalamus** drives the autonomic and endocrine fear response (increased heart rate, blood pressure, cortisol via the HPA axis).
- Projection to the **periaqueductal gray (PAG)** in the midbrain drives behavioral responses such as freezing and defensive posturing.

Joseph LeDoux described **two routes to the amygdala**: a fast, crude **"low road"** (thalamus directly to amygdala) that triggers rapid defensive reactions before conscious awareness, and a slower, accurate **"high road"** (thalamus to sensory cortex to amygdala) that allows a considered response. **Klüver-Bucy syndrome** results from bilateral amygdala (anterior temporal) damage: hyperorality, hypersexuality, placidity (loss of fear), and visual agnosia.

## 5. Basal Ganglia: The Direct and Indirect Motor Loops

The **basal ganglia** are subcortical nuclei that gate and scale movement: the **striatum** (caudate + putamen), the **globus pallidus** (external segment GPe and internal segment GPi), the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)**, and the **substantia nigra** (pars compacta SNc, which makes dopamine, and pars reticulata SNr, an output nucleus). The overall logic: the cortex sends a movement plan to the striatum, and two opposing pathways either **release** or **suppress** the thalamus, which in turn excites or fails to excite the motor cortex. The default state of the output nuclei (**GPi/SNr**) is **tonically active and inhibitory** on the thalamus, so movement requires that this brake be lifted.

### The Direct Pathway (pro-kinetic: "go")
- Cortex excites striatal neurons that carry **D1 dopamine receptors** (excitatory to these neurons).
- These D1 striatal neurons **inhibit GPi/SNr** (the output nuclei).
- Because GPi/SNr normally inhibit the thalamus, inhibiting them **disinhibits the thalamus** (a double negative).
- The disinhibited thalamus then **excites the motor cortex** → movement is facilitated.
- Net effect: **pro-kinetic** (promotes movement). Chain: Cortex → striatum (D1) → (inhibit) GPi/SNr → (release) thalamus → (excite) cortex.

### The Indirect Pathway (anti-kinetic: "stop")
- Cortex excites striatal neurons carrying **D2 dopamine receptors** (inhibitory to these neurons).
- These D2 striatal neurons **inhibit GPe**.
- Inhibited GPe releases its brake on the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)**, which becomes more active.
- The excited STN **drives GPi/SNr harder**, increasing inhibition of the thalamus.
- The over-inhibited thalamus **fails to excite the motor cortex** → movement is suppressed.
- Net effect: **anti-kinetic** (suppresses unwanted movement). Chain: Cortex → striatum (D2) → (inhibit) GPe → (release) STN → (drive) GPi/SNr → (suppress) thalamus.

### How dopamine tilts the balance
**Dopamine from the SNc** acts on both pathways at once: it **excites the D1 direct pathway** (boosting "go") and **inhibits the D2 indirect pathway** (relaxing "stop"). So dopamine on the whole **promotes movement**.

| Disorder | Pathology | Effect on loops | Clinical picture |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Parkinson's disease** | SNc dopamine neuron loss | Less D1 drive, more D2 (indirect) drive → thalamus over-inhibited | **Hypokinetic**: bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, postural instability |
| **Huntington's disease** | Loss of D2 striatal neurons (indirect pathway) | Indirect "stop" pathway fails → thalamus under-inhibited | **Hyperkinetic**: chorea, involuntary writhing movements |
| **Hemiballismus** | Lesion of the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)** | Indirect pathway brake lost | Violent flinging movements of the contralateral limbs |

This direct/indirect framework is one of the most heavily tested topics in neuroscience sections, because it ties receptor pharmacology (D1 vs D2), specific nuclei, and the major movement disorders into a single coherent circuit.

## High-Yield Recap
- **Neocortex = 6 layers.** **Layer IV (internal granular)** = thalamocortical **input** (thick in sensory cortex, absent in agranular motor cortex). **Layer V (internal pyramidal)** = subcortical **output**, contains **Betz cells** of BA4 feeding the corticospinal tract.
- **Brodmann strip:** BA **1,2,3** = somatosensory (postcentral gyrus); BA **4** = primary motor (precentral, Betz cells); BA **6** = premotor + SMA; BA **8** = frontal eye fields.
- **Prefrontal BA 9 to 12:** **DLPFC** = working memory/planning; **VMPFC** = emotion + social decisions (Phineas Gage); **OFC** = impulse control/disinhibition.
- **Vision:** BA **17** = primary visual / striate (calcarine sulcus, stria of Gennari); BA **18, 19** = extrastriate, with **dorsal "where/how"** stream and **ventral "what"** stream.
- **Language (left):** BA **22** Wernicke (comprehension; fluent aphasia); BA **44, 45** Broca (production; non-fluent aphasia); BA **39** angular (reading, Gerstmann); BA **40** supramarginal (phonology, conduction aphasia); BA **41, 42** auditory (Heschl's gyrus).
- **Papez circuit order:** hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → mammillothalamic tract → anterior thalamic nucleus → cingulate → entorhinal cortex → hippocampus. Korsakoff = mammillary body damage from B1 deficiency.
- **Amygdala:** **lateral/basolateral** = input/association; **central nucleus** = output to **hypothalamus** (autonomic) and **PAG** (freezing). LeDoux low road vs high road. Bilateral damage = Klüver-Bucy.
- **Direct pathway (D1):** Cortex → striatum → inhibit GPi/SNr → disinhibit thalamus → **pro-kinetic ("go")**.
- **Indirect pathway (D2):** Cortex → striatum → inhibit GPe → release STN → drive GPi/SNr → inhibit thalamus → **anti-kinetic ("stop")**.
- **Dopamine** excites D1 (direct) and inhibits D2 (indirect): overall promotes movement.
- **Parkinson's** = SNc dopamine loss → hypokinetic. **Huntington's** = indirect-pathway neuron loss → hyperkinetic chorea. **Hemiballismus** = STN lesion.
