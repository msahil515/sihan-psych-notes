# Ch 3: Extended Neurochemistry & Neuropharmacology

This chapter covers the five neurotransmitter systems most heavily tested in RCI and NIMHANS-pattern entrances: dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, glutamate, and GABA. For each you must know the synthesis pathway (with the rate-limiting enzyme), how the transmitter is broken down, where its pathways run in the brain, which receptors it acts on, and the clinical/drug relevance. Examiners love to ask "which enzyme is rate-limiting", "which receptor subtype is ionotropic", and "which drug acts at which site", so commit the named enzymes, receptor families, and degradation products precisely.

## 1. Dopamine (DA)

Dopamine is a **catecholamine** (it shares the catechol ring with norepinephrine and epinephrine) and is one of the most clinically important transmitters because it sits at the center of Parkinson's disease, schizophrenia, addiction, and the reward system.

### Synthesis

Dopamine is built from the amino acid **tyrosine** in two enzymatic steps:

1. **Tyrosine → L-DOPA**, catalyzed by **tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)**. This is the **rate-limiting (slowest, controlling) step** of the entire catecholamine pathway. Tyrosine hydroxylase requires the cofactor **tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)** and is inhibited by its end products (end-product feedback inhibition), so when downstream catecholamine levels are high, TH activity falls.
2. **L-DOPA → Dopamine**, catalyzed by **DOPA decarboxylase** (also called **aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, AADC**), which uses **vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate)** as a cofactor.

Exam point: in noradrenergic neurons dopamine is then converted to norepinephrine by **dopamine beta-hydroxylase**, and in the adrenal medulla norepinephrine becomes epinephrine via **PNMT**. But in dopaminergic neurons the chain stops at dopamine. The drug **L-DOPA (levodopa)** is given in Parkinson's because it crosses the blood-brain barrier (dopamine itself cannot) and is then decarboxylated to dopamine inside the brain; it is co-given with **carbidopa**, a peripheral DOPA-decarboxylase inhibitor, so the conversion does not happen prematurely in the body.

### Catabolism (breakdown)

Dopamine is degraded by two enzymes acting in sequence or in parallel:

- **Monoamine oxidase (MAO)**, located on the outer mitochondrial membrane (intraneuronal).
- **Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT)**, located extracellularly / postsynaptically.

The major final metabolite is **homovanillic acid (HVA)**. Measuring HVA in CSF is a classic index of central dopamine turnover. MAO exists as MAO-A and MAO-B; **MAO-B** is the predominant form metabolizing dopamine in the brain, which is why the MAO-B inhibitor **selegiline** is used in Parkinson's.

### Dopaminergic tracts (memorize all four)

| Tract | Origin → Target | Function | Clinical relevance |
|-------|-----------------|----------|--------------------|
| **Mesolimbic** | Ventral tegmental area (VTA) → nucleus accumbens / limbic system | Reward, motivation, pleasure | **Overactivity → positive symptoms** of schizophrenia (hallucinations, delusions); drug reward |
| **Mesocortical** | VTA → prefrontal cortex | Cognition, executive function, motivation | **Underactivity → negative/cognitive symptoms** of schizophrenia |
| **Nigrostriatal** | Substantia nigra (pars compacta) → striatum (caudate/putamen) | Movement control | Degeneration → **Parkinson's disease**; blockade → **extrapyramidal side effects (EPS)** |
| **Tuberoinfundibular** | Hypothalamus (arcuate) → pituitary | DA inhibits prolactin release | Blockade → **hyperprolactinemia** (galactorrhea, amenorrhea) |

### Receptors: D1-like vs D2-like

All dopamine receptors are **G-protein coupled (metabotropic)**, grouped into two families:

- **D1-like family (D1, D5):** couple to **Gs**, which **stimulates adenylyl cyclase** and **raises cAMP** (excitatory tone).
- **D2-like family (D2, D3, D4):** couple to **Gi**, which **inhibits adenylyl cyclase** and **lowers cAMP** (inhibitory).

The **D2 receptor is the key antipsychotic target**: the **dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia** holds that positive symptoms arise from excess dopamine activity at D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway.

### Typical vs atypical antipsychotics

| Feature | **Typical (first-generation)** | **Atypical (second-generation)** |
|---------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Examples | Haloperidol, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine | Risperidone, olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole |
| Main mechanism | Strong **D2 blockade** | **D2 + 5-HT2A blockade** (serotonin-dopamine antagonists) |
| Symptoms treated | Mainly positive symptoms | Positive and (somewhat) negative/cognitive |
| EPS risk | **High** (nigrostriatal D2 block) | **Lower** |
| Prolactin | Often raised | Lower (except risperidone) |
| Signature risk | Tardive dyskinesia, NMS | Metabolic (weight gain, diabetes); **clozapine → agranulocytosis** (needs blood monitoring) |

## 2. Serotonin (5-HT, 5-hydroxytryptamine)

Serotonin is an **indoleamine** (not a catecholamine) and is central to mood, sleep, appetite, and the action of antidepressants.

### Synthesis

Serotonin is made from the essential amino acid **tryptophan** in two steps:

1. **Tryptophan → 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP)**, catalyzed by **tryptophan hydroxylase**. This is the **rate-limiting enzyme** of serotonin synthesis (parallel logic to tyrosine hydroxylase for dopamine). It is not normally saturated, so serotonin levels are sensitive to dietary tryptophan availability.
2. **5-HTP → Serotonin (5-HT)**, catalyzed by the same **aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC / DOPA decarboxylase)** used in the dopamine pathway, again using vitamin B6.

### Catabolism

Serotonin is broken down by **monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A)** into **5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA)**, the principal serotonin metabolite measured in CSF. Low CSF 5-HIAA has been linked to impulsivity, aggression, and suicide risk, a frequently tested association.

### Raphe nuclei

The cell bodies of serotonergic neurons are clustered in the **raphe nuclei**, a midline series of nuclei in the **brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla)**. From there serotonin neurons project diffusely to almost the entire forebrain and cortex, which is why serotonin modulates global states like mood and arousal.

### Serotonin receptors

There are at least seven families (5-HT1 through 5-HT7). Almost all are **metabotropic (G-protein coupled)**. The one major exception is the **5-HT3 receptor**, which is **ionotropic** (a ligand-gated cation channel). Know these three for the exam:

- **5-HT1A:** Acts as a presynaptic **autoreceptor** on raphe neurons (and a postsynaptic receptor in the hippocampus/cortex). As an autoreceptor it inhibits further serotonin release through negative feedback. This is the **delayed-onset mechanism of SSRIs**: when an SSRI floods the synapse, 5-HT1A autoreceptors are initially stimulated and damp firing, but after about **2 to 4 weeks** they **desensitize**, firing recovers, and clinical antidepressant benefit appears. **Buspirone** is a 5-HT1A partial agonist used for generalized anxiety.
- **5-HT2A:** Postsynaptic; its blockade is a defining feature of atypical antipsychotics. It is also the receptor that classic hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin) act on as agonists.
- **5-HT3:** The **only ionotropic** serotonin receptor; located in the area postrema/gut and mediates nausea and vomiting. Antagonists (**ondansetron**) are antiemetics.

Clinical capstone: **Serotonin syndrome** (autonomic instability, hyperthermia, hyperreflexia/clonus, agitation) results from excess serotonergic activity, classically when an SSRI is combined with an MAO inhibitor.

## 3. Acetylcholine (ACh)

Acetylcholine is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, throughout the autonomic nervous system, and in central pathways governing memory and arousal.

### Synthesis and termination

- **Synthesis:** Acetylcholine is formed from **choline + acetyl-CoA** by the enzyme **choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)**. ChAT is the marker enzyme for cholinergic neurons.
- **Termination:** Unlike monoamines (which are reuptaken or oxidized), acetylcholine is rapidly hydrolyzed in the synaptic cleft by **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** into **choline + acetate**. The choline is taken back up for reuse. This is why **AChE inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) raise ACh and are used in **Alzheimer's disease**, and why organophosphate poisons (which irreversibly inhibit AChE) cause a cholinergic crisis.

### Receptors: nicotinic vs muscarinic

| Feature | **Nicotinic (nAChR)** | **Muscarinic (mAChR)** |
|---------|-----------------------|------------------------|
| Type | **Ionotropic** (ligand-gated Na+/K+ channel) | **Metabotropic** (G-protein coupled) |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Location | Neuromuscular junction, autonomic ganglia, CNS | Heart, smooth muscle, glands, CNS |
| Agonist | Nicotine | Muscarine |
| Subtypes | Muscle-type, neuronal-type | **M1 to M5** |

Muscarinic subtypes: **M1, M3, M5 couple to Gq** (raise IP3/calcium, excitatory), while **M2 and M4 couple to Gi** (lower cAMP; M2 in the heart slows heart rate). The mnemonic "odd-numbered M receptors (M1, M3, M5) are Gq" is exam-useful. Blockade of muscarinic receptors (anticholinergic effect) causes dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, and confusion, a common side-effect cluster of tricyclic antidepressants and low-potency antipsychotics.

### Nucleus basalis of Meynert

The **nucleus basalis of Meynert**, in the **basal forebrain**, is the principal source of cholinergic projections to the cerebral cortex. Its degeneration and the consequent **cholinergic deficit** is a hallmark of **Alzheimer's disease**, providing the rationale for AChE-inhibitor therapy. This is a very high-yield single fact.

## 4. Glutamate

Glutamate is the **principal excitatory neurotransmitter** of the central nervous system (used at the majority of fast excitatory synapses).

### Synthesis and removal

- **Synthesis:** Glutamate is produced from **glutamine** by the enzyme **glutaminase**. The glutamate-glutamine cycle shuttles the transmitter between neurons and astrocytes.
- **Removal:** There is no extracellular enzyme that destroys glutamate. Instead it is cleared by reuptake through **excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs)**, located mainly on astrocytes, which convert it back to glutamine.

### Receptors

Glutamate acts on both ionotropic and metabotropic receptors. The three ionotropic types are named after selective agonists:

- **NMDA receptor:** A ligand-gated channel with three special properties tested constantly: (1) it is **blocked by Mg2+** at resting membrane potential, and the block is removed only when the membrane is **depolarized** (so it is both ligand-gated and voltage-dependent); (2) it requires **glycine (or D-serine) as an obligatory co-agonist** in addition to glutamate; (3) once open it is highly permeable to **calcium (Ca2+)**, the calcium influx triggering the intracellular cascades behind **long-term potentiation (LTP)**, the cellular basis of learning and memory. **Ketamine** and PCP are NMDA antagonists.
- **AMPA receptor:** Mediates the fast component of excitatory transmission; permeable mainly to Na+ (and K+). AMPA receptor activation provides the depolarization that relieves the Mg2+ block on NMDA receptors.
- **Kainate receptor:** A third ionotropic subtype with modulatory pre- and postsynaptic roles.

The metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR1 to mGluR8) are G-protein coupled and modulatory.

### Excitotoxicity

**Excitotoxicity** is cell death caused by **excessive glutamate** (for example after stroke, head injury, or hypoxia). Overactivation of NMDA receptors lets in too much **calcium**, which activates destructive enzymes (proteases, lipases, endonucleases) and free radicals, killing the neuron. This concept links glutamate to neurodegeneration; **memantine**, a low-affinity NMDA antagonist, is used in moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's partly on this rationale.

## 5. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid)

GABA is the **principal inhibitory neurotransmitter** of the brain, the counterweight to glutamate.

### Synthesis

GABA is made from **glutamate** by the enzyme **glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)**. Note the elegant pairing: the brain's main excitatory transmitter is the direct precursor of its main inhibitory one. GAD requires **vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate)** as a cofactor, which is why severe B6 deficiency (or B6 antagonists like isoniazid) can lower GABA and provoke **seizures**.

### Receptors: GABA-A vs GABA-B

| Feature | **GABA-A** | **GABA-B** |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Type | **Ionotropic** ligand-gated **chloride (Cl-) channel** | **Metabotropic**, G-protein coupled (**Gi**) |
| Effect | Cl- influx → fast hyperpolarization / inhibition | Opens K+ channels, closes Ca2+ channels → slow inhibition |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Agonist/drug | Muscimol; benzodiazepines, barbiturates | **Baclofen** (agonist, used for spasticity) |

The **GABA-A receptor** is a major pharmacological hub. It is a chloride channel with several distinct **allosteric modulatory binding sites**:

- **Benzodiazepine site:** Drugs like diazepam and lorazepam bind here and **increase the frequency** of channel opening. Benzodiazepines require GABA to be present (they are positive allosteric modulators, not direct openers), which is why they have a comparatively wide safety margin.
- **Barbiturate site:** Drugs like phenobarbital bind here and **increase the duration** of channel opening; at high doses they can open the channel directly without GABA, which is why barbiturate overdose is far more dangerous (respiratory depression) than benzodiazepine overdose.
- **Ethanol** also potentiates GABA-A, contributing to alcohol's sedative effect and to the cross-tolerance and overlapping withdrawal seen with benzodiazepines.
- **Neurosteroids** (such as allopregnanolone) bind the receptor and enhance GABAergic inhibition; this underlies the rapid antidepressant **brexanolone** used in postpartum depression.

Mnemonic for the exam: **benzodiazepines increase Frequency (F = Five letters in Benzo? use "Frequent Benzos"), barbiturates increase Duration ("Barbie Dolls last a long Duration")**. Both ultimately enhance chloride conductance and CNS inhibition.

## High-Yield Recap

- **Dopamine synthesis:** Tyrosine → L-DOPA (**tyrosine hydroxylase**, rate-limiting, needs BH4) → Dopamine (**DOPA decarboxylase / AADC**, needs B6). Breakdown by **MAO + COMT → HVA**.
- **DA receptors:** **D1-like (D1, D5) = Gs (↑cAMP); D2-like (D2, D3, D4) = Gi (↓cAMP)**. D2 is the antipsychotic target.
- **DA tracts:** mesolimbic (reward; positive symptoms), mesocortical (cognition; negative symptoms), **nigrostriatal (movement; Parkinson's, EPS)**, tuberoinfundibular (prolactin).
- **Antipsychotics:** typical = strong D2 block, high EPS; atypical = D2 + **5-HT2A** block, lower EPS, metabolic risk; **clozapine → agranulocytosis**.
- **Serotonin:** Tryptophan → 5-HTP (**tryptophan hydroxylase**, rate-limiting) → 5-HT; broken down by **MAO-A → 5-HIAA**; cell bodies in **raphe nuclei**. **5-HT1A autoreceptor desensitization (2 to 4 weeks)** explains SSRI lag. **5-HT3 is the only ionotropic** serotonin receptor.
- **Acetylcholine:** made by **ChAT** from choline + acetyl-CoA; terminated by **AChE** (not reuptake). **Nicotinic = ionotropic; muscarinic M1-M5 = metabotropic** (M1/M3/M5 Gq, M2/M4 Gi). **Nucleus basalis of Meynert** degeneration = Alzheimer's cholinergic deficit.
- **Glutamate:** main **excitatory** transmitter; made by **glutaminase** from glutamine; cleared by **EAATs**. **NMDA: Mg2+ block (voltage-dependent), glycine co-agonist, Ca2+ influx → LTP.** AMPA (fast Na+), kainate. Excess glutamate → **excitotoxicity** via calcium.
- **GABA:** main **inhibitory** transmitter; made from glutamate by **GAD (needs B6)**. **GABA-A = ionotropic Cl- channel** with benzodiazepine (↑frequency), barbiturate (↑duration), ethanol, and neurosteroid sites. **GABA-B = metabotropic Gi** (agonist baclofen).
