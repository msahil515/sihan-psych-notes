# Ch 14: Psychoanalytic, Humanistic-Existential & Systemic Paradigms

This chapter covers the three great non-behavioural traditions in clinical psychology: the psychoanalytic family (Freud and the schools that grew out of and against him), the humanistic-existential paradigm (Rogers, Perls, Frankl, Yalom), and the systemic / family paradigm (Minuchin, Bowen). These are heavily tested on RCI and NIMHANS-pattern papers as named theorists matched to their signature constructs, so master who said what.

## 1. Freud's Classical Psychoanalysis

### 1.1 The Topographical Model
Freud's first map of the mind (1900, *The Interpretation of Dreams*) divided mental life by **accessibility to awareness**, not by function. The **conscious** holds whatever you are aware of right now (a tiny fraction of mental life). The **preconscious** holds material not currently in awareness but readily retrievable (your phone number, what you ate yesterday). The **unconscious** is the vast reservoir of repressed wishes, traumatic memories, and instinctual drives that are actively kept out of awareness because they are threatening; access requires special techniques (free association, dream interpretation). The topographical model's key claim is **psychic determinism**: nothing mental is accidental, slips of the tongue (parapraxes, "Freudian slips") and dreams are meaningful unconscious products.

### 1.2 The Structural Model (1923, *The Ego and the Id*)
Because the topographical model could not explain why defences themselves were unconscious, Freud replaced it (without discarding it) with a tripartite **structural model** organised by function.

| Structure | Operates on | Process | Function |
|-----------|-------------|---------|----------|
| **Id** | **Pleasure principle** | **Primary process** (illogical, timeless, wish-fulfilling) | Reservoir of innate drives (libido/Eros, Thanatos); demands immediate gratification |
| **Ego** | **Reality principle** | **Secondary process** (logical, reality-testing, delays gratification) | Executive; mediates among id, superego, reality; seat of defence mechanisms |
| **Superego** | **Morality principle** | Conscience + ego-ideal | Internalised parental/social standards; produces guilt and pride |

The **id** is present from birth, entirely unconscious, and seeks tension reduction. The **ego** develops from the id to deal with reality through **reality testing** and the **secondary process** (rational, planful thought). The **superego** forms around age 5 to 6 as the resolution of the Oedipus complex, internalising the same-sex parent; it has two parts, the **conscience** (punishes with guilt) and the **ego-ideal** (rewards with pride). **Anxiety** is the ego's signal of danger and comes in three forms: reality anxiety (external threat), neurotic anxiety (fear the id will overwhelm the ego), and moral anxiety (fear of the superego, i.e. guilt).

### 1.3 Defence Mechanisms
**Defence mechanisms** are unconscious strategies the ego uses to manage anxiety by distorting reality. They are normal in moderation and pathological when rigid or dominant. George Vaillant's hierarchy (widely tested) ranks them by maturity.

**Mature (adaptive) defences:**
- **Sublimation**: channelling unacceptable impulses into socially valued activity (aggression into surgery or sport). Considered the healthiest.
- **Humor**: expressing feelings without personal discomfort or unpleasant effect on others.
- **Suppression**: the *conscious, deliberate* postponement of attention to an impulse or conflict (note: suppression is conscious; **repression** is the unconscious counterpart).
- **Altruism**: vicarious but constructive service to others that brings pleasure.

**Neurotic (intermediate) defences:**
- **Reaction formation**: converting an unacceptable impulse into its opposite (unconscious hatred expressed as exaggerated love).
- **Projection**: attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to others ("He hates me," when I hate him).
- **Displacement**: redirecting an impulse from a threatening target to a safer one (yelling at the dog after being scolded by the boss).
- **Intellectualization**: dealing with emotional conflict through excessive abstract thinking, stripping away affect.
- **Rationalization**: offering a plausible but false reason for behaviour driven by unacceptable motives (sour grapes).
- **Regression**: retreating to an earlier developmental stage under stress (an adult sulking, bed-wetting in a stressed child).
- **Isolation (of affect)**: separating an idea from its associated feeling so the thought is recalled without emotion.

**Psychotic (immature/primitive) defences:**
- **Denial**: refusing to acknowledge an external reality (a terminal patient insisting they are not ill).
- **Distortion**: grossly reshaping external reality to suit inner needs (delusional grandiosity).
- **Projective identification** (a Kleinian concept): projecting a part of the self into another person, then unconsciously pressuring that person to actually feel or act it out.

### 1.4 Core Therapeutic Concepts
- **Free association**: the patient says whatever comes to mind without censorship, the "fundamental rule" of analysis, used to bypass conscious defences and reach unconscious material.
- **Dream analysis**: dreams are the "royal road to the unconscious." The remembered **manifest content** is decoded into the underlying **latent content** through **dream-work** mechanisms (condensation, displacement, symbolization, secondary revision).
- **Transference**: the patient unconsciously redirects feelings from significant childhood figures onto the analyst; analysing it is central to cure. **Countertransference** is the analyst's emotional reaction to the patient (originally seen as an obstacle, now also used as diagnostic data).
- **Resistance**: any conscious or unconscious obstruction of the therapeutic process (lateness, silence, missing sessions); resistance marks where defended material lies.
- **Working through**: the repeated re-examination of conflicts and resistances until insight produces lasting change.

## 2. Extended Psychoanalytic Schools

### 2.1 Ego Psychology
Ego psychology shifted attention from the id to the **ego's adaptive functions**. **Anna Freud** (*The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence*, 1936) systematised the defence mechanisms and pioneered child analysis. **Heinz Hartmann** (often called the father of ego psychology) introduced the **conflict-free ego sphere**, arguing the ego has its own primary autonomy and energy (e.g. perception, memory, motor control) that develop independently of conflict, and the concept of the **average expectable environment** to which the infant adapts.

### 2.2 Object Relations Theory
Object relations theory holds that the basic human drive is **relationship-seeking**, not pleasure-seeking, and that internalised images of early caregivers ("objects") shape later relationships.

- **Melanie Klein** described two early mental positions (not fixed stages, but configurations one can revisit). The **paranoid-schizoid position** (first ~3 to 4 months) uses **splitting** of the object into all-good and all-bad ("part-objects," e.g. the good vs bad breast) and is dominated by **persecutory anxiety**. The **depressive position** (from ~4 to 6 months) integrates good and bad into a whole object, bringing the capacity for ambivalence, guilt, and **reparation**.
- **Margaret Mahler** mapped **separation-individuation**. Phases: **normal autism** (0 to 1 month), **symbiosis** (1 to 5 months, infant fused with mother), then separation-individuation proper with subphases: **differentiation** (5 to 9 mo, "hatching"), **practicing** (9 to 15 mo, exploring), **rapprochement** (15 to 24 mo, the toddler shuttles back for reassurance, the key crisis), and **object constancy** (24 to 36 mo, internalising a stable image of mother).
- **Donald Winnicott** introduced the **good-enough mother** (the caregiver need not be perfect, only adequately responsive, frustrating the infant in tolerable doses). The **transitional object** (the blanket or teddy) occupies the "transitional space" between inner fantasy and outer reality and helps the child separate. The **false self** is a compliant facade built to meet others' demands when the mother fails to mirror, hiding and protecting the spontaneous **true self**. He also coined **holding environment**.

### 2.3 Self Psychology
**Heinz Kohut** founded self psychology, focusing on **narcissism** and the development of a cohesive **self**. The child needs **selfobjects** (people experienced as part of the self) who provide three needs: **mirroring** (being admired and validated: "the gleam in the mother's eye"), **idealizing** (merging with an admired, calm, powerful figure), and **twinship/alter-ego** (feeling alike, belonging among similar others). Failures in these lead to narcissistic pathology; therapy supplies **empathic attunement** so development can resume (via "transmuting internalization").

## 3. Humanistic-Existential Paradigm

The "third force" (after psychoanalysis and behaviourism) emphasises subjective experience, free will, growth, and self-actualization.

### 3.1 Carl Rogers, Person-Centred Therapy
Rogers held that people have an innate **actualizing tendency** and that distress arises from a gap between the **organismic self** and **conditions of worth** imposed by others. The therapist does not direct; instead three **core (necessary and sufficient) conditions** create growth:
- **Empathy** (accurate empathic understanding): sensing the client's inner world "as if" it were one's own, without losing the "as if."
- **Congruence (genuineness)**: the therapist is real and transparent, not hiding behind a professional facade.
- **Unconditional positive regard (UPR)**: prizing the client warmly and non-judgementally regardless of behaviour.

These foster **self-actualization** and movement toward the "fully functioning person." Rogers' approach is also called client-centred and is the root of modern motivational interviewing.

### 3.2 Fritz Perls, Gestalt Therapy
Gestalt therapy emphasises **here-and-now awareness** and taking responsibility for one's experience. Central goal: increase **awareness** so that the organism's natural self-regulation can complete "unfinished business." Key techniques: the **empty chair** (the client dialogues with an imagined person or a disowned part of the self placed in an empty chair, integrating splits) and **two-chair work**. The **paradoxical theory of change** (Arnold Beisser) states that **change occurs when one becomes what one is, not when one tries to become what one is not**, i.e. genuine change follows full acceptance of the present state, not coercion.

### 3.3 Viktor Frankl, Logotherapy
Logotherapy (the "third Viennese school" after Freud and Adler) holds that the primary human motivation is the **will to meaning**. When meaning is blocked, the person falls into an **existential vacuum** (a pervasive sense of emptiness and boredom). Two signature techniques:
- **Paradoxical intention**: the patient is asked to intend (even exaggerate) the very thing they fear (e.g. an insomniac tries to stay awake), breaking the anticipatory-anxiety cycle. Used for phobias and obsessions.
- **Dereflection**: redirecting excessive self-observation and hyper-reflection away from the symptom toward meaningful activity (used for sexual dysfunction and over-monitoring).

### 3.4 Irvin Yalom, Four Ultimate Concerns
Yalom's existential psychotherapy centres on four **ultimate concerns** (givens of existence) that generate anxiety: **death** (the tension between wanting to live and knowing we will die), **freedom** (the dread of responsibility and groundlessness, since we author our own lives), **isolation** (existential aloneness, the unbridgeable gap between self and others), and **meaninglessness** (the need to find meaning in an indifferent universe). Therapy confronts these directly.

## 4. Systemic / Family Paradigm

Systemic therapy treats the family as a system: the identified patient's symptom serves a function within the whole, so the unit of treatment is the family, not the individual.

### 4.1 Salvador Minuchin, Structural Family Therapy
Structural family therapy targets the family's **structure**: its **subsystems** (spousal, parental, sibling) and the **boundaries** between them. Boundaries lie on a continuum: **rigid boundaries** produce **disengagement** (emotional distance, low support), **clear boundaries** are healthy, and **diffuse boundaries** produce **enmeshment** (over-involvement, poor autonomy). The therapist first **joins** the family, **maps** its structure, then uses **enactment** (having the family act out a problematic interaction live in session so the therapist can observe and restructure it) and **boundary-making** to reorganise the system. Watch for **hierarchy** and parent-child coalitions.

### 4.2 Murray Bowen, Bowenian / Multigenerational Family Therapy
Bowen's central construct is **differentiation of self**: the capacity to separate intellectual from emotional functioning and to stay autonomous (a clear "I") while remaining connected; low differentiation means one is **fused** into the family's emotional mass and easily flooded. **Triangulation** is the process by which a two-person system under stress recruits a third party to stabilise (lower) the tension; the basic stable unit is the **triangle**. The **multigenerational transmission process** is the passing of differentiation levels (and dysfunction) across generations, with poorly differentiated people marrying others of similar differentiation. Other Bowenian concepts: **emotional cutoff**, the **nuclear family emotional system**, and the **family projection process**.

### 4.3 Irvin Yalom, Group Therapy and the 11 Therapeutic Factors
Yalom identified **eleven therapeutic factors** that account for change in group psychotherapy. Memorise the list:
1. **Instillation of hope**
2. **Universality** (realising one is not alone in one's problems)
3. **Imparting of information** (didactic instruction, advice)
4. **Altruism** (helping others raises self-worth)
5. **Corrective recapitulation of the primary family group** (the group re-enacts and corrects family dynamics)
6. **Development of socializing techniques** (social skills)
7. **Imitative behaviour** (modelling)
8. **Interpersonal learning** (the central factor; insight through here-and-now feedback)
9. **Group cohesiveness** (the analogue of the therapeutic relationship; considered most important)
10. **Catharsis** (emotional release)
11. **Existential factors** (accepting responsibility, mortality, life's unfairness)

## High-Yield Recap
- **Topographical** = conscious / preconscious / unconscious (by awareness). **Structural** = id (pleasure principle, primary process) / ego (reality principle, secondary process, reality testing) / superego (morality, conscience + ego-ideal).
- Superego forms ~age 5 to 6 via Oedipus resolution. Three anxieties: reality, neurotic, moral.
- Vaillant defence hierarchy: **mature** = sublimation, humor, suppression (conscious), altruism; **neurotic** = reaction formation, projection, displacement, intellectualization, rationalization, regression, isolation; **psychotic/primitive** = denial, distortion, projective identification (Kleinian). Suppression is conscious, repression is unconscious.
- Free association = fundamental rule; dreams = royal road (manifest vs latent content via dream-work). **Transference** = patient onto analyst; **countertransference** = analyst onto patient; **resistance** blocks therapy.
- **Ego psychology**: Anna Freud (defences, child analysis), Hartmann (conflict-free sphere, autonomous ego, average expectable environment).
- **Klein**: paranoid-schizoid position (~0 to 4 mo, splitting, persecutory anxiety) vs depressive position (~4 to 6 mo, whole object, guilt, reparation).
- **Mahler** separation-individuation: normal autism (0 to 1 mo), symbiosis (1 to 5 mo), differentiation, practicing, **rapprochement** (15 to 24 mo, the crisis), object constancy (24 to 36 mo).
- **Winnicott**: good-enough mother, transitional object, true vs false self, holding environment.
- **Kohut self psychology**: selfobject needs = **mirroring, idealizing, twinship**; empathic attunement.
- **Rogers** core conditions: **empathy, congruence, unconditional positive regard**; actualizing tendency; conditions of worth.
- **Perls Gestalt**: awareness, empty chair, **paradoxical theory of change** (change happens when you become what you are).
- **Frankl logotherapy**: will to meaning, **existential vacuum**, **paradoxical intention** (intend the feared thing), **dereflection** (shift focus off symptom).
- **Yalom four ultimate concerns**: death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness.
- **Minuchin structural**: subsystems, boundaries (rigid→disengagement, diffuse→enmeshment), enactment, joining.
- **Bowen**: differentiation of self, triangulation (the triangle as basic unit), multigenerational transmission, emotional cutoff.
- **Yalom 11 group factors**: hope, universality, information, altruism, corrective family recapitulation, socializing techniques, imitative behaviour, **interpersonal learning**, **group cohesiveness** (most important), catharsis, existential factors.
