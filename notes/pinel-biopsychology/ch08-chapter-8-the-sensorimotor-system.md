# Chapter 8: The Sensorimotor System

**What this chapter is really about.** Most people picture voluntary movement as a single act of will: you decide to pick up the cup, and the brain "tells" the hand to do it. Pinel and Barnes replace that picture with a layered organisation in which the decision and the execution are carried out by different parts of the nervous system that know very different things. The top of the system (association cortex) knows *what* you want to achieve and *where* things are in space; it knows nothing about which muscle fibre to fire. The bottom of the system (spinal circuits and muscles) knows how to keep a limb steady against a push, how to pull a hand off a hot pot, and even how to produce the alternating rhythm of walking; it knows nothing about your goals. Movement emerges from orders passing downward through intermediate levels that translate goals into programmes of muscle activity, while sensory feedback flows back in at every level and keeps adjusting the output. The chapter's title word, *sensori*motor, is an argument in itself: you cannot understand motor output without the sensory input that guides it.

The second big idea is that the system is **plastic**. The same cortical site can drive a limb towards a target from any starting position; the same signature can be written with the hand or with a toe; a sequence that once demanded attention at every step becomes, with practice, a single smoothly run unit handled by lower levels. Learning does not merely make movements better. It changes *which part of the nervous system* is running them.

**How to read this chapter.** 8.1 gives you the three principles and the general model (a diagram you should be able to draw from memory), and these organise everything after. 8.2 to 8.4 walk *down* the cortical part of the hierarchy: association cortex, secondary motor cortex, primary motor cortex. 8.5 steps sideways to the two great modulators, the cerebellum and the basal ganglia, which are not on the main descending route but tune it. 8.6 follows the signals out of the brain along the descending pathways, and 8.7 reaches the bottom: muscles, their receptors, and the spinal reflex circuits that do far more than relay orders. 8.8 pulls it together with central sensorimotor programs and the neural changes that accompany motor learning. The exam-heavy material is concentrated in 8.2 (apraxia, neglect), 8.3 (mirror neurons), 8.6 (dorsolateral versus ventromedial) and 8.7 (spindles, tendon organs, reflexes); do not shortchange 8.7, which is the most mechanistic section of the chapter.

## The Case of Rhonelle, the Dexterous Cashier

The chapter opens in a supermarket queue. John Pinel, waiting to pay, finds his attention drifting from the tabloid headlines to the cashier, Rhonelle, the most skilful one in the shop, and he starts to analyse what her body is doing. Three observations come out of his daydream, and the rest of the chapter is built on them.

**First, even a trivial action recruits nearly the whole body.** When Rhonelle lifts a bag of tomatoes onto the scale, the visible action is in her fingers, hand, arm and shoulder, but her head and eyes also move so that her gaze follows her hand, and muscles in her feet, legs, trunk and other arm make adjustments that stop her toppling forward as her weight shifts. The precision of all this suggests it is being steered by the visual, somatosensory and vestibular consequences of the movements themselves. The word *sensorimotor* exists precisely to acknowledge this contribution of sensory input to motor output.

**Second, very little of what she does is under conscious control.** While moving items with her left hand, scanning them with her right and chatting with the bagger, Rhonelle is consciously deciding only *which* item to pick up and *where* to put it. Any one of those reaches could be achieved by an unlimited number of combinations of finger, wrist, elbow, shoulder and trunk adjustments, and something in her nervous system silently chooses one. The higher levels (perhaps her cortex) seem to issue general conscious commands, and lower levels convert them, without her awareness, into a specific pattern of muscle activity.

**Third, it was not always like this.** On her first days at the job, Rhonelle's movements were slow and effortful. Experience has stitched her separate movements into smooth sequences and appears to have handed their control from a conscious, effortful mode to one that runs without conscious effort.

The episode ends with Rhonelle, amused, having to call Pinel back from his reverie to pay his bill. Those three observations become the chapter's three principles:

1. The sensorimotor system is **hierarchically organised**.
2. **Motor output is guided by sensory input.**
3. **Learning can change the nature and the locus of sensorimotor control.**

## 8.1 Three Principles of Sensorimotor Function

### The company metaphor

Pinel and Barnes ask you to think of the sensorimotor system as a large, efficient company. The analogy is not decoration; they suggest it works because both are systems for controlling output that have evolved under competition, and they use it throughout the chapter (and return to it explicitly in 8.8). Keep the mapping in your head:

| Company | Sensorimotor system |
|---|---|
| President | Association cortex |
| Executives | Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and secondary motor cortex |
| Director of operations | Primary motor cortex |
| Channels of communication | Four descending motor pathways |
| Office managers | Spinal sensorimotor circuits |
| Workers | Muscles |

### Principle 1: The sensorimotor system is hierarchically organised

In a well-run company, orders pass downward from the president through layers of management to the workers. In the sensorimotor system, commands pass downward from **association cortex** (the top) to the **muscles** (the bottom) (Sadnicka et al., 2017). The crucial property of the top level is the *kind* of command it issues. A company president does not tell the warehouse staff which hand to lift a box with; she sets a goal and leaves the means to others. In the same way, association cortex specifies **general goals rather than specific plans of action**, and it does not routinely concern itself with the details.

Why organise things this way? The **main advantage** of a hierarchy is that it leaves the higher levels **free to perform more complex functions**. If association cortex had to compute the contraction of every motor unit for every reach, it would have no capacity left for deciding what to reach for.

Three further features sharpen the picture.

- **It is a parallel hierarchy.** Signals flow between levels over **multiple paths**, not one chain of command. This lets the top level exert control in more than one way: it can work through the intermediate levels, or it can reach down directly. The book's example is the eye blink: normally a reflex handled at a low level, it can be directly **inhibited by association cortex** so that you can put in a contact lens (McDougle, Ivry, & Taylor, 2016), just as a company president can personally arrange a delivery for an important customer.
- **It is functionally segregated.** Each level is made up of different units (neural structures in the brain, departments in the company), and each unit performs a different function. Secondary motor cortex, for example, is not one area but several, each with its own role.
- **Its main direction of flow is downward.** In summary, the sensorimotor system is, like the sensory systems of Chapter 7, a **parallel, functionally segregated, hierarchical** system. The one major difference is the **primary direction of information flow**: in sensory systems information flows mainly **up** the hierarchy; in the sensorimotor system it flows mainly **down**.

> **Trap.** "Hierarchical" does not mean "serial". An option that describes the motor system as a single chain in which each level can only talk to the next one down is wrong: the 11th edition stresses that it is a **parallel** hierarchy, and that higher levels can bypass intermediate ones (the contact-lens blink example). The same three adjectives (parallel, functionally segregated, hierarchical) describe the sensory systems; what differs is only the **direction** of the main flow.

### Principle 2: Motor output is guided by sensory input

Efficient companies keep track of the effects of their own activities and use that information to fine-tune what they do next. The sensorimotor system does the same (Azim, Fink, & Jessell, 2014; Danna & Velay, 2015; Scott, 2016). The eyes, the organs of balance, and receptors in the skin, muscles and joints all monitor the body's responses and feed what they detect back into the sensorimotor circuits. In most cases this **sensory feedback** helps steer the continuation of the very response that produced it: a reach is corrected in flight, a grip tightens as an object starts to slip.

There is one important class of exceptions. **Ballistic movements** are brief, all-or-none, high-speed movements, such as **swatting a fly**, and they are the only responses that are not normally influenced by sensory feedback. The logic is simple: a ballistic movement is over before feedback about it could arrive and be used, so it must be fully specified in advance and then launched.

> **Trap.** Ballistic movements are not "reflexes", and they are not "unguided by the brain". They are voluntary, centrally programmed movements that are *too fast* for feedback to steer. The defining features to recall are **brief, all-or-none, high-speed**, and **not normally influenced by sensory feedback**.

### The Case of G.O., the Man with Too Little Feedback

How much does movement depend on feedback? The cleanest answer comes from a person who lost one source of it. **G.O.**, a former **darts champion**, was studied by **Rothwell and colleagues (1982)**. An **infection had selectively destroyed the somatosensory nerves of his arms**, leaving the motor nerves working. He could still move, and he could still see; what he had lost was the feedback from his arms about what they were doing. His difficulties fell into three groups.

1. **Intricate responses were very hard, even with vision.** Fastening buttons or picking up coins defeated him even when he watched his hands.
2. **He could not correct for unexpected disturbances.** If someone bumped into him while he held a cup of coffee, he could not compensate in time and the coffee spilled. Normally, as you will see in 8.7, the stretch reflex makes exactly this correction automatically, but that reflex depends on sensory nerves from the muscle.
3. **His greatest problem was maintaining a constant level of muscle contraction.** Any task requiring a steady output to the hand needed continuous visual monitoring. Carrying a suitcase, he had to keep looking at it to be sure he had not let it go. For tasks requiring a constant *force*, such as holding a pen while writing or holding a cup, even vision was of little help, because vision tells you where an object is but not how hard you are pressing on it. All G.O. could see was the pen or the cup starting to slip from his grasp, by which time it was too late.

Notice what the case shows and what it does not. It does not show that the brain cannot produce movement without feedback; G.O. moved. It shows that **ongoing adjustment** of movement, and above all the maintenance of a **steady force**, depends on somatosensory feedback from the moving limb, and that **visual feedback cannot substitute** for it in force-dependent tasks.

> **Trap.** G.O.'s deficit was **sensory** (somatosensory nerves of the arms destroyed by infection), not motor. A question describing a patient who cannot keep hold of a cup despite normal strength and vision, and who spills drinks when jostled, is pointing to **loss of somatosensory feedback** (deafferentation), not to apraxia, ataxia or paralysis.

The book adds one more point under this principle, and it links principles 1 and 2. Many of the adjustments that motor output undergoes in response to sensory feedback are handled **unconsciously by the lower levels** of the hierarchy, **without the involvement of the higher levels** (Deliagina, Zelenin, & Orlovsky, 2012). A company runs more smoothly if the interns do not phone the president every time they hit a minor snag.

### Principle 3: Learning changes the nature and locus of sensorimotor control

A start-up company's president makes every decision personally and deliberately. As the company matures, many individual actions get bundled into routine procedures that are carried out by staff lower down without reference to the top.

Sensorimotor learning shows the same shift (Bassett et al., 2015). In the early stages of learning a skill, **each individual response is performed under conscious control**. After extensive practice, the individual responses become organised into **continuous, integrated sequences** of action that flow smoothly and are **adjusted by sensory feedback without conscious regulation**. Consider any skill you have acquired: typing, swimming, knitting, basketball, dancing, playing the piano. Two changes characterise nearly all of them:

- a change in the **nature** of control: separate responses are reorganised into **continuous motor programs**; and
- a change in the **locus** of control: those programs are handed down to **lower levels of the CNS**.

This is exactly what happened to Rhonelle between her first slow days and her expert present, and 8.8 returns to both changes under the names **response chunking** and **shifting control to lower levels**.

### General model of sensorimotor system function

Figure 8.1 is the model that frames the chapter, and it is worth being able to draw. From top to bottom the levels are:

1. **Association cortex**
2. **Secondary motor cortex**
3. **Primary motor cortex**
4. **Brain-stem motor nuclei**
5. **Spinal motor circuits**
6. **Muscles**

Four features of the diagram are the four ideas of 8.1 made visible:

- its **hierarchical structure** (levels stacked from goals to muscles);
- the **functional segregation** of levels (secondary motor cortex, for example, is drawn as several separate boxes);
- the **parallel descending connections** (signals can skip levels; some pathways run from cortex straight to the spinal circuits, others go via the brain stem); and
- the numerous **feedback circuits** running back up.

The chapter concentrates on the structures that control **voluntary behaviour** (the book's example is picking up an apple). It starts at association cortex and follows the main motor signals as they descend the hierarchy to the skeletal muscles that finally carry out the movement. Two important structures are absent from the main column of the model, the **cerebellum** and the **basal ganglia**; 8.5 explains why they sit off to the side.

> **Trap.** In the general model the **brain-stem motor nuclei** sit *between* primary motor cortex and the spinal motor circuits, and the **cerebellum and basal ganglia are not part of the descending chain at all**. A question asking which structures form the main descending route of the hierarchy should not include the cerebellum or basal ganglia; they **interact with** several levels and modulate them.

## 8.2 Sensorimotor Association Cortex

Association cortex is the top of the sensorimotor hierarchy, and it has **two major areas** with sensorimotor functions: the **posterior parietal association cortex** and the **dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex**. Each is really a collection of several areas with different functions (Davare et al., 2011; Wilson et al., 2010), but the book is candid that there is **no general consensus** on how best to divide either of them up, or on how closely the subdivisions correspond across humans, monkeys and rats (Teixeira et al., 2014; Turella & Lingnau, 2014). So learn the two regions as functional units and do not expect a fixed list of sub-areas.

### Posterior parietal association cortex: what it needs to know

Before any effective movement can begin, the nervous system needs two pieces of information: **where the parts of the body that are about to move currently are**, and **where any external objects the body will interact with are**. Reaching for a cup is impossible to plan if you do not know where your hand starts or where the cup sits.

The **posterior parietal association cortex** is the part of the parietal neocortex lying **behind (posterior to) the primary somatosensory cortex**. It plays an important role in three things (Freedman & Ibos, 2018; Hutchinson et al., 2014; Kuang, Morel, & Gail, 2015; Wilber et al., 2014):

1. **integrating** those two kinds of information (body position and object position);
2. **directing behaviour by supplying spatial information**; and
3. **directing attention**.

### Inputs and outputs of the posterior parietal cortex

Recall from Chapter 7 that a region is classified as **association cortex** when it receives input from **more than one sensory system**. The posterior parietal cortex receives input from the three systems that locate the body and external objects in space (Sereno & Huang, 2014):

- the **visual** system,
- the **auditory** system, and
- the **somatosensory** system.

Much of its output goes forward to motor-related areas of the **frontal** cortex:

- the **dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex**,
- the various areas of **secondary motor cortex**, and
- the **frontal eye field**, a small area of prefrontal cortex that controls both **eye movements** and **shifts of attention** (Moore & Zirnsak, 2015).

Single-neuron recordings in macaque monkeys, together with **fMRI** and **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** studies in humans, indicate that the posterior parietal cortex is not uniform: it is a **mosaic of small areas**, each specialised for guiding particular movements of the **eyes, head, arms or hands** (Man et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015).

> **Trap.** The **frontal eye field** is in **prefrontal** cortex, not in parietal or occipital cortex, even though it receives a major input from posterior parietal cortex and has "eye" in its name. Its two functions, controlling eye movements *and* shifting attention, belong together because where you look is usually where you attend.

### Stimulating the posterior parietal cortex: Desmurget and colleagues (2009)

An unusual experiment suggests that posterior parietal cortex is involved in the very *intention* to move. **Desmurget and colleagues (2009)** electrically stimulated the **inferior** part of the posterior parietal cortex in **conscious neurosurgical patients**. The results depended on current strength:

- at **low** currents, patients reported an **intention to perform** a particular action (a desire to move a hand, for example);
- at **higher** currents, they reported feeling that they **had actually performed** it;
- in **neither** case did any movement actually take place (see Desmurget & Sirigu, 2012).

The finding is striking because it separates the experience of willing and of having acted from the act itself. Whatever this region contributes, it includes something like the representation of a planned movement, and that representation can be activated without the downstream machinery producing any action.

> **Trap.** In the Desmurget (2009) study the stimulated patients **did not move**. Options claiming that parietal stimulation produced the movement the patients reported, or that the *higher* current produced the intention while the *lower* produced the feeling of having moved, are reversed or wrong. Low current = intention; higher current = belief the movement had occurred; no actual movement at either.

### Effects of posterior parietal damage

Damage to the posterior parietal cortex can produce a range of deficits: in the **perception and memory of spatial relationships**, in **accurate reaching and grasping**, in **controlling eye movements**, and in **attention** (Andersen et al., 2014; Turella & Lingnau, 2014). Two consequences, however, are far more striking than the rest, and they are the ones examiners ask about: **apraxia** and **contralateral neglect**.

### Apraxia

**Apraxia** is a disorder of voluntary movement that cannot be explained by a simple motor deficit (it is not paralysis or weakness) nor by any problem of comprehension or motivation (Niessen, Fink, & Weiss, 2014). The patient understands the request, wants to comply, and has the strength and range of movement to do so, yet cannot produce the movement on demand.

The puzzling feature is the **dependence on context**. Patients with apraxia find it hard to make a particular movement **when asked to**, especially when the movement is taken **out of its natural context**, yet they can often make the same movement **perfectly well under natural conditions**, when they are not thinking about it. The book's illustration is a carpenter with apraxia who hammers nails without any difficulty while working but cannot demonstrate a hammering movement when the examiner asks for one, particularly if no hammer is in her hand. What is lost is not the movement itself but the ability to call it up deliberately, out of context.

The lateralisation is the exam point. Although the **symptoms are bilateral** (both hands are affected), apraxia is often caused by **unilateral damage to the left posterior parietal cortex** or its connections (Hoeren et al., 2014; Niessen, Fink, & Weiss, 2014).

> **Trap.** Apraxia is a **left** hemisphere syndrome with **bilateral** symptoms; contralateral neglect (next) is mostly a **right** hemisphere syndrome with **one-sided** (left) symptoms. The pairing "left parietal: apraxia, right parietal: neglect" is a classic one-mark question, and so is the reversal "apraxia affects only the hand opposite the lesion", which is wrong.

### Contralateral neglect

**Contralateral neglect** is a failure to respond to stimuli that lie on the **opposite (contralateral)** side to a brain lesion, even though **basic sensation and movement on that side are intact**. The eyes and skin still register what is there, and the limbs on that side can still move, but the patient does not attend to it, respond to it, or act on it.

Most patients with contralateral neglect behave as though the **left side of their world does not exist**, and they **often fail to appreciate that they have a problem at all** (Li & Malhotra, 2015). The disorder is commonly associated with **large lesions of the right posterior parietal cortex**, although damage to other brain regions has also been implicated (Karnath & Otto, 2012). Because right-hemisphere damage is the usual cause, it is the **left** side that is neglected.

> **Trap.** "Absence of simple sensory or motor deficits" is part of the definition. A patient who does not respond to stimuli on the left because the left visual field is blind (hemianopia) or because the left arm is paralysed does not, on that basis alone, have neglect. Neglect is a failure to attend and respond, which is why the patient is typically unaware of it.

### The Case of Mrs. S., the Woman Who Turned in Circles

**Mrs. S.**, described by **Oliver Sacks (1999)**, developed left contralateral neglect after a **massive stroke in the posterior part of her right hemisphere**. She failed to respond to things on her left, and this included **parts of her own body**: she would, for instance, apply makeup only to the right side of her face and leave the left untouched.

Her most practical problem was getting enough to eat. Given a plate of food, she noticed only the food on its right half and ate only that, however hungry she still was. She solved the problem with a clever workaround that did not depend on ever noticing the left side. If she was still hungry, she **rotated her wheelchair to the right, through a full circle**, until the uneaten portion came round into her intact right field, and then ate the right half of *that*. If still hungry, she turned again and ate half of what remained, and so on, each time consuming half of the leftover food.

Two features make the case instructive. Her strategy is a **rightward** turn, the direction in which her attention still worked, rather than a leftward one, which would have required attending to the neglected side. And she never "discovers" the left half of the plate; she brings it into the right side of space. The neglect is spatial and attentional, not a defect of the eyes.

### Left of what? Egocentric and object-based neglect

If neglect patients fail to respond to things "on the left", one has to ask: left of *what*? There are several possible reference frames.

- **Egocentric left.** For most patients the deficit concerns stimuli to the left of **their own body**, which is called **egocentric left** (Karnath, 2015). Egocentric left is not defined simply by the head or eyes; it is **partly defined by gravitational coordinates**. When these patients **tilt their heads**, the neglected region usually **does not tilt with them**.
- **Object-based contralateral neglect.** Some patients also fail to respond to the **left side of objects**, wherever the objects are in their visual field (Karnath, 2015). They may ignore the left hand of a statue, for example, and continue to do so when the object is presented **horizontally or upside down** (Adair & Barrett, 2008). In other words, "left" here is defined by the object's own left side, not by where it falls relative to the patient.

> **Trap.** "Object-based" neglect means neglect of the **left half of each object**, independent of where the object is located; it does **not** mean neglect of objects lying in left space (that is egocentric neglect). And egocentric neglect is anchored partly to **gravity**, so tilting the head does not usually tilt the neglected field.

### Unconscious perception in contralateral neglect

As earlier chapters showed, failing to perceive something *consciously* does not mean it has not been perceived at all. Two kinds of evidence suggest that information about stimuli the neglect patient does not notice is nevertheless being **processed unconsciously** (Jerath & Crawford, 2014).

- **Geng and Behrmann (2002)** repeatedly presented objects in the same location to the left of neglect patients. On later trials, the patients tended to **look more in that general direction**, even though they had **no awareness** of the objects. The unseen objects were still shaping where attention went.
- **Vuilleumier and colleagues (2002)** showed patients complete drawings in their neglected left field, where they were not consciously perceived. Later, the patients were better at identifying **fragmented (partial) versions** of those same drawings presented on the right. The unnoticed left-field drawings had **primed** recognition.

Both findings point the same way: in neglect, the problem lies in conscious access and responding, not in the arrival of the sensory information in the brain.

### Dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex

The other large area of association cortex with important sensorimotor functions is the **dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex** (often abbreviated **DLPFC**; Kaller et al., 2011). Its connections place it just below posterior parietal cortex in the flow of control:

- it **receives projections from the posterior parietal cortex** (and sends major projections back to it, not shown on the book's figure);
- it **sends projections** to areas of **secondary motor cortex**, to **primary motor cortex**, and to the **frontal eye field**.

What its neurons do comes mainly from monkey recordings made while the animals identified and responded to objects (for example **Rao, Rainer, & Miller, 1997**). The neurons fall into groups:

- some respond according to the **characteristics of objects** (what the object is);
- some according to the **locations of objects** (where it is);
- some according to a **combination of both**;
- and others are related to the **response** rather than the object. These typically **start firing before the response** and **keep firing until it is complete**.

Anticipatory firing is common in cortical motor areas (Rigato, Murakami, & Mainen, 2014; Siegel, Buschman, & Miller, 2015), but **dorsolateral prefrontal neurons tend to fire first**. That timing, together with the mix of object, location and response coding, suggests that **decisions to initiate voluntary movements may be made in this area**. The book qualifies the claim: such decisions **depend on critical interactions** with posterior parietal cortex and with other areas of frontal cortex (Lee, Seo, & Jung, 2012; Ptak, Schnider, & Fellrath, 2017). So the safe formulation is that dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is a strong candidate for where the decision to move is made, working together with parietal and frontal partners, not that it is a self-contained "decision centre".

> **Trap.** Keep the division of labour between the two association areas straight. **Posterior parietal**: spatial information (where the body and objects are) and attention; damage produces apraxia and neglect. **Dorsolateral prefrontal**: evaluation of what and where in relation to the response, and the probable site of the **decision to initiate** a voluntary movement; its response-related neurons are the **first** to fire. Posterior parietal projects *to* dorsolateral prefrontal (and receives projections back).

## 8.3 Secondary Motor Cortex

**Secondary motor cortex** is defined by its connections: these are the areas that receive much of their input from **association cortex** (posterior parietal and dorsolateral prefrontal) and send much of their output to **primary motor cortex**. They sit, in other words, between the goal-setting level and the level that issues commands to the brain stem and spinal cord.

### Identifying the areas of secondary motor cortex

For many years only **two** areas of secondary motor cortex were recognised, and both are large and easy to see on the lateral surface of the frontal lobe, immediately **in front of (anterior to) primary motor cortex**:

- the **supplementary motor area (SMA)**, which **wraps over the top** of the frontal lobe and continues **down its medial surface into the longitudinal fissure**; and
- the **premotor cortex**, which runs as a **strip on the lateral surface** from the supplementary motor area down to the **lateral fissure**.

A simple way to fix the geography: both lie just anterior to the precentral gyrus; the SMA is the **upper, medial** one, and the premotor cortex is the **lower, lateral** one.

That two-area picture is now known to be too simple. Neuroanatomical and neurophysiological research in monkeys has made a case for **at least eight areas of secondary motor cortex in each hemisphere**, each with subdivisions of its own (Nachev, Kennard, & Husain, 2008). Most of this work has been done in monkeys, but functional brain-imaging studies suggest that **human** secondary motor cortex is organised in a comparable way (Caminiti, Innocenti, & Battaglia-Mayer, 2015). The book's Figure 8.4 groups them into **three sorts**: the **supplementary motor area**, the **premotor cortex**, and the **cingulate motor areas**, the last lying on the medial surface in the cortex of the **cingulate gyrus**, all sending output to primary motor cortex.

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel itemised the eight areas: **three supplementary motor areas** (SMA, preSMA and the supplementary eye field), **two premotor areas** (dorsal and ventral), and **three cingulate motor areas** in the cingulate gyrus. The 11th edition gives only the total ("at least eight in each hemisphere", Nachev, Kennard, & Husain, 2008) and the three sorts shown in Figure 8.4. If a paper asks for the 3 + 2 + 3 breakdown, it is using the older key; the total of eight is the same in both.

### What qualifies an area, and what secondary motor cortex does

Three criteria identify an area as secondary motor cortex:

1. **Connections.** It must have the right connections (input from association cortex, output to primary motor cortex, as in Figure 8.4).
2. **Stimulation effects.** Electrical stimulation typically elicits **complex movements**, often involving **both sides of the body**. (Contrast primary motor cortex in the conventional view, where brief stimulation gave a simple movement on the **contralateral** side.)
3. **Timing of activity.** Its neurons often become **more active just before a voluntary movement begins** and **remain active throughout** it.

The general function assigned to these areas follows from their position in the hierarchy. Secondary motor cortex is thought to **program specific patterns of movement** after receiving **general instructions from dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** (Pearce & Moran, 2012). Prefrontal cortex supplies the "what"; secondary motor cortex works out the "how" as a pattern of movements.

The evidence for a programming role comes largely from **brain imaging**: activity patterns in secondary motor areas have been measured while volunteers **imagine** themselves performing a particular sequence of movements, or **plan** to perform it, without actually moving (Olshansky et al., 2015; Park et al., 2015). Activity in the absence of movement is what you would expect of an area that composes movement plans rather than executes them.

> **Trap.** **SMA versus premotor cortex.** Location: the **SMA** is dorsal and **medial** (top of the frontal lobe, continuing into the longitudinal fissure); the **premotor cortex** is **lateral** (a strip from the SMA to the lateral fissure). Both are anterior to primary motor cortex and both are secondary motor cortex. A distinction widely taught outside the 11th edition, and consistent with it, is that the SMA is more involved in **internally generated** (self-initiated, memorised) sequences and the lateral premotor cortex in **externally cued**, especially visually guided, movements. Note also where the **mirror neurons** of the classic monkey studies were found: the **ventral premotor** area, not the SMA. And in the Jenkins (1994) PET study in 8.8, the **premotor** cortex was more active for a **new** sequence while the **SMA** was more active for a **well-practised** one.

### Mirror neurons: the discovery

Few discoveries in neuroscience have attracted as much interest as **mirror neurons** (Rizzolatti & Fogassi, 2014). A mirror neuron is a neuron that **fires both when an individual performs a particular goal-directed movement and when it observes the same goal-directed movement being performed by another individual**.

They were discovered in the **early 1990s** in the laboratory of **Giacomo Rizzolatti** (Ferrari & Rizzolatti, 2014), in **macaque monkeys**. The discovery was a by-product of other work. Rizzolatti's group had been studying a class of neurons in the **ventral premotor area** that seemed to encode particular **goal objects**: a given neuron fired when the monkey reached for one particular object (a specific toy, say) but not when it reached for a different one. Then the team noticed something odd. Some of these neurons fired **just as strongly when the monkey watched the experimenter pick up that same object**, while staying silent when the experimenter picked up other objects. These were the cells later named mirror neurons.

Figure 8.5 lays out the four conditions you need for an exam answer, using a ball as the goal object:

| Who picks up | Object | Mirror neuron |
|---|---|---|
| Monkey | Ball | Fires |
| Experimenter | Ball | Fires |
| Monkey | Other object | Does not fire |
| Experimenter | Other object | Does not fire |

The pattern rules out two cheap explanations. The cell is not simply a motor cell (it fires when the monkey is still and only watching), and it is not simply a visual cell for balls (it fires during the monkey's own unseen grasp of the ball and does not fire for any grasp of any object). It is tied to a **specific action on a specific goal**, whoever performs it.

### Why mirror neurons caused a stir

The excitement came from what mirror neurons might explain. They offer a possible neural mechanism for **social cognition**, meaning knowledge of the perceptions, ideas and intentions of others. If your brain represents another person's action using the same neurons that would produce that action in you, then **mapping others' actions onto your own action repertoire** could support understanding of what they are doing and why, and could also support **cooperation and imitation** (Farina, Borgnis, & Pozzo, 2020; Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia, 2016; Wood et al., 2016). The book is careful to cite dissent alongside these claims (Fitch, 2017; Kennedy-Constantini, 2017).

The strongest support for a social-cognition role is evidence that mirror neurons respond to the **purpose of an action**, not to its superficial features (Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia, 2016; but see Churchland, 2014). Two observations make the point:

- Mirror neurons that responded to the **sight** of an action that makes a noise, such as **cracking a peanut**, responded **just as strongly to the sound alone**. The cell cared about the action and its goal, however the monkey came to know about it.
- Many **ventral premotor** mirror neurons fire even when the monkey **does not perceive** the key part of the action at all but has formed a **mental representation** of it (for example, when the final grasp is hidden from view but the monkey knows it is happening).

### Mirror neurons in humans: what the evidence actually shows

This is where precise reading matters. Mirror neurons have been found in **several areas of macaque frontal and parietal cortex** (Bonini & Ferrari, 2011; Giese & Rizzolatti, 2015). In humans, however, the picture is very different:

- There have been **more than 600 published studies** of "mirror systems" in humans, yet **descriptions of individual mirror neurons in humans are rare** (Molenberghs, Cunnington, & Mattingley, 2012). The authors say they know of **only one**: **Mukamel and colleagues (2010)**.
- The reason is practical: there are **few opportunities to record from single neurons in humans** while running the required behavioural tests.
- Most human work has used **fMRI**. Numerous studies report regions of human motor cortex that become active whether a person **carries out** an action, **observes** it, or merely **imagines** it (e.g., Farina, Borgnis, & Pozzo, 2020; Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia, 2016; Vogeley, 2017).
- But there is **no direct evidence that mirror neurons are responsible** for these fMRI findings. It is entirely possible that **different neurons in the same cortical areas** produce the activity in the different conditions (some during doing, others during watching), which a technique that averages over thousands of neurons cannot distinguish.
- In favour of the link, the "mirror mechanisms" identified with fMRI in humans tend to lie in the **same cortical areas** as those identified by single-cell recording in macaques (Molenberghs et al., 2012).

> **Trap.** "Mirror neurons have been extensively recorded in humans" is false on the 11th edition's account. Single-cell evidence in humans is **rare** (the authors cite **one** study, **Mukamel et al., 2010**), despite **600+** human "mirror system" studies, which are mostly **fMRI**. fMRI activation during doing and watching is **not** direct evidence of mirror *neurons*, because different neurons in the same area could be responsible. Mirror neurons were discovered in **macaques**, in the **ventral premotor** area, in Rizzolatti's lab in the **early 1990s**.

## 8.4 Primary Motor Cortex

The **primary motor cortex** lies in the **precentral gyrus** of the frontal lobe. Two descriptions of its place in the system are worth memorising word for word in meaning:

- it is the **major point of convergence** of cortical sensorimotor signals; and
- it is the **major, but not the only, point of departure** of sensorimotor signals from the cerebral cortex.

That "not the only" will matter when you reach the effects of lesions. Understanding of what primary motor cortex does has changed radically over roughly the last two decades (Graziano, 2016), and the book presents the change as a contrast between a conventional view and a current view.

### The conventional view: Penfield and Boldrey's map

In **1937**, **Penfield and Boldrey** mapped the primary motor cortex of **conscious human patients during neurosurgery**. They applied **brief, low-intensity** electrical stimulation to many points on the cortical surface and recorded which part of the body moved each time. Two findings followed.

1. Stimulating a given site activated a **particular contralateral muscle** and produced a **simple movement**.
2. When the sites were plotted against the muscles they activated, the primary motor cortex turned out to be laid out **somatotopically**, that is, **according to a map of the body**.

The somatotopic layout of human primary motor cortex is called the **motor homunculus** (the book's figure is based on **Penfield & Rasmussen, 1950**). Its most important feature is the **disproportion**: most of the primary motor cortex is devoted to the parts of the body capable of **intricate movements**, above all the **hands and the mouth**. The amount of cortex given to a body part reflects the fineness of its control, not its size. Running from the medial surface down the lateral surface, the map goes roughly from toes, ankle and knee (medially, in the longitudinal fissure), through hip, trunk, shoulder, elbow, wrist and hand (with a large area for the individual fingers and thumb), to neck, brow, eyelid and eyeball, face, lips, jaw, tongue and swallowing near the lateral fissure.

> **Trap.** The motor homunculus belongs to **Penfield and Boldrey (1937)** (mapping), with the familiar figure based on **Penfield and Rasmussen (1950)**. The stimulation was **brief and low-intensity**, which is exactly why it produced **simple** movements of single **contralateral** muscles. The current view (below) used **longer** stimulation and got **complex** movements. The feet and legs are represented **medially**, the face and tongue **laterally**, near the lateral fissure.

### Sensory feedback to primary motor cortex, and stereognosis

Each site in primary motor cortex **receives sensory feedback** from receptors in the **muscles and joints** that the site itself influences. This is principle 2 built into the anatomy: the area issuing commands to a muscle is continuously told what that muscle is doing.

There is one interesting exception, described in **monkeys**. Monkeys have **at least two different hand areas** in the primary motor cortex of each hemisphere, and one of them receives input from receptors in the **skin** rather than from muscles and joints. This arrangement presumably serves **stereognosis**, the process of **identifying objects by touch**. Try it: close your eyes and explore an object with your hands, and notice that you identify it by a continuous interplay between the movements you make and the touch sensations those movements produce (Kappers, 2011). Stereognosis is a sensorimotor skill, not a purely sensory one, which is why a motor area receiving skin input makes sense.

### The conventional view of what single neurons encode: direction

Until recently, each neuron in primary motor cortex was thought to **encode the direction of a movement**. The main evidence was that each neuron in the **arm area** fires **maximally when the arm reaches in a particular direction**, and that **different neurons have different preferred directions**. On this view, the cortex steers the arm by the combined vote of neurons each preferring a different direction.

### The current view: long stimulation produces complex, natural movements

The newer mapping studies changed one thing about the method, and the results changed dramatically (Graziano, 2016). Instead of brief pulses just above the threshold for producing any response, investigators used **longer bursts of current, 0.5 to 1 second** (see Van Acker et al., 2014), a duration closer to that of an actual motor response.

Rather than contractions of single muscles, the long bursts elicited **complex, natural-looking sequences of movement**. At one site, for example, stimulation reliably produced a **feeding response**: the arm reached forward, the hand closed as though grasping food, the closed hand was brought to the mouth, and the mouth opened.

These studies also revealed a **looser somatotopic organisation** than the homunculus implied:

- Stimulating the **face area** does tend to produce facial movements, but they are **complex, species-typical movements** (an **aggressive facial expression**, for instance) rather than the contraction of individual facial muscles (Ejaz, Hamada, & Diedrichsen, 2015).
- Sites that move one body part **overlap greatly** with sites that move others (**Sanes et al., 1995**).

The overlap explains a clinical observation: **small lesions in the hand area** of primary motor cortex, in humans (**Scheiber, 1999**) or in monkeys (**Scheiber & Poliakov, 1998**), **do not selectively disrupt the movement of a single finger**. If each finger had its own patch of cortex, a small lesion should knock out one finger; because the representations overlap, it does not.

### Target, not direction: end-point coding and the action map

The conventional idea that primary motor cortex neurons are tuned to a **direction** of movement has also been challenged, and the challenge comes from changing the behavioural task.

In the studies that supported direction tuning, monkeys were **trained to make arm movements from a fixed central starting point**, which made it easy to relate each neuron's firing to the angle of movement, and each neuron fired only for movements at a particular angle. But when individual neurons were recorded while monkeys **moved about freely** (Graziano, 2016; Harrison & Murphy, 2014), the firing of many primary motor cortex neurons turned out to be related to the **particular end point** of a movement rather than its direction. If a monkey reached towards a given location, the neurons sensitive to *that target location* tended to become active **whatever direction** the arm had to travel to get there from its starting point. The direction tuning of the earlier studies may have been an artefact of always starting from the same point, where each target corresponds to exactly one direction.

**Stimulation studies** point the same way (Graziano, 2016; Harrison & Murphy, 2014). Suppose stimulating a site makes a **straight** arm bend at the elbow to **90 degrees**. Stimulating the **same site** when the arm starts **tightly bent** (flexed past 90 degrees towards the body) makes it **straighten** to the same **90-degree** angle. The same stimulation produces **opposite movements** depending on the starting position, while the **end position stays the same**.

The book asks you to pause on two implications, which are as important as they are counter-intuitive:

1. **The output of each site diverges widely.** Every site in primary motor cortex must be able to get a body part (an arm, say) to its target position **from any starting position**, which requires access to many different muscles.
2. **The sensorimotor system is inherently plastic.** Each location in primary motor cortex can apparently produce the **innumerable patterns** of muscle contraction and relaxation (Davidson et al., 2007) needed to bring a body part from any starting point to a specific target.

Accordingly, it has been suggested that primary motor cortex contains an **action map** (Graziano, 2016) **in addition to** a topographic map: a map of **goal postures and actions**, not merely of muscles.

> **Trap.** On the current view, many primary motor cortex neurons code the **end point (target)** of a movement, not its **direction**. Direction tuning was found with monkeys making **trained movements from a central starting point**; end-point coding emerged in **freely moving** monkeys and in **long-duration stimulation** studies (the 90-degree elbow result). "Action map" is the term for the current view, and it is described as existing **in addition to**, not instead of, the somatotopic map.

### Belle: The Monkey That Controlled a Robot with Her Mind

If primary motor cortex neurons play a major role in initiating movement, could their signals, given the right interface, drive a machine (Georgopoulos & Carpenter, 2015)? The case of **Belle** says yes.

Belle was a small **owl monkey** in the laboratory of **Miguel Nicolelis and John Chapin**. She had learned that if she moved a **joystick with her right hand** towards a light that came on in a control panel, she would get a **drop of fruit juice**. While she did this, **100 microelectrodes** recorded extracellular unit activity from neurons in her **primary motor cortex**. That activity moved her own arm towards the light, but the same signals were also analysed by a computer and transmitted to a laboratory **several hundred kilometres away**, at the **Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)**, where they were fed into the circuits of a **robotic arm**. On each trial, Belle's motor cortex activity moved her own arm towards the light and moved the robotic arm in the **same direction**.

### Brain-computer interfaces

Belle's achievement pointed to a possibility that is now beginning to be realised, through **brain-computer interfaces**: direct communication between a computer and the brain, usually through an **array of electrodes placed in the brain**.

- **Paralysed patients** have learned to control **robotic arms** using neural signals picked up by **multi-electrode arrays implanted in primary motor cortex** (Collinger et al., 2013; Golub et al., 2016; Pruszynski & Diedrichsen, 2015).
- Interfaces have also been used to get round **spinal-cord damage**. In a study by **Capogrosso and colleagues (2016)**, monkeys with **transected spinal cords** each had a **wireless transmitter implanted in the motor cortex** that recorded and broadcast motor cortex activity. A receiver positioned on the **spinal cord just below the transection** converted the transmitted signal into a **pattern of spinal stimulation** that produced movement of the **paralysed limb**. The cortical command thus bypassed the break in the cord. Such **brain-spine-computer interfaces** might one day allow substantial recovery from spinal cord damage (Jackson, 2016).

### Effects of primary motor cortex lesions

Given that primary motor cortex is the main point of departure of motor fibres from the cortex, you might expect extensive damage to it to abolish voluntary movement. It does not; its effects are **smaller than you might expect**. Large lesions of human primary motor cortex may:

- disrupt the ability to move **one body part independently** of others (one finger, for example) (Ebbesen & Brecht, 2017);
- produce **astereognosia**, a deficit in stereognosis (identifying objects by touch); and
- **reduce the speed, accuracy and force** of movements.

They do **not eliminate voluntary movement**. The presumed reason is the **parallel** organisation of 8.1: there are pathways that descend **directly from secondary motor and association areas** to subcortical motor circuits **without passing through primary motor cortex**. This is the anatomical meaning of "the major, but not the only, point of departure".

> **Trap.** Damage to primary motor cortex does **not** cause total paralysis of voluntary movement in Pinel's account. The expected answer is loss of **independent (fractionated) movement** of body parts such as single fingers, **astereognosia**, and reduced **speed, accuracy and force**, with voluntary movement preserved via **parallel pathways** from secondary motor and association cortex. Astereognosia is a feature of **primary motor cortex** damage in this chapter, which surprises students who file stereognosis as purely somatosensory.

## 8.5 Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia

### Modulators, not relays

The **cerebellum** and the **basal ganglia** (introduced anatomically in Chapter 3, Figures 3.20 and 3.28) are both important sensorimotor structures, and they are **heavily interconnected with each other** (Bostan & Strick, 2018). Yet **neither is a major part of the pathway** by which signals descend through the sensorimotor hierarchy. That is why they do not appear in the main column of the general model in Figure 8.1. Instead, both **interact with different levels** of the hierarchy and, in doing so, **coordinate and modulate** its activity. In the company metaphor of 8.8, they are the mechanisms that make sure the programs running at different levels are well coordinated and effective.

### The cerebellum: size, connections and the comparison it performs

The structure of the cerebellum and its complex connections with the rest of the brain hint at its functional complexity (Bostan & Strick, 2018; Buckner, 2013). One number captures this and is often examined: the cerebellum makes up only about **10 percent of the brain's mass**, yet it contains **more than half of the brain's neurons** (Azevedo et al., 2009).

The cerebellum receives **three sources of input**:

1. information from **primary and secondary motor cortex** (what the cortex intends);
2. information about **descending motor signals** from the **brain-stem motor nuclei** (what has actually been sent down); and
3. **feedback from the motor responses** themselves, via the **somatosensory and vestibular** systems (what actually happened).

The cerebellum is thought to **compare these three sources** and **correct ongoing movements that deviate from their intended course** (Bastian, 2006; Bell, Han, & Sawtell, 2008; Herzfeld & Shadmehr, 2014). Intention, command and outcome are matched, and mismatches are corrected while the movement is still under way. By doing this, the cerebellum is believed to play a major role in **motor learning**, particularly in learning **sequences of movements in which timing is critical** (Pritchett & Carey, 2014).

### Effects of cerebellar damage

The effects of **diffuse cerebellar damage** on motor function are devastating, and they map neatly onto the comparison function. The patient:

- **loses accurate control** of the **direction, force, velocity and amplitude** of movements;
- loses the ability to **adapt patterns of motor output to changing conditions**;
- has particular difficulty **maintaining steady postures** (standing, for example), and attempts to do so frequently produce **tremor**;
- shows **severe disturbances of balance, gait, speech and the control of eye movements**; and
- finds it difficult to **learn new motor sequences** (Thach & Bastian, 2004).

Together these effects suggest that the cerebellum plays a major role in **monitoring and adapting ongoing patterns of movement** (Peterburs & Desmond, 2016). Movement still happens after cerebellar damage, so the cerebellum is not needed to *generate* it; what goes is the moment-to-moment correction that keeps it accurate and smooth.

> **Trap.** The clinical word for the uncoordinated, inaccurate movement of cerebellar damage is **ataxia** (Pinel describes the signs without using the word). Do not confuse three look-alike terms. **Apraxia**: cannot produce a movement *on request*, out of context, without weakness or comprehension problems; usually **left posterior parietal** damage. **Contralateral neglect**: fails to respond to stimuli on the side opposite the lesion, without sensory or motor loss; usually **right posterior parietal** damage. **Ataxia**: movements are produced but with poor control of direction, force, velocity and amplitude, with tremor on trying to hold a posture and disturbed balance, gait and speech; **cerebellar** damage. The cerebellar tremor appears when trying to hold a posture or perform an action, unlike the resting tremor of Parkinson's disease (basal ganglia).

### The cerebellum beyond movement

The conventional view, that cerebellar functions are **entirely sensorimotor**, is **no longer tenable** (Buckner, 2013; Koziol et al., 2014). Two lines of evidence overturned it:

- **Patients** with cerebellar damage often show a range of **sensory, cognitive, emotional and memory deficits** (Sokolov, Miall, & Ivry, 2017).
- **Healthy volunteers** often show **cerebellar activity** during **sensory, cognitive and emotional** tasks.

There are several competing theories of cerebellar function (Koziol et al., 2014). A popular one is that the cerebellum plays an important role in **learning from one's errors** and in **predicting errors** (Herzfeld et al., 2018; Sokolov, Miall, & Ivry, 2017). Notice that this is a generalisation of the motor comparison function above: compare what was intended with what happened, and use the discrepancy to improve the next attempt, whether the "attempt" is a reach or a thought.

### The basal ganglia: anatomy and loops

The basal ganglia contain **fewer neurons than the cerebellum**, but in one sense they are **more complex**. The cerebellum is organised **systematically**, in lobes, columns and layers; the basal ganglia are a **complex, heterogeneous collection of interconnected nuclei**. (Chapter 3 named the main ones: the **caudate** and **putamen**, together called the **striatum**, which receive input from the neocortex and send their major output to the **globus pallidus**; a projection from the **substantia nigra** to the striatum degenerates in Parkinson's disease.)

Their anatomy suggests that, like the cerebellum, the basal ganglia perform a **modulatory** function (Nelson & Kreitzer, 2014). They contribute **few fibres to the descending motor pathways**. Instead, they form **neural loops** through numerous **reciprocal connections with cortical areas and with the cerebellum** (Bostan & Strick, 2018; Nelson & Kreitzer, 2014; Oldenburg & Sabatini, 2015). Many of these cortical loops carry signals **to and from the motor areas** of cortex (Nambu, 2008): cortex projects into the basal ganglia, and the basal ganglia project back, via the thalamus, to cortex.

### The basal ganglia: from motor modulation to cognition, motivation and learning

Theories of basal ganglia function have changed in much the same way as theories of the cerebellum. The **traditional view** was that the basal ganglia, like the cerebellum, play a role in **modulating motor output**. They are now thought also to be involved in:

- a variety of **cognitive functions** (Eisinger et al., 2018; Hikosaka et al., 2014; Lim, Fiez, & Holt, 2014; Rektor et al., 2015);
- many aspects of **motivation** (Bostan & Strick, 2018); and
- **learning**, in particular:
  - **habit learning**, a form of learning that is usually acquired **gradually, trial by trial** (Ashby, Turner, & Horovitz, 2010); and
  - **classical conditioning** (Stephenson-Jones et al., 2016).

### Movement vigour and movement suppression

One theory of basal ganglia sensorimotor function builds on their known roles in **both movement and motivation** (Averbeck & Costa, 2017; Schultz, 2016). It makes **two assertions**.

1. **Movement vigour.** The basal ganglia are responsible for **movement vigour** (Dudman & Krakauer, 2016): the control of the **speed and amplitude** of movement **on the basis of motivational factors**. The same concert pianist can play the same piece with more or less vigour; on this theory the basal ganglia set that level according to how motivated the performer is.
2. **Active suppression of unwanted movement.** Movement involves not only executing actions but also **actively suppressing** motor activity that would be inappropriate or unhealthy. We have to suppress the urge to **yawn or scratch** at a social gathering, and we must also suppress unwanted movements generated by the **ongoing spontaneous activity of our muscles**, such as **tremor, twitching and coughing**. When this **movement inhibition fails**, symptoms of a **neurological or psychiatric disorder** can emerge (Duque et al., 2017).

The second assertion is worth connecting to what you know clinically. Disorders of the basal ganglia include both too little movement (the poverty of movement in Parkinson's disease, Chapter 4's opening case) and too much (the involuntary movements of Huntington's disease, tics), which is exactly the pattern expected from a system that both energises movement and holds unwanted movement in check.

> **Trap.** **Cerebellum versus basal ganglia.** Both modulate rather than relay, both form loops, both are now credited with non-motor functions. The differences an examiner will use: the **cerebellum** has **more than half the brain's neurons** in about **10 percent** of its mass, is organised **systematically** (lobes, columns, layers), **compares** intended, sent and actual movement to **correct errors**, and is tied to **timing** of sequences and **error-based** learning. The **basal ganglia** have **fewer neurons** but are a **heterogeneous** set of nuclei, and are tied to **movement vigour**, **suppression** of unwanted movement, **motivation**, **habit learning** and **classical conditioning**. "Movement vigour" is a key term of the **basal ganglia**, not the cerebellum.

## 8.6 Descending Motor Pathways

Neural signals travel from primary motor cortex to the motor neurons of the spinal cord over **four pathways**. **Two** descend in the **dorsolateral** region of the spinal cord and are collectively called the **dorsolateral motor pathways**; **two** descend in the **ventromedial** region and are collectively called the **ventromedial motor pathways**. Signals over all four act **together** in the control of voluntary movement (Iwaniuk & Whishaw, 2000). In the company metaphor, these are the lines of communication from the executive level (the cortex) to the office staff (the spinal motor circuits) and the workers (the muscles); a company with poor lines of communication does not function, however good its executives.

### What the two systems share

The dorsolateral and the ventromedial systems are built on the **same plan**. Each consists of **two major tracts**:

- one whose axons **descend directly to the spinal cord** from cortex; and
- one whose axons **synapse in the brain stem** on neurons that **in turn descend** to the spinal cord.

So in each system there is a **direct** route and an **indirect (brain-stem relayed)** route.

### How the dorsolateral and ventromedial systems differ

They differ in **two major respects**, and both are standard exam material.

| Feature | Dorsolateral pathways | Ventromedial pathways |
|---|---|---|
| Termination | Each axon ends in the **contralateral half of one spinal segment**, sometimes **directly on a motor neuron** | **Diffuse**: many axons innervate **interneurons on both sides** of the spinal grey matter, across **several segments** |
| Muscles reached | **Distal** muscles (e.g., **finger** muscles) | **Proximal** muscles of the **trunk and limbs** (e.g., **shoulder** muscles) |
| Function (Lawrence & Kuypers) | **Movements of the limbs**, especially independent reaching and hand use | **Posture** and **whole-body movements** (walking, climbing), including the limb movements involved in them |

The logic of the pairing is worth seeing. Fine, individuated control of a single finger needs a focused, one-sided, one-segment projection, possibly right onto the motor neuron. Postural control of the trunk needs the opposite: broad, bilateral activation of many segments at once, because keeping the body upright requires coordinated action of muscles on both sides and along the whole spine.

### The four tracts by name, as taught in older editions

The 11th edition describes the pathways only in the general terms above. Earlier editions of Pinel, and most Indian question banks built on them, name all four tracts and give their routes. You need these names.

**Dorsolateral pathways (distal muscles; contralateral, focused):**

1. **Dorsolateral corticospinal tract** (the *direct* dorsolateral route). Axons from primary motor cortex descend through the **medullary pyramids** (the two bulges on the ventral surface of the medulla), where most **decussate** (cross over), and continue down the **contralateral** dorsolateral spinal white matter. Among its cells of origin are the **Betz cells**, exceptionally large pyramidal neurons of primary motor cortex. Most of its axons synapse on spinal interneurons that drive motor neurons of **distal** muscles of the wrists, hands, fingers and toes; in primates and other mammals with highly dexterous digits, some synapse **directly** on motor neurons of the digits. In standard clinical anatomy this is the **lateral corticospinal tract**.
2. **Dorsolateral corticorubrospinal tract** (the *indirect* dorsolateral route). Cortical axons synapse in the **red nucleus** of the midbrain (*rubro-* means red); red nucleus axons **decussate** and descend, some ending in the **cranial nerve nuclei controlling facial muscles**, the rest descending in the dorsolateral spinal cord to reach motor neurons of **distal** muscles of the **arms and legs**. Clinically, the **rubrospinal tract**.

**Ventromedial pathways (proximal muscles; bilateral, diffuse):**

3. **Ventromedial corticospinal tract** (the *direct* ventromedial route). Axons descend **ipsilaterally** from primary motor cortex straight into the ventromedial spinal white matter; each axon **branches diffusely** and innervates interneuron circuits in **several segments on both sides**. Clinically, the **anterior (ventral) corticospinal tract**.
4. **Ventromedial cortico-brainstem-spinal tract** (the *indirect* ventromedial route). Cortical axons feed into a network of brain-stem structures, whose descending axons run **bilaterally** in the ventromedial cord, so each side carries signals from **both hemispheres**, and reach interneurons of several segments controlling **proximal trunk and limb** muscles. The four brain-stem structures are the **tectum** (auditory and visual information about spatial location), the **vestibular nucleus** (balance information from the semicircular canals), the **reticular formation** (programs for complex species-typical movements such as walking, swimming and jumping), and the **motor nuclei of the cranial nerves that control the face**. Clinically, the **tectospinal, vestibulospinal and reticulospinal** tracts.

> **Trap.** **Which pathway is which.** "Dorsolateral" goes with **distal** limb muscles, **contralateral**, **one segment**, sometimes **monosynaptic** onto motor neurons, and **red nucleus** (for the indirect tract). "Ventromedial" goes with **proximal**/trunk muscles, **bilateral**, **diffuse across segments**, and the **tectum, vestibular nucleus, reticular formation** (for the indirect tract). Both the corticospinal *and* the corticorubrospinal tracts are **dorsolateral**; a common wrong option places the red-nucleus tract in the ventromedial system. And "ventromedial corticospinal" descends **ipsilaterally** but ends up influencing **both** sides through its branches, while "dorsolateral corticospinal" **decussates** at the pyramids.

### Lawrence and Kuypers (1968): cutting the pathways in monkeys

Because all four descending tracts start in the cerebral cortex, all four are presumed to mediate **voluntary** movement. Their very different routes and destinations, however, suggest different functions, and this was first demonstrated experimentally by **Lawrence and Kuypers (1968)** in monkeys.

**Group 1: dorsolateral tracts transected.** One group of monkeys had **complete transections of the dorsolateral tracts**. Afterwards they could still **stand, walk and climb**. But when they were **sitting**, their **arms hung limply** at their sides (monkeys normally use their arms in standing and walking, so arm use as such was not abolished, only arm use outside whole-body activity). On the rare occasions they reached for something, they used the arm like a **rubber-handled rake**: they flung it out from the **shoulder** and dragged small objects of interest back towards themselves along the floor. Individuated use of the hand and forearm had gone; only the proximal, shoulder-driven sweep remained.

**Group 2: ventromedial tracts transected.** The other group had **complete transections of the ventromedial tracts**, and the picture was the reverse. These monkeys had **severe postural abnormalities**: they had great difficulty **walking or sitting**. If they managed to sit or stand without clinging to the bars of the cage, the **slightest disturbance**, even a **loud noise**, often made them **fall**.

**What the experiments show.** Put side by side, the two groups dissociate the systems:

- the **ventromedial** tracts are involved in controlling **posture and whole-body movements** (walking, climbing), and they can exert control over the **limb movements involved in such activities** (which is why Group 1 could still use their limbs to climb);
- the **dorsolateral** tracts control the **movements of the limbs** themselves, in particular their independent use for reaching and manipulation (Ruder & Arber, 2019).

> **Trap.** The **rubber-handled rake** belongs to the **dorsolateral** transection, not the ventromedial one; the **falls at a loud noise** belong to the **ventromedial** transection. Older editions split the work into two papers. In the first (**1968a**), only the **dorsolateral corticospinal** tracts were cut, at the medullary pyramids; the monkeys recovered reaching but permanently lost **independent finger movement** and the ability to **release** a grip voluntarily (although they let go of the cage bars when climbing, a context-dependent release reminiscent of apraxia). In the second (**1968b**), cutting the **corticorubrospinal** tract as well produced the limp arms and the rake-like reach, and cutting the **ventromedial** tracts produced the postural collapse. The older conclusion was that the corticorubrospinal tract can take over most functions of the corticospinal tract, **except independent movement of the fingers**. The 11th edition compresses this into a single comparison of "dorsolateral" versus "ventromedial" transections.

## 8.7 Sensorimotor Spinal Circuits

### The spinal cord is not a cable

We have now reached the bottom of the hierarchy: the spinal circuits and the muscles they control. Psychologists, the authors admit, tend to be brain-centred, and it is easy to think of the spinal motor circuits as mere cables carrying the brain's instructions to the muscles. That picture is wrong. The motor circuits of the spinal cord show **considerable functional complexity independent of signals from the brain** (Dasen, 2017; Giszter, 2015; Kiehn, 2016; Ruder & Arber, 2019). The company metaphor puts it well: can the office managers (spinal circuits) and workers (muscles) carry on effectively while all the executives are away at a convention in Hawaii? Of course they can. The stretch reflex, the withdrawal reflex and above all walking (at the end of this module) are the proof.

### Motor units

The **motor unit** is the **smallest unit of motor activity**. It consists of **a single motor neuron plus all the individual skeletal muscle fibres it innervates**. When that motor neuron fires, **all** the fibres of its unit **contract together**; the nervous system cannot contract part of a motor unit.

Motor units vary a great deal in **how many muscle fibres** they contain, and this has a direct consequence for control. The units with the **fewest fibres**, found in the **fingers and face**, permit the **highest degree of selective motor control**, because each firing of a motor neuron moves only a small amount of muscle. Units with many fibres, in large postural muscles, trade precision for force. (Connect this with the motor homunculus: the body parts with the largest cortical representation are those with the smallest motor units.)

> **Trap.** A motor unit is **one motor neuron and all the fibres it innervates**. The set of **all motor neurons** that innervate a **single muscle** is a different thing, the **motor pool**. Small motor units (few fibres per neuron) mean **fine** control; they are characteristic of the **fingers and face**.

### Muscle fibres, the motor end-plate and the motor pool

A **skeletal muscle** is made of **hundreds of thousands of thread-like muscle fibres**, bound together in a tough membrane and attached to bone by a **tendon**. The chain from nerve impulse to contraction runs like this:

1. The motor neuron releases **acetylcholine** at the **neuromuscular junction**.
2. Acetylcholine activates the **motor end-plate**, the receptive area on each muscle fibre.
3. The fibre **contracts**.

Two consequences of the design follow. First, **contraction is the only way a muscle can generate force**, so **any muscle can pull in only one direction**; it cannot push. Second, because a muscle is served by many motor neurons, it is useful to have a name for all of them together: the **motor pool** is the full set of motor neurons that innervate the fibres of a single muscle.

### Fast and slow muscle fibres

Although the book flags it as an **oversimplification** (Gollnick & Hodgson, 1986), skeletal muscle fibres are often classified into two basic types.

| | **Fast muscle fibres** | **Slow muscle fibres** |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Contract and relax **quickly** | **Slower** |
| Force | Capable of **great force** | **Weaker** |
| Fatigue | **Fatigue quickly** | Capable of more **sustained** contraction |
| Blood supply | **Poorly vascularised** (few blood vessels), hence **pale** | **Richly vascularised**, hence much **redder** |
| Typical use | Quick movements such as **jumping** | Gradual movements such as **walking** |

The fatigue difference follows from the blood supply: a fibre with few blood vessels cannot be resupplied fast enough to keep working. Note that **each muscle has both** fast and slow fibres; the types are mixed within a muscle, not assigned to separate muscles.

> **Trap.** Pale = fast = strong but fatigable (poorly vascularised); red = slow = weaker but fatigue-resistant (richly vascularised). Options that call fast fibres "red" or say fast fibres are "more richly vascularised" are reversed. Every muscle contains **both** types.

### Flexors and extensors, synergists and antagonists

Because each muscle can pull in only **one direction**, any joint that moves in **more than one direction** must be controlled by **more than one muscle**. Many skeletal muscles fall clearly into one of two categories:

- **Flexors** act to **bend (flex)** a joint.
- **Extensors** act to **straighten (extend)** it.

The standard example is the elbow: the **biceps** is its **flexor** and the **triceps** its **extensor**. When the elbow flexes, the biceps contracts and the triceps is stretched; when it extends, the triceps contracts and the biceps is stretched.

Two further terms describe how muscles relate to each other:

- **Synergistic muscles** are any two muscles whose contraction produces the **same movement**, whether flexion or extension.
- **Antagonistic muscles** are muscles that act in **opposition**, like the biceps and triceps.

> **Trap.** "Synergistic" and "antagonistic" describe the **relation between muscles** (same movement versus opposite movements), not whether a muscle is a flexor or an extensor. Two flexors of the same joint are synergists; a flexor and an extensor of the same joint are antagonists. The term **agonist**, used later for the muscle producing a movement, is the counterpart of antagonist.

### Isometric and dynamic contraction

To understand how muscles work you need to realise that they are **elastic**, not rigid cables. Picture the muscle as an **elastic band joining two bones**. Increasing its tension can have two different results, which define the two types of contraction:

- **Isometric contraction**: the muscle's tension on the two bones **increases without the muscle shortening** and pulling them together (pushing against a wall, holding a heavy bag still).
- **Dynamic contraction**: the muscle **shortens and pulls the bones together**.

How does the nervous system increase a muscle's tension? In three ways: by **increasing the number of neurons in its motor pool that are firing** (recruiting more motor units), by **increasing the firing rate** of those already firing, or, **most commonly, by a combination of both**.

### Receptor organs of tendons and muscles

Muscle activity is monitored by **two kinds of receptor**, and their differences are the most examined facts of the module.

- **Golgi tendon organs** sit in the **tendons**, the tough attachments that anchor a skeletal muscle to bone.
- **Muscle spindles** lie within the **muscle tissue** itself.

The difference in location is what makes them sensitive to **different aspects** of muscle activity:

| | **Golgi tendon organs** | **Muscle spindles** |
|---|---|---|
| Location | In the **tendon** | In the **muscle** tissue |
| Arrangement relative to muscle fibres | **In series** (between muscle and bone) | **In parallel** (alongside the extrafusal fibres) |
| Respond to | Increases in **muscle tension** (the pull of the muscle on the tendon) | Changes in **muscle length** |
| Insensitive to | Changes in **length** (completely) | Changes in **tension** |
| Extra function | **Protective**: in extreme contraction, excite **inhibitory interneurons** in the cord that make the muscle **relax** | Drive the **stretch reflex** |

The "in series" and "in parallel" labels (standard physiology, consistent with the book's locations) explain the table. A tendon organ sits in the line of pull between muscle and bone, like a spring balance hung in a rope: whenever the muscle pulls on the tendon, the organ is loaded, so it reports **tension**. A spindle lies alongside the working fibres: when the whole muscle is stretched the spindle is stretched with it, so it reports **length**.

Under normal conditions, the Golgi tendon organs simply provide the CNS with information about **muscle tension**. But they also serve a **protective function**. When a contraction becomes so extreme that it risks damaging the muscle or tendon, the tendon organs excite **inhibitory interneurons** in the spinal cord, which make the muscle **relax**.

> **Trap.** This is the single most reliable trap of the chapter. **Spindles = length, in parallel, in the muscle belly. Tendon organs = tension, in series, in the tendon.** Pinel states each is insensitive to the other's variable: Golgi tendon organs are **completely insensitive to changes in length**; spindles **do not respond to changes in tension**. The protective relaxation in extreme contraction comes from the **Golgi tendon organs** acting through **inhibitory** interneurons, not from the spindles. An option saying "muscle spindles detect tension" or "Golgi tendon organs detect stretch of the muscle belly" is wrong.

### Why a receptor needs its own muscle: the intrafusal system

Figure 8.9 shows the **muscle-spindle feedback circuit**, and it contains a surprise. Each muscle spindle has its own thread-like **intrafusal muscle**, and that intrafusal muscle is innervated by its own **intrafusal motor neuron**. The ordinary working muscle around it is the **skeletal muscle**, also called **extrafusal muscle**, and it is driven by **extrafusal motor neurons**. The spindle's sensory output travels to the cord in a **spindle afferent neuron**.

Why would a sensory receptor need its own muscle and motor neuron? Imagine a spindle without them. Every time the extrafusal muscle **contracted** and shortened, the spindle, lying in parallel, would be shortened too and would **go slack**. A slack spindle cannot do its job, which is to detect **slight changes in the length** of the extrafusal muscle. It would be blind precisely while the muscle was working.

The intrafusal motor neuron solves this (Figure 8.10). Each time the extrafusal muscle shortens, the intrafusal motor neuron **shortens the intrafusal muscle** to match, keeping enough tension on the **middle, stretch-sensitive portion** of the spindle for it to stay responsive to slight length changes **whatever the current length of the extrafusal muscle**. The spindle is continuously re-tuned to the muscle's current length.

> **Trap.** In standard physiology the extrafusal motor neurons are the **alpha** motor neurons and the intrafusal motor neurons are the **gamma** motor neurons; the main spindle afferents are **type Ia** fibres. Pinel uses only the descriptive terms (**extrafusal/intrafusal motor neuron**, **spindle afferent neuron**), so be ready to translate. The purpose of the intrafusal system is to keep the spindle **from going slack** during contraction, i.e., to maintain its **sensitivity**, not to produce force.

### The stretch reflex

Most people's picture of a reflex is the knee jerk at the doctor's: a tap on the knee with a rubber hammer and the leg kicks out. The resulting leg extension is the **patellar tendon reflex** (*patella* means "knee"), and it is an example of a **stretch reflex**: a reflex elicited by a **sudden external stretching force on a muscle**.

The sequence (Figure 8.11):

1. The hammer strikes the **tendon** below the knee, which **stretches the extensor muscle** running along the front of the thigh.
2. The sudden stretch **stretches the muscle spindles** in that muscle.
3. The spindles fire a **volley of action potentials** that is carried into the spinal cord by the **spindle afferent neurons**, entering through the **dorsal root**.
4. In the cord, this volley **excites motor neurons in the ventral horn** (Illert & Kummel, 1999).
5. Those motor neurons send action potentials **back to the very muscle whose stretch excited them**.
6. The result is a **compensatory contraction** of the thigh muscle and a sudden **leg extension**.

The circuit has only **one synapse** in the cord, between the spindle afferent and the motor neuron, which is why the stretch reflex is described as **monosynaptic** (the book makes the point by contrast with the withdrawal reflex below).

### What the stretch reflex is really for

The doctor's version, a sharp blow to the tendon of a **completely relaxed** muscle, is designed to make the reflex easy to see. It tells you almost nothing about why the reflex exists. In real life, the job of the stretch reflex is to **keep external forces from changing the intended position of the body**. If something unexpectedly pushes on your arm while you hold a cup of coffee, the push stretches the extrafusal muscle without your intending it; the muscle-spindle feedback circuit responds at once with a **compensatory contraction** that counters the force, and the coffee stays in the cup.

Figure 8.12 shows this with Pinel's cat, **Arnold**, landing on an outstretched forearm that is holding a coffee mug. The extra weight stretches the **biceps**, increasing the firing of the **biceps spindle afferent neurons**; that increase drives the **biceps motor neurons**, and the arm stays where it was.

Study the figure closely, because it illustrates **two of the chapter's principles** at once:

- the important role of **sensory feedback** in regulating motor output (principle 2); and
- the ability of **lower circuits** to take care of the "business details" **without involving higher levels** (principle 1). No cortical decision is needed to keep the coffee level.

This is also what G.O. (8.1) had lost. Without somatosensory nerves from his arms, his spindles could not report the stretch, and a bump spilled his coffee.

### The withdrawal reflex

Touch something painful, a hot pot for example, and you pull your hand away before you have thought about it. This is a **withdrawal reflex**, and the first thing to learn about it is that, **unlike the stretch reflex, it is not monosynaptic**.

The evidence is a timing argument. When a painful stimulus is applied to the hand, the **first responses** appear in the motor neurons of the **arm flexor** muscles about **1.6 milliseconds** later. That is about the time a signal needs to cross **two synapses**. So the **shortest route** through the withdrawal-reflex circuit must include **one interneuron** (sensory neuron, then interneuron, then motor neuron).

After that initial volley, **further responses** appear in the flexor motor neurons. These are carried by **multisynaptic pathways**, some of which **involve the cortex**. The withdrawal reflex is therefore layered in time: a fast spinal response, then slower contributions from longer circuits, including the brain.

> **Trap.** **Stretch reflex: monosynaptic**, spindle afferent to motor neuron of the **same** muscle, **contraction of the stretched muscle**. **Withdrawal reflex: not monosynaptic**; shortest path has **one interneuron**, first response at about **1.6 ms** in **flexor** motor neurons, later components via **multisynaptic** paths, **some through the cortex**. The 1.6 ms figure is the evidence for the interneuron, so expect it in a question.

### Reciprocal innervation

**Reciprocal innervation** is a key principle of spinal circuitry: **antagonistic muscles are innervated so that when one contracts, the other relaxes**, which allows a smooth, unimpeded movement.

The withdrawal reflex shows how the wiring achieves this (Figure 8.13, where the painful stimulus is a thumbtack). The "bad news" of a sudden painful event in the hand arrives in the **dorsal horn** of the cord and does **two things at once**:

- it excites **excitatory interneurons**, which **excite the motor neurons of the elbow flexor** (biceps); and
- it excites **inhibitory interneurons**, which **inhibit the motor neurons of the elbow extensor** (triceps).

The simultaneous contraction of the biceps and relaxation of the triceps produce a **rapid flexion** of the elbow, pulling the hand away. The important conceptual point is that **a single sensory input produces a coordinated pattern of motor output**: the activities of the **agonists** and **antagonists** are coordinated automatically by the cord's own internal circuitry (Nielsen, 2016), without any instruction from the brain to "relax the triceps".

### Cocontraction

From reciprocal innervation you might conclude that every movement is made by fully exciting the agonists and fully shutting off the antagonists. That arrangement does produce the **quickest** movements, and it is what happens in a withdrawal reflex, but it is **not how voluntary movement is normally produced**.

Most muscles are **always contracted to some degree**, and movements are produced by **adjusting the level of relative cocontraction** between antagonists: the balance of pull between, say, biceps and triceps is shifted, with both remaining partly active. **Cocontraction** has three advantages:

1. Movements produced this way are **smooth**.
2. They can be **stopped with precision** by a slight increase in the contraction of the antagonist.
3. Cocontraction **insulates us from the effects of unexpected external forces**, because a limb held between two partly active muscles is stiffer and resists being knocked off course.

> **Trap.** **Reciprocal innervation** (one antagonist relaxes as the other contracts) gives the **fastest** movement and is the pattern of the **withdrawal reflex**. **Cocontraction** (both antagonists partly active, movement by shifting the balance) is the **normal** pattern of voluntary movement and gives **smooth, precisely stoppable, disturbance-resistant** movement. They are not competing theories; they are two operating modes.

### Recurrent collateral inhibition

Like workers, muscle fibres and the motor neurons that drive them need an occasional break, and inhibitory neurons in the spinal cord make sure they get one. The circuit is elegantly local:

1. Each motor neuron **branches just before it leaves the spinal cord**.
2. The branch (a recurrent collateral) synapses on a **small inhibitory interneuron**.
3. That interneuron **inhibits the very motor neuron** that excited it (Illert & Kummel, 1999).

The inhibition produced by these local feedback loops is called **recurrent collateral inhibition**, and the small inhibitory interneurons that mediate it are **Renshaw cells**. The consequence is that each time a motor neuron fires, it **briefly inhibits itself** and so **shifts the work of contracting the muscle to other members of the muscle's motor pool**. Load is rotated around the pool rather than borne by the same few units.

> **Trap.** Renshaw cells are **inhibitory interneurons** in the **spinal cord** that mediate **recurrent collateral inhibition** of the **same** motor neuron that excites them. Their function in Pinel's account is to **share the load** across the motor pool. Do not confuse them with the inhibitory interneurons of **reciprocal innervation**, which inhibit the motor neurons of the **antagonist** muscle.

### Everything that converges on a motor neuron

Figure 8.14 summarises the module by showing all the **direct excitatory and inhibitory influences** on a single motor neuron, here one supplying the **biceps**:

- **Excitatory**: axons of **primary motor cortex neurons** descending in the cord; the **spindle afferent neuron** from the biceps itself (the stretch-reflex input).
- **Inhibitory**, through inhibitory interneurons: the **spindle afferent neuron from the antagonistic muscle** (the triceps), which is reciprocal innervation again; the axon from the **Golgi tendon organ**, the protective input; and the **Renshaw cell**, which is recurrent collateral inhibition.

Every movement of the biceps is the net result of these inputs added together at the motor neuron, the same integration of EPSPs and IPSPs you met in Chapter 4.

### Walking: a complex sensorimotor reflex

Most reflexes are far more complex than the stretch and withdrawal reflexes. Consider what a program of reflexes for **walking** has to do. It has to integrate:

- **visual** information from the eyes;
- **somatosensory** information from the feet, knees, hips, arms and so on;
- **balance** information from the **semicircular canals** of the inner ears; and
- information from **newly discovered sensory receptors in the spinal cord** itself, which detect **mechanical and chemical** information from the **cerebrospinal fluid in the central canal** (Böhm & Wyart, 2016).

On the basis of all this it must generate an integrated series of movements of the muscles of the trunk, legs, feet and upper arms. It must also be extremely **plastic**, adjusting its output immediately to changes in the **slope** of the ground, to **instructions from the brain**, or to sudden **external forces**. Remarkably, **similar patterns of neural activity control walking in humans, other mammals and birds** (Dominici et al., 2011; Grillner, 2011).

How much of this program lives in the spinal cord? **Grillner (1985)** showed that the spinal cord can control walking **with no contribution at all from the brain**. His subjects were **cats whose spinal cords had been separated from their brains by transection**. He suspended each cat in a **sling over a treadmill**. When the treadmill was started, so that the cats received the kind of sensory feedback that normally accompanies walking, **they began to walk**. Similar results have been obtained in other species (Kiehn, 2016).

Two lessons come from this. First, the basic **rhythm and pattern** of stepping is generated by circuits within the spinal cord; the standard name for such circuits, not used in the 11th edition text but universal in the literature, is **central pattern generators**. Second, the spinal walking program was **triggered and shaped by sensory feedback** from the moving treadmill, so even here principle 2 holds: the program runs on feedback, not in isolation from it.

> **Trap.** In Grillner's (1985) experiment the cats were **spinal** (cord transected from the brain), not decerebrate with an intact brain stem, and they walked only when the **treadmill provided sensory feedback**. The correct conclusion is that **spinal circuits can generate walking without the brain**, not that walking needs no sensory input. The 11th edition titles this section "a complex sensorimotor **reflex**".

## 8.8 Central Sensorimotor Programs and Learning

### The company, from top to bottom

The final module opens by running the company metaphor through the whole hierarchy (the table in 8.1): executives (dorsolateral prefrontal and secondary motor cortex) informed by the posterior parietal cortex, a director of operations (primary motor cortex), four channels of communication (the descending pathways), office managers (spinal circuits) and workers (muscles). It then asks what is actually stored and run at each level, and how practice changes it.

### A hierarchy of central sensorimotor programs

One influential view is that the sensorimotor system is a **hierarchy of central sensorimotor programs**. On this view, **every level except the highest** has certain **patterns of activity programmed into it**, and complex movements are produced by **activating the right combinations** of these programs.

Take the book's example of wanting to look at a magazine. Your **association cortex** activates high-level cortical programs, which activate lower-level programs (perhaps in the **brain stem**) for **walking, bending over, picking up and thumbing through**. These in turn activate **spinal** programs that control the individual elements of each sequence and make the muscles carry out the objective (Grillner & Jessell, 2009).

The key property is **semi-autonomy**. Once activated, **each level can operate on the basis of current sensory feedback without direct control from higher levels**. The highest levels keep the **option** of taking direct control, but most of your individual responses are made **without direct cortical involvement**, and you are often **barely aware** of them (Custers & Aarts, 2010). In the company, a president who wants a new branch office simply tells one executive, who issues the usual series of commands to people further down, and so on; each person knows how to do many tasks and carries them out in light of current conditions. Good companies also have mechanisms that keep the programs at different levels coordinated and effective; in the sensorimotor system this appears to be the job of the **cerebellum and basal ganglia**.

### Characteristic 1: Central programs are capable of motor equivalence

Like an efficient company, the sensorimotor system does not always accomplish a task in exactly the same way. **Motor equivalence** is the fact that **the same basic movement can be carried out in different ways, using different muscles**. You learned to sign your name with stereotyped finger and hand movements, yet if you wrote it with your **toe in the sand** of a beach, the signature would still keep many of its characteristic features.

What motor equivalence implies is more important than the phenomenon. If the program for your signature were stored in the circuits that **directly control your preferred hand**, there would be nothing to drive the toe. Motor equivalence therefore suggests that **general programs are stored higher in the hierarchy** and **adapted to the situation** (the hand, the toe, a pen, a stick) as required. It is another illustration of the system's inherent plasticity.

**Rijntjes and colleagues (1999)** looked for where the signature program lives, using **fMRI**. The central sensorimotor programs for signing one's name appeared to be stored in areas of **secondary motor cortex that control the preferred hand**. The remarkable finding was that **these same hand areas were also activated when the signature was written with the toe**. The program is held in a hand-related secondary motor area but can be executed through a completely different effector.

> **Trap.** In Rijntjes et al. (1999) the signature program was located in **secondary** motor cortex (the **hand** areas of the preferred hand), and those **hand** areas were active even when **signing with the toe**. An option claiming that toe-signing activated only the **foot** area of primary motor cortex, or that the program is stored in **primary** motor cortex, contradicts the finding. Motor equivalence = same movement goal, **different muscles**.

### Characteristic 2: Sensory information that controls central programs is not necessarily conscious

Chapter 6 introduced the idea that the neural mechanisms of **conscious visual perception** (the **ventral stream**) are not necessarily the same as those mediating the **visual control of behaviour** (the **dorsal stream**). The first evidence came from neuropsychological patients: some could respond to visual stimuli they were barely aware of, and others could not interact effectively with objects they perceived perfectly well.

Is there evidence for the same separation in **intact** people? **Haffenden and Goodale (1998)** supplied it (see also Ganel, Tanzer, & Goodale, 2008; Goodale & Westwood, 2004), using a **three-dimensional version of the Ebbinghaus illusion** (Figure 8.15). In this illusion, two identical central disks look different in size because one is surrounded by small circles and the other by large ones; the disk among small circles looks larger.

The volunteers did two things with the same two disks:

- **Perceptual judgement.** Asked to **indicate** the size of each central disk with the right **thumb and pointing finger**, they reported the left disk as **bigger** than the right one, falling for the illusion.
- **Action.** Asked to **reach out and pick up** the disks with the **same two digits**, their **preparatory grip** (the gap between thumb and finger as the hand approached) was scaled to the **actual** size of each disk, not its **perceived** size.

The same person, using the same fingers, on the same object, showed the illusion in conscious report and was immune to it in action. The sensory information controlling the grasping program was not the information that reached consciousness.

> **Trap.** In Haffenden and Goodale (1998) the **grip aperture** during reaching reflected the **actual** size; the **manual size estimate** reflected the **illusory** size. Reversing these is the standard error. The illusion is the **Ebbinghaus** illusion, and the study supports the **dorsal stream (action) versus ventral stream (perception)** distinction in **healthy** people. The book uses it to illustrate the "consciousness" theme.

### Characteristic 3: Central programs can develop without practice

Some central sensorimotor programs are established by practising the behaviour, but the programs for many **species-typical behaviours** are established **without explicit practice**. The classic demonstration is **Fentress (1973)**. He showed that **adult mice raised from birth without forelimbs** still produced the **shoulder movements typical of their species' grooming**, and that these were **well coordinated with normal tongue, head and eye movements**. For example, the mice **blinked** each time they made the shoulder movement that would have swept a forepaw across their eyes, though there was no forepaw. The grooming program had developed, intact and coordinated, in animals that had never been able to practise it.

Fentress's study also showed the **importance of sensory feedback** in running central programs. Lacking the normal **contact between tongue and forepaw** during face grooming, the forelimbless mice would often **break off** an apparent grooming sequence to **lick a cage-mate or even the floor**, as if searching for the missing feedback. So the program could develop without practice, but it still expected the sensory consequences it normally produces.

### Characteristic 4: Practice can create central programs

Although many species-typical programs develop without practice, practice can **generate or modify** central programs. Theories of sensorimotor learning stress **two processes**:

1. **response chunking**; and
2. **shifting control to lower levels** of the sensorimotor system.

These are the 8.8 names for the two changes promised in principle 3: a change in the **nature** of control (chunking) and a change in its **locus** (shifting downward).

### Response chunking

According to the **response-chunking hypothesis**, practice **combines the central programs that control individual responses into programs that control sequences (chunks) of behaviour**. A **novice typist** triggers and controls each keystroke of a word separately; a **skilled typist** activates sequences of letters as a **single unit**, with a marked gain in speed and continuity.

An important principle is that **chunks can themselves be combined into higher-order chunks**. The individual letter and digit responses of your home address may be chunked into words and numbers, and those chunks in turn combined so that the whole address is typed as one unit. Chunking is therefore hierarchical, mirroring the hierarchy of programs above.

### Shifting control to lower levels

In learning a central sensorimotor program, **control is shifted from higher levels of the hierarchy to lower levels** (Bassett et al., 2015; Kawai et al., 2015; Makino et al., 2016). This has **two advantages**:

1. **It frees the higher levels** to deal with more esoteric aspects of performance. A skilled pianist can concentrate on **interpreting** a piece because she no longer has to attend to pressing the right keys.
2. **It permits great speed**, because different circuits at the lower levels can **act simultaneously** without interfering with one another. Typing at **120 words per minute** is possible only because the circuits for each key press can become active **before the previous key press has finished**.

> **Trap.** The **two** processes of sensorimotor learning in Pinel are **response chunking** and **shifting control to lower levels**. The **two advantages** of shifting control are **freeing higher levels** and **speed** (through simultaneous, overlapping activity of lower circuits, as in **120 words per minute** typing). An option offering "greater conscious accuracy" as an advantage of shifting control has it backwards.

### Functional brain imaging of sensorimotor learning: Jenkins and colleagues (1994)

Functional brain imaging lets researchers watch the gross neural correlates of motor learning: by recording brain activity while volunteers learn new motor sequences, they can form hypotheses about what each structure contributes. One of the first studies of this kind was the **PET** study of **Jenkins and colleagues (1994)**.

**Method.** Volunteers performed **two different sequences of key presses**:

- there were **four keys**, and each sequence was **four presses long**;
- presses were made with the **right hand**, **one every 3 seconds**;
- **tones** signalled when to press and whether each press was correct.

There were **three conditions**:

1. a **rest** control condition;
2. performance of a **newly learned** sequence; and
3. performance of a **well-practised** sequence.

**Results** (Figure 8.16). The book asks you to notice two things. First, the cortical sensorimotor areas introduced in this chapter were all involved. Second, the involvement of **association areas and the cerebellum diminished** once the sequences were well practised. Reading the figure area by area:

| Area | Newly learned sequence | Well-practised sequence |
|---|---|---|
| Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex | Very active | **Not active** |
| Posterior parietal cortex | Very active | Active (reduced) |
| Premotor cortex | Very active | Active (reduced) |
| Supplementary motor area | Active | **Very active** (increased) |
| Primary motor and somatosensory cortex | Very active | Very active |
| Cerebellum | Very active | Active (reduced) |

**Why the study summarises the chapter.** It shows the hierarchy at work (association, secondary and primary motor cortex all engaged), the role of the cerebellum in acquiring new sequences, and principle 3 in action: as a sequence is learned, the **highest** level (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, where decisions to act are made) **drops out**, the parietal and cerebellar contributions shrink, and the work is concentrated in **motor** areas, with the SMA becoming more prominent. Control has shifted downward.

Later studies in the same tradition (e.g., Bassett et al., 2015; Ostry & Gribble, 2015) have helped identify **where** in the brain changes occur during sensorimotor learning. Imaging, however, shows *where*; it does not show *what kind* of change occurs. The next section turns to that.

> **Trap.** In Jenkins et al. (1994) the area that **drops out completely** with practice is the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**; the area that **increases** is the **supplementary motor area**. Posterior parietal, premotor and cerebellar activity **decrease** but remain; primary motor/somatosensory activity stays high. It was a **PET** study (not fMRI), with **four keys**, **four-press** sequences, **right hand**, **one press every 3 seconds**, and **three conditions** (rest, new, well-practised).

### Neuroplasticity associated with sensorimotor learning

Learning new sensorimotor tasks is accompanied by changes at **two levels**, and the book gives one example of each.

1. **Cortical.** In the motor cortex, learning **strengthens the inputs from the thalamus and from other areas of motor cortex**. The strengthening is related to an **increase in the number of dendritic spines**, which suggests an increase in the number of **synapses** (Peters et al., 2017).
2. **Subcortical.** Just after sensorimotor learning there is a **large increase in the number of oligodendrocytes** (the glial cells that form **myelin** sheaths in the CNS, Chapter 4) in the **subcortical white matter**. This presumably reflects an increased demand for **myelination** of new or existing axonal connections (Xiao et al., 2016).

> **Trap.** The two neuroplastic changes named in 8.8 are **more dendritic spines** (strengthened thalamic and motor-cortical inputs to motor cortex; cortical) and **more oligodendrocytes** in subcortical **white matter** (myelination; subcortical). Oligodendrocytes myelinate in the **CNS**; Schwann cells do so in the PNS. An option attributing the white-matter change to Schwann cells, or to neurogenesis, is wrong.

### The Case of Rhonelle, Revisited

The chapter ends back in the supermarket, a few days after the writing was finished. Pinel, again in Rhonelle's queue (the longest, but he is a creature of habit), watches her with new understanding and wonders whether she appreciates her finely tuned sensorimotor system as much as he now does. Then he plans a mischievous experiment on her **muscle-spindle feedback system**: next time he will take a paper bag from the mushroom counter, blow it up, drop in a single mushroom, and fold the top so it looks full. How would her system cope with a bag that looks heavy but is almost weightless? His smile at the idea fades when he notices Rhonelle grinning at him, having once again caught him daydreaming.

The proposed test is a good revision question in disguise. Presumably her programs would set the force of the lift from the bag's **visual appearance**, before any feedback could arrive, so the bag would fly up too fast; feedback from her spindles and other receptors would then have to correct the movement in mid-course. Anticipatory programming from vision, followed by feedback correction, is the whole chapter in one movement.

### Themes revisited

The chapter touches all four of the book's major themes and one of its two emerging themes.

- **Clinical implications** (the most prominent): research with neuropsychological patients with sensorimotor deficits (G.O., Mrs. S., apraxia, cerebellar damage, primary motor cortex lesions) and with healthy volunteers has shaped current theories of sensorimotor function.
- **Evolutionary perspective**: many of the key experiments are comparative, mostly in non-human primates. Their sensorimotor functions are similar to ours but **not identical** (monkeys walk on both hands and feet, for instance). Programs for walking are remarkably similar across humans, other mammals and birds.
- **Thinking creatively**: the use of a **metaphor** (the company) to think productively about science, and the recent analyses suggesting that primary motor cortex encodes the **end point** of movements rather than the movements themselves.
- **Neuroplasticity**: the sensorimotor system is fundamentally **plastic**. Cortical circuits issue general commands, but exactly how a movement is executed depends on current conditions such as body position, and the system keeps changing itself with learning and practice (dendritic spines, oligodendrocytes).
- **Consciousness** (one of the two emerging themes): the recurring contrast between conscious and unconscious control of movement, demonstrated most clearly with the **Ebbinghaus illusion**.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the eight modules.

1. **Goals at the top, muscles at the bottom.** Association cortex specifies what and where; secondary motor cortex programs patterns; primary motor cortex issues commands (increasingly understood as targets and actions); brain stem and spinal circuits turn those into muscle activity. Each level knows things the others do not, and the higher levels are freed by not having to know the details.
2. **Parallel routes make the system robust.** Large primary motor cortex lesions spare voluntary movement because secondary and association areas have their own descending routes; Lawrence and Kuypers' monkeys kept whichever functions the uncut pathways served; Capogrosso's interface showed a cortical command can even be rerouted around a cut cord.
3. **Feedback at every level.** G.O.'s spilled coffee, the stretch reflex that saves it, the cerebellum's comparison of intended and actual movement, the forelimbless mice licking the floor for missing tongue-paw contact, the spinal cats that walked only once the treadmill moved: motor output is everywhere steered by its sensory consequences, the ballistic movement being the one exception.
4. **Much control is unconscious.** Rhonelle's automaticity, unconscious perception in neglect, the Ebbinghaus grip, Desmurget's patients who felt they had moved without moving: the information that guides action and the information that reaches awareness are not the same.
5. **The system is plastic.** One cortical site drives a limb to one end point from any start; one signature program runs through a hand or a toe; learning reorganises programs into chunks and pushes them down the hierarchy, with growth of dendritic spines and myelinating glia.
6. **Old views keep being replaced.** Direction coding gave way to end-point coding and an action map; the purely motor cerebellum and basal ganglia gave way to structures with cognitive, emotional and motivational roles; the "eight areas" of secondary motor cortex replaced the old two; and the human mirror-neuron story remains far weaker than the monkey one.

## Worked example

*Four patients are seen on a neurology ward in the same week. (A) A right-handed accountant, after a stroke, shaves only the right side of his face, eats only from the right side of his tray, and insists nothing is wrong. Visual field testing is normal. (B) A right-handed carpenter cannot show the examiner how to use a screwdriver or wave goodbye on request, though both hands are strong and she understands the instructions; the nurses report she waved to her daughter normally at visiting time. (C) A young man after a road accident reaches past objects, overshoots and undershoots, shakes when trying to hold his arm still, walks with a wide unsteady gait and slurs his speech. (D) A woman after a viral illness has normal strength but keeps dropping her cup unless she watches it, and spills her tea whenever someone brushes past.*

Work through the chapter's machinery rather than matching keywords.

**Patient A.** One-sided failure to respond, involving his own body as well as external space, with normal visual fields and denial of the problem: this is **contralateral neglect**. Because the neglected side is the left, the lesion is most likely in the **right posterior parietal cortex**, typically a large lesion. Normal visual fields matter, because the definition requires the absence of a simple sensory deficit. To probe further you would test whether the neglect is **egocentric** (does it stay put when he tilts his head?) or **object-based** (does he ignore the left half of a picture even when it is presented on his right?). Mrs. S. (Sacks, 1999) is the textbook parallel, and her rightward turning strategy suggests a practical workaround for the meal problem.

**Patient B.** Inability to make a movement on request, out of context, with intact strength and comprehension, while the same movement occurs naturally in context (the spontaneous wave): this is **apraxia**. Expect damage to the **left posterior parietal cortex** or its connections, with symptoms in **both** hands. The carpenter example in the book is deliberately similar.

**Patient C.** Movements happen, but their direction, force, velocity and amplitude are poorly controlled, there is tremor on holding a posture, and balance, gait and speech are disturbed: this is the picture of **diffuse cerebellar damage** (clinically, **ataxia**). The mechanism is the loss of the cerebellum's comparison of intended, sent and actual movement. You would also expect difficulty **learning new motor sequences**, and possibly cognitive or emotional changes, since the 11th edition no longer treats the cerebellum as purely motor.

**Patient D.** Strength is normal and she can see, yet she cannot hold a steady grip without watching and cannot correct for a bump: this is the pattern of **G.O.** (Rothwell et al., 1982), loss of **somatosensory feedback** from the arms. The spilled tea in particular reflects loss of the **stretch-reflex** correction, which needs the spindle afferents. Vision helps her with position but not with force, exactly as it did for G.O.

**What would distinguish these four if an exam option tried to blur them?** Ask three questions of each patient. Can the movement be produced at all, in some context? (Yes for A, B and D; yes but badly for C.) Is the problem one-sided? (Only A.) Does the patient know? (A usually does not; B, C and D do.) Those three questions separate neglect, apraxia, ataxia and deafferentation more reliably than any list of symptoms.

## MCQ traps

1. **Hierarchy is parallel.** The sensorimotor system is a **parallel, functionally segregated, hierarchical** system, like the sensory systems; the only major difference is that information flows mainly **down**, not up. Its main advantage: higher levels are **freed for more complex functions**.
2. **Ballistic movements** (brief, all-or-none, high-speed, e.g., swatting a fly) are the only responses **not normally influenced by sensory feedback**.
3. **G.O.** (Rothwell et al., 1982), former **darts champion**: infection destroyed **somatosensory** nerves of the arms; greatest problem was **maintaining constant muscle contraction**; vision did not help with force.
4. **Two sensorimotor association areas**: posterior parietal and dorsolateral prefrontal. Posterior parietal receives **visual, auditory, somatosensory** input; projects to **dorsolateral prefrontal, secondary motor cortex** and the **frontal eye field** (which is in **prefrontal** cortex).
5. **Desmurget et al. (2009)**: inferior posterior parietal stimulation; low current = **intention** to move, higher current = **belief** of having moved; **no actual movement** at either.
6. **Apraxia** = **left** posterior parietal damage, **bilateral** symptoms; cannot perform movements **on request, out of context**; not due to weakness, comprehension or motivation.
7. **Contralateral neglect** = usually large **right** posterior parietal lesions; **left** side ignored; **no simple sensory or motor deficit**; patients often **unaware** of the problem.
8. **Mrs. S.** (Sacks, 1999): right posterior stroke; ate the right half of the plate; turned her wheelchair **to the right** in a full circle to find the rest.
9. **Egocentric left** is partly defined by **gravitational** coordinates (tilting the head does not tilt the neglected field). **Object-based** neglect = left side of **each object**, even upside down or horizontal (Adair & Barrett, 2008).
10. **Unconscious perception in neglect**: **Geng & Behrmann (2002)** (looked more towards locations of unseen objects); **Vuilleumier et al. (2002)** (unseen left drawings primed fragmented drawings on the right).
11. **Dorsolateral prefrontal** neurons (Rao, Rainer, & Miller, 1997) code object, location, both, or response; response neurons **fire first** among cortical motor areas; probable site of the **decision to initiate** movement.
12. **Secondary motor cortex**: input from association cortex, output to primary motor; stimulation gives **complex, often bilateral** movements; at least **eight areas per hemisphere** (Nachev, Kennard, & Husain, 2008). Older keys: 3 supplementary + 2 premotor + 3 cingulate.
13. **SMA** = top of frontal lobe and **medial** surface into the longitudinal fissure; **premotor** = **lateral** strip from SMA to the lateral fissure.
14. **Mirror neurons**: discovered **early 1990s**, **Rizzolatti**, **macaque ventral premotor** area; fire for performing **and** observing the same goal-directed action; respond to the **sound** of a peanut cracking alone.
15. **Human mirror neurons**: 600+ "mirror system" studies, mostly **fMRI**; single-neuron evidence from only **one** study known to the authors (**Mukamel et al., 2010**); fMRI is **not direct** evidence.
16. **Primary motor cortex** = **precentral gyrus**; major point of **convergence** and major, **but not the only**, point of **departure**.
17. **Penfield and Boldrey (1937)**: brief, low-intensity stimulation, simple **contralateral** movements, somatotopic map (**motor homunculus**), most cortex for **hands and mouth**.
18. **Current view**: **0.5 to 1 s** stimulation gives **complex natural sequences** (feeding response, aggressive facial expression); representations **overlap** (Sanes et al., 1995); small hand-area lesions do not knock out a single finger (Scheiber, 1999; Scheiber & Poliakov, 1998).
19. **End point, not direction**: same site moves the elbow to **90 degrees** from straight **or** from tightly bent; primary motor cortex has an **action map** in addition to a topographic map (Graziano, 2016).
20. **Belle**: **owl monkey**, Nicolelis and Chapin lab, **100 microelectrodes** in primary motor cortex, robotic arm at **MIT** several hundred km away.
21. **Primary motor cortex lesions** do **not** abolish voluntary movement; they impair **independent finger movement**, cause **astereognosia**, reduce **speed, accuracy, force**.
22. **Cerebellum**: about **10%** of brain mass, **more than half** of its neurons (Azevedo et al., 2009); compares cortical intention, brain-stem output and sensory feedback; **timing** of sequences; **error** learning and prediction.
23. **Basal ganglia**: fewer neurons, **heterogeneous** nuclei; **loops** with cortex and cerebellum; **movement vigour**, **suppression** of unwanted movement, **habit learning**, **classical conditioning**.
24. **Dorsolateral** pathways: **distal** muscles, **contralateral**, **one segment**, sometimes directly on motor neurons. **Ventromedial**: **proximal**/trunk, **diffuse**, **both sides**, **several segments**.
25. **Corticorubrospinal** (red nucleus) is a **dorsolateral** tract; **cortico-brainstem-spinal** (tectum, vestibular nucleus, reticular formation, cranial motor nuclei) is **ventromedial**. **Betz cells** belong to the **dorsolateral corticospinal** tract (older editions).
26. **Lawrence and Kuypers (1968)**: dorsolateral cut = arms hang limply when sitting, **rubber-handled rake** reaching; ventromedial cut = **postural** collapse, falls at a loud noise.
27. **Motor unit** = one motor neuron + all its fibres; **motor pool** = all motor neurons of one muscle. **Fewest fibres** per unit in **fingers and face**.
28. **Fast fibres**: quick, strong, **fatigue quickly**, **pale** (poorly vascularised). **Slow fibres**: weaker, sustained, **red**. Every muscle has **both**.
29. **Spindles** = **length**, in the muscle (**in parallel**). **Golgi tendon organs** = **tension**, in the tendon (**in series**), completely insensitive to length, **protective** relaxation via **inhibitory** interneurons.
30. **Stretch reflex** is **monosynaptic**; **withdrawal reflex** is **not**: first flexor response at about **1.6 ms**, i.e., **two synapses**, **one interneuron**; later components multisynaptic, some via cortex.
31. **Reciprocal innervation** (flexor excited, extensor inhibited) gives the fastest movement; normal voluntary movement uses **cocontraction**, which is smooth, precisely stoppable and resists external forces.
32. **Renshaw cells** mediate **recurrent collateral inhibition** of the **same** motor neuron, shifting work to other members of the **motor pool**.
33. **Grillner (1985)**: **spinal** cats in a sling on a **treadmill** walked; walking can be generated by the spinal cord without the brain, given sensory feedback.
34. **Motor equivalence** (Rijntjes et al., 1999, fMRI): signature program in **secondary** motor cortex **hand** areas, active even when signing with the **toe**.
35. **Haffenden & Goodale (1998)**: 3D **Ebbinghaus** illusion; finger-thumb **estimate** shows the illusion, **grip aperture** follows **actual** size.
36. **Fentress (1973)**: mice **without forelimbs from birth** groomed with coordinated shoulder movements and **blinked** on time; programs develop **without practice**, but sensory feedback still matters (licked cage-mates or the floor).
37. **Jenkins et al. (1994)**, **PET**: 4 keys, 4-press sequences, right hand, 1 press per **3 s**; with practice **DLPFC drops out**, **SMA increases**, parietal, premotor and cerebellum decrease.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sensory feedback** | Sensory signals produced by a response, used to guide the continuation of that response |
| **Ballistic movements** | Brief, all-or-none, high-speed movements (e.g., swatting a fly); not normally influenced by sensory feedback |
| **Parallel hierarchical system** | Hierarchy in which signals flow between levels over multiple paths |
| **Functional segregation** | Each level of the hierarchy is made of different units with different functions |
| **Posterior parietal association cortex** | Parietal neocortex posterior to primary somatosensory cortex; integrates body and object position, supplies spatial information, directs attention |
| **Frontal eye field** | Small area of prefrontal cortex controlling eye movements and shifts of attention |
| **Apraxia** | Disorder of voluntary movement not due to simple motor deficit or to comprehension or motivation problems; movements fail on request out of context; often left posterior parietal damage, bilateral symptoms |
| **Contralateral neglect** | Failure to respond to stimuli on the side opposite a brain lesion without simple sensory or motor deficits; usually right posterior parietal damage |
| **Egocentric left** | Space to the left of the patient's own body, partly defined by gravitational coordinates |
| **Object-based contralateral neglect** | Failure to respond to the left side of objects wherever they are and however oriented |
| **Dorsolateral prefrontal association cortex** | Association cortex receiving posterior parietal input and projecting to secondary and primary motor cortex and frontal eye field; probable site of decisions to initiate movement |
| **Secondary motor cortex** | Areas receiving input mainly from association cortex and projecting mainly to primary motor cortex; program specific movement patterns |
| **Supplementary motor area (SMA)** | Secondary motor area on the top and medial surface of the frontal lobe, anterior to primary motor cortex |
| **Premotor cortex** | Secondary motor area forming a lateral strip from the SMA to the lateral fissure |
| **Cingulate motor areas** | Secondary motor areas in the cortex of the cingulate gyrus |
| **Mirror neurons** | Neurons firing both when an individual performs a goal-directed action and when it observes another perform the same action |
| **Social cognition** | Knowledge of the perceptions, ideas and intentions of others |
| **Primary motor cortex** | Precentral gyrus; major point of convergence and major (not only) point of departure of cortical sensorimotor signals |
| **Somatotopic** | Organised according to a map of the body |
| **Motor homunculus** | Somatotopic map of human primary motor cortex (Penfield & Boldrey, 1937) |
| **Stereognosis** | Identifying objects by touch |
| **Astereognosia** | Deficit in stereognosis; can follow large primary motor cortex lesions |
| **Action map** | Proposed map of goal end points and actions in primary motor cortex, in addition to its topographic map |
| **Brain-computer interface** | Direct communication between a computer and the brain, usually via implanted electrodes |
| **Habit learning** | Learning acquired gradually, trial by trial; involves the basal ganglia |
| **Movement vigour** | Control of the speed and amplitude of movement according to motivation; attributed to the basal ganglia |
| **Dorsolateral motor pathways** | Two descending pathways in the dorsolateral cord; contralateral, focused, distal muscles |
| **Ventromedial motor pathways** | Two descending pathways in the ventromedial cord; diffuse, bilateral, proximal and trunk muscles |
| **Betz cells** | Very large pyramidal neurons of primary motor cortex contributing to the dorsolateral corticospinal tract (older editions) |
| **Red nucleus** | Midbrain relay of the dorsolateral corticorubrospinal tract |
| **Motor unit** | A single motor neuron plus all the muscle fibres it innervates |
| **Motor end-plate** | Receptive area of a muscle fibre at the neuromuscular junction, activated by acetylcholine |
| **Motor pool** | All the motor neurons innervating one muscle |
| **Fast / slow muscle fibres** | Quick, strong, fatigable, pale / slower, weaker, sustained, red |
| **Flexors / extensors** | Muscles that bend / straighten a joint |
| **Synergistic muscles** | Muscles whose contraction produces the same movement |
| **Antagonistic muscles** | Muscles that act in opposition (e.g., biceps and triceps) |
| **Isometric contraction** | Increase in muscle tension without shortening |
| **Dynamic contraction** | Contraction that shortens the muscle and pulls the bones together |
| **Golgi tendon organs** | Tension receptors in tendons; insensitive to length; protective relaxation in extreme contraction |
| **Muscle spindles** | Length receptors embedded in muscle tissue |
| **Intrafusal muscle** | Thread-like muscle within each spindle |
| **Intrafusal motor neuron** | Motor neuron of the intrafusal muscle; keeps the spindle taut and sensitive |
| **Skeletal (extrafusal) muscle** | The ordinary working muscle in which spindles lie |
| **Spindle afferent neurons** | Sensory neurons carrying spindle signals into the cord via the dorsal root |
| **Patellar tendon reflex** | Knee-jerk stretch reflex of the thigh extensor |
| **Stretch reflex** | Monosynaptic reflex elicited by sudden external stretch of a muscle; resists unintended changes in position |
| **Withdrawal reflex** | Reflex withdrawal from a painful stimulus; shortest path includes one interneuron (about 1.6 ms) |
| **Reciprocal innervation** | Wiring of antagonists so that when one contracts the other relaxes |
| **Cocontraction** | Simultaneous partial contraction of antagonists; movement by adjusting their balance |
| **Recurrent collateral inhibition** | Self-inhibition of a motor neuron via its axon collateral and an inhibitory interneuron |
| **Renshaw cells** | Spinal inhibitory interneurons mediating recurrent collateral inhibition |
| **Central pattern generator** | Standard term for spinal circuits generating rhythmic movement such as walking (not used in the 11th ed. text) |
| **Central sensorimotor programs** | Patterns of activity programmed into all but the highest levels of the hierarchy and combined to produce movement |
| **Motor equivalence** | Carrying out the same basic movement in different ways with different muscles |
| **Response-chunking hypothesis** | Practice combines programs for single responses into programs for sequences (chunks) |
| **Ebbinghaus illusion** | Illusion in which identical central disks look different sizes because of surrounding circles |

## Likely exam points

1. **Three principles**: hierarchical organisation; motor output guided by sensory input; learning changes the nature and locus of control.
2. **General model order**: association cortex, secondary motor cortex, primary motor cortex, brain-stem motor nuclei, spinal motor circuits, muscles; cerebellum and basal ganglia off the main line.
3. **Ballistic movements** as the exception to feedback control.
4. **G.O.** (Rothwell et al., 1982): deafferented arms; failure to maintain constant contraction; spilled coffee when brushed.
5. **Posterior parietal cortex** inputs (visual, auditory, somatosensory) and outputs (DLPFC, secondary motor cortex, frontal eye field).
6. **Desmurget et al. (2009)**: intention versus sense of having moved, without movement.
7. **Apraxia**: definition, context dependence (carpenter example), left parietal, bilateral.
8. **Contralateral neglect**: definition, right parietal, unawareness of the deficit, **Mrs. S.** (Sacks, 1999).
9. **Egocentric versus object-based neglect** (gravitational definition of egocentric left) and **unconscious perception in neglect** (Geng & Behrmann, 2002; Vuilleumier et al., 2002).
10. **DLPFC** neurons fire **first**; decision to initiate movement; Rao, Rainer, & Miller (1997).
11. **Secondary motor cortex**: definition, three criteria, eight areas, SMA/premotor/cingulate motor areas; role in programming and imagining movements.
12. **Mirror neurons**: Rizzolatti, early 1990s, ventral premotor, the four-condition design, social cognition, sound-only and hidden-action responses.
13. **Human mirror evidence**: mostly fMRI; Mukamel et al. (2010) the single-cell exception; why fMRI is indirect.
14. **Motor homunculus** (Penfield & Boldrey, 1937) and its disproportion for hands and mouth; **stereognosis** and the skin-input hand area in monkeys.
15. **Conventional versus current view of primary motor cortex**: brief versus 0.5 to 1 s stimulation; simple versus complex movements; direction versus end point; the 90-degree elbow result; action map.
16. **Belle** and **brain-computer interfaces**, including **Capogrosso et al. (2016)**.
17. **Effects of primary motor cortex lesions** (independent finger movement, astereognosia, speed, accuracy, force) and why voluntary movement survives.
18. **Cerebellum**: 10% of mass, more than half the neurons; three inputs compared; effects of diffuse damage; non-motor roles; error learning.
19. **Basal ganglia**: heterogeneous nuclei, loops, cognition, motivation, habit learning, classical conditioning; movement vigour and suppression.
20. **Dorsolateral versus ventromedial pathways**: termination pattern and distal versus proximal muscles; the four named tracts and brain-stem structures (older keys).
21. **Lawrence and Kuypers (1968)**: rubber-handled rake versus postural collapse; what each shows.
22. **Motor unit, motor pool, motor end-plate**; acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction; muscles only pull; fast versus slow fibres.
23. **Flexors, extensors, synergists, antagonists**; isometric versus dynamic contraction; recruitment and firing rate.
24. **Golgi tendon organs versus muscle spindles**: location, variable, protective function.
25. **Intrafusal system** and why spindles need their own muscle.
26. **Stretch reflex** sequence (patellar tendon reflex), its monosynaptic circuit and its real function (cat Arnold, coffee cup).
27. **Withdrawal reflex** (1.6 ms, one interneuron), **reciprocal innervation** and **cocontraction**.
28. **Recurrent collateral inhibition** and **Renshaw cells**; the inputs converging on a motor neuron (Fig 8.14).
29. **Grillner (1985)**: spinal walking in cats; similar walking programs in humans, mammals and birds.
30. **Characteristics of central programs**: motor equivalence (Rijntjes et al., 1999); unconscious sensory control (Haffenden & Goodale, 1998); development without practice (Fentress, 1973); creation by practice.
31. **Response chunking** and **shifting control to lower levels**, with the two advantages (freeing higher levels; speed, 120 wpm).
32. **Jenkins et al. (1994)** PET study: design and the new-versus-practised activity pattern; neuroplasticity (dendritic spines; oligodendrocytes).
