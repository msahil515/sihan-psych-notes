# Chapter 12: Hunger, Eating, and Health

**What this chapter is really about.** Almost every chapter in a biopsychology text asks you to learn a mechanism. This one asks you to *unlearn* one first. Nearly everybody, including most people who have already studied some psychology, believes that hunger works like a fuel gauge: the body burns energy, the level drops, a warning light called hunger comes on, you eat, the tank refills, and the light goes out. That belief has a name in the literature, the **set-point assumption**, and Pinel and Barnes spend the chapter dismantling it. The alternative they build in its place, the **positive-incentive perspective**, says that you are not normally *pushed* to eat by a deficit inside you but *pulled* to eat by the anticipated pleasure of the food in front of you.

Do not read this as an academic quarrel. The whole clinical half of the chapter, obesity, dieting, leptin, gastric surgery, anorexia, bulimia, only makes sense once you have made the switch. If the set-point account were true, an obesity epidemic could not happen, dieting would work, and a starving anorexia patient would be unable to resist food. All three of those predictions fail, and the chapter's job is to show you why, and what to believe instead.

**How to read this chapter.** Sections 12.1 gives you the plumbing (digestion, storage, metabolism) that everything else refers back to. 12.2 sets the two theories against each other. 12.3 collects the behavioural evidence, 12.4 the physiological evidence. 12.5 extends the argument from *eating* to *body weight*. 12.6 and 12.7 apply all of it clinically. Nothing after 12.2 makes full sense unless you can state both theories cleanly, so do not skim that section.

## The Case of the Man Who Forgot Not to Eat

Before any theory, one observation. R.H. was a 48-year-old man whose graduate studies were interrupted by a severe **anterograde amnesia for long-term explicit memory**, produced by bilateral damage to the medial temporal lobes and confirmed on MRI. His pattern of deficit was much like H.M.'s from Chapter 11: intact short-term memory, intact old memories, no ability to lay down new conscious ones.

Rozin and colleagues (1998) offered him a meal he liked, veal parmigiana, about 750 calories, plus all the apple juice he wanted. Fifteen minutes after he finished, they offered him a second full meal. He ate it. Fifteen minutes after *that*, they offered a third. He ate that too. He turned down the fourth only on the grounds that his "stomach was a little tight". A few minutes later he announced he was going out for a walk and a meal. Asked what he intended to eat, he said: veal parmigiana.

Hold this case in mind, because it is the single cleanest disproof in the chapter. R.H.'s body was, by any physiological measure, massively over-supplied with energy after two full meals. If hunger were the read-out of an energy deficit, he could not have been hungry. What he had lost was not a fuel gauge but a **memory of having eaten**. His hunger tracked his memory, not his metabolism. Higgs and colleagues (2008) have since reported the same pattern in other amnesic patients. Whatever hunger is, it is not simply the sensation of an empty tank.

## 12.1 Digestion, Energy Storage, and Energy Utilisation

### The gastrointestinal tract, step by step

**Digestion** is the gastrointestinal process of breaking food down and absorbing its constituents into the body. Pinel offers a deliberately unglamorous way to picture it: ignore the arms, legs and head, and think of the body as a living tube with a hole at each end. Food goes in the end with the teeth, travels along an internal canal where it is broken apart and partly absorbed *through* the canal wall into the body proper, and the leftovers are ejected from the other end. Strictly speaking, food has not been consumed until it has been digested. Much of the actual breakdown work is done not by you but by your **gut microbiome**, the bacteria and other organisms living inside the tract; keep that term, because it comes back in 12.6 as a possible cause of individual differences in obesity.

The sequence, which you should be able to recite in order:

1. **Chewing** breaks food up and mixes it with saliva.
2. **Saliva** lubricates the food and begins digestion.
3. **Swallowing** moves it down the **oesophagus** to the stomach.
4. The **stomach** is primarily a *storage reservoir*. Its **hydrochloric acid** breaks food into small particles and its enzyme **pepsin** begins breaking protein molecules down into amino acids.
5. The stomach empties gradually through the **pyloric sphincter** into the **duodenum**, the upper portion of the small intestine, where most absorption occurs.
6. **Digestive enzymes in the duodenum**, many arriving from the **gall bladder** and **pancreas**, break proteins into amino acids and starches and complex sugars into simple sugars. Simple sugars and amino acids pass readily through the duodenal wall into the bloodstream and are carried to the **liver**.
7. **Fats** are **emulsified** (broken into droplets) by **bile**, which is manufactured in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, and released into the duodenum. Emulsified fat *cannot* pass through the duodenal wall; it is carried instead by small ducts into the **lymphatic system**.
8. The **large intestine** absorbs most of the remaining water and electrolytes, and the remainder is ejected.

> **Trap.** Two exam-favourite details hide in that list. The stomach's primary job is *storage*, not absorption, and fats take a *different route* out of the gut (lymphatic system) from sugars and amino acids (bloodstream to liver). Questions are routinely written on both.

### Why the body stores most of its energy as fat

Digestion delivers energy to the body in three forms: **lipids** (fats), **amino acids** (the breakdown products of proteins), and **glucose** (a simple sugar, the breakdown product of complex carbohydrates, and the body's main directly utilisable fuel).

The body uses energy *continuously* but takes it in *intermittently*, so it must store energy to cover the gaps between meals. Storage takes three forms, in wildly unequal proportions:

| Stored as | Where | Share of reserves |
|---|---|---|
| **Fat** | Adipose tissue | ~85% |
| **Protein** | Muscle | ~14.5% |
| **Glycogen** | Liver and muscle | ~0.5% |

Because fat dominates the reserves, changes in an adult's body weight are largely changes in the amount of **stored body fat**.

Now the question worth understanding rather than memorising: why is fat, not glycogen, the preferred store? Glycogen looks like the better candidate, because it converts readily to glucose, the fuel the body can use directly. There are two reasons it loses:

1. **Energy density.** A gram of fat stores almost **twice** as much energy as a gram of glycogen.
2. **Water.** Glycogen, unlike fat, attracts and holds substantial quantities of water. If all your fat calories were stored as glycogen instead, you would likely weigh well over **275 kg (600 lb)**.

That second reason is the one students forget, and it is the one that makes the design decision obvious: an animal that had to carry its reserves as hydrated glycogen could not move well enough to find the next meal.

### The three phases of energy metabolism

**Energy metabolism** is the set of chemical changes by which energy is made available for use. It runs in three phases, and every hormonal fact later in the chapter attaches to one of them.

- The **cephalic phase** is the *preparatory* phase. It typically begins with the sight, the smell, or even just the *thought* of food, and it ends when food starts to be absorbed into the bloodstream. Note carefully: this phase begins **before** anything is eaten. It is triggered by expectation.
- The **absorptive phase** is the period during which energy absorbed from the meal is meeting the body's immediate needs, with the excess being stored.
- The **fasting phase** is the period during which all the unstored energy from the previous meal has been used and the body is withdrawing energy from its reserves. It ends with the beginning of the next cephalic phase.

One clinically important detail: during periods of rapid weight gain, people often go straight from one absorptive phase into the next cephalic phase **without an intervening fasting phase at all**. Somebody eating frequently never mobilises reserves; they only ever store.

### Insulin and glucagon: the two hormones that run the phases

The flow of energy across the three phases is controlled by two pancreatic hormones, **insulin** and **glucagon**, which move in opposition.

**Cephalic and absorptive phases: insulin high, glucagon low.** Insulin does three things:

1. It promotes the **use of glucose** as the body's primary energy source.
2. It promotes the **conversion of bloodborne fuels into storable forms**: glucose into glycogen and fat, amino acids into proteins.
3. It promotes the **storage** of those forms: glycogen in liver and muscle, fat in adipose tissue, protein in muscle.

Read the function of insulin separately for each of the two phases, because this is a favourite distinction. In the **cephalic** phase its job is to *lower* the level of bloodborne fuels, primarily glucose, **in anticipation** of the influx that is about to arrive. In the **absorptive** phase its job is to *minimise the rise* in bloodborne fuels by using and storing them.

**Fasting phase: glucagon high, insulin low.** Without high insulin, glucose has difficulty entering most body cells, so glucose stops being the body's primary fuel. This is not a malfunction; it is a rationing device. Because **insulin is not required for glucose to enter most brain cells**, the effect is to *reserve the remaining glucose for the brain*. Meanwhile, low insulin promotes conversion of glycogen and protein into glucose; the conversion of **protein to glucose** is **gluconeogenesis**.

High glucagon does the complementary work. It promotes release of **free fatty acids** from adipose tissue and their use as the body's primary fasting fuel, and it stimulates conversion of free fatty acids into **ketones**, which muscles burn during fasting. After a *prolonged* period without food, the brain also starts to use ketones, conserving glucose still further.

> **High-yield.** Cephalic + absorptive = insulin high, glucagon low. Fasting = glucagon high, insulin low. Fasting fuel for muscle = free fatty acids and ketones; fasting fuel for brain = glucose first (spared because brain uptake is insulin-independent), then ketones in prolonged fasting. Gluconeogenesis = protein → glucose.

## 12.2 Two Theories of Hunger: Set Points versus Positive Incentives

### The set-point assumption

Most people attribute hunger, meaning the *motivation* to eat, to the presence of an energy deficit, and they view eating as the means by which the body's energy resources are returned to their optimal level, the **energy set point**. On this view, after a meal your energy resources sit near the set point and decline as the body burns fuel; when they fall far enough below it you become hungry and start another meal; the meal continues until the level returns to the set point, at which moment you feel **satiated**.

The near-universal analogy is the thermostat-controlled heater. The heater warms the house until the temperature reaches the thermostat setting, then shuts off; the house cools until the temperature is low enough to switch the heater back on.

### The anatomy of any set-point system

It pays to know the general architecture, because exams ask you to map it onto the thermostat.

Every set-point system has **three components**:

| Component | What it does | Heating system |
|---|---|---|
| **Set-point mechanism** | Defines the set point | Thermostat |
| **Detector mechanism** | Detects deviations from it | Thermometer |
| **Effector mechanism** | Acts to eliminate the deviations | Heater |

All set-point systems are **negative feedback systems**: feedback from a change in one direction elicits compensatory effects in the opposite direction. Negative feedback systems are common in mammals precisely because they maintain **homeostasis**, a stable internal environment, which mammalian survival requires. A set-point system is therefore *negative feedback plus a fixed target*. Hold that phrase; the settling-point model in 12.5 keeps the negative feedback and throws away the fixed target, and if you cannot state the difference in one sentence you will lose the mark.

One historical point that Pinel makes and that is easy to miss: set-point regulation *seemed logically necessary* while the adult brain was assumed to be immutable. If the brain cannot change, its fuel supply must be held rigidly constant. We now know the adult brain is plastic and capable of considerable adaptation, so that logical imperative is gone.

### Glucostatic theory

In the 1940s and 1950s, researchers who took set-point regulation for granted argued about *which* variable had the set point. One family of answers, collectively the **glucostatic theory**, proposed a **blood glucose set point**: we become hungry when blood glucose drops significantly below its set point and satiated when eating returns it to that point. The reasoning was sensible on its face, since glucose is the brain's primary fuel.

### Lipostatic theory

The rival, the **lipostatic theory**, proposed a set point for **body fat**: deviations from it produce compensatory adjustments in eating that return body fat to the set point. Its most frequently cited support was simply that adult body weights stay relatively constant.

The two theories were treated as **complementary rather than mutually exclusive**: glucostatic regulation was thought to handle *meal initiation and termination* (short-term), lipostatic regulation to handle *long-term body-weight regulation*. The dominant 1950s view was therefore of two nested set-point systems. Their simplicity is appealing, and they are still presented as established fact in popular media and even parts of the scientific literature. They are wrong.

### Three fatal problems with set-point theories

You already know one problem: **there is an epidemic of overeating**, which simply should not be possible if eating is regulated to a set point. Three further problems:

**First, they are inconsistent with eating-related evolutionary pressures.** The dominant eating problem faced by our ancestors was the *inconsistency and unpredictability of the food supply*. Under those conditions, survival favoured eating large quantities of good food whenever it was available so that calories could be banked as body fat. A warm-blooded animal, which needs a continuous energy supply just to hold its body temperature, needs a system that **prevents** energy deficits, not one that merely **responds** to them once they have developed. It is hard to see how a purely set-point feeding system could have evolved in mammals at all.

**Second, the theories' major predictions have not been confirmed.** Early work appeared supportive: starvation-induced reductions in body fat, and insulin-induced reductions in blood glucose, both increase eating in laboratory animals. The catch is that the *magnitude* of glucose reduction needed to reliably induce eating **rarely occurs naturally**. And attempts to reduce meal size by having volunteers unknowingly drink a high-calorie preload before eating have been unsuccessful. Worse for the theory, high levels of fat deposits at the time of eating are associated with **increased**, not decreased, hunger (Ribeiro et al., 2018), which is the wrong sign entirely.

**Third, they ignore taste, learning and social influence.** Pinel invites you to test this on yourself. Picture the sight, smell and taste of your favourite food. Are you starting to feel hungry? If a plate of it were in front of you now, would you eat one, or the lot? Have you never felt uncomfortably full after a main course and then finished a substantial dessert? Every honest answer to those questions is incompatible with rigid deficit control.

### The positive-incentive perspective

The failure of set-point theories to account for basic phenomena produced an alternative, associated particularly with **Berridge (2004)** and now called **positive-incentive theory**. Its central claim: humans and other animals are not normally *driven* to eat by internal energy deficits but are *drawn* to eat by the **anticipated pleasure of eating**. The anticipated pleasure of any behaviour is its **positive-incentive value**.

The theory's guiding analogy is sexual behaviour. We do not engage in sex because of an internal sexual deficit; we do it because we evolved to crave it. In the same way, the evolutionary pressure of unpredictable food shortage shaped warm-blooded animals to take advantage of good food when it is present. On this view it is the **presence of good food, or the anticipation of it, that normally makes us hungry**, not an energy deficit.

Crucially, the degree of hunger you feel at any moment depends on the *interaction of everything* that influences the positive-incentive value of eating right now: the flavour of the food you are likely to eat, what you have learned about that food from eating it before or from other people, how long it has been since you last ate, the type and quantity of food already in your gut, whether other people are present and eating, and whether your blood glucose is in the normal range. Notice the structural difference from set-point theory. Set-point theories **single out one variable** as *the* determinant of hunger and treat the rest as noise. Positive-incentive theories do not privilege any one factor. Blood glucose is on the list, but it is one item among many, and that is exactly the right status for it given the evidence in 12.4.

> **Trap.** Positive-incentive theory does **not** claim energy deficits never influence eating. Major energy deficits clearly increase hunger. The claim is that for people with ready access to food, deficits are **not the usual cause** of eating. A question that asks you whether the theory "denies any role for physiological state" is baiting you.

## 12.3 What, When, and How Much We Eat

This module collects the behavioural facts. As you go, notice which theory accounts for each one. Notice also what is *absent* from the list of determinants: energy deficits. If you have easy access to food, you may believe that your body is short of energy just before a meal. It is not.

### What we eat: species-typical preferences

Certain tastes have high positive-incentive value for virtually all members of a species. Humans have a species-typical fondness for **sweet, fatty and salty** tastes, and a species-typical aversion to **bitter** tastes. This pattern is adaptive because, in nature, sweet and fatty tastes characterise high-energy foods rich in vitamins and minerals, salty tastes characterise sodium-rich foods, and bitter tastes are frequently associated with **toxins**.

The adaptiveness is contingent on the environment those preferences evolved in. In an environment where sweet and fatty things are rare and nutritious, a preference for them is a survival advantage. In an environment where they are engineered, cheap and continuously available, the same preference is a liability. That mismatch is the whole argument of 12.6, and it starts here.

### What we eat: learned preferences and aversions

Superimposed on species-typical preferences is individual learning, running in three ways:

- Animals **learn to prefer tastes followed by an infusion of calories**, and **learn to avoid tastes followed by illness** (conditioned taste aversion).
- Animals learn what to eat **from conspecifics**. Rats learn to prefer flavours experienced in their mother's milk and flavours smelled on the breath of other rats (Galef, Whiskin, & Bielavska, 1997).
- In humans, many preferences are **culturally specific**: in some cultures various non-toxic insects are a delicacy.

### Learning to eat vitamins and minerals

How does an animal assemble a diet containing all the vitamins and minerals it needs, given that most of them are tasteless? Two distinct patterns emerge, and the distinction is examinable.

**Sodium is a special case.** An animal deficient in sodium develops an **immediate and compelling preference** for the taste of sodium salt (Jarvie & Palmiter, 2017). No learning is required; the deficiency itself makes the taste attractive.

**Everything else must be learned.** For any vitamin or mineral other than sodium, the nutrient normally has **no detectable taste** in food, so the animal must learn to consume foods rich in it by experiencing their **positive after-effects**. Rats maintained on a diet deficient in **thiamine (vitamin B1)** develop an aversion to the taste of that diet; offered two new diets, one thiamine-deficient and one thiamine-rich, they gradually come to prefer the thiamine-rich one as it becomes associated with improved health.

If we can do this, why are dietary deficiencies still common in industrialised societies? Two reasons:

1. **Manufacturers optimise for taste, not nutrition.** To maximise profit, food is produced to have the tastes we prefer while lacking nutrients we need. Even rats prefer chocolate chip cookies to nutritionally complete rat chow.
2. **The learning problem becomes unsolvable when the choice set is large.** In the classic study of **Harris and associates (1933)**, thiamine-deficient rats offered **two** new diets, one complete and one deficient, almost all learned to eat the complete one. Offered **ten** new diets, only one of which contained the badly needed thiamine, **few** developed a preference for the complete diet. The number of different substances a person in an industrialised society eats each day is immense, which makes it difficult or impossible for the body to work out which ones are doing it good.

> **High-yield.** Sodium deficiency → *immediate innate* preference. All other vitamin/mineral deficiencies → *learned* preference via post-ingestive consequences. Harris et al. (1933): 2 diets, learning succeeds; 10 diets, learning fails.

### When we eat: premeal hunger is a preparation, not a plea

Collier and colleagues (Collier, 1986) found that most mammals, given continuous access to food, choose to eat **many small meals** across the day. Most humans, particularly those living in family groups, instead eat **a few large meals at regular times**. And here is the tell: each person's regular mealtimes are the very times at which that person is most likely to feel hungry. Many people even experience **malaise**, headache, nausea and inability to concentrate, when they miss a regularly scheduled meal.

Subjectively, premeal hunger feels like the strongest possible evidence for set-point theory. Your body seems to be shouting that it needs energy. **Woods** straightened this out (Begg & Woods, 2013; Woods & Begg, 2015), and his account is the conceptual centre of the chapter.

The key is that **eating a meal stresses the body**. Before a meal, the body's energy reserves are in reasonable homeostatic balance. Then a meal is consumed and there is a **major homeostasis-disturbing influx of fuels** into the bloodstream. The body does what it can to defend homeostasis. At the first indication that food is coming, for example when the usual mealtime approaches, the body enters the **cephalic phase** and takes steps to soften the impact of the coming influx, principally by releasing insulin and thereby **lowering blood glucose**.

So the unpleasant sensations you call mealtime hunger are not cries from the body for food. They are the **sensations of your body preparing** for a meal it expects. **Mealtime hunger is caused by the expectation of food, not by an energy deficit.** Pinel gives the personal illustration: as a high-school student he ate lunch at exactly 12:05 and was overwhelmed by hunger as that time approached; with a different schedule now, he never feels noontime hunger, but gets hungry just before whatever time he now usually eats.

This inverts the naive reading of the physiology. The premeal fall in blood glucose is not the *cause* of eating. It is a *consequence* of the intention to eat.

### When we eat: Pavlovian conditioning of hunger

If expectation causes hunger, then hunger should be conditionable. **Weingarten (1983, 1984, 1985)** showed exactly that in a classic series.

> **Key study, Weingarten (1983).** In the conditioning phase, rats received **six meals a day at irregular intervals**, each preceded by a **buzzer-and-light conditional stimulus**, for **11 days**. In the test phase, food was **continuously available** and the rats were **never deprived**. Nevertheless, they **began to eat each time the buzzer and light were presented**, even if they had *just finished a meal*.

Read the design carefully, because the controls are what make it decisive. Irregular intervals during conditioning rule out simple time-of-day anticipation. Continuous availability during testing rules out deprivation. Eating immediately after a completed meal rules out any residual deficit. What is left is a learned signal producing hunger in a sated animal. That is positive-incentive theory's prediction and set-point theory has no account of it.

### How much we eat: satiety signals and nutritive density

**Satiety** is the motivational state that causes us to stop eating a meal while food is still available. Satiety mechanisms, not energy repletion, are what determine meal size.

Food in the gut and glucose entering the blood induce **satiety signals** that inhibit further consumption, and these signals depend on both the **volume** and the **nutritive density** (calories per unit volume) of the food.

The limits of this adjustment are instructive. Rats held on a single diet at a stable baseline, then switched to a diet of different nutritive density, *partly* compensate: some eventually learn to adjust the volume they eat so that caloric intake and body weight stay roughly stable. But the compensation breaks down at the extremes. Rats rarely increase intake enough to maintain body weight if nutritive density is cut by **more than 50 percent**, or if the diet's **palatability** changes substantially. Compensation is real but weak and easily overridden by taste, which is precisely the pattern positive-incentive theory predicts and set-point theory does not.

### How much we eat: sham eating

**Sham eating** is the cleanest experimental test of whether gut or blood satiety signals are *necessary* to end a meal. In a sham-eating preparation the animal chews and swallows normally, but the food, instead of passing down the oesophagus into the stomach, passes out of the body through an implanted tube. No energy is added to the body at all.

Set-point theory makes an unambiguous prediction here: since sham eating never repletes the reserves, **every sham meal should be enormous**. It is not.

> **Key study, sham eating.** A rat's **first** sham meal of its usual diet is usually **the same size as its previous normal meals**. Only **after the first few sham meals** do rats begin to sham-eat larger meals.

Unpack the logic, because this is the sort of reasoning MET-level papers reward. If the meal ends at normal size on the first sham trial, then meal termination on that trial cannot have been caused by nutrients arriving, because none did. It must have been caused by something already in place before the meal, namely the animal's **previous experience** of how much of this food produces satiety. Satiety, on the first trial, is a *learned expectation*. The later growth of sham meals is that expectation being updated once the food stops delivering its usual consequences.

### How much we eat: the appetizer effect, serving size, and social facilitation

Three further determinants of meal size, all of them awkward for set-point theory:

- **The appetizer effect.** Small amounts of food consumed *before* a meal **increase** hunger rather than reducing it. On a deficit account this is backwards: adding calories should reduce the deficit and reduce hunger. On a positive-incentive account it is expected, because small amounts of food are particularly effective at eliciting **cephalic-phase responses**, and cephalic-phase responses are what mealtime hunger *is*.
- **Serving size.** The larger the serving, the more we eat (Hollands et al., 2015). Meal size is partly set by the plate.
- **Social influence.** People consume **more** when eating with others than when eating alone. Laboratory rats do the same.

### How much we eat: sensory-specific satiety

The number of different tastes available at a meal has a major effect on how much is eaten, and the effect is dramatic.

> **Key study, cafeteria diet (Rogers & Blundell, 1980).** Adult rats offered **bread and chocolate in addition to their usual laboratory diet** increased average caloric intake by **84 percent** and, after **120 days**, had increased average body weight by **49 percent**.

Nothing about those rats' energy requirements changed. Only the variety of available tastes changed. A system rigidly governed by internal energy set points cannot produce a 49 percent weight gain in response to being offered bread.

The mechanism is **sensory-specific satiety**. As you eat one food, the positive-incentive value of *all* foods declines slightly, but the positive-incentive value of *that particular food* plummets. You become satiated on that food and stop eating it. Offer a different food and you will often start eating again.

> **Key study, Rolls et al. (1981).** Volunteers rated the palatability of **eight** foods, ate a meal of **one** of them, then re-rated all eight. The rating of the food just eaten had dropped **substantially more** than the ratings of the other seven. Offered an unexpected second meal, they ate most of it **unless it was the same food as the first**.

**Booth (1981)** added the time course. He asked volunteers to rate the momentary pleasure produced by the flavour, smell, sight or even the thought of various foods at different intervals after a large, high-calorie, high-carbohydrate liquid meal. There was an **immediate** sensory-specific decline in the palatability of foods of the *same or similar* flavour, followed about **30 minutes later** by a **general** decline in the palatability of all substances. The interpretation is a two-signal system: signals from **taste receptors** produce an immediate fall in the incentive value of *similar* tastes, while signals associated with the **post-ingestive consequences** of eating produce a later, *general* fall in the incentive value of all foods.

**Rolls (1990)** distinguished two timescales of effect: relatively **brief** effects that govern food selection *within* a single meal, and relatively **enduring** effects that govern selection *from meal to meal*. Some foods are relatively immune to the long-lasting form; rice, bread, potatoes, sweets and green salads can be eaten almost daily with only slight decline in palatability. That is not a trivial aside, it is why staples are staples.

**Two adaptive consequences**, both worth stating in an answer:

1. It **encourages a varied diet**. Without sensory-specific satiety, an animal would eat its single preferred food and nothing else, and the result would be malnutrition.
2. It **encourages animals with access to variety to eat a lot**. An animal that has eaten its fill of one food will start again on a different one, which lets it exploit times of abundance, and in nature abundance is rare.

Note the sting in the second consequence. The mechanism that protects a wild animal from malnutrition is the same mechanism that makes a supermarket, a buffet or a food court so effective at making you overeat. The adaptation has not changed; the environment has.

## 12.4 Physiological Research on Hunger and Satiety

Six lines of physiological research, each of which began by looking for the machinery of a set point and each of which ended up telling a different story.

### Blood glucose: the premeal dip and what it really means

Efforts to link blood glucose levels to eating were largely unsuccessful, but interest revived in the 1990s once continuous blood glucose monitoring became possible (Grayson, Seeley, & Sandoval, 2013).

> **Key study, Campfield & Smith (1995).** Rats with free access to food and water had blood glucose monitored continuously. Baseline levels rarely fluctuated by more than **2 percent**. But about **10 minutes before a meal was initiated**, levels dropped quickly by about **8 percent**.

At first glance that is glucostatic theory vindicated: glucose falls, then eating begins. Four observations say the causal arrow points the other way, that **the intention to start eating triggers the decline in blood glucose**:

1. **The time course is wrong.** The decline occurs *suddenly, just before eating begins*. A gradual depletion of the body's energy would produce a gradual decline, not a step change ten minutes out.
2. **Eliminating the premeal drop does not eliminate the meal.**
3. **If an expected meal is not served, blood glucose soon returns to its previous level.** A genuine deficit would not resolve itself because lunch was cancelled.
4. **Glucose levels in the extracellular fluid surrounding CNS neurons stay relatively constant** even when general circulating blood glucose drops (Seeley & Woods, 2003). The neurons that would have to *read* the signal never see it.

This is the single best worked example in the chapter of the general lesson: a correlation between a physiological variable and eating is not evidence that the variable *causes* eating. The premeal glucose dip is a **cephalic-phase response**, part of the body's preparation for an expected meal, exactly as Woods's account requires.

### The myth of hypothalamic hunger and satiety centres

In the 1950s, lesion experiments on rats seemed to show that eating is controlled by two hypothalamic regions: **satiety** by the **ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH)** and **feeding** by the **lateral hypothalamus (LH)**. The theory was wrong, but it stimulated a great deal of important work, and both syndromes remain examinable in their own right.

> **Key study, Hetherington & Ranson (1940).** Large bilateral **electrolytic lesions of the VMH** produce **hyperphagia** (excessive eating) and extreme obesity in rats.

This **VMH syndrome** has **two phases**:

| Phase | Onset | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Dynamic phase** | Begins as soon as the rat regains consciousness after surgery | Several weeks of grossly excessive eating and rapid weight gain |
| **Static phase** | Follows the dynamic phase | Consumption declines to just enough to **maintain the new weight** |

The most important feature of the static phase is that the animal **maintains and defends its new body weight**. Deprive a static-phase rat of food until it loses substantial weight and it regains the loss once deprivation ends; force-feed it to gain more and it loses the excess once force-feeding stops. In other words, the lesion appears to have *raised the set point* rather than abolished regulation, which is why the finding looked like such strong support for set-point thinking.

> **Key study, Anand & Brobeck (1951).** Bilateral electrolytic **LH lesions** produce **aphagia**, a complete cessation of eating that ultimately leads to death. Even rats first made hyperphagic by VMH lesions were rendered aphagic by adding LH lesions.

> **Key study, Teitelbaum & Epstein (1962).** Two further features of the **LH syndrome**: the aphagia is accompanied by **adipsia**, complete cessation of drinking; and LH-lesioned rats **partially recover** if kept alive by tube feeding. After tube feeding ceases they begin to eat wet, palatable foods, such as chocolate chip cookies soaked in milk, and eventually dry pellets, provided water is concurrently available.

### Why the VMH is not a satiety centre

Two lines of evidence dismantled the satiety-centre interpretation.

**First, the causal order is reversed. VMH-lesioned rats do not become obese because they overeat; they overeat because they are becoming obese.** Bilateral VMH lesions **increase blood insulin**, which in turn **increases lipogenesis** (production of body fat) and **decreases lipolysis** (breakdown of body fat into usable energy). Calories ingested by these rats are converted into fat at such a high rate that the rats must keep eating simply to keep enough calories circulating to meet immediate energy needs. The animal is, in effect, starving in the bloodstream while growing fat in the tissue. This is the single most important reinterpretation in the section and a perennial MCQ item.

**Second, many effects of VMH lesions are not attributable to VMH damage at all.** A large bundle of axons projecting from the nearby **paraventricular nuclei** courses past the VMH and is inevitably destroyed by a large electrolytic lesion. Bilateral lesions of **that fibre bundle**, or of the **paraventricular nuclei themselves**, produce hyperphagia and weight gain just as VMH lesions do. The lesion was never anatomically specific.

### Why the LH is not a hunger centre

Most of the evidence against the LH-as-hunger-centre idea came from looking beyond eating. Early research attended exclusively to the aphagia and adipsia. Later work showed that LH lesions also produce **a wide range of severe motor disturbances** and **a general lack of responsiveness to sensory input**, of which food and drink are only two examples. An animal that responds to nothing will not eat, but that does not make the structure a feeding centre. The idea that the LH is dedicated specifically to hunger no longer warrants serious consideration.

> **Trap.** The exam will not usually ask "is the VMH a satiety centre?". It will ask *why* the theory failed, and the two answers are different for the two structures: for the **VMH**, the lesion's effect is **metabolic** (insulin → lipogenesis) and **anatomically non-specific** (paraventricular fibres); for the **LH**, the deficit is **not specific to feeding** (general sensorimotor unresponsiveness). Do not blur them together.

### Modern hypothalamic research: the arcuate nucleus

The failure of the centres model did not mean the hypothalamus is irrelevant. Recent evidence shows that distinct **cell populations** within it influence hunger and satiety. Certain neurons in the **paraventricular nucleus** act as **nutrient sensors** capable of influencing feeding and satiety (Lagerlöf et al., 2016; Schwartz, 2016), and several distinct neuronal populations within the **arcuate nucleus** influence the metabolism of consumed food. The arcuate nucleus in particular appears to be **the centre of a neural network that interacts with receptors in the blood and gut** (Clemmensen et al., 2017).

The shift is from *centres* to *circuits*: not one nucleus for hunger and another for satiety, but populations of chemically defined neurons embedded in a two-way brain-gut network.

### The gastrointestinal tract and satiety: Cannon and Washburn

> **Key study, Cannon & Washburn (1912).** A famous collaboration: Cannon had the ideas, Washburn had the ability to swallow a balloon. Washburn swallowed an empty balloon attached to a thin tube; Cannon pumped air into it and connected the tube to a water-filled glass U-tube, so that Washburn's **stomach contractions** produced a momentary rise in the water level at the far end. Washburn reported a **"pang" of hunger each time a large stomach contraction was recorded**.

This produced the theory that **hunger is the feeling of contractions of an empty stomach and satiety the feeling of stomach distension**. Support collapsed with a clinical observation: patients whose **stomachs had been surgically removed**, with the oesophagus connected directly to the **duodenum**, continued to report normal feelings of hunger and satiety and maintained normal body weight by eating **more meals of smaller size**. No stomach, still hunger. Whatever the gut contributes, it is not contractions.

### Koopmans's transplanted stomach

Interest in the gut revived in the 1980s with an experiment whose design is worth learning in full, because the conclusion is forced by the design rather than by statistics.

> **Key study, Koopmans (1981).** An **extra stomach and length of intestine** were transplanted into rats, with the major arteries and veins of the implant joined to the recipient's circulatory system. Food injected into the **transplanted** stomach, and held there by a noose tightened around its pyloric sphincter, **decreased eating in proportion to both its caloric content and its volume**.

Now reason through what that rules out:

- The transplanted stomach had **no functional nerves**. Therefore the satiety signal reaching the brain **cannot be neural**; it must travel in the **blood**.
- **Nutrients are not absorbed from the stomach**. Therefore the bloodborne signal **cannot be an absorbed nutrient** such as glucose.
- The signal was graded by **both calories and volume**, so whatever it is must be sensitive to both.

What remains is a **chemical released from the stomach in proportion to the caloric value and volume of the food it contains**. That inference is what sent the field looking for peptides.

### Hunger and satiety peptides

**Peptides** are short chains of amino acids that can act as hormones and as neurotransmitters. Ingested food interacts with receptors in the gastrointestinal tract, causing the tract to release peptides into the bloodstream.

> **Key study, Gibbs, Young, & Smith (1973).** Injecting the gut peptide **cholecystokinin (CCK)** into hungry rats caused them to eat **smaller meals**, supporting the hypothesis that circulating gut peptides inform the brain about the quantity and nature of food in the gut and thereby contribute to satiety.

Many gut peptides bind to receptors in the brain, especially in hypothalamic areas involved in energy metabolism such as the **arcuate nucleus**, and several of them produce metabolic changes that reduce intake. These are the **satiety peptides**, peptides that decrease appetite: **CCK, bombesin, glucagon, somatostatin**, and the melanocortin **alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone**.

There is an important methodological caveat here that examiners like. Any drug that makes an animal ill will reduce its eating, so an appetite-reducing effect is not by itself evidence of a satiety signal. **CCK does induce illness**: given to rats after they eat an unfamiliar substance it produces a **conditioned taste aversion** to that substance, and it induces **nausea** in humans. CCK survives as a legitimate satiety peptide only because it **reduces appetite and eating at doses substantially below** those needed to produce taste aversion. That dose-separation argument is the reason CCK counts and, by implication, the test any candidate satiety peptide must pass.

**Hunger peptides**, peptides that increase appetite, tend to be synthesised **in the brain**, particularly the hypothalamus, and produce metabolic effects that increase eating. The most widely studied are **neuropeptide Y, galanin, orexin-A, and ghrelin**.

Clemmensen and colleagues (2017) argue that this line of work has been unusually productive, and Pinel agrees with **one substantial qualification**. On the positive side, three contributions:

1. The sheer **number** of hunger and satiety peptides shows that the neural system controlling eating reacts to **many different signals**, not to one or two variables such as glucose and fat. That is a direct blow to any single-variable set-point theory.
2. The discovery that many of these peptides have **hypothalamic receptors** led to the mapping of numerous hypothalamic circuits involved in food metabolism.
3. It produced **an entirely different view of the gut**: not a sack that holds food, but an organ of analysis and communication, in many respects brain-like, engaged in **two-way communication with the brain** about eating, digestion and energy regulation.

**The qualification, and you should be able to state it.** Nearly all of this work has been about **food metabolism**, not about eating. Where eating effects were observed, they generally looked like **secondary consequences of metabolic changes**. We still do not understand how these hypothalamic circuits feed into the circuits that **directly control eating behaviour**. So the field has a great deal of information about the neural and gut mechanisms of metabolism and very little about the chapter's actual subject, the control of eating. This is a live limitation, not a settled story, and saying so is a mark of a good answer.

### Serotonin and satiety

The monoamine neurotransmitter **serotonin** also plays a role in metabolism and eating. The initial evidence came from rat satiety studies, in which serotonin-produced satiety showed **three characteristic properties** (Blundell & Halford, 1998):

1. It caused rats to **resist the powerful attraction of highly palatable cafeteria diets**.
2. It reduced the **amount of food consumed per meal** rather than the **number of meals** (Clifton, 2000).
3. It was associated with a **shift in food preference away from fatty foods**.

That profile suggested serotonin might be useful against human obesity, and serotonin agonists (**fenfluramine, dexfenfluramine, fluoxetine**) do reduce hunger and eating in some people who overeat (Voigt & Fink, 2015). What happened when those drugs reached the clinic is dealt with in 12.6.

> **High-yield.** Serotonin reduces meal **size**, not meal **frequency**, and shifts preference **away from fat**. Both details are commonly tested and commonly misremembered as the reverse.

### Prader-Willi syndrome

**Prader-Willi syndrome** is the closest thing to a natural experiment on satiety, and it may prove critical in identifying the neural mechanisms of hunger (Tauber et al., 2014).

**Aetiology.** It results from an accident of chromosomal replication that **deletes or disrupts a section of chromosome 15 contributed by the father**. The paternal origin is examinable; do not write "chromosome 15" alone.

**Core triad.** Individuals experience **insatiable hunger**, **little or no satiety**, and an **exceptionally slow metabolism**. In short, the patient behaves as though permanently starving.

**Other common physical and neurological features.** Weak muscles (**hypotonia**), small hands and feet, feeding difficulties in infancy, tantrums, compulsivity, skin picking, and frequently intellectual disability and psychiatric problems.

**Prognosis if untreated.** Most patients accumulate an enormous amount of body fat and often die in early adulthood of diabetes, heart disease or other consequences of increased fat mass. Some have died from **gorging until the stomach ruptured**.

> **Case of Miss A. (Martin et al., 1998).** Born with little muscle tone and a sucking reflex so weak she had to be tube fed. By age 2 the hypotonia had resolved, but characteristic deformities and developmental delays appeared. At **3½ years** she suddenly developed a **voracious appetite** and gained weight rapidly. Her family kept her on a low-calorie diet and **kept all food locked away**. She is moderately intellectually disabled with psychiatric problems, her main difficulty being **tantrums whenever anything in her environment changes**, such as a substitute teacher at school. Excellent family and paediatric care minimised the complications related to fat accumulation.

Note what the treatment actually is: environmental control of access to food, because the satiety mechanism cannot be repaired. That is a clinically important point, and it is also a reminder that "the patient acts as if starving" is a description of the *behaviour*, not of the energy state.

## 12.5 Body-Weight Regulation: Set Points versus Settling Points

Set-point theories have one apparent strength left. Even granted that they explain *eating* badly, they seem to explain the *stability of body weight*. Many people believe that when fat deposits fall below their set point they get hungrier and eat more, returning body fat to the set point, and that when fat rises above it they get less hungry and eat less. This module asks whether that survives contact with the evidence. It does not.

### Variability of body weight

A genuine set-point mechanism should make it **virtually impossible** for an adult to gain or lose large amounts of weight. Yet many adults experience large and lasting changes in body weight, and set-point thinking simply cannot survive the epidemic of overeating sweeping fast-food societies.

There is a second, subtler argument here. Set-point theory implies that the best way to maintain constant body weight is to **eat every time you are motivated to eat**, since on that theory the function of hunger is to defend the set point. In reality, many people avoid overeating **only by resisting their urges to eat**. A regulatory system you must consciously override is not regulating.

### Set points and health: the calorie-restriction evidence

A further implication of set-point theory is that each person's set point is optimal for that person's health, or at least compatible with it. This is why pop psychologists advise people to "listen to the wisdom of the body" and eat as much as they need to satisfy their hunger. The experimental results indicate this advice could hardly be more wrong. Three kinds of evidence show that typical **ad libitum** (free-feeding) levels of consumption are unhealthy.

**First, non-experimental human studies.**

> **Okinawa (Kagawa, 1978).** Adult Okinawans consumed on average **20 percent fewer calories** than other adult Japanese, and Okinawan schoolchildren **38 percent fewer** than public-health recommendations, to the point where officials were concerned. Yet rates of mortality and of all ageing-related diseases were substantially **lower** than in the rest of Japan, itself a country whose caloric intake and obesity rates are far below Western norms. Death rates from **stroke, cancer and heart disease** were only **59, 69 and 59 percent** respectively of the rest of Japan, and the proportion of Okinawans living past **100** was up to **four times** that of the United States.

Interpret this cautiously, and say so if asked: the Okinawan data are **not controlled**. Perhaps it is not the calorie count but the fact that people who eat less also eat better.

**Second, controlled calorie-restriction experiments** in more than a dozen mammalian species including monkeys and humans (Colman et al., 2014; Mattison et al., 2017; Ravussin et al., 2015). In the standard design, one group eats freely while other groups have caloric intake of the *same* diet reduced by between 25 and 65 percent. The results are remarkably consistent: substantial reductions in the caloric intake of **balanced** diets improve numerous indices of health and increase longevity.

> **Weindruch et al. (1996).** Mice had caloric intake of a well-balanced commercial diet reduced after weaning by **25, 55 or 65 percent**. **All** levels of restriction substantially improved health and increased longevity, and the **benefits were greatest in the most restricted group**: those mice had the lowest incidence of cancer, the best immune responses, and the greatest maximum lifespan, living **67 percent longer** than free-feeding mice.

Dietary restriction has beneficial effects **even when it is not begun until later in life** (Mair et al., 2003; Vaupel, Carey, & Christensen, 2003), and the shorter human trials, around two years, support the animal findings (Ravussin et al., 2015).

**Third, and clinically striking**, dietary restriction can be used to treat some neurological conditions: caloric restriction **reduces seizure susceptibility** in humans with epilepsy (Maalouf, Rho, & Mattson, 2008) and **improves memory in the elderly** (Witte et al., 2009).

The consequence for the theory is direct. If free-feeding levels of intake are unhealthy, then whatever level your hunger defends is not the level that is best for you, and the set point cannot be doing the job the theory assigns to it.

### Regulation by changes in the efficiency of energy utilisation

How much you eat obviously matters to your body weight. But the body also controls its fat levels to a large degree by **changing the efficiency with which it uses energy**, and this is the part most people never account for.

As body fat **declines**, the person begins to use energy resources **more efficiently**, which limits further loss (Tremblay et al., 2013). Conversely, weight **gain** is limited by a progressive **decrease** in efficiency.

> **Rothwell & Stock (1982).** Rats fattened on a cafeteria diet showed a **resting level of energy expenditure 45 percent greater** than controls. The extra intake was partly burned off rather than stored.

The mechanism has a name, **diet-induced thermogenesis**: increases in body fat produce increases in body temperature, which require additional energy to maintain, and decreases in body fat have the opposite effect (Jun et al., 2014).

Humans differ markedly both in **basal metabolic rate**, the rate at which energy is used to maintain bodily processes at rest, and in the ability to adjust metabolic rate in response to changes in body fat. Everybody knows somebody who stays slim while eating enormously. The calorie-restriction literature, however, suggests these people do not eat with impunity: there appears to be a **health cost to overeating even in the absence of significant weight gain** (Colman et al., 2014; Mattison et al., 2017).

The most familiar consequence of variable efficiency is the **diet plateau**. A low-calorie diet initially produces substantial weight loss, but the rate of loss diminishes each successive week until an equilibrium is reached and little or no further loss occurs. A mirror-image effect occurs with weight-gain programmes. Every dieter knows the phenomenon; the point is that it needs no set point to explain it.

### The settling-point model and the leaky barrel

Several prominent reviews now accept that a strict set-point model cannot account for the facts of weight regulation and argue for something more flexible (Berthoud, 2002; Mercer & Speakman, 2001; Woods et al., 2000). The leading alternative is the **settling-point model**.

According to it, body weight **drifts around a natural settling point**, the level at which the various factors influencing body weight reach **equilibrium**. As body-fat levels rise, changes occur that tend to limit further rises, until a balance is struck between everything encouraging weight gain and everything discouraging it.

The model provides a **loose kind of homeostatic regulation without a set-point mechanism and without any mechanism for returning weight to a target**. Body weight stays stable as long as the factors influencing it do not change in the long term; when they do change, the impact is limited by negative feedback.

State the difference precisely, because this is the examinable core of the module:

| | **Set-point model** | **Settling-point model** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Set point + detector + effector | Equilibrium of interacting factors |
| Role of negative feedback | **Triggers a return** to the set point | **Merely limits further change** in the same direction |
| Is the level predetermined? | Yes, and actively defended | No, and not defended |
| Response to a lasting change in conditions | Weight returns to the old level | Weight **drifts to a new settling point** |
| Analogy | Thermostat | Leaky barrel |

Both models contain negative feedback. Only one contains a **target**. If you can say that sentence, you can answer any question in this module.

A useful cross-reference Pinel gives: a neuron's **resting potential** is another well-known biological settling point. It is stable, it is restored after disturbance, and yet nothing in the neuron stores the value −70 mV; it is simply where the ionic forces balance.

**The leaky-barrel analogy**, mapped term by term. Water flows from a hose into a barrel that leaks, and the barrel rests on the hose:

| Element of the model | What it represents |
|---|---|
| Amount of water entering the hose | Amount of **food available** |
| Water pressure at the nozzle | **Positive-incentive value** of the available food |
| Amount of water entering the barrel | Amount of **energy consumed** |
| Water level in the barrel | Level of **body fat** |
| Amount of water leaking from the barrel | Amount of **energy expended** |
| Weight of the barrel pressing on the hose | Strength of the **satiety signal** |

Learn all six. Pinel notes that instructors differ on whether the analogy is examinable; in a paper that likes applied reasoning, assume it is.

### Four facts of weight regulation, explained twice

The settling-point model's advantage is that it fits the data, and where both models predict the same outcome it does so **more parsimoniously**, with a simpler mechanism and fewer assumptions. Work through the four classic facts.

**1. Body weight remains relatively constant in many adults.** This is the observation always cited as proving a set point. It does not. In the leaky barrel, as water enters, the weight of water in the barrel increases; that increases leakage *and* decreases inflow by pressing harder on the hose. The system settles into equilibrium and the level stays constant. But that level was **neither predetermined nor actively defended**. It is a settling point.

**2. Many adults experience enduring changes in body weight.** Set-point systems exist precisely to maintain internal constancy against external fluctuation, so long-term weight change is a strong argument *against* them. The settling-point model predicts it directly: change one of the parameters durably, for instance a major increase in the positive-incentive value of available food, and weight **drifts to a new settling point**.

**3. When intake is reduced, metabolic changes limit the loss; when a person overeats, the opposite occurs.** Cited as evidence of set-point regulation, but note what these changes actually do: they **limit further change**, they do not **eliminate the change that has occurred**. That is the settling-point signature. In the barrel: reduce inflow and the level starts to fall, but the fall is limited because falling water pressure means less leakage and slightly more inflow. A new settling point is reached, and the drop is smaller than you would naively expect.

**4. After substantial weight loss, weight is regained once the person returns to their previous lifestyle.** Offered as irrefutable proof of a body-weight set point. The barrel handles it without difficulty. Reduce the level temporarily, whether by decreasing input (dieting), increasing output (exercise), or scooping water out (surgical removal of fat), and you produce only a **temporary drop in the settling point**. Restore the original conditions and the level inexorably drifts back to the original settling point. No memory of a target is required, only unchanged parameters.

### Why the distinction matters

Is this hair-splitting? No, for two reasons.

For the scientist, understanding weight as settling-point regulated allows better prediction of what weight will do in a given situation and points to the kinds of physiological mechanisms likely to mediate the change.

For you, and for a patient, the difference is practical and large. **If the set-point model were correct, trying to change your body weight would be futile**; you would inevitably be dragged back to your set point. The leaky-barrel model says something different and more hopeful: it is possible to **permanently change body weight by permanently changing any of the factors that influence energy intake or output**. Not by dieting temporarily, which changes a parameter temporarily, but by changing the parameters and leaving them changed. That is the theoretical justification for the clinical slogan in the next module: the key to permanent weight loss is a permanent lifestyle change.

## 12.6 Human Overeating: Causes, Mechanisms, and Treatments

The chapter opened with the epidemic and its costs. Having replaced set points with settling points and deficits with incentives, we can now say something coherent about why the epidemic exists and why most of what we do about it fails.

Some scale first. The average American consumes about **3,800 calories per day**, roughly **twice** the average daily requirement. A 2016 meta-analysis reported that, for the first time in recorded history, **overweight individuals outnumbered underweight individuals** (Di Angelantonio et al., 2016). An estimated **400,000 US citizens die unnecessarily each year** from disorders caused by excessive eating (Masters et al., 2013), and many other countries are not far behind. At the same time, and not coincidentally, disorders of eating too little have increased: about **1.2 percent of Americans** will suffer anorexia or bulimia at some point in their lives (Swanson et al., 2011).

### Who needs to be concerned

Essentially everyone. If you currently overeat, the relation between accumulated body fat and poor health is repeatedly documented (Flegal et al., 2013; Oestrich & Moley, 2017; Simonds & Cowley, 2013). Even people with high body fat who are **metabolically healthy** run a greater risk of developing health problems. The risk extends beyond the individual: women with high levels of body fat are at increased risk of having infants with health problems (Bell, 2017; Zeltser, 2018). And even if you overeat while remaining slim, there is cause for concern, both because many people who are slim in youth accumulate significant fat deposits with age, and because of the calorie-restriction evidence in 12.5 that overconsumption itself carries a health cost independent of weight gain.

### Why there is an epidemic: the evolutionary argument

Start with the selection pressures that shaped the system (Genné-Bacon, 2014). Through most of evolution, **inconsistent food supply** was among the main threats to survival. Under that pressure the fittest individuals were those who:

- **preferred high-calorie foods**,
- **ate to capacity whenever food was available**,
- **stored as many excess calories as possible as body fat**, and
- **used their stored calories as efficiently as possible**.

Individuals without those characteristics were unlikely to survive a food shortage or a harsh winter, so those characteristics were passed on.

Culture then amplified evolution rather than correcting it. Many cultures hold that one should eat **several meals a day at set times whether hungry or not**; that **food should be the focus of most social gatherings**; that **meals should be served in courses of progressively increasing palatability**; and that **salt, sugar and fats should be added to food** to improve flavour and therefore increase consumption. Parents' tendency to make unhealthy food choices is passed to their children (Campbell et al., 2007).

The conclusion writes itself. Each of us possesses an eating and weight-regulation system that evolved to deal with **periodic food shortage**, and many of us live in cultures whose eating practices evolved for the same purpose, but our actual environment offers **an endless variety of foods of the highest positive-incentive and caloric value, readily and continuously available**. The consequence is an appallingly high level of consumption. Note that this is a positive-incentive explanation from beginning to end: nothing about our energy needs changed, only the incentive value and availability of what is on offer.

### Why some people gain weight from overeating and others do not

Many factors lead some people to eat more than others with comparable access to food: **stronger preferences for the taste of high-calorie foods** (Blundell & Finlayson, 2004; Epstein et al., 2007), being **raised in families or cultures that promote excessive eating**, and having **particularly large cephalic-phase responses** to the sight or smell of food (Rodin, 1985).

But the sharper question is why some people who overeat accumulate fat while others do not. Superficially the answer is trivial, those with high body fat are those whose energy intake exceeded their energy output, but framing it that way is useful because it splits the problem into **differences in energy input** and **differences in energy output**, and then you can list the real mechanisms.

**Differences in energy expenditure.** People differ markedly in their ability to dissipate excess consumed energy. The obvious difference is the **amount of exercise** they get, but there are three more:

1. **Basal metabolic rate.**
2. **Diet-induced thermogenesis**, the ability to react to fat increases by raising energy expenditure.
3. **Non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT)**, energy expended in activities such as **fidgeting** and the maintenance of **posture and muscle tone**. NEAT plays a small role in dissipating excess energy (Villablanca et al., 2015).

**Differences in gut microbiome composition.** Our gastrointestinal tract is full of microbes that help digest food, collectively the **gut microbiome**, and they are numerous enough to outnumber our own cells by about **10 to 1**. Microbes can influence brain and behaviour in surprising ways, including neurodevelopment, the blood-brain barrier, and even myelination of some CNS axons. Whether the microbiome protects against or predisposes to obesity is now a live question.

> **Key study, Ridaura et al. (2013).** Mice raised **germ-free** were colonised with faecal microbiota from **human twin pairs discordant for body fat** (one twin with high body fat, the co-twin lean). Mice colonised with microbes from the **high-body-fat** twins **gained more weight and had greater body fat** than mice colonised with microbes from the lean co-twins.

Read the design: because the mice were germ-free and genetically comparable, and the donors were twins, the microbiome is doing the work rather than host genes or diet. This is one of the strongest demonstrations in the chapter that a factor outside the classic hunger circuitry can change fat accumulation.

**Genetic and epigenetic factors.** Given the number of factors influencing consumption and metabolism, many genes influence body weight: about **100 human chromosome loci** have already been linked to it (Locke et al., 2015). Some of them appear to act **by affecting the gut microbiome**. Untangling the interactions among genetic factors in healthy people is proving difficult, but **single-gene mutations** have been linked to pathological conditions involving excessive weight gain, and you will meet one shortly in the leptin section. There is also evidence that **transgenerational epigenetic effects** can predispose later generations to increased body-fat levels (Lopomo, Burgio, & Migliore, 2016; Rohde et al., 2019).

### Why weight-loss programmes usually fail

Most weight-loss programmes fail in a specific sense: **most of the lost weight is regained** once the dieter stops following the programme and the original conditions are re-established. That is exactly what the settling-point model predicts, and it is worth walking through the standard five-stage course of a diet, because the stages *are* the model in action:

1. **Weight loss occurs rapidly at the beginning of the diet.**
2. **As weight declines, energy "leakage" is automatically reduced**, which slows the rate of loss.
3. **The reduced rate of intake is gradually matched by the reduced energy output, and a new stable settling point is reached.** This is the plateau.
4. **When the diet is terminated, weight gain is rapid**, because the incentive value of food is high and the level of energy leakage is low.
5. **As weight accumulates, the incentive value of food gradually decreases and energy leakage increases, until the original settling point is regained.**

The key to permanent weight loss is therefore a **permanent lifestyle change**, which is easier said than done (Nymo et al., 2018). A temporary parameter change produces a temporary settling point.

**A word about exercise,** because the popular belief is wrong in a way worth being precise about. Exercise has many health-promoting effects, but several studies show it contributes **less to weight loss than is generally believed** (Dhurandhar et al., 2015; Riou et al., 2015). Three reasons:

- Physical exercise normally accounts for only a **small proportion of total energy expenditure**; most of what you expend goes on maintaining resting physiological processes such as body temperature and brain function, and on **digesting your food**.
- Our bodies are **efficient machines**, burning a fairly small number of calories in a typical workout.
- After exercising, many people **consume extra food and drink containing more calories than the workout expended** (Freedman, 2011).

None of that is an argument against exercising. It is an argument against treating exercise as a weight-loss technology.

### Leptin: the discovery

Fat is not a passive storehouse. It actively releases a peptide hormone, **leptin**, and the story of leptin is the best-documented case in the chapter of a beautiful animal result that did not transfer to humans.

> **The ob/ob mouse.** In 1950 a spontaneous genetic mutation appeared in the mouse colony at the Jackson Laboratory in Bar Harbor, Maine. Mice **homozygous for the ob gene** had very high body fat, weighing up to **three times** as much as typical mice. **Ob/ob mice** eat more than controls, **convert calories to fat more efficiently**, and **use their calories more efficiently**. **Coleman (1979)** hypothesised that they lack a critical hormone that normally inhibits fat production and maintenance.

> **Friedman and colleagues (1994)** characterised and cloned the gene mutated in ob/ob mice. It is **expressed only in fat cells**, and the peptide hormone it normally encodes was named **leptin**. Ob/ob mice lack leptin because of the mutation.

The hypothesis this generated was irresistible: leptin is a **negative feedback signal released from fat stores to decrease appetite and increase fat metabolism**. If so, could it be given to humans who overeat?

### Leptin, insulin, and the arcuate melanocortin system

Leptin arrived with great fanfare, but it was **not the first** peptide hormone proposed as a negative feedback signal for body fat. More than forty years ago **Woods and colleagues (1979)** suggested that the pancreatic peptide hormone **insulin** does this job.

The suggestion met scepticism for two good reasons: insulin **rises and falls with every meal**, so how could it inform the brain about *gradually changing* fat levels, and insulin **does not readily cross the blood-brain barrier**. Four findings nevertheless supported it:

- **Insulin receptors were found in the brain** (Baura et al., 1993).
- **Brain insulin levels correlate positively with body-fat levels** (Seeley et al., 1996).
- **Genetically modified mice with lower brain insulin have higher body fat** (Heni et al., 2015).
- **Humans with high body fat have lower brain insulin** (Heni et al., 2015).

**Why two fat signals?** One plausible answer: **leptin levels correlate more closely with subcutaneous fat**, stored under the skin, whereas **insulin levels correlate more closely with visceral fat**, stored around the internal organs of the body cavity (Heni et al., 2015). Each signal therefore carries different information. Visceral fat is more common in males than females and **poses the greater threat to health** (Palmer & Clegg, 2015).

Because leptin and insulin inform the brain about fat levels, tracing their receptors mapped part of the circuit. Receptors for both are located in many parts of the nervous system, but **most are in the hypothalamus, particularly the arcuate nucleus**, and within the arcuate they are **not randomly distributed**. They sit on **two classes of neuron**:

1. Neurons that release **neuropeptide Y**, the hunger peptide met earlier.
2. Neurons that release **melanocortins**, a class of peptides including the satiety peptide **alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone**. These are collectively called the **melanocortin system**.

Most attention has gone to the melanocortin system, because injections of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone suppress eating and promote weight loss in some people (Kim, Leyva, & Diano, 2014). But keep the finding in proportion, and note this if asked to evaluate: the melanocortin system appears to be only a **minor component of a much larger system**, since eliminating leptin receptors from it produces **only a slight weight gain** (Münzberg & Myers, 2005).

### Leptin as a treatment

Early studies looked decisive. Leptin receptors were found in the brain, and injecting leptin into ob/ob mice reduced **both their eating and their body fat**. All that remained was to demonstrate it in humans.

Two snags ended that:

1. **Humans with high body fat have high, not low, leptin levels** (Münzberg & Myers, 2005). They are not leptin-deficient.
2. **Injections of leptin did not reduce either eating or body weight** in humans who were overweight (Heymsfield et al., 1999).

Current thinking is that such individuals are **resistant** to leptin's effects, possibly including a **reduced ability of leptin to cross the blood-brain barrier** (Cui, López, & Rahmouni, 2017; Pan & Myers, 2018). Exactly why leptin acts so differently in humans and ob/ob mice **has yet to be explained**, and you should say so rather than assert a mechanism.

The effort was not a total failure, though. Leptin **is close to a cure for the rare individuals who genuinely lack it** (Blüher & Mantzoros, 2015).

> **The case of the child with no leptin (Farooqi et al., 1999).** Normal weight at birth, but weight soon increased rapidly. She demanded food continually and was disruptive when denied it. Deformities of her legs developed from her extreme body fat and required surgery. Referred for treatment at age **9**, she weighed **94.4 kg (about 210 lb)** and was still gaining at an alarming rate. She was **homozygous for the ob gene** with **no detectable leptin**, so leptin therapy was begun. It **immediately curtailed the weight gain**. She began to eat less and lost weight steadily over the 12-month study, a total of **16.5 kg (about 36 lb)**, almost all of it fat, with **no obvious side effects**.

The contrast between this case and the failed trials in common obesity is the teaching point: the same hormone is a near-miracle in **leptin deficiency** and useless in **leptin resistance**. Matching mechanism to patient is the whole of it.

### Treatment: serotonergic agonists

Because serotonin agonists reduce food consumption in humans and non-humans, they have considerable potential against overeating (Voigt & Fink, 2015). Note that their mechanism is **different** from leptin's and insulin's: leptin and insulin produce **long-term satiety signals based on fat stores**, whereas serotonin agonists appear to **increase short-term satiety signals associated with consuming a meal** (Halford & Blundell, 2000). That distinction is examinable.

In studies of overweight patients, serotonin agonists reduced: the urge to eat high-calorie foods, the consumption of fat, the subjective intensity of hunger, meal size, the number of between-meal snacks, and bingeing.

That profile was so favourable, and the problem so severe, that **fenfluramine and dexfenfluramine were rushed into clinical use**. They were subsequently **withdrawn from the market** because chronic use was associated with **heart disease** in a small but significant number of users. This is the cautionary tale in the chapter about moving from a positive effect profile to mass prescription without long-term safety data.

Currently only one serotonin agonist with a more favourable side-effect profile is approved for treating overweight individuals, **lorcaserin** (Halpern & Halpern, 2015; Nigro, Luon, & Baker, 2013), and its efficacy is only **modest** (Adan, 2013). There is still hope for pharmacological intervention: drugs that raise **multiple monoamines**, serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine together, appear more effective than serotonin agonists alone (González-Muniesa et al., 2017).

### Treatment: gastric surgery

For individuals who are extremely overweight, surgery is sometimes recommended.

**Gastric bypass** short-circuits the normal path of food through the digestive tract so that absorption is reduced, which in turn produces downstream effects that collectively lower weight (Sandoval & Seeley, 2017; Sinclair, Brennan, & le Roux, 2018). The first gastric bypass was performed in **1967**, and it is currently the **most commonly prescribed** surgical treatment for extreme overweight.

The **adjustable gastric band** procedure instead positions a hollow silicone band around the stomach to slow the flow of food through it. The band's circumference can be adjusted by **injecting saline through a port implanted just beneath the skin**. Its main advantage over bypass is that the band can readily be **removed**.

A meta-analysis comparing the two found **both highly effective**, with **gastric bypass generally more effective** than the adjustable band but **associated with more surgery-related complications** (Chang et al., 2014). That trade-off, greater efficacy against greater complication rate, is the answer to any question asking you to compare them.

## 12.7 Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa

### Anorexia nervosa

**Anorexia nervosa** is a disorder of **underconsumption**. Individuals eat so little that they experience health-threatening weight loss, and **despite an emaciated appearance they often perceive themselves as fat** (Gardner & Brown, 2014). It is a serious condition: in approximately **4 percent** of diagnosed cases, complications from starvation result in death, and there is a **high rate of suicide** among persons with anorexia.

### Bulimia nervosa

**Bulimia nervosa** is characterised by **periods of not eating interrupted by bingeing**, eating large amounts of food in short periods, **followed by efforts to eliminate the consumed calories immediately**, either by **purging** (vomiting, or excessive use of laxatives, enemas or diuretics) or by **excessive exercise**.

Persons with bulimia may be **overweight or of normal weight**. Those who are **underweight** are usually diagnosed instead with **binge-eating/purging anorexia**, which is a diagnostic boundary worth remembering. The mortality rate for individuals with bulimia is about **4 percent**.

### Are they the same disorder?

The answer depends on your perspective, and a good exam answer gives both.

**From the physician's perspective, distinguish them**, because starvation produces different health problems from repeated bingeing and purging:

| | **Anorexia nervosa** | **Bulimia nervosa** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical complications | Reduced metabolism, **bradycardia** (slow heart rate), **hypotension**, **hypothermia**, **anaemia** | Irritation and inflammation of the **oesophagus**, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, **electrolyte imbalance**, dehydration, **acid reflux** |

(Westmoreland, Krantz, & Mehler, 2015.)

**From the scientist's perspective, treat them as variations of one disorder.** On this view, both begin with an **obsession about body image and slimness** and **extreme efforts to lose weight**. Both attempt weight loss through strict dieting, but those with bulimia are **less able to control their appetites** and so enter a cycle of starvation, bingeing and purging. Two further similarities support the single-disorder view (Kaye et al., 2005):

- Both groups tend to have **distorted body images**, seeing themselves as heavier and less attractive than they are (Tabri et al., 2015).
- **Many patients straddle the two diagnoses** and cannot readily be assigned to one, and **many flip-flop between the two diagnoses** as their circumstances change (Kaye et al., 2013).

### Anorexia and positive incentives

The positive-incentive perspective suggests that the decline in eating defining anorexia and bulimia is likely a consequence of a corresponding **decline in the positive-incentive value of food**. This possibility has received little attention, partly because anorexia patients often display substantial *interest* in food, continually talking about it, thinking about it, and preparing it for others.

That apparent contradiction dissolves once you make a distinction, and the distinction is the examinable point: **the positive-incentive value of *interacting with* food is not the same as the positive-incentive value of *eating* food**, and it is the latter that matters for anorexia.

The few studies that have measured the positive-incentive value of various tastes in anorexia patients generally find it **lower than in controls** (Drewnowski et al., 1987; Sunday & Halmi, 1990; Roefs et al., 2006). Pinel argues these studies **underestimate** the importance of that reduction, because they use the wrong comparison group. The right comparison is not healthy controls but **starving people of the same weight**. Here is how a semi-starvation volunteer described the experience:

> "I wait for mealtime. When it comes I eat slowly and make the food last as long as possible. The menu never gets monotonous even if it is the same each day or is of poor quality. It is food and all food tastes good. Even dirty crusts of bread in the street look appetizing." (Keys et al., 1950, p. 852)

That is what starvation normally does to the incentive value of food. Set that against a person with anorexia at the same body weight who can decline a meal, and the size of the anomaly becomes clear.

### The conditioned-taste-aversion hypothesis

The dominance of set-point theories left one of the major puzzles of anorexia largely unexamined: **why does the adaptive, massive increase in the positive-incentive value of eating that occurs in starvation victims not occur in people starving from anorexia?** Under starvation, that value normally rises so high that it is hard to imagine anyone, however controlled, rigid, obsessive or motivated, refraining from eating palatable food. Why the protective mechanism is not activated in severe anorexia is a pressing question about aetiology.

Pinel's proposed answer has two parts, and it is built out of material from earlier in the chapter, which is exactly why it is worth learning as a model of theory-driven reasoning.

**Part one comes from Woods's work on the aversive physiological effects of meals.** At the start of a meal a person is normally in reasonable homeostatic balance, and that balance is **disrupted by the sudden infusion of calories**. Meals are physiologically stressful events.

**Part two is that these aversive effects are much greater in people who have been eating little** (Brooks & Melnik, 1995). Meals that produce adverse but tolerable effects in healthy individuals may be **extremely aversive** for someone who has been food-deprived. The historical evidence is grim and decisive: many World War II concentration camp survivors were **made ill by refeeding, and some were killed by the food given to them by their liberators** (Keys et al., 1950; Solomon & Kirby, 1990).

Put the two parts together. **Meals consumed by a person with anorexia may produce a variety of conditioned taste aversions that reduce the motivation to eat.** The food is repeatedly paired with genuine illness; conditioned taste aversion is the best-established form of learning in the whole of the chapter; the result is a progressive collapse in the positive-incentive value of eating.

**The treatment implication is direct and counter-intuitive, and it is the reason the hypothesis matters:** patients with anorexia, or anyone severely undernourished, should **not be encouraged, or even permitted, to eat meals**. They should be **fed, or infused with, small amounts of food intermittently throughout the day**, so that no single feeding is large enough to produce the aversive post-ingestive consequences that drive the conditioning.

Pinel presents this openly as a **hypothesis**, not as established fact, and offers it to illustrate that the test of a new theory is whether it generates new hypotheses. He also notes a striking convergence: an article on global famine and malnutrition observed that **one clinical complication of feeding meals to famine victims is anorexia** (Blackburn, 2001). If you are asked to evaluate this hypothesis, say clearly that it is a hypothesis, name the two components, give the treatment implication, and cite the famine observation as convergent but not confirmatory evidence.

### The case of the student with anorexia

The chapter closes with a case Pinel reports in the first person, and it is worth reading because the patient does the theorising.

A student came to tell him how much she had enjoyed the course and how sorry she was to be dropping out of university. She was articulate, personable, and her grades were very high. She weighed **82 pounds** and was about to be hospitalised. Asked, naively, whether she did not want to eat, she said that of course she wanted to eat, that she knew she was terribly thin because her friends told her so, that she knew it was wrecking her life, and that she could not force herself, and that in a strange way she was pleased with her thinness.

Then she offered her own account: her problem began when eating started to lose its positive-incentive value for her, because in her mind she associated eating with being fat and with boyfriend problems. This made dieting easy. Occasionally she would get hungry and binge, or her parents would force a big meal on her; she would eat so much that she felt ill, then put her finger down her throat and vomit. That kept her from gaining weight, but, as she put it, it also **taught her body to associate her favourite foods with illness, a kind of conditioned taste aversion**.

Two things to take from this. First, it is a patient-generated statement of the very hypothesis the module has just built, which is why Pinel places it last. Second, note the clinical detail that makes it plausible: the illness in her case was not merely the physiological stress of the meal but the **vomiting that reliably followed her favourite foods**, which is about as clean a taste-aversion conditioning protocol as could be designed.

## How the chapter fits together

Seven threads run through all seven modules. If you can trace each one, you understand the chapter rather than just remembering it.

1. **Hunger is anticipatory, not reactive.** The cephalic phase begins before food arrives; premeal hunger is preparation; the premeal glucose dip is caused by the intention to eat; Weingarten's rats got hungry to a buzzer. Every one of these is the same fact seen from a different angle.
2. **Correlation with eating is not causation of eating.** The glucose dip, the stomach contraction, the peptide level: each looked like the signal and turned out to be a companion of the signal.
3. **Lesion effects are rarely what they appear.** The VMH result was metabolic and anatomically non-specific; the LH result was not feeding-specific. Ask always what *else* the lesion did.
4. **The evolutionary mismatch.** Every mechanism in the chapter, taste preferences, sensory-specific satiety, efficient storage as fat, eating to capacity, is adaptive in an environment of scarce and unpredictable food and pathogenic in an environment of abundance.
5. **Negative feedback without a target.** Settling points, not set points, in body weight; a neuron's resting potential is the model case.
6. **Learning is everywhere in eating.** Conditioned taste aversions and preferences, learned satiety in the sham-eating rat, learned vitamin preferences, conditioned hunger, and finally the conditioned-taste-aversion account of anorexia.
7. **Theory choice has clinical consequences.** Set point says weight change is futile and that starving patients should be fed meals. Settling point plus positive incentive says permanent parameter change works and that starving patients should be fed small amounts intermittently. The theory you hold determines what you do to a patient.

## Worked example

*A 34-year-old man is referred after gaining 22 kg over three years. He reports that he is "never really hungry" but "always eating". He works from home, keeps snacks on his desk, eats while working, and finds that he can finish a large meal and then eat a dessert he did not want. A previous six-month diet produced 9 kg of loss, which plateaued at month four; he regained all of it within a year. He asks whether his metabolism is "broken" and whether he has a genetically fixed weight he cannot escape.*

Work through the chapter's machinery rather than reciting facts.

**Is he eating because of energy deficits?** Almost certainly not. He says so himself, and 12.3 gives you the account: with continuous access to food, deficits are not the usual cause of eating. His eating is being pulled by positive-incentive value, not pushed by need.

**Why does having food on the desk matter so much?** Two mechanisms. Continuous availability of varied palatable food exploits **sensory-specific satiety**: the incentive value of the food he has just eaten plummets, but the value of a *different* snack does not, so he restarts. And small amounts of food eaten between meals produce the **appetizer effect**, eliciting cephalic-phase responses that generate hunger rather than reducing it.

**Why did he finish a dessert he did not want?** Sensory-specific satiety again. Satiety for the main course does not transfer to a different taste.

**Is his metabolism "broken"?** No, it is doing exactly what 12.5 describes. As his fat increased, efficiency of energy utilisation **decreased** (diet-induced thermogenesis), which limited further gain. When he dieted, efficiency **increased**, which limited further loss, producing his month-four plateau. The plateau is the mechanism working, not failing.

**Why did he regain the weight?** The five-stage account in 12.6. He changed a parameter temporarily. Weight moved to a new settling point; when he restored the original conditions, incentive value was high and energy leakage was low, so weight rapidly drifted back to the original settling point.

**Does he have a fixed weight he cannot escape?** This is the question the whole chapter answers, and you should answer it explicitly. Under a **set-point** model, yes, and attempting change is futile. Under the better-supported **settling-point** model, no: his weight is where the current parameters balance, and it will move, and stay moved, if the **parameters** change permanently. That reframes the clinical task from "diet harder" to "change what is permanently true about availability, incentive value and expenditure": get the varied palatable food off the desk (availability and sensory-specific satiety), stop eating while working (breaks the conditioned cue-eating link Weingarten demonstrated), and treat exercise as health-promoting rather than as the main weight lever, because it is a small fraction of total expenditure and is easily offset by post-exercise intake.

**What would you not tell him?** Not to "listen to the wisdom of his body" and eat until satisfied. The calorie-restriction literature says free-feeding intake is not the healthy level, and his satiety mechanisms are being driven by incentive value, not by his energy needs.

## MCQ traps

1. **"Hunger is caused by low blood glucose."** The premeal dip is real (Campfield & Smith, 1995, ~8 percent, ~10 minutes before the meal) but is a **cephalic-phase consequence of the intention to eat**, not its cause. Four disconfirmations: wrong time course, removing the dip does not remove the meal, glucose recovers if no meal comes, CNS extracellular glucose stays constant.
2. **"The VMH is the satiety centre."** VMH lesions raise insulin → increased **lipogenesis**, decreased **lipolysis**; the rats **overeat because they are becoming obese**, and much of the effect is from damaged **paraventricular** fibres, not the VMH itself.
3. **"The LH is the hunger centre."** LH lesions produce **general** sensorimotor unresponsiveness, not a feeding-specific deficit. Aphagia **plus adipsia**.
4. **Dynamic vs static phase.** Dynamic = immediate, gross overeating, rapid gain. Static = stabilised intake, new weight **defended**.
5. **Fat vs glycogen.** Fat wins on **two** counts: about twice the energy per gram **and** glycogen holds water. Naming only the energy density loses the mark.
6. **Gluconeogenesis** is **protein → glucose**, a **fasting-phase** process. Not fat → glucose.
7. **Fasting-phase brain fuel.** Glucose is **spared for the brain** because brain uptake is **insulin-independent**; only in **prolonged** fasting does the brain switch to **ketones**. Muscles use free fatty acids and ketones throughout.
8. **Serotonin reduces meal SIZE, not meal NUMBER**, and shifts preference **away from** fat.
9. **Satiety vs hunger peptides.** Satiety: **CCK, bombesin, glucagon, somatostatin, alpha-MSH** (largely gut). Hunger: **neuropeptide Y, galanin, orexin-A, ghrelin** (largely brain/hypothalamus).
10. **CCK's illness problem.** It induces conditioned taste aversion in rats and nausea in humans; it qualifies as a satiety peptide only because it reduces eating at **doses well below** those producing aversion.
11. **Prader-Willi** = deletion/disruption on the **paternally contributed chromosome 15**. Insatiable hunger, little/no satiety, **slow metabolism**, hypotonia.
12. **Sham eating.** The **first** sham meal is **normal-sized**, which shows satiety is driven by **prior experience**, not by nutrients arriving. Only later sham meals grow.
13. **Koopmans (1981).** No nerves → signal is **bloodborne**; no absorption from stomach → signal is **not an absorbed nutrient**; therefore a **chemical released by the stomach**, graded by calories **and** volume.
14. **Appetizer effect** *increases* hunger. If an option says a pre-meal snack reduces subsequent intake, it is wrong.
15. **Set point vs settling point.** Both use negative feedback. Only the set-point model has a **defended target**; in the settling-point model feedback **limits further change** rather than restoring a level.
16. **Leaky-barrel mapping.** Water level = **body fat**; leakage = **energy expended**; nozzle pressure = **positive-incentive value**; barrel weight on hose = **satiety signal**.
17. **Leptin in human obesity.** Levels are **high**, not low, and leptin injections **do not** reduce weight in ordinary obesity. It works dramatically only in genuine **congenital leptin deficiency** (Farooqi et al., 1999).
18. **Leptin vs insulin fat signals.** Leptin tracks **subcutaneous** fat, insulin tracks **visceral** fat; **visceral** fat is the greater health threat and is more common in males.
19. **Fenfluramine/dexfenfluramine were withdrawn** for **heart disease**, not for ineffectiveness. Only **lorcaserin** remains approved, with **modest** efficacy.
20. **Gastric bypass vs adjustable band.** Bypass is generally **more effective** but has **more surgical complications**; the band is **removable**.
21. **Bulimia patients are not necessarily underweight.** An underweight bingeing/purging patient is usually diagnosed **binge-eating/purging anorexia**.
22. **Anorexia and interest in food.** Preoccupation with food is about the incentive value of **interacting with** food, which is **not** the incentive value of **eating** it.
23. **Anorexia treatment implication of the CTA hypothesis:** small amounts intermittently, **not meals**. A question offering "encourage regular full meals" as the theory-consistent answer is a trap.
24. **Exercise** contributes **less** to weight loss than commonly believed; most expenditure is resting processes and digestion.
25. **Mortality figures.** Anorexia ~**4 percent** die of starvation complications, plus high suicide rate. Bulimia mortality ~**4 percent**.

| **Homeostasis** | A stable internal environment |
| **Set-point / detector / effector mechanism** | Defines / detects deviations from / corrects deviations from the set point |
| **Glucostatic theory** | Eating regulated to defend a blood glucose set point (short-term control) |
| **Lipostatic theory** | Eating regulated to defend a body-fat set point (long-term control) |
| **Positive-incentive theory** | Eating is drawn by the anticipated pleasure of eating, not driven by deficit |
| **Positive-incentive value** | The anticipated pleasure of a behaviour |
| **Satiety** | The motivational state that stops a meal while food remains |
| **Nutritive density** | Calories per unit volume |
| **Sham eating** | Chewed and swallowed food exits through an implanted tube before reaching the stomach |
| **Appetizer effect** | Small amounts of food before a meal increase rather than reduce hunger |
| **Cafeteria diet** | Varied, highly palatable diet; produces large overconsumption and weight gain |
| **Sensory-specific satiety** | Incentive value of the eaten food plummets while that of other foods declines only slightly |
| **Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH)** | Once thought a satiety centre; lesions cause hyperphagia and obesity via metabolic change |
| **Lateral hypothalamus (LH)** | Once thought a feeding centre; lesions cause aphagia + adipsia and general unresponsiveness |
| **Hyperphagia / aphagia / adipsia** | Excessive eating / cessation of eating / cessation of drinking |
| **Dynamic phase / static phase** | VMH syndrome's rapid-gain phase / stabilised, defended-weight phase |

## Likely exam points

1. **Fat vs glycogen:** twice the energy per gram **and** glycogen holds water; fat ~85%, protein ~14.5%, glycogen ~0.5% of reserves.
2. **Three metabolic phases and their hormones:** cephalic + absorptive = insulin high, glucagon low; fasting = glucagon high, insulin low. Cephalic insulin *lowers* glucose in anticipation.
3. **Gluconeogenesis** = protein → glucose; **ketones** from free fatty acids; brain uses ketones only in **prolonged** fasting.
4. **Set-point system components:** set-point, detector, effector mechanisms = thermostat, thermometer, heater. All set-point systems are negative feedback systems maintaining homeostasis.
5. **Glucostatic (short-term, blood glucose) vs lipostatic (long-term, body fat)** and the 1950s view that they were complementary.
6. **Three problems with set-point theories:** inconsistent with evolutionary pressure (needs a system that *prevents* deficits); predictions unconfirmed (natural glucose drops too small, preloads fail, high fat is associated with *more* hunger); ignores taste, learning and social influence.
7. **Positive-incentive theory (Berridge, 2004):** eating driven by anticipated pleasure, the positive-incentive value; multiple interacting determinants rather than one privileged variable.
8. **R.H. (Rozin et al., 1998):** amnesic patient ate three full meals in under an hour. Hunger tracks memory, not energy state.
9. **Weingarten (1983):** buzzer-light CS elicited eating in non-deprived, recently fed rats. Conditioned hunger.
10. **Woods on premeal hunger:** the meal is homeostasis-disturbing; premeal sensations are cephalic-phase preparation, not a demand for fuel.
11. **Sodium vs other nutrients:** sodium deficiency produces an immediate innate preference; all other vitamin/mineral preferences must be learned from post-ingestive effects. **Harris et al. (1933):** learning succeeds with 2 diets, fails with 10.
12. **Sham eating:** first sham meal normal-sized → satiety is learned from prior experience.
13. **Sensory-specific satiety:** Rolls et al. (1981) eight-food study; Booth (1981) immediate taste-specific then general 30-minute decline; Rolls (1990) brief within-meal vs enduring between-meal effects; two adaptive consequences (varied diet, exploiting abundance). **Rogers & Blundell (1980):** cafeteria diet, +84% calories, +49% body weight in 120 days.
14. **Campfield & Smith (1995)** and the four reasons the premeal dip does not support glucostatic theory.
15. **Hetherington & Ranson (1940)** VMH hyperphagia, dynamic and static phases; **Anand & Brobeck (1951)** LH aphagia; **Teitelbaum & Epstein (1962)** adipsia and recovery through tube feeding.
16. **Why both centre theories failed:** VMH = metabolic (insulin → lipogenesis, decreased lipolysis) and non-specific (paraventricular fibres); LH = general sensorimotor unresponsiveness.
17. **Cannon & Washburn (1912)** balloon study, and its refutation by stomach-removal patients.
18. **Koopmans (1981)** transplanted stomach: satiety signal is bloodborne, non-neural, and not an absorbed nutrient.
19. **Gibbs, Young & Smith (1973):** CCK reduces meal size; the dose-separation argument that keeps CCK a genuine satiety peptide.
20. **Peptide lists** and the qualification that this research is mostly about **metabolism**, not about the control of eating.
21. **Serotonin:** reduces meal size, resists cafeteria diets, shifts preference from fat.
22. **Prader-Willi:** paternal chromosome 15; Miss A. (Martin et al., 1998); management is environmental control of food access.
23. **Calorie restriction:** Okinawa (Kagawa, 1978; uncontrolled), Weindruch et al. (1996) 25/55/65% with the 65% group living 67% longer, benefits even when started late, plus seizure and memory findings.
24. **Diet-induced thermogenesis and Rothwell & Stock (1982)**, resting expenditure 45% greater in cafeteria-fed rats; explains the diet plateau.
25. **Settling point vs set point:** both negative feedback, only one has a defended target; the four facts of weight regulation each explained better by the leaky barrel; the practical consequence for whether weight change is possible.
26. **Evolutionary account of the epidemic:** four selected characteristics plus culturally transmitted eating practices, meeting an environment of continuous high-incentive food.
27. **Individual differences in fat accumulation:** BMR, diet-induced thermogenesis, NEAT, gut microbiome (Ridaura et al., 2013 twin-discordant germ-free mice), ~100 loci, transgenerational epigenetic effects.
28. **Five stages of a weight-loss programme** and why permanent change requires permanent parameter change; exercise contributes less than believed.
29. **Leptin:** ob/ob mice, Coleman (1979), Friedman (1994); high leptin and leptin resistance in human obesity; Farooqi et al. (1999) congenital deficiency case (16.5 kg lost in 12 months).
30. **Leptin/insulin receptors in the arcuate nucleus** on NPY neurons and melanocortin neurons; melanocortin system is only a minor component.
31. **Treatments:** serotonergic agonists (short-term satiety signal; fenfluramine/dexfenfluramine withdrawn for heart disease; lorcaserin modest; multi-monoamine drugs more effective), gastric bypass vs adjustable band.
32. **Anorexia vs bulimia:** physician's view (different complications) vs scientist's view (variants of one disorder); ~4% mortality each; distorted body image; diagnostic straddling and flip-flopping.
33. **Anorexia and positive incentives:** interacting with food vs eating food; the right comparison group is starving people of the same weight (Keys et al., 1950 quotation).
34. **The conditioned-taste-aversion hypothesis of anorexia:** meals are aversive, more so in the undernourished (concentration camp refeeding), producing CTAs; treatment implication of small intermittent feeding; Blackburn (2001) famine-refeeding anorexia observation; presented as hypothesis, not fact.
