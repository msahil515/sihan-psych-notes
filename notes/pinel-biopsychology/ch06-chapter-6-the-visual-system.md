# Chapter 6: The Visual System

**What this chapter is really about.** Ask most people what their eyes and brain do and they will describe a camera: light comes in, a picture forms, and the brain looks at the picture. Pinel and Barnes build the whole chapter to knock that idea down. What actually lands on the back of each eye is a tiny, blurred, upside-down, flat image, and two of them that never quite match. From that unpromising material the visual system builds a three-dimensional, richly detailed, stable, coloured world, and in some respects the world it builds is *better* than the physical light that entered the eye. Edges are sharpened beyond what is physically there, a surface keeps its colour when the light falling on it changes completely, gaps in the retina are filled in so seamlessly that you never notice them. The visual system is not a copier. It is a constructor, and almost every finding in the chapter is evidence for that one claim.

A second thread runs underneath the first: **seeing is not the same thing as being aware of seeing**. By the end of the chapter you will meet a man who can point accurately at objects he insists he cannot see, a woman who shapes her hand perfectly to pick up a block whose width she cannot report, and patients whose skin sweats to familiar faces they say they do not recognise. These cases matter for clinical psychology because they show that "the patient says they cannot see it" and "the brain has not processed it" are two different statements.

**How to read this chapter.** The book has six modules, and they follow the signal. 6.1 takes light into the eye through the pupil and lens. 6.2 is the retina: its cells, its two kinds of receptor (the duplexity theory is the most examined idea in the first half of the chapter), and how light is turned into a neural signal. 6.3 follows the main pathway from retina through thalamus to the primary visual cortex. 6.4 and 6.5 then ask how this machinery lets you see the two most important things in a scene, **edges** and **colour**; this is where Hubel and Wiesel and the colour theories live. 6.6 leaves the primary visual cortex for the rest of the cortex and is the clinical half: scotomas, blindsight, the two visual streams, prosopagnosia and akinetopsia. Learn 6.2 and 6.3 thoroughly before 6.4, because the receptive-field story in 6.4 assumes you can trace a signal from rod to cortex without thinking about it.

## The Case of Mrs. Richards: Fortification Illusions and the Astronomer

The chapter opens with a case that asks a deceptively philosophical question: are one person's private visual experiences like anyone else's? Whitman Richards reported it in **1971**, and the patient was his own wife.

Mrs. Richards had **migraine headaches**. Like about **20 percent of migraine sufferers**, she regularly saw a striking visual display shortly *before* an attack. These displays are called **fortification illusions**, because the zigzag outline they produce reminded observers of the plan of a star-shaped military fortification seen from above.

Her illusions followed a consistent course:

1. Each began as a **grey patch of blindness close to the centre** of her visual field.
2. Over the next few minutes the patch grew into a **horseshoe shape**, and its leading edge was lined with a **zigzag of flickering lines**.
3. The lines, with the blind area trailing behind them, took roughly **20 minutes** to travel out to the edge of her visual field. When they got there, her headache usually started.

Because the display crept outwards so slowly, Mrs. Richards could keep her gaze on a point at the centre of a blank sheet and periodically sketch what she saw. Her drawings revealed two regularities that turn out to be important later: as the illusion moved towards the periphery, the **zigzag lines got thicker**, and the **area of blindness expanded faster**. It accelerated as it went.

Then came the part of the case that answers the opening question. Dr. Richards found that a nearly identical set of drawings had been published in **1870** by the British astronomer **George Biddell Airy**, who suffered the same kind of illusion. Two people, a century apart, with no contact, produced virtually the same pictures. That is strong evidence that the experience is a product of how *every* human visual system is built, not an idiosyncrasy of one mind.

The book deliberately leaves the case unexplained at this point. You cannot make sense of the horseshoe, the straight lines, the acceleration, or why closing one eye makes no difference until you have learned how the primary visual cortex is organised, so the case returns, solved, in 6.4. Keep three questions in mind as you read: **where** in the visual system is the disturbance, **why** does it accelerate, and **why** is it made of straight lines?

## 6.1 Light Enters the Eye and Reaches the Retina

### No light, no vision

Start with a popular myth that the book uses to make a basic point: cats, owls and other nocturnal animals are *not* able to see in total darkness. Some species have adaptations for very dim light, but no animal sees when there is no light at all. Vision is the detection of light reflected from objects into the eye; take away the light and there is nothing to detect.

### Light as particles and as waves

Physics offers two descriptions of light, and the book asks you to accept both. Light can be treated as a stream of discrete energy packets called **photons**, travelling at about **300,000 kilometres (186,000 miles) per second**, or as **waves** of electromagnetic energy. Each description works for some purposes and not for others, and physicists simply tolerate the inconsistency.

For vision, the wave description is the useful one. Light is commonly defined as electromagnetic waves between about **380 and 760 nanometres** (a nanometre is a billionth of a metre) in length. Nothing physically special happens at those boundaries; that band is "light" only because it is the band the *human* visual system happens to respond to. Other species draw the boundaries elsewhere. **Rattlesnakes** detect **infrared** radiation, which is too long in wavelength for us to see, and can therefore locate warm-blooded prey in what would be pitch darkness to a human. A textbook written for rattlesnakes would need a different definition of light.

### Wavelength and intensity are not colour and brightness

Two physical properties of light matter most in this chapter:

| Physical property (of the light) | Psychological property (of the perception) it chiefly influences |
|---|---|
| **Wavelength** | Perceived **colour** |
| **Intensity** | Perceived **brightness** |

In everyday speech we treat the two columns as interchangeable. We call an intense 700-nanometre light "a bright red light". Strictly that is wrong: the light has a wavelength and an intensity; *brightness* and *redness* are properties of our perception of it. The distinction sounds pedantic now, but the chapter repeatedly depends on it. In 6.2 you will see that two lights of the same intensity can differ in brightness because of their wavelength, and in 6.5 you will see that an object's colour can stay the same while the wavelengths it reflects change completely. Neither finding makes sense unless you keep the physical and the perceptual apart.

> **Trap.** "Wavelength = colour" and "intensity = brightness" are the classic loose equations. The correct statement is that wavelength *plays an important role in* colour perception and intensity *plays an important role in* brightness perception. Wavelength also affects brightness (the spectral sensitivity curves in 6.2), and colour is not fixed by wavelength (colour constancy in 6.5). An option that equates a physical property with a perceptual one is the wrong option.

### The iris and pupil: trading sensitivity against acuity

The amount of light that reaches each retina is controlled by the **iris**, a ring (the book says "donut-shaped") of contractile tissue that gives the eye its colour, blue, brown or otherwise. Light enters through the **pupil**, which is simply the hole in the middle of the iris.

The size of the pupil is a compromise between two things the visual system wants and cannot fully have at once:

- **Sensitivity**: the ability to detect dimly lit objects.
- **Acuity**: the ability to see the fine details of objects.

The logic works in both directions:

- **Bright light.** Sensitivity is not a problem, because there is plenty of light. The pupils **constrict**. A small pupil gives a **sharper image** on each retina and a **greater depth of focus**, meaning that a wider range of distances is in focus at the same time.
- **Dim light.** Now there is too little light to activate the receptors adequately, so the pupils **dilate** to let more in. The price is paid in **acuity** and **depth of focus**: the image is less sharp and less of the scene is in focus at once.

So the pupil is the first example in the chapter of a trade-off between sensitivity and acuity. You will meet the same trade-off again, built into the wiring of the retina itself, when cones and rods are compared.

> **Trap.** The direction is easy to reverse under exam pressure. **Constricted** pupil = bright light = better acuity and **greater** depth of focus. **Dilated** pupil = dim light = better sensitivity, at the cost of acuity and depth of focus.

### The lens and accommodation

Behind each pupil sits a **lens**, whose job is to focus incoming light onto the retina. Focusing works by **refraction**, the bending of light, and the amount of bending depends on the lens's shape.

The lens is held in place by ligaments, and the tension on those ligaments is adjusted by the **ciliary muscles**:

- To look at something **near**, the ciliary muscles adjust the tension so that the lens takes on its natural, more rounded (the book says "cylindrical") shape. A rounder lens refracts light more strongly, which brings close objects into sharp focus.
- To look at something **far away**, the lens is **flattened**, refracting less.

The process of adjusting the shape of the lenses to bring images into focus on the retina is called **accommodation**.

It helps to know the standard physiology behind the book's phrasing: when the ciliary muscle contracts, the ligaments slacken and the elastic lens rounds up for near vision; when the muscle relaxes, the ligaments pull taut and flatten the lens for distance. The age-related loss of near focus (presbyopia) happens because the lens stiffens and can no longer round up.

> **Trap.** Accommodation is a change in the **shape of the lens**, not a change in pupil size and not a turning of the eyes. Three different near-vision adjustments are easily confused: the pupil constricts (amount and sharpness of light), the lens rounds up (accommodation), and the two eyes turn inwards (convergence, below).

### Why two eyes, and why on the front

The most obvious feature of vertebrate eyes is that they come in pairs. The simplest reason is that vertebrates are built with a left and a right side. The most common arrangement by far is **one eye on each side of the head**, which lets an animal see in nearly every direction without turning its head.

So why do some species, humans included, have both eyes on the **front** of the head? That arrangement gives up the ability to see behind in exchange for having the region in front of the animal seen by **both eyes at once**. Seeing the same scene through two eyes is an important basis for the visual system's ability to build a **three-dimensional** perception, to see depth, from two flat retinal images.

The book's Check It Out demonstration compares animals with front-facing eyes (a human, an owl, a lion) with animals whose eyes are on the sides (an antelope, a canary, a squirrel). The general rule:

| Eye placement | Typical of | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| Front-facing, side by side | **Predators** | Accurate judgement of **how far away prey is** (depth) |
| One on each side | **Prey** animals | A **wide field of view**, so predators can be spotted approaching from most directions |

### Convergence and binocular disparity

The movements of your two eyes are coordinated so that each point in the visual world falls on **corresponding points** of the two retinas. To achieve this for nearby objects, the eyes must **converge**, turning slightly inwards; convergence is greatest when you inspect something close.

Even so, the two retinal images can never match exactly, because the two eyes view the world from slightly different positions. **Binocular disparity** is the difference in the position of the same image on the two retinas. The key fact is how disparity varies with distance: it is **greater for close objects** than for distant ones. That systematic relationship is what makes disparity useful. The visual system can read the degree of disparity as a cue to distance and so combine two two-dimensional retinal images into one three-dimensional perception.

You can check the distance rule yourself by holding a finger in front of your face and alternately closing each eye: the finger jumps a long way when it is close and much less when it is at arm's length. The book's second Check It Out, the **"mysterious cocktail sausage"**, makes the same point more dramatically. If you touch the tips of your two index fingers together at arm's length and look past them at a distant wall, a small floating "sausage" appears between the fingertips. It is not there; it is what your visual system makes of two disparate images of the fingertips. Close one eye and it vanishes, which proves that it depends on having two eyes.

> **Trap.** "Convergence" has **two unrelated meanings** in this chapter. Here it is a **movement of the eyes**: both eyes turning inwards to fixate a near object. In 6.2 it will be a feature of **neural wiring**: many receptors feeding into one retinal ganglion cell. If a question asks why scotopic vision is sensitive, the answer is neural convergence, not eye convergence.

> **Trap.** **Binocular disparity** (also called **retinal disparity**) is a *difference between the two retinal images*, not an eye movement. It is larger for **near** objects. Binocular cells in the visual cortex that respond best to disparity (6.4) are thought to contribute to depth perception.

## 6.2 The Retina and Translation of Light into Neural Signals

Once light has passed through the pupil and the lens it arrives at the **retina**, the sheet of neural tissue lining the back of the eye. The retina does three jobs, and it is worth holding all three in mind because students often think of it as a mere detector: it **converts light into neural signals**, it **conducts** those signals towards the central nervous system, and it **takes part in processing** them before they ever leave the eye.

### Five types of retinal neuron

The retina is built from **five types of neurons**. Learn them in the order the signal meets them:

1. **Receptors**: the **rods** and **cones**, which absorb light.
2. **Horizontal cells**: spread sideways at the level where receptors meet bipolar cells.
3. **Bipolar cells**: carry signals from receptors towards the ganglion cells (the "straight-through" route).
4. **Amacrine cells**: spread sideways at the level where bipolar cells meet ganglion cells.
5. **Retinal ganglion cells**: the output neurons of the retina; their axons leave the eye as the optic nerve.

The straight-through chain is receptor to bipolar cell to retinal ganglion cell. The two remaining types, **horizontal cells** and **amacrine cells**, are specialised for **lateral communication**: they carry signals *across* the main channels of input rather than along them. That sideways wiring is what makes it possible for the retina to compare neighbouring points, which becomes important when you reach contrast enhancement in 6.4. Retinal neurons communicate both **chemically**, through synapses, and **electrically**, through **gap junctions**.

Each of the five types comes in many subtypes. **More than 60 different kinds of retinal neuron** have been identified, including **about 30 different kinds of retinal ganglion cell**.

> **Trap.** The two "sideways" cells are a favourite matching item. **Horizontal cells** work at the receptor to bipolar junction; **amacrine cells** work at the bipolar to ganglion junction. Neither is part of the straight-through route. Also note the two different subtype counts the book gives for ganglion cells: about **30** in the retina-structure section, and, later in 6.4, about **20 in primates and 40 in mice** distinct functional sorts. Read the question's context before choosing.

### The inside-out retina

Here is the feature of retinal anatomy that most surprises students: the retina is arranged **inside out**. You might expect the receptors to face the incoming light. They do not. They sit at the **back** of the retina, against the back of the eyeball, and light has to pass through all the other layers (ganglion cells, amacrine cells, bipolar cells, horizontal cells) before it reaches them. Once the receptors are activated, the signal travels back out through the layers to the retinal ganglion cells, whose axons run across the **inner surface of the retina** (the surface nearest the light) and then gather into a bundle that leaves the eyeball as the optic nerve.

This arrangement creates two problems for vision:

1. **Distortion.** Incoming light is scattered and distorted by the retinal tissue it must cross before reaching the receptors.
2. **A hole.** For the bundle of ganglion cell axons to get out of the eye, there has to be a gap in the receptor layer. That gap is the **blind spot**.

The visual system has a solution for each problem, and the two solutions are quite different in kind. The first is anatomical; the second is computational.

### The fovea

The distortion problem is reduced by the **fovea**, an **indentation about 0.33 centimetre in diameter at the centre of the retina**. The fovea is the part of the retina specialised for **high-acuity vision**, the seeing of fine detail. At the fovea the ganglion cell layer is **thinned**, pushed aside so that light has less tissue to cross on its way to the receptors there. Less tissue means less distortion exactly where detail matters most.

### The blind spot and completion

The blind spot cannot be engineered away, because the axons have to leave somewhere. The visual system's answer is to **fill it in**.

The book's Check It Out lets you prove both halves of this. First, close your left eye and fix your right eye on a letter on the page while moving the book; at about **13 centimetres (5 inches)** a black dot beside the letter falls on your blind spot and vanishes. So the blind area is real. Second, fix on a different letter at the same distance, one with a broken line beside it, and the gap in the line disappears: you see an unbroken line, even though part of it falls on the blind spot.

This is **completion** (also called **filling in**). The visual system uses information from the receptors **around** the blind spot to fill the gap in the retinal image. If a straight bar enters one side of the blind spot and a straight bar leaves the other side, the system supplies the missing segment and you perceive one continuous bar, whatever is actually in the gap. The book treats completion as one of the most persuasive demonstrations that vision does far more than make a faithful copy of the world.

### Surface interpolation: completion in ordinary vision

It would be a mistake to think of completion as a patch applied only to the blind spot. Completion is part of **normal vision** everywhere in the visual field.

Here is the counter-intuitive idea, stated as plainly as possible. When you look at an object, the visual system does **not** send a picture of it from the retina to the cortex. It extracts a small amount of key information, mainly **where the edges are**, and sends that. The cortex then **constructs** a perception of the whole object from this partial description. In particular, the colour and brightness of large, unpatterned surfaces are not perceived directly at all. They are inferred from the edges and filled in. This form of completion is called **surface interpolation**: the process by which we perceive surfaces, in which the visual system extracts information about edges and infers from it the appearance of the large areas between them.

An everyday example: a plain painted wall looks evenly coloured across its whole extent, but the neurons signalling about the middle of the wall are largely silent, because nothing is changing there. What you "see" in the middle is the visual system's inference from the wall's borders. The book flags this as one of the most important and least intuitive ideas in the chapter, and it links forward to two things: the edge-detecting receptive fields of 6.4 (the system is built to signal edges) and Karl Lashley's migraine scotoma in 6.6 (where a friend's missing head was filled in with wallpaper).

> **Trap.** **Completion** is the general phenomenon of filling in missing parts of the visual image (at the blind spot, across a scotoma). **Surface interpolation** is the specific completion process by which surfaces are perceived from edge information in normal vision. If the question is about perceiving the colour of a large uniform surface, the answer is surface interpolation.

### Duplexity theory: cones and rods

The human retina contains two kinds of receptor, named for their shapes: **cones** and **rods**. Why two? The puzzle stood until **1866**, when it was noticed that species active **only by day** tend to have retinas with **only cones**, and species active **only by night** tend to have retinas with **only rods**.

From that comparative observation came the **duplexity theory** of vision: the theory that **cones and rods mediate different kinds of vision**.

- **Photopic vision** is **cone-mediated** vision. It predominates in **good light** and gives **high-acuity, coloured** perceptions of the world.
- **Scotopic vision** is **rod-mediated** vision. In **dim light** there is not enough light to drive the cones reliably, so the more **sensitive** rod system takes over. Its sensitivity has a price: scotopic vision **lacks both the detail and the colour** of photopic vision.

### Photopic and scotopic vision compared

| Feature | Photopic system (cones) | Scotopic system (rods) |
|---|---|---|
| Lighting in which it predominates | Good (daylight) | Dim |
| **Acuity** (detail) | High | Low |
| **Colour** | Yes | No (shades of grey) |
| **Sensitivity** | Low | High |
| **Neural convergence** onto a retinal ganglion cell | Low: a **few** cones per ganglion cell | High: **several hundred** rods per ganglion cell |
| Where on the retina | Concentrated in the **fovea**; the fovea has **only** cones | **None** in the fovea; peak density about **20 degrees** from its centre |
| Peak spectral sensitivity | About **560 nm** | About **500 nm** |
| Species with only this receptor type | Diurnal (day-active) | Nocturnal (night-active) |

> **Trap.** The rods-versus-cones pairing is the single most testable table in the first half of the chapter, and the commonest error is to flip one row. A reliable anchor: **R**ods are for **R**educed light and cannot see colour; **C**ones give **C**olour and **C**lear detail. **Scotopic** shares its "sco-" with *scotoma* and darkness; **photopic** shares "photo-" with light. The fovea has **no rods at all**, which is why a dim star can disappear when you look straight at it and reappear when you look slightly to one side.

### Convergence: why rods buy sensitivity at the cost of acuity

The difference between the two systems is not only in the receptors themselves. Much of it comes from how they are **wired**, specifically from their different degrees of **convergence** onto retinal ganglion cells.

**Scotopic system, high convergence.** The output of **several hundred rods** converges on a **single** retinal ganglion cell. Follow what happens to a dim light falling on a patch of rods. Each rod's response is small, but because so many of them feed the same ganglion cell, their effects **summate** (add up) there, and the ganglion cell fires. That is where the rod system's **sensitivity** comes from.

Now follow the cost. When that ganglion cell changes its firing, the brain has **no way of knowing which of the several hundred rods** produced the change. The location of the light within that large patch is lost. That is where the rod system's **poor acuity** comes from.

**Photopic system, low convergence.** Only **a few cones** feed each retinal ganglion cell. The same dim light falling on a sheet of cones cannot summate to the same degree, and the ganglion cells may not respond at all, so the system is **less sensitive**. But when a cone-fed ganglion cell does fire, it is driven by only a few cones, so there is **little ambiguity about where the stimulus is**: hence **high acuity**.

So the duplexity theory is really the same sensitivity-versus-acuity trade-off you met at the pupil, now built into the wiring of the retina. You cannot have both from the same circuit: pooling many inputs buys sensitivity and loses location; keeping inputs separate preserves location and loses sensitivity. The eye solves the problem by running **two systems in parallel** and letting light level decide which one dominates.

### Where the cones and rods are

Cones and rods are not evenly spread across the retina (the book's Figure 6.9 plots receptors per square millimetre against distance from the fovea):

- The **fovea contains no rods at all, only cones.**
- At the edges of the foveal pit the proportion of cones falls sharply and the number of rods rises.
- **Rod density peaks at about 20 degrees from the centre of the fovea.**
- The **nasal hemiretina** (the nose-side half of each retina) holds **more rods** than the **temporal hemiretina** (the temple-side half).

The terms **nasal hemiretina** and **temporal hemiretina** come back in 6.3, where they decide which fibres cross at the optic chiasm. Learn them now as positional labels.

### Spectral sensitivity curves

In general, a more intense light looks brighter. But **wavelength** also affects brightness, substantially, because the visual system is not equally sensitive to all wavelengths in the visible range. Two lights of identical intensity but different wavelength can look very different in brightness.

A **spectral sensitivity curve** is a graph of the relative brightness of lights of the **same intensity** presented at **different wavelengths**.

The single most important thing to remember about these curves is that humans, and any animal with both rods and cones, have **two** of them:

- The **photopic spectral sensitivity curve** is measured by having participants judge the relative brightness of different wavelengths shone on the **fovea** (where there are only cones).
- The **scotopic spectral sensitivity curve** is measured by having them judge lights shone on the **periphery** of the retina at intensities **too low to activate the few peripheral cones** there (so only rods respond).

Notice the logic of the method: each curve is isolated by exploiting the anatomy you have just learned. The fovea gives you pure cones; dim light in the periphery gives you pure rods.

The two curves peak in different places:

| Curve | Peak sensitivity | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Photopic** | About **560 nm** | In good light, a 500-nm light must be much more intense than a 560-nm light to look equally bright |
| **Scotopic** | About **500 nm** | In dim light, a 560-nm light must be much more intense than a 500-nm light to look equally bright |

### The Purkinje effect

The difference between the two curves produces a visible effect as light fades from photopic to scotopic conditions. In **1825**, **Jan Purkinje** described what he saw in his garden one evening. Just before dusk his yellow and red flowers looked brighter than his blue ones. A few minutes later, after the sun had gone down, the relationship had **reversed**: the whole garden now looked grey, as scotopic vision does, but most of the blue flowers appeared as *lighter* shades of grey than the yellow and red ones. This shift in relative brightness during the transition to dim light is the **Purkinje effect**.

Explain it from the curves rather than memorising it:

1. In daylight, vision is photopic, peaking near **560 nm**. Yellow and red flowers reflect longer wavelengths close to that peak, so they look relatively bright; blue flowers reflect short wavelengths far from the peak, so they look relatively dim.
2. At dusk, vision becomes scotopic, peaking near **500 nm**. Now the short-wavelength (blue) light is closer to the peak, and long-wavelength red light is far out on the curve's tail. Blue flowers therefore look brighter than red ones.
3. Because rods do not support colour vision, everything is seen in greys; only the *relative brightness* of the greys tells you which system is in charge.

> **Trap.** The Purkinje effect is a shift in **relative brightness**, not in colour, as illumination falls. At dusk **blues** gain brightness relative to **reds**, because the peak sensitivity shifts from about **560 nm (cones)** to about **500 nm (rods)**, towards the short-wavelength end. Do not confuse **Jan Purkinje (1825)**, the physiologist who described this effect, with the Purkinje cells of the cerebellum, which are named after the same man but are a different topic.

### Seeing a detailed coloured world with a tiny fovea

There is a puzzle hidden in the receptor distribution. If cones mediate detailed colour vision and almost all of them are packed into the fovea, then high-acuity colour vision is restricted to a few degrees at the centre of the **visual field** (the whole area you can see at a given moment). Yet when you look around, you do not see a small coloured, detailed spot in the middle of a grey, blurred surround. You see an expansive, detailed, coloured world.

The book's Check It Out shows where the answer lies. Close one eye, hold your gaze absolutely fixed on a point, and try to read letters printed at increasing distances out from it: at **20 degrees or more** from fixation it is hard to make out either detail or colour, because there are so few cones there. Then look at the same letters while letting your eyes move, and the difficulty disappears.

What we see at any instant is not just what is projected on the retina at that instant. Without our noticing it, the eyes **continually scan** the visual field, and the perception at any moment is a **summation of recent visual information**. Because of this **temporal integration**, the world does not vanish each time we blink.

### Fixational eye movements

Even when you try to hold your eyes perfectly still, fixated on a point, they keep moving. These **involuntary fixational eye movements** come in **three kinds**:

1. **Tremor**: a very small, rapid oscillation.
2. **Drifts**: slow wandering of the eye's position.
3. **Saccades** (pronounced "sah-KAHDS"): small, jerky movements, or **flicks**.

We are normally unaware of them, but they are essential. If eye movements, or their main effect (the movement of images across the retina), are blocked, **visual objects begin to fade and disappear**. When images are **artificially stabilised**, held still on the retina, they fade out and reappear. The reason is that **most visual neurons respond only to change**. A perfectly unchanging input stops driving them. Fixational eye movements keep the image moving across the receptors, and that is what lets us keep seeing an object we are staring at.

> **Trap.** In this chapter, **saccades** appear in the list of *involuntary fixational* eye movements, defined as small jerky flicks during fixation (often called microsaccades elsewhere). In wider usage, and in Chapter 8 and many exam keys, "saccades" also means the large, rapid, voluntary jumps of gaze from one point to another, for example during reading. If an option says "saccades are only voluntary" or "fixational eye movements are voluntary", it is wrong by this chapter's account. The three fixational movements are **tremor, drifts and saccades**.

> **Trap.** The consequence of stopping eye movements is that the image **fades**, not that it becomes sharper. The underlying principle, that visual neurons respond mainly to **change**, returns repeatedly: in the on and off responses of 6.4, in edge detection, and in the dependence of the system on contrast rather than on absolute light level.

### Visual transduction: rhodopsin

**Transduction** is the conversion of one form of energy into another. **Visual transduction** is the conversion of **light into neural signals** by the visual receptors.

The breakthrough came in **1876**, with the extraction from rods of a red **pigment** (the word means any substance that absorbs light). It had a curious property. Exposed to continuous intense light, it was **bleached**: it lost its colour and its ability to absorb light. Returned to darkness, it regained both its redness and its capacity to absorb light. The pigment became known as **rhodopsin**.

It is now clear that **rhodopsin's absorption of light, and the bleaching that goes with it, is the first step in rod-mediated vision**. The evidence is a close match between chemistry and perception: how well rhodopsin absorbs light in a given situation predicts how well humans see under the same conditions. The cleanest example is wavelength. The **absorption spectrum** of rhodopsin (a graph of how well it absorbs light of each wavelength) is **nearly identical** to the human **scotopic spectral sensitivity curve**. If our dim-light sensitivity to each wavelength simply mirrors rhodopsin's ability to absorb that wavelength, it is hard to avoid the conclusion that dim-light vision *is* rhodopsin absorbing light.

> **Trap.** The rhodopsin absorption spectrum matches the **scotopic** curve (peak near 500 nm), not the photopic one. Rhodopsin is the **rod** pigment. The three cone pigments, with their own absorption spectra, are the subject of 6.5.

### How light turns a rod off

Rhodopsin is a **G-protein-coupled receptor**. It works like the metabotropic receptors of Chapter 4, initiating a cascade of chemical events inside the cell when activated, except that what activates it is **light rather than a neurotransmitter**.

The surprising part is the direction of the response. Walk through it in two states:

| | In the **dark** | In the **light** |
|---|---|---|
| Rhodopsin | Inactive | **Bleached** by light, triggering an intracellular cascade |
| Sodium channels | **Partially open** | **Close** |
| Sodium ions | Flow into the rod | Cannot enter |
| Membrane potential | **Slightly depolarised** | **Hyperpolarised** |
| Glutamate release | **Steady, continuous** release of excitatory glutamate | **Reduced** |

So a rod is most active in the **dark**. Light does not switch a rod on; it switches it **down**. The signal that "light has arrived" is a **decrease** in the rod's glutamate output.

For completeness, the standard biochemistry behind the book's "cascade" (not detailed in this edition): rhodopsin consists of a protein, opsin, bound to retinal, a derivative of vitamin A. In the dark, the second messenger **cyclic GMP** holds the sodium channels open, producing a steady inward "dark current". Light-activated rhodopsin activates a G protein (transducin), which activates an enzyme (phosphodiesterase) that breaks down cyclic GMP; with less cyclic GMP the channels close and the rod hyperpolarises.

The book draws a general lesson from this: **signals are often transmitted through neural systems by decreases in activity**. Do not assume that "stimulus detected" always means "neuron fires more".

> **Trap.** Light **hyperpolarises** rods and **reduces** glutamate release. In darkness rods are **depolarised** and release glutamate **continuously**. Options saying that light depolarises rods, opens sodium channels, or increases transmitter release are the classic distractors. Glutamate is excitatory, but the rod's message about light is carried by releasing *less* of it.

## 6.3 From Retina to Primary Visual Cortex

Many pathways in the brain carry visual information, but the largest and best studied by far are the **retina-geniculate-striate pathways**. The name lists the three stations in order: they carry signals from each **retina** to the **primary visual cortex** (also called the **striate cortex** or **V1**) by way of the **lateral geniculate nuclei** of the **thalamus**.

### The retina-geniculate-striate pathways

About **90 percent** of retinal ganglion cell axons join the retina-geniculate-striate pathways. No other sensory system has such a dominant pair (left and right) of routes to the cortex. The remaining tenth or so of the axons go elsewhere; the standard example, not developed in this chapter, is the projection to the **superior colliculus** of the midbrain. That minority route becomes relevant in 6.6, because it is one of the candidate explanations for blindsight.

The anatomy can be followed step by step:

1. **Retinal ganglion cell axons** leave each eye as the **optic nerve**.
2. The two optic nerves meet at the **optic chiasm**, where some of the axons cross to the other side of the brain and some do not.
3. Beyond the chiasm, the regrouped bundles are called the **optic tracts**. Each optic tract now carries information about the **opposite half of the visual field** from **both eyes**.
4. The optic tracts end in the **lateral geniculate nuclei** of the thalamus.
5. Lateral geniculate neurons project to the **primary visual cortex** in the occipital lobe.

### Which hemiretina crosses

The rule to take from the book's Figure 6.13 is a single sentence: **every signal from the left visual field ends up in the right primary visual cortex, and every signal from the right visual field ends up in the left primary visual cortex.**

How does the wiring achieve that? Think about where light from the **left visual field** lands. Light travels in straight lines through the pupil, so the image is reversed: light from the left side of the world falls on the **right** half of each retina.

- In the **right eye**, the right half of the retina is the half next to the temple: the **temporal hemiretina**. Its axons stay on the same side (**ipsilateral**) and go to the right hemisphere without crossing.
- In the **left eye**, the right half of the retina is the half next to the nose: the **nasal hemiretina**. Its axons **cross at the optic chiasm** (**contralateral**) and go to the right hemisphere.

So the rule is: **nasal hemiretinas cross; temporal hemiretinas do not.** The result is that each hemisphere receives the opposite visual field from both eyes. Note that the projection is **not** "each eye goes to the opposite hemisphere"; it is "each **visual field** goes to the opposite hemisphere".

> **Trap.** This is the most frequently mis-answered anatomy item in the chapter. **Nasal** hemiretina fibres **cross** at the optic chiasm; **temporal** hemiretina fibres stay **ipsilateral**. The book's own review exercise puts it this way: input from the right visual field reaches the nasal hemiretina of the right eye, which projects to the **contralateral** (left) hemisphere, and the temporal hemiretina of the left eye, which projects to the **ipsilateral** (left) hemisphere. Each hemisphere sees the **opposite visual field** with **both eyes**. A clinical corollary: damage to one primary visual cortex causes a scotoma in the opposite visual field **of both eyes** (6.6), whereas damage to one optic **nerve**, before the chiasm, affects only **one eye**.

### The lateral geniculate nucleus and layer IV

Each **lateral geniculate nucleus** has **six layers**. Each layer receives input from **all parts of the contralateral visual field of one eye**. Put differently, each lateral geniculate nucleus receives input only from the **contralateral visual field**, with **three layers fed by one eye and three by the other**. (Standard anatomy specifies which: in each nucleus, layers 1, 4 and 6 receive from the contralateral eye and layers 2, 3 and 5 from the ipsilateral eye; the book does not go to that level.) Signals from the two eyes are therefore still kept **separate** at the thalamus; they are first combined in the cortex, a fact that solves part of Mrs. Richards's case in 6.4.

Most lateral geniculate neurons that project to the primary visual cortex end in the **lower part of cortical layer IV**. Seen in cross-section, this dense band of incoming fibres forms a visible **stripe, or striation**, and that is why the primary visual cortex is called the **striate cortex**.

The figure in the book shows only the axons running **from** the lateral geniculate nuclei **to** the primary visual cortex. The book adds a correction that students rarely remember: there are **just as many projections running the other way**, from the primary visual cortex back to the lateral geniculate nuclei. The thalamus is not a simple one-way relay.

> **Trap.** Three facts about the striate cortex are often scrambled. Its name comes from the **stripe** formed by geniculate input to **lower layer IV**, not from any striped pattern of activity. "**Primary visual cortex**", "**striate cortex**" and "**V1**" are three names for the **same** area. And the geniculostriate connection is **reciprocal**: there are as many cortex-to-thalamus fibres as thalamus-to-cortex fibres.

### Retinotopic organisation

The retina-geniculate-striate system is **retinotopic**: **each level is laid out like a map of the retina**. Two stimuli falling on **adjacent** points of the retina excite **adjacent** neurons at every level of the system, in the lateral geniculate nucleus and again in the cortex. Neighbourhood relations on the retina are preserved all the way up.

The map is not drawn to scale. The **fovea**, though only a small part of the retina, is given a **disproportionately large** share of the primary visual cortex: **about 25 percent** of V1 is devoted to analysing its input. That magnification makes sense once you remember that the fovea is where high-acuity vision happens; fine detail needs more processing machinery. It also means that the **periphery** of the visual field gets **proportionally less** cortex, which is the fact that will explain why Mrs. Richards's fortification illusion accelerated.

> **Trap.** "About 25 percent of primary visual cortex is dedicated to the fovea" and "about 90 percent of retinal ganglion cell axons enter the retina-geniculate-striate pathways" are the two percentage facts of this module. Do not swap them.

### Dobelle's cortical prosthesis

A striking demonstration of retinotopy in humans came from **Dobelle, Mladejovsky and Girvin (1974)**. Their patients had lost their sight through **eye damage**, so the cortex itself was intact but no longer received input. The researchers placed an **array of electrodes** on the surface of the **primary visual cortex**. When current was passed simultaneously through a group of electrodes arranged in a shape, for example a **cross**, on the cortical surface, the patient reported **seeing a glowing image of that shape**.

Why does this demonstrate retinotopic organisation? Because the spatial arrangement of stimulated points on the *cortex* was experienced as the same spatial arrangement in the *visual field*. That can only happen if neighbouring cortical points stand for neighbouring points in visual space; that is, if the cortex holds a map. The finding, together with more recent work on **retinal implants**, points towards **visual prostheses** that might one day help many blind people.

### The M and P channels

Figure 6.13 hides a further level of organisation: at least **two parallel channels** of communication run through each lateral geniculate nucleus.

| | **P channel (parvocellular)** | **M channel (magnocellular)** |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral geniculate layers | The **top four** layers | The **bottom two** layers |
| Cell bodies | **Small** (*parvo* = small) | **Large** (*magno* = large) |
| Particularly responsive to | **Colour**, **fine pattern detail**, **stationary or slowly moving** objects | **Movement** |
| Main receptor input | **Cones** provide most of the input | **Rods** provide most of the input |
| Projection | To its own area of **lower layer IV** of striate cortex | To a different area of **lower layer IV** |

The two channels stay separate beyond the thalamus: parvocellular and magnocellular neurons project to **different parts of lower layer IV**, and those M and P regions of layer IV in turn project to **different areas of visual cortex**. This is the first hint of the principle that dominates 6.6, that the visual system splits its analysis into parallel streams handled by different cortical areas.

The pairing in the table is worth reasoning through rather than memorising. The P channel inherits the virtues of the cones: colour and detail. The M channel inherits the virtues of the rod system's convergent wiring: sensitivity to change and movement rather than to fine detail.

> **Trap.** **Parvo = small = P = top four layers = colour, fine detail, stationary objects = mostly cone input.** **Magno = large = M = bottom two layers = movement = mostly rod input.** The "four and two" split and the "small and large" meanings are the commonest MCQ hooks. An option that gives magnocellular cells colour sensitivity, or puts them in the top four layers, is wrong.

## 6.4 Seeing Edges

### What an edge is

Edge perception sounds like a minor topic. It is not. **Edges are the most informative features of any visual scene**, because they mark where each object begins and ends and therefore define its extent and position. Given how much depends on finding edges, and the steady pressure of natural selection, it is no surprise that many species have visual systems that are especially good at it.

Before looking at mechanisms, be precise about what an edge *is*. In a sense an edge is nothing at all: it is simply the **place where two different areas of an image meet**. So perceiving an edge is really **perceiving a contrast** between two adjacent regions of the visual field. This module deals with edges between regions that differ in **brightness**, that is, with **brightness contrast**. (Edges defined by colour differences are, in effect, picked up again in 6.5.)

### Contrast enhancement and Mach bands

Look at a display of uniform vertical stripes that step from lighter to darker grey (the book's Figure 6.14). Physically, each stripe is **homogeneous**: a photometer would show a flat level of light across each one, with an abrupt step at each border. That is not what you see. Next to each border, the **lighter stripe looks lighter than it really is** and the **darker stripe looks darker than it really is**. Narrow illusory bands of extra brightness and extra darkness appear alongside every edge.

These non-existent bands are called **Mach bands** (after the physicist Ernst Mach, although Mach generated them with a different figure from the one in the book). Their effect is to **enhance the contrast at each edge**, making the edge easier to see. The general phenomenon is **contrast enhancement**: the intensification of the perception of edges.

The book's point is broader than one illusion. Contrast enhancement is not something that happens only on textbook pages. Every edge you ever look at is quietly **highlighted** by contrast-enhancing mechanisms in your nervous system. Your perception of edges is, in a measurable sense, **better than the real thing**, that is, sharper than the physical light entering your eyes would justify. This is the "constructor, not copier" theme again.

### Lateral inhibition: the classic explanation

The 11th edition describes contrast enhancement and then moves directly to receptive fields, but the mechanism earlier editions of Pinel used to explain Mach bands, and the one most exam keys still expect, is **lateral inhibition**. It is standard physiology and fits everything in this module, so learn it.

The classic work was done on the lateral eye of the **horseshoe crab** (*Limulus*), whose receptors (ommatidia) are large and each have their own axon, so they can be recorded individually (older Pinel editions cite **Ratliff, 1972**). The key finding is that when a receptor fires, it **inhibits its neighbours** through lateral connections, and the inhibition is strongest from **intensely stimulated** receptors onto their **immediate** neighbours.

Now apply that to an edge between a bright region and a dim region, focusing on two receptors right at the border:

1. **Receptor D, on the bright side next to the edge.** It is strongly stimulated, like all the bright-side receptors. But its neighbours on one side are dimly lit and fire weakly, so D receives **less lateral inhibition** than receptors deeper in the bright region. D therefore fires **more** than other bright-side receptors. Perceptual result: a **bright band** along the bright side of the edge.
2. **Receptor E, on the dim side next to the edge.** It is weakly stimulated, like all the dim-side receptors. But its neighbours on one side are brightly lit and fire strongly, so E receives **more lateral inhibition** than receptors deeper in the dim region. E therefore fires **less** than other dim-side receptors. Perceptual result: a **dark band** along the dim side of the edge.

The difference in firing on either side of the edge is thus exaggerated beyond the physical difference in light. Those exaggerations are the Mach bands. In the human retina the cells specialised for this kind of sideways communication are the **horizontal and amacrine cells** you met in 6.2.

> **Trap.** Two edition points. The **11th edition** explains contrast enhancement and then goes straight to **Hubel and Wiesel's receptive fields**; the **horseshoe crab** and the term "lateral inhibition" belong to older editions. Older keys may ask "Mach bands are explained by ___": the answer is **lateral inhibition**. The mechanism is that the receptor on the **bright** side of an edge gets **less** inhibition and fires **more**, and the receptor on the **dark** side gets **more** inhibition and fires **less**. Students often reverse which side gets more inhibition.

### Hubel and Wiesel's method

The fitting centre of this module is the **Nobel Prize**-winning research of **David Hubel** and **Torsten Wiesel** (the prize was awarded in 1981). Their findings told us a great deal about visual mechanisms, and their **method** was adopted by later generations of sensory neurophysiologists. Their subjects were **cats and monkeys**.

The method, step by step:

1. Position the tip of a **microelectrode** near a **single neuron** in the part of the visual system being studied.
2. **Block eye movements** by paralysing the eye muscles, so the image cannot slide across the retina. Focus the images on a screen in front of the animal sharply on the retina with an adjustable lens.
3. **Find the neuron's receptive field.** The **receptive field** of a visual neuron is the **area of the visual field within which a visual stimulus can influence that neuron's firing**.
4. **Characterise the field**: record the neuron's responses to various simple stimuli presented within the receptive field, to find which kinds of stimulus most influence its activity.
5. **Advance the electrode** a little and repeat the whole procedure for another neuron, and another, and another.

The overall strategy is to begin with neurons close to the receptors and work gradually upwards through "higher" levels of the system, to see how the complexity of the neural responses increases at each level.

> **Trap.** A **receptive field** belongs to a **neuron** and is defined as a region of the **visual field** (equivalently, of the retina), not a region of cortex. Paralysing the eye muscles is a control: without it, eye movements would shift the image on the retina and make the receptive field impossible to map.

### Receptive fields at the first three levels

**Hubel and Wiesel (1979)** began by recording from the three levels of the retina-geniculate-striate system in order: first **retinal ganglion cells**, then **lateral geniculate neurons**, then the **striate neurons of lower layer IV** (the ones that receive geniculate input). They tested each neuron with **stationary spots of achromatic (uncoloured) light** shone on the retina. Strikingly, the receptive fields **changed very little** as they worked up through the three levels. Four properties were common to all three:

1. **Foveal fields are small.** At each level, receptive fields in the **foveal** area were **smaller** than those in the periphery, as you would expect if the fovea mediates fine-grained, high-acuity vision. (Small fields mean precise location; compare the convergence argument in 6.2.)
2. **Circular fields.** All the neurons had **circular** receptive fields.
3. **Monocular.** All the neurons were **monocular**: each had a receptive field in one eye but not the other.
4. **Centre and surround.** Many neurons at each level had receptive fields made of an **excitatory area and an inhibitory area separated by a circular boundary**.

### On-centre and off-centre cells

The fourth commonality is the important one. When Hubel and Wiesel shone a spot of light on different parts of one of these receptive fields, they got one of two kinds of response, depending on where in the field the spot fell:

- **"On" firing**: a burst of firing when the light is turned **on**.
- **"Off" firing**: **inhibition** of firing while the light is on, followed by a **burst of firing when the light is turned off**.

For most neurons at these levels, which response a spot produced in a given part of the field was predictable, because the cells came in two types:

| | Light in the **centre** of the field | Light in the **periphery (surround)** of the field |
|---|---|---|
| **On-centre cell** | "On" firing | Inhibition, then "off" firing when the light goes off |
| **Off-centre cell** | Inhibition, then "off" firing when the light goes off | "On" firing |

Now the crucial consequence. Because centre and surround work against each other, these cells respond best to **contrast**. For an on-centre cell:

- Illuminating the **whole centre** while leaving the surround **dark** produces the **maximum** firing.
- Illuminating the **whole surround** while leaving the centre **dark** produces the **minimum** firing (maximum inhibition).
- **Diffuse light over the entire field**, centre and surround together, has **little effect**, because the excitation and inhibition cancel.

From this, Hubel and Wiesel concluded that one function of many neurons in the retina-geniculate-striate system is to **signal the degree of brightness contrast between the two parts of their receptive fields** (Livingstone and Hubel, 1988). That connects straight back to the start of the module: a system built from contrast detectors is a system built to find edges, and it explains why a uniform surface needs to be filled in by surface interpolation rather than seen directly.

> **Trap.** On-centre and off-centre cells respond **poorly to diffuse light**. They do not report how much light there is; they report **contrast** between centre and surround. "Off" firing does not mean "no firing"; it means **inhibition while the light is on and a burst when it goes off**.

### Spontaneous activity

The book pauses on a detail visible in its response records: **most visual neurons are continually active even with no visual input at all**. A stimulus changes the rate of an ongoing discharge rather than starting firing from silence; inhibition shows up as a drop below the spontaneous rate. This spontaneous activity is characteristic of most neurons in the cerebrum, and responses to external stimuli use up only a **small fraction of the energy** the brain spends on its ongoing activity (Zhang and Raichle, 2010).

> **Trap.** Older editions (and the old version of these notes) illustrated spontaneous activity with **Arieli and colleagues (1996)**, who linked the level of ongoing cortical activity to the size of the response. The 11th edition cites **Lee and colleagues (2013)** and **Zhang and Raichle (2010)** instead. The underlying fact, that visual neurons fire spontaneously, is the same.

### Simple cells

Moving beyond lower layer IV, Hubel and Wiesel found that the neurons of lower layer IV are the exception in the striate cortex. **Most** primary visual cortex neurons have receptive fields of one of two classes, **simple** or **complex**, and **neither class includes the lower layer IV neurons**.

**Simple cells** resemble lower layer IV neurons in three ways: their receptive fields can be divided into antagonistic **"on" and "off" regions**; they are therefore **unresponsive to diffuse light**; and they are **all monocular**. The main difference is the shape of the border: in simple cells the boundaries between "on" and "off" regions are **straight lines**, not circles. Consequently:

- Simple cells respond best to **bars of light on a dark background**, **dark bars on a light background**, or **single straight edges** between dark and light areas.
- Each simple cell responds maximally only when its preferred straight-edge stimulus is in a **particular position** and a **particular orientation** within the field.
- Their receptive fields are **rectangular**, not circular.

### Complex cells

**Complex cells** are **more numerous** than simple cells. They share several properties with simple cells: **rectangular** receptive fields, a best response to **straight-line stimuli in a specific orientation**, and **unresponsiveness to diffuse light**. They differ from simple cells in **three** important ways:

1. **Larger receptive fields.**
2. **No static "on" and "off" regions.** A complex cell responds to a straight edge of its preferred orientation **wherever it falls** within the receptive field. Sweep a 45-degree bar that excites a particular complex cell across its field, and the cell keeps responding throughout the sweep. Many complex cells respond more strongly to a line **moving in a particular direction** across the field.
3. **Many are binocular.** All simple cells are monocular, but many complex cells respond to stimulation of **either eye**. In monkeys, **more than half** of complex cells are binocular.

| Feature | Lower layer IV neurons | **Simple cells** | **Complex cells** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receptive field shape | Circular | **Rectangular** | **Rectangular** |
| On and off regions | Circular centre-surround | **Straight-line** boundaries, static | **None**: cannot be divided into static on and off regions |
| Best stimulus | Spot contrast | Bar or edge at a **particular orientation and position** | Line at a **particular orientation, any position**; many prefer a **direction of movement** |
| Diffuse light | Little response | Little response | Little response |
| Eyes | Monocular | **All monocular** | **Many binocular** (over half in monkeys) |
| Field size | Small | Smaller | **Larger** |
| Relative number | | Fewer | **More numerous** |

> **Trap.** Simple versus complex is the most examined comparison in the module. The fastest discriminators: **simple** cells have **static on and off areas** and care about **position**; **complex** cells have **no static on and off areas** and respond to their preferred orientation **anywhere** in a larger field. **All** simple cells are monocular; **many** complex cells are binocular. Both prefer oriented straight lines and both ignore diffuse light, so options built on those shared features cannot distinguish them.

### Binocular complex cells, ocular dominance and disparity

Binocular complex cells reward closer study. If you map a binocular cell's receptive field through one eye and then through the other, the two fields lie in almost exactly the **same position** in the visual field and have the **same orientation preference**. Whatever you learn by stimulating one eye is confirmed through the other. And stimulating **both eyes together** with the appropriate stimulus usually makes the cell fire **more strongly** than stimulating either eye alone.

Two refinements:

- **Ocular dominance.** Most binocular cells in monkey primary visual cortex respond **more strongly to one eye** than to the same stimulation of the other. This is called **ocular dominance**.
- **Disparity tuning.** For some binocular cells, the strongest response comes when each eye receives the preferred stimulus simultaneously, with the two images falling at **slightly different retinal positions** (Ohzawa, 1998). These cells respond best to **retinal disparity** and so are likely to play a role in **depth perception** (Livingstone and Tsao, 1999). This is the neural counterpart of the binocular disparity you met in 6.1.

### Organisation of primary visual cortex: three conclusions

Having described the receptive fields, Hubel and Wiesel asked how neurons with different fields are **arranged** in the primate primary visual cortex. They reached **three** conclusions.

**1. Functional vertical columns.** The primary visual cortex is organised into **vertical columns** ("vertical" here means at right angles to the cortical layers). All neurons in the same column:

- respond to stimuli in the **same area of the retina**;
- are dominated by the **same eye** (if they show ocular dominance or monocularity); and
- "prefer" the **same straight-line angle** (if they prefer straight-line stimuli).

**2. Columns are arranged systematically.** Where a column sits in V1 depends on three things: the **retinal location** of its visual field, its **dominant eye**, and its **preferred line orientation**. Hubel and Wiesel concluded that all the functional columns analysing input from **one area of the retina** are **clustered together**; that **half of each cluster receives input from the left eye and half from the right eye**; and that each cluster contains neurons preferring straight lines of **every orientation**. In other words, each small patch of cortex holds a complete kit for analysing one small patch of the visual field: both eyes, all orientations. (Later literature calls the eye-specific slabs **ocular dominance columns** and the complete clusters **hypercolumns**; the book uses neither term in this passage, but the book's own review exercise links "ocular dominance columns" with "striate cortex".)

**3. Preferences become more complex up the hierarchy, by convergence.** As their recordings moved from **retina**, to **thalamus**, to **lower layer IV**, to **simple cells**, to **complex cells**, the neurons' "preferences" became **more complex**. Hubel and Wiesel concluded that this happens because **neurons with simpler preferences converge onto neurons with more complex preferences**. For example, a row of circular centre-surround fields lined up at an angle and feeding one cell would give that cell an oriented, rectangular field.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition lists **three** organisational conclusions (vertical functional columns; systematic clustering by retinal location, eye and orientation; convergence of simpler onto more complex preferences). Older editions and the old notes framed it as **two** conclusions. If a question asks "according to Hubel and Wiesel, why do receptive fields become more complex at higher levels?", the answer is **convergence** of neurons with simpler preferences onto neurons with more complex ones. Note that this "convergence" is a third use of the word, after eye convergence (6.1) and rod convergence (6.2); all three are examinable.

### The Case of Mrs. Richards, Revisited

You now have everything needed to solve the opening case. Recall the three questions: where is the disturbance, why does it accelerate, and why straight lines?

**Where? A one-eye test.** Mrs. Richards was asked to close one eye, then the other, and report what happened to the illusion each time. Her answer was that **nothing** changed. Reason it through. If the disturbance were in one eye, or anywhere in the system where the two eyes' signals are still separate (retina, optic nerve, lateral geniculate layers, lower layer IV, all monocular), then closing the affected eye should remove or change the illusion. It did not. The **visual cortex** is the first level of the retina-geniculate-striate system with neurons that receive input from **both** eyes (the binocular complex cells). So the disturbance must be **cortical**.

**Why does it accelerate? A calculation.** The illusion's speeding up as it spread outwards fits a wave of disturbance moving across the primary visual cortex at a **constant rate of about 3 millimetres per minute**, starting in the "foveal area" and spreading to the edges. The wave itself does not speed up. But retinotopic magnification means that a millimetre of cortex near the foveal representation covers only a tiny piece of the visual field, while a millimetre in the peripheral representation covers a much larger piece, because **proportionally less cortex is devoted to the periphery**. A constant cortical speed therefore becomes an **accelerating** sweep across the visual field. The thickening of the lines towards the periphery follows from the same mapping.

**Why straight lines?** What else would you expect from a disturbance of a region whose neurons **prefer straight-line stimuli**? Activating orientation-tuned simple and complex cells produces the experience of oriented line segments, and the zigzag is what you get when the wavefront crosses columns with different orientation preferences.

> **Trap.** The one-eye test localises the fault to the cortex because the cortex is the **first binocular level**. Options locating the fortification illusion in the retina or the lateral geniculate nucleus are wrong for exactly that reason. The rate to remember is **about 3 mm per minute**, **constant**; the *perceived* acceleration is produced by the cortical magnification of the fovea, not by the wave speeding up.

### Receptive fields are more varied than Hubel and Wiesel thought

Since Hubel and Wiesel's work, a great deal of research has gone into describing receptive fields in the retina-geniculate-striate system more fully, and the general finding is that receptive fields are **much more complex** than originally recognised. The book focuses on retinal ganglion cells and lateral geniculate neurons, the cells once thought to have only on-centre and off-centre fields.

- **Retinal ganglion cells.** Recent analyses show about **20** distinct sorts of retinal ganglion cell in **primates** and about **40** in **mice**, each with its own kind of receptive field. Besides the on-centre and off-centre fields Hubel and Wiesel described, some ganglion cells have fields selective for one or more of: **uniform illumination**, **orientation**, **motion**, and **direction of motion**.
- **Lateral geniculate cells.** Some lateral geniculate neurons, too, are sensitive to more than contrast: their fields can be selective for **orientation**, **motion** and **direction of motion** (Sun, Tan and Ji, 2016), resembling those of retinal ganglion cells.

So properties once thought to emerge only in the cortex (orientation and direction selectivity) can already be present in some cells before the signal reaches the cortex.

### Contextual influences: receptive fields are plastic

The second revision is deeper. Most studies of visual neurons rested on **two implicit assumptions**:

1. That visual processing is best understood using **simple, controllable, artificial stimuli** (spots, bars, edges).
2. That a neuron's receptive-field properties are **static**, fixed and unchanging features of that neuron.

Research using **video clips of real scenes involving natural movement** (Haslinger and colleagues, 2012) suggests that **neither assumption is correct**, at any of the three levels of the retina-geniculate-striate system.

Studies of visual cortex responses to **natural scenes**, the kind of input the visual system evolved to handle, show that a neuron's response depends not only on what is in its receptive field but also on the **larger scene** in which that stimulus is embedded. Influences on a visual neuron's activity produced by stimuli **outside its receptive field** are called **contextual influences** (Gilbert and Li, 2013). They take many forms, depending on:

- the exact **timing**, **location** and **shape** of the stimuli, and the **ambient light level**;
- the animal's **prior exposure** to them; and
- signals linked to particular **actions or states**, for example whether the animal is **running** (locomotion) or is seeing a stimulus with **biological relevance**, such as one previously paired with **food**. These can help shape the receptive field itself.

Consider what this does to the concept. A receptive field was first thought of as a **fixed** property of a neuron, the product of **hard-wired convergence** of circuits. It is now seen as a **plastic** property, **continually fine-tuned** by prior experience and by current signals from the animal's environment. This is the chapter's contribution to the book's **neuroplasticity** theme, and it is also why studies using natural scenes matter: simple artificial stimuli reveal only part of what a neuron does.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition's term is **contextual influences**: effects on a neuron of stimuli **outside** its receptive field. The old notes cited Felsen and Dan (2006) and Bair (2005) for the same general idea; this edition cites Haslinger and colleagues (2012) and Gilbert and Li (2013). If an option describes receptive fields as "fixed by hard-wired convergence and unaffected by experience", that is the **old** view the chapter rejects.

## 6.5 Seeing Colour

### Black, white, grey and hue

Colour is one of the most obvious qualities of visual experience, yet so far the chapter has dealt only in black, white and grey. Start by placing those three:

- **Black** is experienced when there is an **absence of light**.
- **White** is produced by an **intense mixture** of a wide range of wavelengths in roughly **equal proportions**.
- **Grey** is produced by the **same mixture at lower intensity**.

This module is about colours such as blue, green and yellow. The technically correct term for these is **hues**; the book, like everyday speech, simply calls them colours.

What determines the colour we perceive? To a large degree, the **wavelengths of light an object reflects** into the eye. The book's Figure 6.2 links individual wavelengths to colours, but outside the laboratory nobody ever meets an object reflecting a single wavelength. Sunlight and most artificial light contain complex mixtures of most visible wavelengths; objects absorb different wavelengths to different extents and reflect the remainder. That reflected mixture influences an object's perceived colour, but, as the end of the module shows, it is not the whole story.

### Component (trichromatic) theory

The **component theory**, also called the **trichromatic theory**, was proposed by **Thomas Young in 1802** and refined by **Hermann von Helmholtz in 1852**. It holds that:

- there are **three different kinds of colour receptor (cones)**, each with a **different spectral sensitivity**; and
- the colour of a stimulus is encoded by the **ratio of activity** across the three kinds.

Young and Helmholtz derived the theory from a colour-matching observation: **any colour in the visible spectrum can be matched by mixing three different wavelengths of light** in suitable proportions. Any three wavelengths will do, provided that none of them can itself be matched by mixing the other two. Because **three is normally the minimum** number of wavelengths needed to match every colour, they inferred that there must be **three types of receptor**.

### Opponent-process theory

The **opponent-process theory** was proposed by **Ewald Hering in 1878**. Hering proposed **three classes of cell**: **two** for encoding **colour** and **one** for encoding **brightness**. Each class encodes **two complementary perceptions** by changing its activity in opposite directions:

| Class of cell | One direction (e.g., hyperpolarisation) | Opposite direction (e.g., depolarisation) |
|---|---|---|
| Colour-coding class 1 | **Red** | **Green** |
| Colour-coding class 2 | **Blue** | **Yellow** |
| Brightness-coding class | **Black** | **White** |

**Complementary colours** are pairs of colours (for example, red light and green light) that **produce white or grey when combined in equal measure**.

Hering based his theory on behavioural observations that the component theory could not explain:

1. **Complementary colours cannot coexist in one perception.** There is no such thing as a **bluish yellow** or a **reddish green**. If red and green were signalled by opposite states of one cell, a reddish green would require a cell to be in two opposite states at once, which is impossible.
2. **Complementary afterimages.** Stare at red and the afterimage is green; stare at green and it is red; yellow gives a blue afterimage and blue a yellow one. The book's Check It Out asks you to stare at a coloured display for **one minute** without moving your eyes and then look at a blank fixation point, where squares in the complementary colours appear.

> **Trap.** The two theories differ in **who**, **when**, **how many** and **what evidence**. **Component (trichromatic)**: Young **1802**, Helmholtz **1852**; **three receptors**; colour coded by the **ratio** of their activity; evidence: **three-wavelength colour matching**. **Opponent-process**: Hering **1878**; **three classes of cell** working in **opposite pairs** (red-green, blue-yellow, black-white); evidence: **no reddish green or bluish yellow** and **complementary afterimages**. Afterimages are the standard "which theory explains this?" item, and the answer is **opponent-process**.

### Both theories are right, at different levels

For many years supporters of the two theories argued as if only one could be true. The book calls the debate "somewhat misguided", because it was driven more by scientists' adversarial habits than by any real incompatibility. Research eventually showed that **both colour-coding mechanisms coexist** in our visual systems (DeValois and colleagues, 2000).

**The component level: the cones.** Confirmation of Young's idea had to wait for a technique, developed in the **early 1960s**, for measuring the absorption spectrum of the photopigment in a **single cone**. Using it, researchers (for example **Wald, 1964**) confirmed what Young had concluded more than a century and a half earlier: vertebrates with good colour vision have **three different kinds of cone**, each with a **different photopigment** and its own characteristic **absorption spectrum**. Some cones are most sensitive to **short** wavelengths, some to **medium** wavelengths and some to **long** wavelengths, but **all three respond to most wavelengths of the visible spectrum** (the curves overlap heavily). The colour coding done by the cones themselves seems to be purely **component**.

**The opponent level: everything after the cones.** At **all subsequent levels** of the retina-geniculate-striate system there is evidence of **opponent processing**: cells that respond in one direction (for example, increased firing) to one colour and in the opposite direction (decreased firing) to its complement.

So the synthesis is simple to state: **trichromatic coding at the receptors, opponent coding from there on**.

> **Trap.** The examiner's favourite question here is "which theory is correct?", and the correct answer is **both**: component processing at the level of the **cones**, opponent processing at **all later levels** of the retina-geniculate-striate system. Note also that the three cone types each respond to **most** wavelengths; colour is carried by the **pattern** across them, not by one cone type per colour.

### Trichromats, dichromats and Jacobs's mice

The number of colour photopigments varies across species:

| Group | Photopigments | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Most **primates** | **Three** (**trichromats**) | |
| Most other **mammals** | **Two** (**dichromats**) | Lack the **long-wavelength** pigment, so have difficulty seeing light at the **red** end of the spectrum |
| Some **birds, fish and reptiles** | **Four** | |
| Some **insects** | **Five or more** | The **dragonfly** wins with **ten** |

In a remarkable study, **Jacobs and colleagues (2007)** inserted into mice a **gene for a third photopigment**, turning them from dichromats into **trichromats**. Behavioural tests showed that the **transgenic mice** had acquired the ability to see **additional wavelengths** of light. The striking implication is that the rest of the mouse visual system could make use of a new receptor input without having evolved alongside it.

> **Trap.** **Dichromats** lack the **long-wavelength (red-end)** pigment, not the short one. Most mammals, not most primates, are dichromats. The species record for photopigments in the book is the **dragonfly**, with **ten**.

### Colour constancy

Neither component nor opponent processing can explain what the book calls the single most important characteristic of colour vision: **colour constancy**. Colour constancy is the fact that the perceived colour of an object is **not a simple function of the wavelengths it reflects**; it is the tendency of an object to **stay the same colour despite major changes in the wavelengths of light it reflects**.

The book makes the idea concrete with an example from one of its authors. Working in an office through a single day, the light changes from a small incandescent desk lamp before dawn, to fluorescent ceiling lights in mid-morning, to natural sunlight in the afternoon. These light sources emit very different mixtures of wavelengths, so the wavelengths reflected by a blue shirt change substantially over the day. Yet the shirt looks equally blue throughout.

The advantage is easy to see once the phenomenon is clear. Colour constancy lets us tell objects apart in a **stable, memorable** way and respond appropriately to them. If an object's colour changed every time the illumination changed, colour would be useless for recognising it. Without colour constancy, colour vision would have **little survival value**.

Normally we are completely unaware of colour constancy, because in everyday life we have no way of measuring how much the wavelengths from an object vary while its colour stays put. Only in a controlled laboratory can you appreciate that colour constancy is not just one factor in colour vision but its **essence**.

### Land's Mondrian demonstrations

**Edwin Land (1977)** produced several dramatic laboratory demonstrations of colour constancy.

**The set-up.** Land used **three adjustable projectors**, each emitting light of **only one wavelength**: one **short**, one **medium** and one **long**. That way he knew exactly which three wavelengths were involved. He shone all three onto a test display made of rectangles of different colours. These displays are called **Mondrians**, because they resemble the paintings of the Dutch artist **Piet Mondrian**.

**Finding 1.** Changing the amount of light from each projector, and therefore the amount of each wavelength reflected from the display, had **no effect at all** on the perceived colours of the rectangles.

**Finding 2, the decisive version.** Land used a **photometer** to measure exactly how much of each of the three wavelengths was reflected by a rectangle his participants judged to be **pure blue**. He then took a **different Mondrian** and adjusted the projectors until a **red** rectangle on it reflected **exactly the same** amounts of the three wavelengths as the blue rectangle had. Shown the new display, participants saw the red rectangle as **red**, even though it was sending the eye precisely the wavelengths that had previously been seen as blue.

**What it shows.** Blue objects stay blue, green objects stay green, and so on, **regardless of the wavelengths they reflect**. The wavelength-to-colour link that Figure 6.2 seemed to promise does not hold for objects in scenes. Land identified two conditions for colour constancy:

1. The object must be lit by light containing **some short, some medium and some long wavelengths**, as daylight, firelight and almost all manufactured lighting do.
2. The object must be seen **as part of a scene**, not **in isolation**.

The second condition is the clue to the explanation.

### Retinex theory

Land's **retinex theory** of colour vision says that an object's colour is determined by its **reflectance**: the **proportion of light of each wavelength that a surface reflects**.

Here is why reflectance solves the puzzle. When the illumination changes, the absolute amounts of each wavelength reflected by a surface change dramatically. But the **efficiency** with which the surface absorbs each wavelength and reflects the rest does **not** change; a surface that reflects 60 percent of the long-wavelength light falling on it does so under any lamp. Reflectance is a stable property of the surface itself, which is exactly what a stable colour needs.

How can the visual system know reflectance, when all it receives is reflected light? According to retinex theory, it **calculates** reflectance by **comparing the light reflected by adjacent surfaces** in at least **three wavelength bands** (short, medium and long). If the light falling on the whole scene shifts towards long wavelengths, every surface reflects more long-wavelength light, but the *ratio* between neighbouring surfaces stays the same. Comparing neighbours cancels out the illuminant. This is why constancy fails for an object seen in isolation: with no neighbours there is nothing to compare.

The book ties this back to 6.4. Just as **context** turned out to matter for processing **spatial contrast** (edges, and the contextual influences on receptive fields), retinex theory says context matters equally for processing **colour** (Shevell and Kingdom, 2008).

> **Trap.** Three colour terms are routinely confused. **Component (trichromatic)** theory explains colour **matching** and the three **cone** types. **Opponent-process** theory explains **afterimages** and the impossibility of **reddish green**. **Retinex** theory (**Land, 1977**) explains **colour constancy**, through **reflectance** computed by comparing **adjacent surfaces**. Colour constancy is explained by **neither** component nor opponent processing. And "retinex" combines *retina* and *cortex*, signalling that the comparison is not done by receptors alone.

## 6.6 Cortical Mechanisms of Vision and Conscious Awareness

So far the signal has been followed from the eyes to the primary visual cortex. That is far from the end of the story. Humans are visual animals: the **entire occipital cortex** and **large parts of the temporal and parietal cortex** are involved in vision.

### Three classes of visual cortex

Visual cortex is usually divided into three classes, defined by **where each gets its input**:

| Class | Receives most of its input from | Location |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary visual cortex** | The visual relay nuclei of the thalamus (the **lateral geniculate nuclei**) | **Posterior occipital lobe**, much of it hidden in the **longitudinal fissure** |
| **Secondary visual cortex** | The **primary visual cortex** | Mainly two regions: the **prestriate cortex** and the **inferotemporal cortex** |
| **Visual association cortex** | **Secondary visual cortex**, plus the secondary areas of **other sensory systems** | Several parts of cortex; the **largest single area** is in the **posterior parietal cortex** |

Two of those regions need defining:

- The **prestriate cortex** is the band of occipital tissue that **surrounds the primary visual cortex**.
- The **inferotemporal cortex** is the cortex of the **inferior temporal lobe**.

The main flow of visual information runs **primary to secondary to association** cortex. As you move up this hierarchy, neurons have **larger receptive fields**, and the stimuli they respond to become **more specific and more complex**. This continues the trend Hubel and Wiesel saw from retina to complex cells.

> **Trap.** The three classes are defined by **input**, not by location alone. Association cortex is the class that also receives input from **other senses**, which is what makes it "association". The **posterior parietal cortex** is association cortex (the largest visual association area); the **prestriate** and **inferotemporal** cortices are **secondary** visual cortex.

### Damage to primary visual cortex: scotomas and perimetry

Damage to an area of primary visual cortex produces a **scotoma**, an **area of blindness**, in the **corresponding part of the contralateral visual field of both eyes**. That follows directly from 6.3: each hemisphere's V1 receives the opposite visual field from both eyes, and the map is retinotopic, so a hole in the map is a hole in the corresponding part of the field.

Neurological patients suspected of primary visual cortex damage are usually given a **perimetry test**:

1. The patient's head is held still on a **chin rest**.
2. The patient fixes **one eye** on a fixation point on a screen.
3. A small dot of light is flashed at various positions on the screen, and the patient presses a button whenever the dot is seen.
4. The procedure is repeated for the **other eye**.

The result is a **map of the left and right visual fields of each eye**, showing any areas of blindness. The book illustrates the perimetric maps of a man with a **bullet wound in his left primary visual cortex**: each eye shows a **large scotoma in the right visual field**, exactly as the contralateral, both-eyes rule predicts.

### Completion across a scotoma

Many patients with scotomas are **not consciously aware** of their deficits. One reason is **completion**, the same filling-in you met at the blind spot. A patient looking at a complex figure part of which falls in the scotoma often reports seeing the **complete** figure.

Sometimes this might depend on **residual vision** inside the scotoma. But completion also occurs where that explanation can be **ruled out**. **Hemianopsic** patients (those with a **scotoma covering half the visual field**) may see a whole face when they look at a person's nose, even when the side of the face falling in the scotoma has been **covered with a blank card**. There is no light from that half of the face to detect, so the half-face they report must be constructed.

> **Trap.** **Hemianopsia** is a scotoma covering **half** the visual field (from damage to one hemisphere's primary visual cortex it is the **contralateral** half of **both** eyes, so the clinical name is homonymous hemianopia). A scotoma is any area of blindness; hemianopsia is a large, specific kind of scotoma. Patients often do not notice their scotomas because of **completion**, not because they have learned to ignore them.

### The Physiological Psychologist Who Made Faces Disappear

The book illustrates completion with a case from a famous source: **Karl Lashley**, the eminent physiological psychologist and an early leader in brain-behaviour research, describing his own experience in **1941**. During migraine attacks Lashley often developed a **large scotoma next to his fovea**.

In the episode he described, he was talking with a friend and happened to direct his gaze just to the right of the friend's face. The friend's **head vanished** into the scotoma. His shoulders and necktie remained visible, and the vertical stripes of the **wallpaper** behind him appeared to run **continuously down to the necktie**, filling the space where the head should have been. Lashley found that he could not see the area as a blank patch when it lay over a uniformly patterned surface such as the striped wallpaper, even though any object actually in that region went unseen.

This is a vivid demonstration of **surface interpolation** at work (the link the book itself draws). Lashley's visual system took the edge and pattern information surrounding the scotoma and extended it across the gap, replacing the missing head with the most likely continuation of its surround: more wallpaper. The book's later summary describes it as Lashley using his scotoma to turn his friend's head into a wallpaper pattern.

### Blindsight

Everyday thinking treats perceiving and being aware of perceiving as inseparable: if you saw something, you can say that you saw it and describe it. The rest of this module presents cases where that assumption fails. **Blindsight** is the first.

**Blindsight** is the capacity to **respond to visual stimuli presented in a scotoma while having no conscious awareness of them**. It is sometimes shown by patients whose scotomas result from damage to primary visual cortex. Of all visual abilities, the **perception of motion** is the one most likely to survive damage to the primary visual cortex. A patient with blindsight might, for example, **reach out and catch a moving object** in the scotoma while insisting throughout that she cannot see it.

### The Case of D.B., the Man Confused by His Own Blindsight

**D.B.** was blind in his **left visual field** after **surgical removal of his right occipital lobe** (**Weiskrantz and colleagues, 1974**). Note that the lesion side and the field side are opposite, as 6.3 predicts.

The key observations:

- D.B. reported **no awareness of seeing** anything in his left field; asked about it, he usually said he saw nothing.
- Nevertheless, he could **reach accurately** for visual stimuli presented there.
- When **forced to guess**, he could reliably tell whether a line in his blind field was **horizontal or diagonal**.
- When shown a **video of his own accurate performance** (viewed through his intact right field), he was **astonished** and maintained that he had only been guessing.

D.B.'s own confusion is the point of the case: the information was clearly being processed, because his behaviour was accurate, but the processing did not reach the level of experience he could report.

### Two explanations of blindsight

Two neurological interpretations have been proposed:

1. **Islands of spared striate cortex.** The striate cortex is **not completely destroyed**, and surviving islands of functional cells mediate some visual abilities without conscious awareness (Wüst, Kasten and Sabel, 2002).
2. **Pathways that bypass V1.** Visual pathways ascend directly from **subcortical visual structures** to the **secondary visual cortex**, without passing through the primary visual cortex, and these can sustain some visual abilities without awareness (Schmid and Maier, 2015). This is where the roughly 10 percent of ganglion cell axons that do not join the geniculostriate route (for example the projection to the superior colliculus) become relevant.

There is **some support for both**, but it is **far from conclusive** in either case, and **both mechanisms may contribute**.

> **Trap.** Blindsight is **not** malingering and **not** residual conscious vision: it is accurate **behavioural** responding with **denial of awareness**. The ability most likely to survive is **motion** perception. D.B.'s lesion was in the **right** occipital lobe and his blind field was the **left**. The two explanations (spared islands of V1; subcortical routes to secondary cortex that bypass V1) are both still open; an option saying one has been proven is wrong.

### Functional areas of secondary and association visual cortex

Secondary visual cortex and the visual parts of association cortex are not uniform. Each is made up of **many different areas**, each specialised for a particular kind of visual analysis.

The **macaque monkey** has the most thoroughly mapped visual cortex, with **32 different functional areas**: the primary visual cortex plus **24 areas of secondary visual cortex** and **7 areas of association visual cortex**. Three kinds of evidence distinguish the areas:

- neurons in each area respond most vigorously to **different aspects** of visual stimuli (for example colour, movement or shape);
- **selective lesions** of different areas produce **different visual losses**; and
- the areas differ **anatomically and organisationally**.

The areas are prodigiously **interconnected**. **Anterograde and retrograde tracing** studies have identified **more than 300 interconnecting pathways** (Markov and Kennedy, 2013), and connections between areas are **virtually always reciprocal**.

In **humans**, areas of visual cortex have been identified with **PET, fMRI and evoked potentials** (the methods of Chapter 5), by monitoring brain activity while volunteers look at different kinds of stimuli and finding the regions activated by particular properties such as movement or colour. About **a dozen** functional areas of human visual cortex have been delineated so far (Grill-Spector and Malach, 2004), and most resemble areas already identified in the macaque in location, anatomy and function. The book's map labels **V1 (primary)**, **V2**, **V3**, **V3A**, **V4** and **MT (also called V5)**.

> **Trap.** Macaque numbers: **32** functional areas in total = **1** primary + **24** secondary + **7** association; **more than 300** interconnecting pathways, almost all **reciprocal**. Humans: **about a dozen** areas identified so far. MT and **V5** are the same area. The 11th edition does not discuss V4 beyond labelling it, but older keys associate **V4 with colour**, and you may see that pairing in Indian papers.

### Dorsal and ventral streams

Most visual information enters the primary visual cortex from the lateral geniculate nuclei. There the inputs from the two lateral geniculate nuclei are **combined** and then **segregated again** into **multiple pathways** projecting separately to the various functional areas of secondary and then association cortex.

Many of these pathways can be grouped into **two major streams** (**Ungerleider and Mishkin, 1982**):

| Stream | Route |
|---|---|
| **Dorsal stream** | Primary visual cortex to **dorsal prestriate cortex** to **posterior parietal cortex** |
| **Ventral stream** | Primary visual cortex to **ventral prestriate cortex** to **inferotemporal cortex** |

What do neurons in each stream respond to?

- Most neurons in the **dorsal stream** respond most strongly to **spatial** stimuli, such as those indicating an object's **location** or its **direction of movement**.
- Most neurons in the **ventral stream** respond to the **characteristics of objects**, such as **colour and shape**. The ventral stream even contains **clusters** of neurons each responding to a particular **class** of object: one cluster mostly to **faces**, another to **animals**, and so on.

The interesting question is how to interpret this division. The book gives two theories, and the difference between them is the most examined conceptual point in the module.

### The "where" versus "what" theory

**Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** proposed that the two streams carry out different perceptual functions:

- the **dorsal stream** is involved in perceiving **where** objects are;
- the **ventral stream** is involved in perceiving **what** objects are.

A major implication is that damage to some cortical areas could **abolish some aspects of vision while leaving others intact**. The most convincing support came from comparing the effects of damage to the two streams (Ungerleider and Haxby, 1994):

- Patients with **posterior parietal** damage (dorsal stream) often have difficulty **reaching accurately** for objects they have **no difficulty describing**.
- Patients with **inferotemporal** damage (ventral stream) often have **no difficulty reaching accurately** for objects they **cannot describe**.

That double dissociation looks like exactly what the theory predicts: spatial "where" information lost with dorsal damage, object "what" information lost with ventral damage.

### The "control of behaviour" versus "conscious perception" theory

**Goodale and Milner (1992)** offered an alternative reading of the **same evidence**. They argued that the two streams differ not in the **kind of information** they carry but in the **use** to which that information is put:

- the primary function of the **dorsal stream** is to **direct behavioural interactions with objects** (visually guided action);
- the primary function of the **ventral stream** is to mediate **conscious perception of objects**.

The label **"control of behaviour" versus "conscious perception"** was attached to this position by Logothetis and Sheinberg (1996). One of its most interesting features is evolutionary: **Goodale (2004)** suggested that the **conscious awareness** mediated by the ventral stream is one of the things that distinguish **humans and their close relatives** from their evolutionary ancestors.

**How it explains the old evidence.** The new theory accounts readily for the two findings that founded the where-versus-what theory. Look at **how** each kind of test is done:

- Tests of **location and movement** are mostly **performance** measures (reach, point, track). Patients with **dorsal** damage do badly on them because those tests require **visually guided action**.
- Tests of **visual recognition** are mostly **verbal** (name it, describe it), and so depend on **conscious awareness**. Patients with **ventral** damage do badly on them because those tests require **conscious perception**.

So the original dissociation is confounded with response type, and both theories can explain it. What separates them is a **new pair of predictions** that only the control-versus-perception theory makes:

1. Some patients with **bilateral ventral stream** lesions may have **no conscious experience of seeing** an object's properties yet still **interact with the object accurately** under visual guidance.
2. Some patients with **bilateral dorsal stream** lesions may **consciously see** objects yet be **unable to interact with them** accurately under visual guidance.

The confirmation of both predictions, in the two cases that follow, is the major support for the theory.

| | **"Where" vs "what"** (Ungerleider and Mishkin, 1982) | **"Control of behaviour" vs "conscious perception"** (Goodale and Milner, 1992) |
|---|---|---|
| Streams differ in | The **kind of information** carried | The **use** made of the information |
| Dorsal stream | Visual **spatial perception** ("where") | **Visually guided behaviour** |
| Ventral stream | Visual **pattern recognition** ("what") | **Conscious visual perception** |
| Predicts after dorsal damage | Spatial perception disrupted | Visually guided behaviour disrupted, **conscious perception spared** |
| Predicts after ventral damage | Pattern recognition disrupted | Conscious perception disrupted, **visually guided behaviour spared** |

### D.F., the Woman Who Could Grasp Objects She Did Not Consciously See

In **D.F.**, the **ventral prestriate cortex** is damaged on **both sides**, which cuts the ventral stream; her case is described by **Goodale and Milner (2004)**. (Outside this edition, D.F.'s damage is well known to have followed carbon monoxide poisoning, and her condition is usually classed as **visual form agnosia**.)

She cannot consciously recognise the **size, shape or orientation** of objects, yet her hand movements towards the same objects are accurate. Two dissociations make the point:

- **Size.** Asked to *show* the width of a block by separating her index finger and thumb, her estimates were variable and **unrelated to the block's real size**. Asked to **pick up** blocks of different sizes, her finger-thumb opening **scaled correctly** with each block as she reached. Her hand "knew" a size she could not report.
- **Orientation.** Shown a large **slanted slot**, she could not indicate its orientation either in words or by matching it with her hand. Asked to **post a card** through it, she did so as quickly and accurately as healthy volunteers, with her hand **correctly rotated from the very start** of the movement.

This is exactly prediction 1: ventral damage abolishing conscious perception while sparing visually guided action.

### A.T., the Woman Who Could Not Accurately Grasp Unfamiliar Objects That She Saw

**A.T.** is in major respects the mirror image of D.F. She has a lesion of the **occipitoparietal region**, which likely interrupts the **dorsal** stream (**Jeannerod and colleagues, 1995**).

- She could **recognise objects** and **show their size** with her fingers: conscious perception intact.
- But the **preshaping** of her hand as she reached for objects was wrong. She could not pick objects up between her fingertips and instead made **awkward palmar grasps** (grabbing with the whole hand).
- One revealing exception: although she could not preshape her hand for **neutral objects** such as blocks, she grasped a **familiar object of standard size**, such as a **lipstick**, with reasonable accuracy.

This is prediction 2: dorsal damage sparing conscious perception while disrupting visually guided action. The lipstick exception has a natural interpretation in the theory's own terms: for a familiar object, the intact ventral stream can supply stored knowledge of its usual size, partly compensating for the missing dorsal computation. (The clinical name for misreaching of this kind after dorsal damage is **optic ataxia**; the book does not use the term.)

> **Trap.** The pairing of patients and lesions is the commonest error. **D.F.** = **ventral** (bilateral ventral prestriate) damage = **cannot report** size, shape or orientation but **grasps accurately**; described by **Goodale and Milner (2004)**. **A.T.** = **dorsal** (occipitoparietal) damage = **can report** size but **grasps badly**, except for familiar objects such as a lipstick; **Jeannerod and colleagues (1995)**. And keep the two theories' authors straight: **Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** = **where vs what**; **Goodale and Milner (1992)** = **control of behaviour vs conscious perception** (the name itself from Logothetis and Sheinberg, 1996).

### Is the two-stream dichotomy still tenable?

The widely held view that the dorsal stream controls visually guided behaviour while the ventral stream controls visual perception is now **under considerable scrutiny**. Recent studies in humans and other species have found that:

- the **dorsal** stream carries information **about objects** (Bi, Wang and Caramazza, 2016; Freud, Plaut and Behrmann, 2016); and
- the **ventral** stream is **active during interactions** with objects (Connor and Knierim, 2017).

Both findings are the **opposite** of what the control-versus-perception theory predicts. They suggest instead that **both streams carry information about what an object is and how to interact with it**. The book's conclusion is that the idea of two functionally separate streams within the higher visual areas **may no longer be tenable**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition does **not** present the Goodale and Milner theory as settled. It presents it as the better account of D.F. and A.T., then reports evidence (dorsal object information, ventral activity during action) that challenges **any** clean two-stream dichotomy. An option claiming the dorsal and ventral streams have been shown to be completely independent is wrong.

### Prosopagnosia

The rest of the chapter deals with two neuropsychological conditions and the cortical damage linked to each:

| Condition | Difficulty | Associated damage |
|---|---|---|
| **Prosopagnosia** | **Recognising faces** | **Fusiform face area (FFA)** and/or **occipital face area (OFA)** |
| **Akinetopsia** | **Perceiving visual motion** | **Area MT** (middle temporal) |

Start with the vocabulary, because it is examined directly:

- **Agnosia** is a **failure of recognition** (*gnosis* means "to know") that is **not attributable** to a sensory deficit or to verbal or intellectual impairment.
- A **visual agnosia** is an agnosia specific to **visual** stimuli. Visual agnosics can see things, but they do not know what they are.
- Visual agnosias are often specific to one aspect of visual input and are named for it: **movement agnosia**, **object agnosia** and **colour agnosia** are difficulties in recognising movement, objects and colours respectively.

**Prosopagnosia**, put briefly, is a **visual agnosia for faces**. Prosopagnosics can recognise a face **as a face**; their problem is recognising **whose** face it is. In extreme cases they **cannot recognise themselves in photographs**.

Prosopagnosia can be **developmental** (arising during development) or **acquired** (resulting from brain injury) (Susilo and Duchaine, 2013).

### Is prosopagnosia specific to faces?

It seems obvious that prosopagnosia is a face-specific deficit, because the diagnosis is normally given to patients who have trouble recognising particular faces but can readily identify other test objects such as a chair, a dog or a tree. The book asks you to see why this evidence is **seriously flawed**.

The flaw is a mismatch of **levels**. Prosopagnosics have no trouble recognising a face **as a face**, which is a **category**-level judgement. Recognising a chair as a chair is also a category-level judgement. So showing that they can name chairs tells you nothing about whether their problem is limited to faces. The fair comparison is at the **individual** level: can they tell **which** chair, **which** pencil, **which** door?

When patients are tested this way, their recognition deficits usually turn out **not to be restricted to faces**. The book's example is a **farmer** who lost the ability to recognise **his individual cows** at the same time as he lost the ability to recognise faces. This suggests that some prosopagnosics have a general problem recognising **specific members of complex classes** of objects (particular cars, particular houses), not a face-specific problem (Behrmann and colleagues, 2005), although in daily life the trouble with faces is naturally the most noticeable and disabling.

Still, it is hard to rule out that **at least a few** prosopagnosics have deficits limited to faces. Several thorough case studies have failed to find recognition deficits unrelated to faces (Duchaine and Nakayama, 2005). The likely conclusion is that **prosopagnosia is not a unitary disorder** (Duchaine and Nakayama, 2006): only some patients with the diagnosis have deficits restricted to faces.

### R.P., a Typical Prosopagnosic

**R.P.** (Laeng and Caviness, 2001) illustrates the testing problem. On **routine** testing he showed a severe deficit in recognising faces and in identifying **facial expressions**, but no other obvious recognition problems. Had testing stopped there, as it often does, he would have been classed as an agnosic whose problem was specific to human faces. **More thorough** testing showed that R.P. was deficient at recognising **all objects with complex curved surfaces**, not just faces.

> **Trap.** "Prosopagnosics can recognise chairs, so the deficit is face-specific" is the flawed argument the book dismantles: the comparison must be **within-category** (which face vs which chair). The book's conclusion is **not** that prosopagnosia is never face-specific; it is that the disorder is **not unitary** and only **some** patients have deficits restricted to faces. **R.P.** = deficit with **all complex curved surfaces**; the **farmer** = could no longer recognise **his cows**.

### Brain pathology in prosopagnosia: FFA and OFA

Acquired prosopagnosia is usually associated with damage to **either or both** of two areas:

- The **fusiform face area (FFA)**, on the **ventral surface** of the boundary between the **occipital and temporal lobes**. It is implicated in **face identification** because parts of it are **selectively activated by human faces**, and because **electrical stimulation** of it in humans can **transform a face being viewed into a completely different face** (Rangarajan and colleagues, 2014).
- The **occipital face area (OFA)**, on the **ventral surface of the occipital lobe**. **Reversible inactivation** of the OFA with **transcranial magnetic stimulation** selectively disrupts the ability to **discriminate between faces** (Freiwald, Duchaine and Yovel, 2016).

Recent evidence also points to a role for the **lateral prefrontal cortex** in face identification (Kornblith and Tsao, 2017). It makes evolutionary sense that specialised face-perception mechanisms exist, given how central faces are to human social behaviour.

Notice the logic of the FFA evidence: activation shows correlation; stimulation that **changes** face perception shows that the area's activity can **cause** a change in what face is seen. The OFA evidence uses a third method, **reversible lesion** by TMS. Three methods converging on the same conclusion is stronger than any one.

### Recognition without awareness: Tranel and Damasio

Can prosopagnosics perceive faces **without conscious awareness**? **Tranel and Damasio (1985)** were the first to show that they can. They showed several prosopagnosic patients a series of photographs, some of **familiar** people and some of **unfamiliar** people. The patients said they recognised **none** of the faces. Yet familiar faces produced a **large skin conductance response**, which did **not** occur to unfamiliar faces. The faces were evidently being **recognised unconsciously** by undamaged parts of the brain.

This places prosopagnosia alongside blindsight and D.F.: in each, the brain demonstrably processes something the patient cannot consciously report.

> **Trap.** **Tranel and Damasio (1985)** measured **skin conductance** (an autonomic response), not reaction time or verbal report; the larger response was to **familiar** faces the patients **denied** recognising. **FFA** = ventral **occipitotemporal** boundary; **OFA** = ventral **occipital** lobe. Electrical stimulation of the **FFA** changed one face into another; **TMS** of the **OFA** disrupted face **discrimination**.

### Akinetopsia

**Akinetopsia** is a deficiency in the ability to see **movement progress in a normal, smooth fashion**; affected people see the world as a series of **periodic snapshots**. It can be either:

- a **permanent** result of **brain damage**, or
- a **transient** result of taking **high doses of certain antidepressants** (Horton, 2009).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition illustrates akinetopsia with **drug-induced** cases. The classic brain-damage case in the wider literature, patient **L.M.** (Zihl and colleagues, 1983), who after bilateral damage near the occipitotemporal junction saw pouring liquid as frozen and people as suddenly jumping position, is not described in this edition. Older keys may still name her, and the anatomy (area MT) is the same.

### Area MT: four lines of evidence

When akinetopsia follows acquired brain injury, it is often associated with damage to **area MT** (the **middle temporal area**, also labelled **V5**), which lies near the **junction of the temporal, parietal and occipital lobes**.

The function of MT appears to be the **perception of motion**. Why should one area be dedicated to that? Some neurons lower in the hierarchy, in V1 for example, respond to movement as well as to colour and shape, but they say little about the **direction** of movement, because their receptive fields are so **small**. MT is different:

- **95 percent** of MT neurons respond to **specific directions of movement** and to little else.
- Each MT neuron has a **large, binocular receptive field**, so it can follow movement over a wide region.

Four lines of research implicate MT in motion perception and MT damage in akinetopsia, and between them they use four different methods:

1. **Lesion (clinical):** patients with akinetopsia tend to have **unilateral or bilateral damage to MT** (Cooper and colleagues, 2012).
2. **Imaging:** **fMRI** shows MT activity **increases when people view movement**.
3. **Reversible lesion:** blocking MT activity with **TMS** produces **motion blindness**.
4. **Stimulation:** **electrical stimulation** of MT in human patients **induces the perception of motion** (Blanke and colleagues, 2002).

The combination is what makes the case strong: removing MT (by damage or TMS) removes motion perception, activating it artificially produces motion perception, and its natural activity tracks viewing motion.

### Two Cases of Drug-Induced Akinetopsia

The book ends with two patients whose akinetopsia was caused by the antidepressant **nefazodone**:

| Patient | Dose | What they saw |
|---|---|---|
| A **47-year-old depressed man** | **100 mg** of nefazodone **twice daily** | Every moving object was trailed by **multiple freeze-frame images**, which disappeared once the motion stopped |
| A **48-year-old woman** | **400 mg** of nefazodone **once daily at bedtime** | Similar symptoms: persistent, **strobe-like trails** of multiple images following moving objects |

In both, **stationary** things looked normal, so the impairment was **selective to motion perception**. And in both, **vision returned to normal once the dose was reduced**. The cases show that akinetopsia can be produced pharmacologically and reversibly, and that the symptom matches the "series of snapshots" description of the brain-damage form.

> **Trap.** Get the doses the right way round: the **man (47)** took **100 mg twice daily**; the **woman (48)** took **400 mg once at bedtime**. The drug is **nefazodone**, an antidepressant, and the akinetopsia was **transient**, ending when the dose was **reduced**. Akinetopsia is linked to **MT**, prosopagnosia to the **FFA/OFA**; do not swap them.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all six modules. If you can trace each one from start to finish, you understand the chapter rather than just remembering it.

1. **Vision constructs; it does not copy.** The blind spot is filled in, large surfaces are inferred from their edges (surface interpolation), Mach bands make edges sharper than the light, colour stays put when the wavelengths change (colour constancy), and Lashley's friend's head turned into wallpaper. Every one of these is the same claim: the percept is built from a few extracted features, and in some respects it is better than the physical input.
2. **The sensitivity-acuity trade-off.** It appears first at the pupil (dilate for sensitivity, constrict for acuity and depth of focus), then in the retina's wiring (high rod convergence buys sensitivity and loses location; low cone convergence keeps location and loses sensitivity), and again in the M and P channels. The duplexity theory is this trade-off solved by running two systems in parallel.
3. **The system signals change and contrast, not absolute levels.** Stabilised images fade; rods signal light by a *decrease* in glutamate; on-centre and off-centre cells ignore diffuse light; simple and complex cells ignore it too; MT neurons fire to direction of motion. A system made of contrast detectors needs completion to supply the uniform parts, which ties this thread back to the first.
4. **Maps and magnification.** Retinotopy runs from retina to cortex, and the fovea's 25 percent share of V1 explains both the acceleration of Mrs. Richards's fortification illusion and why a lesion of V1 gives a scotoma in a predictable place in the contralateral field of both eyes. Dobelle's cortical prosthesis is retinotopy run in reverse.
5. **Complexity grows by convergence and specialisation.** Circular centre-surround fields at three levels, then rectangular, orientation-tuned simple cells, then position-invariant, often binocular complex cells, then 32 macaque areas with specialised functions (MT for motion, FFA and OFA for faces). At each step receptive fields grow larger and preferences more specific. The 11th edition then complicates the picture: receptive fields turn out to be shaped by context and experience, and some "cortical" properties are already present in ganglion cells.
6. **Seeing is not the same as being aware of seeing.** Blindsight (D.B.), D.F.'s accurate grasping, Tranel and Damasio's skin conductance responses to familiar faces, and patients' unawareness of their own scotomas all show visual information being processed without conscious report. The control-of-behaviour versus conscious-perception theory turns this into a claim about brain organisation, and the latest evidence questions even that.

The book frames the chapter around its four major themes. **Evolutionary perspective**: most research on human vision is comparative (cats, monkeys, mice, horseshoe crabs, dragonflies), and adaptive thinking explains eye position, duplexity and colour constancy. **Thinking creatively**: the main lesson is that the intuitive picture of vision as passive image transmission is fundamentally wrong. **Clinical implications**: Mrs. Richards, Lashley, D.B., D.F., A.T., R.P., and the two nefazodone patients. **Neuroplasticity**: receptive fields, once thought fixed, change with visual context. The emerging theme of **consciousness** runs through the dorsal-ventral debate and the unconscious processing seen in scotomas, blindsight and prosopagnosia.

## Worked example

*A 62-year-old retired engineer is referred after a stroke. He bumps into door frames on his left, misses food on the left half of his plate, and says his vision is "fine, just a bit tired". On perimetry, both eyes show a dense scotoma in the left visual field. When an examiner moves a pen slowly into his blind field and asks him to point to it, he says he sees nothing but points close to it on most trials. Shown photographs of family members on the intact side, he names them easily. His daughter asks whether "his eyes are damaged" and whether he is "making it up".*

Reason it through with the chapter rather than recalling isolated facts.

**Where is the lesion?** A scotoma in the **same (left) half of the field of both eyes** cannot come from one eye or one optic nerve, which would affect only that eye. It must be at or beyond the **optic chiasm**, where each hemisphere's fibres carry the opposite visual field from both eyes. Given a stroke and the field pattern, the most likely site is the **right primary visual cortex** (or the right optic radiation feeding it). This is a **hemianopsic** pattern: the contralateral visual field of both eyes, exactly as the bullet-wound perimetry in the book shows for the opposite side.

**Are his eyes damaged?** Probably not. The retina and optic nerves can be perfectly healthy while the **cortical map** that receives their signals has a hole in it. The daughter's question rests on the camera model of vision the chapter rejects.

**Why does he say his vision is fine?** Two mechanisms from the chapter. Many patients are **unaware of their scotomas**, partly because of **completion**: the visual system fills in the missing region from its surround, so he does not experience a black hole, just as hemianopsic patients may see a whole face when half is hidden by a card. Unawareness is a feature of the condition, not denial.

**Is he making up the pointing?** No. Accurate localisation of a moving stimulus in a blind field, together with sincere denial of seeing it, is **blindsight**. **Motion** is the visual ability most likely to survive V1 damage, which fits his best performance with a moving pen. The pattern is the same as **D.B.** (Weiskrantz and colleagues, 1974), who was astonished by video of his own accurate performance and insisted he had been guessing. The two candidate mechanisms are **spared islands of striate cortex** and **subcortical routes to secondary visual cortex that bypass V1**; the evidence does not yet decide between them and both may contribute.

**What should you check next?** Since his face naming is intact on the good side, there is no sign of **prosopagnosia**, which would point to **FFA/OFA** damage on the ventral occipitotemporal surface rather than to V1. If he showed accurate naming but awkward palmar grasps, that would suggest **dorsal stream** (occipitoparietal) involvement, as in **A.T.**; accurate grasping of objects he cannot describe would suggest **ventral** damage, as in **D.F.** Reports of the world moving in jerky snapshots would raise **area MT** damage (akinetopsia).

**What would you tell the family?** That the problem is in how the brain receives the image, not in the eyes; that he is not imagining his blind half-field and is not faking his pointing; that because he is not fully aware of the blind area, safety practices (scanning to the left, placing food and objects on his right) matter more than telling him to "look harder"; and that the residual, unconscious visual abilities seen in blindsight are real and are of research and possible rehabilitation interest.

## MCQ traps

1. **Photopic vs scotopic.** Photopic = **cones**, good light, colour, high acuity, low sensitivity, peak **~560 nm**. Scotopic = **rods**, dim light, no colour, low acuity, high sensitivity, peak **~500 nm**.
2. **The fovea has no rods.** Only cones; rod density peaks about **20 degrees** from the foveal centre; more rods in the **nasal** than the temporal hemiretina.
3. **Convergence, three meanings.** Eyes turning inwards for near objects (6.1); **several hundred rods vs a few cones** feeding one ganglion cell (6.2); simpler-preference neurons feeding complex-preference neurons (Hubel and Wiesel, 6.4).
4. **Duplexity theory origin: 1866**, from the observation that day-active species have cone-only and night-active species rod-only retinas.
5. **Nasal hemiretina fibres cross** at the optic chiasm; **temporal** fibres stay ipsilateral. Each hemisphere receives the **opposite visual field** from **both** eyes.
6. **Pupil.** Constricted (bright light) = sharper image, **greater depth of focus**; dilated (dim light) = sensitivity at the cost of acuity and depth of focus.
7. **Accommodation** = change in **lens shape** via the **ciliary muscles**; rounder for near, flattened for far. Not pupil size, not eye convergence.
8. **Binocular disparity** is **greater for near objects**; predators have front-facing eyes for depth, prey side-facing eyes for a wide field.
9. **Inside-out retina**: light passes through ganglion, amacrine, bipolar and horizontal cell layers **before** reaching receptors at the back. Two costs: distortion (reduced by the **fovea**, ~**0.33 cm**) and the **blind spot**.
10. **Horizontal and amacrine cells** are the **lateral** communicators; more than **60** retinal neuron types, about **30** ganglion cell types.
11. **Surface interpolation** = perceiving surfaces by inferring them from **edge** information; the colour and brightness of large uniform areas are **filled in**, not seen directly.
12. **Purkinje effect (Jan Purkinje, 1825)**: at dusk **blue** flowers look brighter grey than red and yellow ones, because peak sensitivity shifts from ~560 to ~500 nm.
13. **Fixational eye movements** are **involuntary**: **tremor, drifts, saccades**. Stabilised retinal images **fade**, because most visual neurons respond only to **change**.
14. **Rhodopsin** extracted **1876**; red pigment **bleached** by light; its **absorption spectrum** matches the **scotopic** curve. It is a **G-protein-coupled** receptor activated by light.
15. **Light hyperpolarises rods.** Dark: Na+ channels partly open, rods depolarised, **continuous glutamate release**. Light: channels close, hyperpolarisation, **less glutamate**. Signals can be carried by **decreases** in activity.
16. **About 90 percent** of ganglion cell axons enter the retina-geniculate-striate pathways; **about 25 percent** of V1 serves the **fovea**.
17. **LGN**: **six** layers, each from **one eye**, **contralateral visual field** only; three layers per eye. Geniculate input ends in **lower layer IV**, whose stripe gives **striate** cortex its name. V1 sends **as many** fibres back to the LGN.
18. **Parvocellular** = **top four** layers, small cells, colour, fine detail, stationary objects, mostly **cone** input. **Magnocellular** = **bottom two** layers, large cells, **movement**, mostly **rod** input.
19. **Dobelle, Mladejovsky and Girvin (1974)**: electrode array in V1 of **blind** patients; stimulating a cross-shaped pattern produced a **glowing cross**. Demonstrates **retinotopy**.
20. **Mach bands** are illusory: the light side looks lighter and the dark side darker at each edge (**contrast enhancement**). Older editions explain them by **lateral inhibition** (horseshoe crab); the 11th edition goes straight to receptive fields.
21. **Hubel and Wiesel**: cats and monkeys, microelectrode, **eye muscles paralysed**; Nobel Prize. **Hubel and Wiesel (1979)**: ganglion, LGN and lower layer IV fields are **circular, monocular, smaller in the fovea, centre-surround**.
22. **On-centre and off-centre cells** respond best to **contrast**; **diffuse light** over the whole field has **little effect**.
23. **Simple cells**: static straight-line on and off regions, **position- and orientation-specific**, rectangular, **all monocular**. **Complex cells**: **more numerous**, **larger** fields, **no static on/off regions**, respond anywhere in the field, many direction-selective, **many binocular (over half in monkeys)**.
24. **Ocular dominance** = binocular cell responds more to **one eye**; disparity-tuned binocular cells contribute to **depth**.
25. **Hubel and Wiesel's three organisational conclusions** (11th ed): vertical functional columns; clusters for one retinal area with half left-eye and half right-eye input and all orientations; complexity rises by **convergence**.
26. **Mrs. Richards**: illusion unchanged by closing either eye, so **cortical** (first binocular level); wave spreads at a **constant ~3 mm per minute**; perceived acceleration from **less cortex for the periphery**. Airy's drawings **1870**; Richards's report **1971**; **20 percent** of migraine sufferers.
27. **Receptive fields are plastic**: about **20 (primate) / 40 (mouse)** ganglion cell types, some selective for **orientation, motion, direction, uniform light**; stimuli **outside** the field exert **contextual influences**.
28. **Component (trichromatic)**: Young **1802**, Helmholtz **1852**, three receptors, ratio code, colour matching with three wavelengths. **Opponent-process**: Hering **1878**, red-green, blue-yellow, black-white; explains **afterimages** and no reddish green.
29. **Both are right**: component at the **cones** (confirmed by single-cone spectra in the **early 1960s**, e.g. **Wald, 1964**), opponent at **all later levels** (DeValois and colleagues, 2000).
30. **Dichromats** (most non-primate mammals) lack the **long-wavelength** pigment; birds, fish, reptiles can have **four**; dragonfly **ten**. **Jacobs and colleagues (2007)** made **trichromatic mice** with a third photopigment gene.
31. **Colour constancy** is explained by **neither** component nor opponent processing. **Land (1977)**: three single-wavelength projectors, **Mondrians**; a red patch reflecting the exact wavelengths of a blue patch still looked **red**. **Retinex**: colour = **reflectance**, computed by comparing **adjacent surfaces** in three wavelength bands.
32. **Three classes of visual cortex** are defined by **input**: primary (from LGN), secondary (from primary; **prestriate** and **inferotemporal**), association (from secondary plus other senses; largest in **posterior parietal**).
33. **V1 damage** → **scotoma** in the **contralateral** field of **both eyes**; mapped by **perimetry**. **Hemianopsic** = half the field.
34. **Lashley (1941)**: migraine scotoma beside his fovea made a friend's **head vanish**, replaced by the **striped wallpaper**: completion by surface interpolation.
35. **D.B.** (Weiskrantz and colleagues, **1974**): **right** occipital removal, **left** field blind; reached accurately and discriminated horizontal from diagonal lines while denying seeing. **Motion** is the ability most likely to survive V1 damage.
36. **Macaque**: **32** visual areas (1 primary + **24** secondary + **7** association), **300+** reciprocal pathways. **Humans**: about **a dozen** areas.
37. **Where vs what** = **Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)**; **control of behaviour vs conscious perception** = **Goodale and Milner (1992)**. Dorsal = V1 → dorsal prestriate → **posterior parietal**; ventral = V1 → ventral prestriate → **inferotemporal**.
38. **D.F.** = **ventral** damage, grasps blocks and posts cards accurately but cannot report size or orientation. **A.T.** = **dorsal (occipitoparietal)**, reports size but makes **palmar grasps**, except for a familiar **lipstick**.
39. **Prosopagnosia** = recognises a face **as a face** but not **whose**. The "they can name chairs" argument is flawed (compare **which** chair). The **farmer** lost recognition of his **cows**; **R.P.** failed with all **complex curved surfaces**. Not a unitary disorder.
40. **FFA** (ventral occipitotemporal boundary; stimulation morphs faces) and **OFA** (ventral occipital; TMS disrupts discrimination). **Tranel and Damasio (1985)**: **skin conductance** responses to familiar faces not consciously recognised.
41. **Akinetopsia** = motion seen as snapshots; **MT**: **95 percent** of neurons direction-selective, large binocular fields. Nefazodone cases: **man 47, 100 mg twice daily**; **woman 48, 400 mg at bedtime**; both reversed on dose reduction.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Fortification illusion** | Migraine aura beginning as a grey blind patch near the centre of the field and expanding over about 20 minutes as a horseshoe with flickering zigzag lines |
| **Photons** | Discrete particles of light energy travelling at about 300,000 km per second |
| **Visible light** | Electromagnetic waves about 380 to 760 nm long, the band the human visual system responds to |
| **Iris** | Ring of contractile tissue that regulates pupil size and gives the eye its colour |
| **Pupil** | The opening in the iris through which light enters the eye |
| **Sensitivity** | The ability to detect the presence of dimly lit objects |
| **Acuity** | The ability to see the details of objects |
| **Lens** | Structure behind the pupil that focuses light on the retina |
| **Ciliary muscles** | Eye muscles that adjust the tension on the ligaments holding the lens, changing its shape |
| **Accommodation** | Adjusting the shape of the lens to bring images into focus on the retina |
| **Convergence (of the eyes)** | Inward turning of the eyes so that a near point falls on corresponding retinal points |
| **Binocular disparity** | The difference in the position of the same image on the two retinas; greater for near objects |
| **Retina** | Neural layer at the back of the eye that converts light to neural signals, conducts and processes them |
| **Receptors** | Retinal neurons (rods and cones) that absorb light |
| **Horizontal cells** | Retinal neurons specialised for lateral communication at the receptor-bipolar level |
| **Bipolar cells** | Retinal neurons carrying signals from receptors towards retinal ganglion cells |
| **Amacrine cells** | Retinal neurons specialised for lateral communication at the bipolar-ganglion level |
| **Retinal ganglion cells** | Retinal output neurons whose axons form the optic nerve |
| **Blind spot** | Gap in the receptor layer where ganglion cell axons leave the eye |
| **Fovea** | Central retinal indentation about 0.33 cm across, cones only, specialised for high-acuity vision |
| **Completion** | Filling in of missing parts of the visual image (blind spot, scotoma) by the visual system |
| **Surface interpolation** | Perceiving surfaces by extracting edge information and inferring the appearance of the areas between edges |
| **Cones** | Cone-shaped receptors mediating photopic, colour, high-acuity vision |
| **Rods** | Rod-shaped receptors mediating scotopic, sensitive, colourless, low-acuity vision |
| **Duplexity theory** | The theory that cones and rods mediate different kinds of vision |
| **Photopic vision** | Cone-mediated vision; predominates in good light |
| **Scotopic vision** | Rod-mediated vision; predominates in dim light |
| **Nasal hemiretina** | Half of each retina next to the nose; its fibres cross at the optic chiasm |
| **Temporal hemiretina** | Half of each retina next to the temples; its fibres stay ipsilateral |
| **Spectral sensitivity curve** | Graph of the relative brightness of equal-intensity lights of different wavelengths |
| **Photopic spectral sensitivity curve** | Spectral sensitivity measured with light on the fovea; peak about 560 nm |
| **Scotopic spectral sensitivity curve** | Spectral sensitivity measured with dim peripheral light; peak about 500 nm |
| **Purkinje effect** | Shift in relative brightness of colours (blues brighter, reds dimmer) as vision shifts from photopic to scotopic; Purkinje, 1825 |
| **Visual field** | The entire area that can be seen at a given moment |
| **Temporal integration** | Perception at any instant is a summation of recent visual input, so the world does not vanish when we blink |
| **Fixational eye movements** | Involuntary movements during fixation: tremor, drifts and saccades |
| **Saccades** | Small, jerky eye movements or flicks (in this chapter, one of the three fixational movements) |
| **Transduction** | Conversion of one form of energy into another |
| **Visual transduction** | Conversion of light into neural signals by the visual receptors |
| **Pigment** | Any substance that absorbs light |
| **Rhodopsin** | Red rod photopigment, a light-activated G-protein-coupled receptor, bleached by light |
| **Absorption spectrum** | Graph of how well a pigment absorbs light of each wavelength |
| **Retina-geniculate-striate pathways** | The main visual pathways, from retina via lateral geniculate nuclei to primary visual cortex |
| **Optic chiasm** | Point where the optic nerves meet and nasal hemiretina fibres cross |
| **Lateral geniculate nuclei** | Six-layered thalamic visual relay nuclei, each receiving the contralateral visual field |
| **Primary visual cortex (striate cortex, V1)** | Posterior occipital cortex receiving most of its input from the lateral geniculate nuclei; named striate for the stripe in lower layer IV |
| **Retinotopic** | Organised like a map of the retina, so adjacent retinal points excite adjacent neurons |
| **Parvocellular layers** | Top four LGN layers of small neurons; colour, fine detail, stationary objects; mostly cone input |
| **Magnocellular layers** | Bottom two LGN layers of large neurons; movement; mostly rod input |
| **Contrast enhancement** | Intensification of the perception of edges |
| **Mach bands** | Illusory bright and dark bands next to edges that enhance contrast |
| **Lateral inhibition** | Inhibition of neighbouring neurons by an active neuron; the classic (older-edition) explanation of Mach bands |
| **Receptive field** | The area of the visual field within which a stimulus can influence a neuron's firing |
| **Monocular** | Having a receptive field in only one eye |
| **On-centre cells** | Cells giving "on" firing to light in the field centre and inhibition then "off" firing to light in the surround |
| **Off-centre cells** | Cells giving inhibition then "off" firing to light in the centre and "on" firing to light in the surround |
| **Simple cells** | V1 neurons with rectangular fields divided into static straight-line on and off regions; all monocular |
| **Complex cells** | V1 neurons with larger rectangular fields, no static on/off regions, often binocular and direction-selective |
| **Binocular** | Responding to stimulation of either eye |
| **Ocular dominance** | A binocular cell's stronger response to one eye than the other |
| **Contextual influences** | Effects on a visual neuron of stimuli outside its receptive field |
| **Hue** | The technical term for colour (blue, green, yellow and so on) |
| **Component (trichromatic) theory** | Young (1802) and Helmholtz (1852): three receptor types; colour coded by the ratio of their activity |
| **Opponent-process theory** | Hering (1878): cells coding red-green, blue-yellow and black-white by opposite changes in activity |
| **Complementary colours** | Pairs of colours that produce white or grey when combined in equal measure |
| **Trichromats / dichromats** | Animals with three / two colour photopigments |
| **Colour constancy** | Tendency of an object to stay the same colour despite large changes in the wavelengths it reflects |
| **Mondrians** | Land's multi-coloured rectangular test displays, named after the painter Piet Mondrian |
| **Reflectance** | The proportion of light of each wavelength that a surface reflects |
| **Retinex theory** | Land's theory that colour is determined by reflectance, computed by comparing adjacent surfaces in three wavelength bands |
| **Secondary visual cortex** | Cortex receiving most of its input from primary visual cortex |
| **Visual association cortex** | Cortex receiving input from secondary visual cortex and from secondary areas of other senses |
| **Prestriate cortex** | Band of occipital cortex surrounding the primary visual cortex |
| **Inferotemporal cortex** | Cortex of the inferior temporal lobe; end of the ventral stream |
| **Posterior parietal cortex** | Largest visual association area; end of the dorsal stream |
| **Scotoma** | An area of blindness in the visual field |
| **Perimetry test** | Mapping each eye's visual field by flashing dots while the patient fixates |
| **Hemianopsic** | Having a scotoma that covers half of the visual field |
| **Conscious awareness** | Being able to acknowledge and describe what one has perceived |
| **Blindsight** | Responding to visual stimuli in a scotoma without conscious awareness of them |
| **Dorsal stream** | V1 to dorsal prestriate to posterior parietal cortex |
| **Ventral stream** | V1 to ventral prestriate to inferotemporal cortex |
| **"Where" versus "what" theory** | Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982): dorsal stream perceives where objects are, ventral stream what they are |
| **"Control of behaviour" versus "conscious perception" theory** | Goodale and Milner (1992): dorsal stream directs interactions with objects, ventral stream mediates conscious perception |
| **Agnosia** | Failure of recognition not due to sensory, verbal or intellectual impairment |
| **Visual agnosia** | Agnosia specific to visual stimuli: seeing without knowing what is seen |
| **Prosopagnosia** | Visual agnosia for faces; developmental or acquired |
| **Fusiform face area (FFA)** | Face-selective area on the ventral surface of the occipitotemporal boundary |
| **Occipital face area (OFA)** | Face-related area on the ventral surface of the occipital lobe |
| **Akinetopsia** | Deficiency in seeing smooth movement; motion seen as snapshots |
| **Area MT** | Middle temporal area (V5), near the temporal-parietal-occipital junction; specialised for motion perception |

## Likely exam points

1. **Light**: photons at about 300,000 km/s; visible band **380 to 760 nm**; wavelength chiefly affects colour, intensity chiefly affects brightness; rattlesnakes see infrared.
2. **Pupil trade-off**: constriction (bright light) gives acuity and depth of focus; dilation (dim light) gives sensitivity.
3. **Accommodation** by the **ciliary muscles**: rounder lens for near objects, flattened lens for far.
4. **Binocular disparity** is greater for near objects and is the basis of depth from two flat images; predators front-facing eyes, prey side-facing.
5. **Five retinal neuron types**: receptors, horizontal, bipolar, amacrine, retinal ganglion cells; horizontal and amacrine cells are lateral; over 60 types in total.
6. **Inside-out retina**: two problems, distortion (fovea, ~0.33 cm, thinned ganglion layer) and the blind spot (completion).
7. **Completion and surface interpolation**: the visual system sends edge information and constructs surfaces; completion occurs in normal vision, not just at the blind spot.
8. **Duplexity theory (1866)** and the full photopic versus scotopic comparison (acuity, colour, sensitivity, location, peaks).
9. **Convergence explains duplexity**: several hundred rods vs a few cones per ganglion cell; summation gives sensitivity, loss of location gives low acuity.
10. **Receptor distribution**: no rods in the fovea; rod peak at **20 degrees**; more rods in the nasal hemiretina.
11. **Spectral sensitivity curves**: photopic peak **560 nm** (fovea), scotopic peak **500 nm** (dim periphery); the **Purkinje effect (1825)**.
12. **Fixational eye movements**: tremor, drifts, saccades; stabilised images fade because neurons respond to change; temporal integration.
13. **Rhodopsin and transduction**: extracted 1876; bleaching; absorption spectrum matches the scotopic curve; light closes Na+ channels, hyperpolarises rods and **reduces** glutamate.
14. **Retina-geniculate-striate pathways**: 90 percent of ganglion cell axons; nasal fibres cross; each hemisphere receives the contralateral field from both eyes.
15. **LGN**: six layers, each from one eye; projection to lower layer IV (striation); reciprocal V1-to-LGN fibres.
16. **Retinotopy**: adjacent retinal points to adjacent neurons; fovea gets ~25 percent of V1; **Dobelle and colleagues (1974)** cortical prosthesis.
17. **M and P channels**: top four parvocellular (colour, detail, stationary; cones) vs bottom two magnocellular (movement; rods).
18. **Mach bands and contrast enhancement**; lateral inhibition as the classic mechanism.
19. **Hubel and Wiesel's method** and the definition of a **receptive field**.
20. **Four commonalities** of ganglion, LGN and lower layer IV fields; **on-centre vs off-centre** cells respond to contrast, not diffuse light.
21. **Simple vs complex cells**: static on/off vs position invariance; monocular vs often binocular; field size; orientation and direction.
22. **Binocular cells**: ocular dominance; disparity-tuned cells and depth perception.
23. **Hubel and Wiesel's three organisational conclusions**: vertical columns, systematic clustering by retinal location, eye and orientation, convergence of simpler onto complex preferences.
24. **Mrs. Richards revisited**: cortical location from the one-eye test; 3 mm per minute constant wave; acceleration from cortical magnification; lines from orientation-tuned neurons.
25. **Changing concept of receptive fields**: ganglion and LGN cells with orientation and motion selectivity; contextual influences; receptive fields as plastic.
26. **Component vs opponent-process theories**: authors, years, evidence; both correct at different levels (cones vs later stages).
27. **Colour constancy and retinex theory**: Land's (1977) Mondrian experiments; reflectance; comparison of adjacent surfaces; conditions for constancy.
28. **Three classes of visual cortex** and their locations; increasing receptive field size and specificity up the hierarchy.
29. **Scotomas and perimetry**; hemianopsia; completion; **Lashley (1941)** and the wallpaper.
30. **Blindsight** and **D.B.** (Weiskrantz and colleagues, 1974); motion survives best; two competing explanations.
31. **Dorsal vs ventral streams**: routes; **Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** vs **Goodale and Milner (1992)**; the D.F. and A.T. double dissociation; current doubts about the dichotomy.
32. **Prosopagnosia**: definition of agnosia; the flawed face-specificity argument; R.P. and the farmer; FFA and OFA evidence; **Tranel and Damasio (1985)** skin conductance.
33. **Akinetopsia and MT**: 95 percent direction-selective neurons; four lines of evidence (lesion, fMRI, TMS, stimulation); nefazodone cases and their reversibility.
