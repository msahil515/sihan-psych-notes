# Chapter 3: Anatomy of the Nervous System

**What this chapter is really about.** Every later chapter of this book makes claims of the form "structure X does function Y": the hippocampus and memory, the hypothalamus and eating, the substantia nigra and Parkinson's disease, the amygdala and fear. None of those claims can be followed until you know where X is, what it is built from and what it is connected to. Chapter 3 is the map. It has no experiments to evaluate and no theory to dismantle; its job is to hand you a working vocabulary and a mental three-dimensional model of the nervous system, from the level of whole divisions (central versus peripheral, somatic versus autonomic) down to single cells (neurons and the four main kinds of glia), and back up again to the named structures of the spinal cord and the five divisions of the brain.

The difficulty is not conceptual; it is the sheer number of names, many of them Latin or Greek, and the fact that examiners love the small distinctions between them: nuclei and ganglia, tracts and nerves, afferent and efferent, dorsal and ventral (which mean something different in the human head and the human trunk), the Golgi stain and the Nissl stain, the tectum and the tegmentum, the superior and the inferior colliculi. The way to master this material is to attach three things to every structure: **where it is** (which division, which surface, what it sits next to), **what it is made of** (cell bodies, axons, cortex with layers, a cluster of nuclei), and **what it does** (as far as the chapter commits to a function). The notes below teach every structure in exactly that order, and give the literal meaning of each name, because the literal meanings usually tell you the shape or position.

**How to read this chapter.** The 11th edition organises the chapter into four modules, numbered here 3.1 to 3.4. Section 3.1 lays out the **general plan** of the nervous system: its divisions, the three meninges, the ventricles and cerebrospinal fluid, and the blood-brain barrier. Section 3.2 zooms in to the **cells**: the external and internal anatomy of a neuron, the ways neurons are classified, the naming rules for clusters of cell bodies and bundles of axons, and the glial cells. Section 3.3 covers **how neuroanatomists see** the nervous system (stains, electron microscopy, tracing methods) and **how they describe positions** within it (anatomical directions and planes of section). Section 3.4 is the guided ascent through the **central nervous system**, from the spinal cord to the five divisions of the brain and finally the cerebral cortex, limbic system and basal ganglia. Sections 3.1 and 3.4 supply most of the MCQs; 3.3 supplies most of the "which technique would you use" and "which direction is this" questions.

## Before the Tour: A Chapter Without a Case

Unlike most chapters of the book, Chapter 3 does not open with a patient. Pinel and Barnes open instead with a half-joking apology on behalf of the early anatomists, who named brain structures in Latin and Greek because those were the shared languages of educated people in their time, never imagining that later students would not learn either. The authors' remedy is to give the plain-English meaning of each term as it is introduced and to keep the chapter short, covering only the structures that matter for the rest of the book. The payoff they promise is a basic understanding of how the human brain is built and a vocabulary for talking about it.

That remedy is worth using. The literal meaning of a neuroanatomical name very often encodes the structure's shape or position: the *tough mother* is the outermost meninx, the *little hills* are bumps on the midbrain roof, the *sea horse* is a curled strip of cortex, the *ring* (limbic) encircles the thalamus. These notes give each literal meaning where the term is introduced, and the Key terms table at the end repeats them.

> **Trap.** Because there is no named case in this chapter, questions that say "in the chapter on neuroanatomy, Pinel describes the case of..." are distractors. The only clinical conditions the chapter itself discusses are **hydrocephalus** (CSF blockage), **impairment of the blood-brain barrier** in CNS disorders, the use of **cranial nerve testing** to localise tumours, the effects of **cerebellar damage**, and **Parkinson's disease** (degeneration of the substantia nigra to striatum pathway).

## 3.1 General Layout of the Nervous System

This first module gives the big picture: the two main divisions of the nervous system and how each is subdivided, then the three protective arrangements that surround and bathe the central nervous system (the meninges, the cerebrospinal fluid in the ventricles, and the blood-brain barrier).

### Divisions of the nervous system: central and peripheral

The vertebrate nervous system has two divisions, and the dividing line is a bony one. The **central nervous system (CNS)** is, roughly speaking, the part that lies inside the skull and the spine; the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is the part that lies outside them.

The CNS itself has two parts:

- the **brain**, the portion of the CNS inside the skull; and
- the **spinal cord**, the portion inside the spine (the vertebral column).

The PNS also has two parts, the **somatic nervous system** and the **autonomic nervous system**, and they are distinguished not by location but by which environment they deal with.

A detail from Figure 3.1 that is easy to overlook and easy to examine: the PNS is defined by what kind of nerve something is, not strictly by where each piece of it sits. Even the parts of peripheral nerves that run *inside* the spinal cord are counted as belonging to the PNS.

### The somatic and autonomic nervous systems

The **somatic nervous system (SNS)** is the part of the PNS that **interacts with the external environment**. It contains two kinds of nerves:

- **afferent nerves**, which carry sensory signals *toward* the CNS from the skin, the skeletal muscles, the joints, the eyes, the ears and the other external sense organs; and
- **efferent nerves**, which carry motor signals *away from* the CNS to the **skeletal muscles**.

The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** is the part of the PNS that **regulates the body's internal environment**. It too contains both directions of traffic:

- **afferent nerves** carrying sensory signals from the **internal organs** to the CNS; and
- **efferent nerves** carrying motor signals from the CNS to the **internal organs**.

So the somatic/autonomic split is about *outside world versus inside body*, and the afferent/efferent split, which cuts across it, is about *direction of travel relative to the CNS*. Every peripheral nerve fibre can be placed on both dimensions at once: a fibre from a pain receptor in the skin is somatic afferent; a fibre that speeds the heart is autonomic efferent.

> **Trap.** In Pinel the abbreviation **SNS** means the **somatic** nervous system. In many physiology texts and in a good number of Indian question papers, "SNS" is used for the **sympathetic** nervous system. Read the expansion, not the abbreviation. Also note that the autonomic nervous system is not purely motor: Pinel explicitly gives it **afferent** (sensory) nerves from the internal organs as well as efferent ones. An option claiming "the ANS consists only of motor fibres" is wrong by this book's definition, even though the sympathetic/parasympathetic division (below) applies only to the efferent side.

### Afferent and efferent: the a/e rule

The two words differ by one letter and are reversed constantly under exam pressure. Pinel's memory aid works because it uses ordinary English. Words describing movement *toward* something often start with **a** (approach, arrive, advance), so **a**fferent nerves carry signals toward the CNS. Words describing movement *away* often start with **e** (exit, escape, embark), so **e**fferent nerves carry signals away from it. Sensory = afferent = inward; motor = efferent = outward.

### Sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves

The autonomic nervous system's *efferent* (motor) nerves come in two kinds, and the distinction rests first on **where they leave the CNS**.

- **Sympathetic nerves** are autonomic motor nerves that leave the CNS from the **thoracic** (chest) and **lumbar** (small of the back) regions of the spinal cord.
- **Parasympathetic nerves** are autonomic motor nerves that leave the CNS from the **brain** and from the **sacral** (lower back) region of the spinal cord.

Clinical texts compress this into two labels that are worth knowing even though Pinel does not use them: the sympathetic outflow is **thoracolumbar** and the parasympathetic outflow is **craniosacral**. Notice that the two outflows bracket each other: the parasympathetic system takes the two ends of the CNS (the brain at the top and the sacral cord at the bottom), and the sympathetic system takes the middle.

### Two-stage pathways

Both kinds of autonomic motor nerve share a design feature that the somatic motor system lacks: they are **two-stage neural paths**. The neuron that leaves the CNS does not reach the target organ. It travels only part of the way and synapses on a **second-stage neuron**, which carries the signal the rest of the distance.

Where the relay happens is the second difference between the two systems:

- **Sympathetic** neurons leaving the CNS synapse on second-stage neurons that are a **substantial distance from the target organs**. The relay is close to the spinal cord, so the second-stage neuron is long.
- **Parasympathetic** neurons leaving the CNS travel almost all the way and synapse **near the target organs**, on **very short second-stage neurons**.

Anatomists call the first-stage neuron preganglionic and the second-stage neuron postganglionic, because the relay synapse sits in a ganglion. In those terms: sympathetic = short preganglionic, long postganglionic; parasympathetic = long preganglionic, short postganglionic. Pinel's own wording only speaks of where the synapse lies relative to the target, so learn it that way first and translate if the question uses the other vocabulary.

> **Trap.** It is tempting to reason "sympathetic = fast emergency system, so it must have the short path". Length of path is not about speed here. The defining point is the **location of the synapse**: far from the target for sympathetic, near the target (with a very short second-stage neuron) for parasympathetic.

### The conventional view of the two autonomic systems, and its limits

Most students meet the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems as a neat pair of opposites. Pinel states the conventional view as three principles:

1. **Energy.** Sympathetic nerves stimulate, organise and mobilise energy resources when the organism is threatened; parasympathetic nerves act to **conserve** energy.
2. **Dual, opposing control.** Every autonomic target organ receives **both** sympathetic and parasympathetic input, working in opposite directions, so the organ's activity at any moment reflects the **relative** levels of the two.
3. **Psychological meaning.** Sympathetic changes signal **psychological arousal**; parasympathetic changes signal **psychological relaxation**.

The book's position is that all three are **generally correct but have significant qualifications and exceptions** (it cites Guyenet, 2006, and develops the exceptions in an appendix). So "every organ receives opposing sympathetic and parasympathetic input" is a generalisation, not a law. The usual textbook example of an exception (not spelled out in this chapter) is that some structures, such as most blood vessels and the sweat glands, are controlled largely by sympathetic input alone.

### Cranial nerves

Almost all peripheral nerves leave the CNS from the spinal cord. The exceptions are the **cranial nerves**: **12 pairs** of nerves that project from the **brain** instead. Key facts:

- They are **numbered in sequence from front to back** (Roman numerals I to XII).
- Some are **purely sensory**; the book's examples are the **olfactory nerves (I)** and the **optic nerves (II)**. Most cranial nerves, however, are **mixed**, carrying both sensory and motor fibres.
- The **longest** cranial nerves are the **vagus nerves (X)**, which carry both motor and sensory fibres to and from the **gut**.
- The **autonomic motor fibres** that travel in cranial nerves are all **parasympathetic**. This follows from the outflow rule above: the brain is one of the two parasympathetic origins, so any autonomic motor fibre leaving the brain must be parasympathetic.

Cranial nerves matter clinically because each has a specific location and a specific function. Neurologists routinely test them, and a pattern of disrupted cranial nerve functions tells them a great deal about **where** a tumour or other brain pathology is and **how far** it extends. Pinel lists the 12 nerves, their targets and their functions in appendices rather than in the chapter body.

> **Trap.** The book's own fill-in-the-blank exercise loosely calls the vagus "the nerve cell" running from brain to gut. The vagus is a **nerve**, a bundle of many axons, not a single neuron. Standard anatomy adds one more purely sensory cranial nerve to Pinel's two examples, the vestibulocochlear (VIII); Pinel's phrase "such as" means olfactory and optic are examples, not the complete list.

### Meninges

The brain and spinal cord are the **best-protected organs in the body**. They sit inside bone (the skull and vertebral column), and between the bone and the nervous tissue there are **three protective membranes**, the **meninges** (Pinel cites Coles et al., 2017). The singular of meninges is **meninx**.

From outside to inside:

1. **Dura mater** ("tough mother"): the **outer** meninx, a tough membrane.
2. **Arachnoid membrane** ("spider-web-like membrane"): a fine membrane lying immediately inside the dura.
3. **Subarachnoid space**: not a membrane but a gap beneath the arachnoid. It holds **many large blood vessels** and **cerebrospinal fluid**.
4. **Pia mater** ("pious mother"): the **innermost** meninx, delicate, and **adhering directly to the surface** of the brain and spinal cord.

To keep the order: the names soften as you go inward (tough mother, spider web, tender mother), or use **PAD** read from the brain outward (pia, arachnoid, dura).

> **Trap.** The cerebrospinal fluid around the brain is in the **subarachnoid** space, between the arachnoid and the pia, not between the dura and the arachnoid. And note what Pinel stresses about where the meninges are found: only the **CNS** (brain and spinal cord) is wrapped in the three meninges. Peripheral nerves are not.

### Ventricles and cerebrospinal fluid

A second line of protection is liquid. **Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)** fills three interconnected compartments:

- the **subarachnoid space** (just described);
- the **central canal**, a narrow channel running down the middle of the spinal cord for its whole length; and
- the **cerebral ventricles**, the **four** large internal chambers of the brain: the **two lateral ventricles**, the **third ventricle** and the **fourth ventricle** (Figure 3.3).

Because these compartments are linked by a series of openings, they form **one continuous reservoir** of fluid rather than separate pools.

What does the CSF do? It **supports and cushions** the brain: in effect the brain floats in it, so its weight is partly borne by the fluid and sudden movements of the head are buffered. The evidence the book offers is clinical: when some CSF has been drained, patients often suffer severe headaches and a stabbing pain with every sudden head movement, because the brain has lost part of its support and cushioning.

### How CSF is made and drained

The **traditional account** has two halves.

- **Production.** CSF is made by the **choroid plexuses**, networks of capillaries (small blood vessels) that project from the **pia mater** into the ventricles.
- **Absorption.** The fluid is produced continuously, so the excess has to go somewhere. It is absorbed from the **subarachnoid space** into large blood-filled channels called **dural sinuses**, which run through the dura mater and empty into the large **jugular veins** of the neck. Figure 3.4 shows this happening at the large sinus that runs along the top of the brain in the gap between the two cerebral hemispheres.

Put as a circuit: blood → choroid plexus → ventricles → (through the connecting openings) → subarachnoid space → dural sinus → jugular vein → back into the general circulation.

The 11th edition adds a caution: researchers increasingly recognise that both the production and the absorption of CSF are **more complicated** than this traditional picture suggests (Brinker et al., 2014). Learn the traditional circuit, because it is what the book's figures and exam keys use, but do not treat it as the whole story.

### Hydrocephalus

The ventricles are connected to each other by **narrow channels**, and a narrow channel can be blocked. The book's example is the **cerebral aqueduct**, the slender duct that links the **third and fourth ventricles**. If a tumour grows near such a channel and obstructs it, CSF keeps being produced upstream of the block but cannot drain. Fluid accumulates in the ventricles, their walls are pushed outward, and the whole brain expands. This condition is **hydrocephalus** ("water head").

Treatment follows directly from the mechanism: **drain the excess fluid** from the ventricles and **try to remove the obstruction**. (In clinical practice the drainage is usually done with an implanted tube, a shunt, that carries CSF from the ventricles to elsewhere in the body; Pinel does not go into this detail.) The book's journal prompt notes that hydrocephalus is often **congenital**, that is, present from birth, and asks the reader to think about what long-term effects that might have on development.

A localisation point that the mechanism gives you for free: if the block is at the cerebral aqueduct, the ventricles **upstream** of it (the lateral and third ventricles) enlarge while the fourth ventricle, **downstream**, does not. This is exactly the kind of reasoning an applied MCQ will ask for.

### Blood-brain barrier

The brain is described in the book as a finely tuned electrochemical organ, and its workings can be badly disrupted if the wrong chemicals reach it. The **blood-brain barrier** is the mechanism that **impedes the passage of many toxic substances from the blood into the brain**.

The barrier is not a separate membrane; it is a property of the **brain's blood vessels**. Compare capillaries elsewhere in the body with those in the brain:

- **Elsewhere**, the cells that make up the walls of blood vessels are **loosely packed**, so most molecules pass readily through the gaps between them into the surrounding tissue.
- **In the brain**, the cells of the vessel walls are **tightly packed**, closing those gaps. This blocks the passage of many molecules, especially **proteins and other large molecules** (Chow & Gu, 2015).

Three qualifications keep this from being a simple wall:

1. **Active transport.** Some large molecules that the brain needs for normal function are **actively transported** across the vessel walls. The book's example is **glucose**, the brain's main fuel. The barrier therefore is selective rather than absolute.
2. **Regional leakiness.** In **some areas** of the brain the vessel walls let certain large molecules through unimpeded. (Standard anatomy calls these the circumventricular organs; Pinel does not name them here.)
3. **Disease.** **Many CNS disorders** are associated with impairment of the blood-brain barrier (Bentivoglio & Kristensson, 2014; Jiang et al., 2018).

The practical consequence for psychopharmacology is important: **how strongly a therapeutic or recreational drug affects the brain depends on how easily it gets through the blood-brain barrier** (Interlandi, 2013; Siegenthaler, Sohet, & Daneman, 2013). A drug that cannot cross may have powerful effects on the body and none on behaviour.

> **Trap.** Two common wrong answers: (a) "the blood-brain barrier is formed by the meninges" (no: it is the tightly packed cells of the **cerebral blood vessel walls**), and (b) "the barrier blocks all large molecules" (no: **glucose** is actively transported, and some brain regions let certain large molecules pass). Section 3.2 adds a third ingredient: **astrocytes**, whose extensions cover the outer surfaces of cerebral blood vessels, also appear to help control what crosses.

## 3.2 Cells of the Nervous System

Most of the cells in the nervous system belong to one of two fundamentally different types: **neurons** and **glial cells**. This module describes the anatomy of each. Chapter 4 explains how neurons *work*; here the aim is to know their parts by name, location and job.

### Anatomy of neurons: the external features

Recall from Chapter 1 that **neurons** are cells specialised for three tasks: **receiving**, **conducting** and **transmitting** electrochemical signals. They vary enormously in shape and size, and the book cites several recent reviews of that diversity (Sharpee, 2014; Shen, 2015; Underwood, 2015). Many neurons, though, follow the general plan of a **multipolar neuron**, and Figure 3.5 labels its external parts. Follow a signal through it and the parts fall into a natural order.

- **Dendrites** are the short, branching processes that grow out of the cell body. They receive **most of the synaptic contacts** from other neurons, so they are the main input zone.
- The **cell body**, also called the **soma**, is the neuron's **metabolic centre**: it contains the nucleus and most of the machinery that keeps the cell alive.
- The **cell membrane** is the **semipermeable** membrane that encloses the whole neuron.
- The **axon hillock** is the **cone-shaped region** where the axon joins the cell body. (Figure 3.5 also labels the **axon initial segment**, the first stretch of axon just beyond the hillock; Chapter 4 explains why this is where the decision to fire is made.)
- The **axon** is the **long, narrow process** that projects away from the cell body and carries the signal toward other cells.
- **Myelin** is the **fatty insulation** wrapped around many axons.
- **Nodes of Ranvier** (pronounced "RAHN-vee-yay") are the **gaps between successive sections of myelin**, where the bare axon is exposed.
- **Terminal buttons** (labelled simply "buttons" in the figure) are the button-like swellings at the ends of the axon's branches. They **release chemicals into synapses**.
- **Synapses** are the **gaps between adjacent neurons** across which those chemical signals pass.

The direction of flow in a typical multipolar neuron is therefore dendrites and soma (input) → axon hillock and initial segment (decision) → axon (conduction, speeded by myelin with its nodes) → terminal buttons (output) → synapse → next neuron.

> **Trap.** The **axon hillock** is the cone-shaped *junction* between soma and axon, not the swelling at the far end of the axon (that is a **terminal button**). And **nodes of Ranvier** are the *uninsulated gaps between* myelin segments, not the myelin segments themselves. Both are favourite "match the term" items; the book's second Scan Your Brain pairs "axon hillock" with "cone-shaped region" and "synapses" with "gaps".

### Internal features of a neuron

Figure 3.6 opens the cell up. The internal parts are the same organelles found in most body cells, so the task is to attach each to its job.

| Internal feature | What it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | Spherical structure in the cell body containing **DNA** | Holds the genetic instructions |
| **Cytoplasm** | The clear fluid inside the cell | Medium in which the organelles sit |
| **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** | System of folded membranes in the cell body | **Rough ER** (studded with ribosomes): protein synthesis. **Smooth ER** (no ribosomes): fat synthesis |
| **Ribosomes** | Small structures located on the ER | Sites where **proteins are synthesised** |
| **Golgi complex** | Connected system of membranes | **Packages molecules into vesicles** |
| **Mitochondria** | Organelles in the cytoplasm | Sites of **aerobic** (oxygen-consuming) **energy release** |
| **Microtubules** | Tubules running through the neuron | **Rapid transport** of molecules throughout the cell |
| **Synaptic vesicles** | Membrane packages near the terminals | **Store neurotransmitter** molecules ready for release |
| **Neurotransmitters** | Molecules released by active neurons | **Influence the activity of other cells** |

Read the table as a production line and it becomes easy to remember: instructions in the **nucleus** → proteins built on **ribosomes** (on rough ER) → packaged by the **Golgi complex** into vesicles → shipped down the axon along **microtubules** → stored in **synaptic vesicles** at the terminals → released as **neurotransmitters**. **Mitochondria** power the whole operation.

> **Trap.** The **Golgi complex** (an organelle that packages molecules) and the **Golgi stain** (a staining method, Section 3.3) are both named after Camillo Golgi, and matching questions put them side by side. Golgi complex = "packaging membranes"; Golgi stain = "black" silhouettes of a few whole neurons.

### The neuron cell membrane

The neuron's membrane is a **lipid bilayer**: two layers of fat molecules (Figure 3.7). Fat on its own would make a passive, featureless boundary. What gives the membrane most of its working properties is the set of **protein molecules embedded in the bilayer**. Pinel picks out two kinds:

- **Channel proteins**, which form passages through which **certain molecules can pass** across the membrane; and
- **Signal proteins**, which act as messengers across the membrane: a specific molecule **binding on the outer face** triggers a **signal on the inner side**, inside the neuron.

These two kinds of protein are the anatomical basis of the ion channels and receptors that Chapter 4 builds on.

### Classes of neurons: unipolar, bipolar, multipolar and interneurons

Figure 3.8 shows one way to classify neurons: **count the processes** (projections) that emerge from the cell body.

| Class | Number of processes from the cell body | Notes from the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Unipolar neuron** | One | Example in this chapter: the sensory neurons of the dorsal root (Section 3.4) |
| **Bipolar neuron** | Two | |
| **Multipolar neuron** | More than two | **Most neurons are multipolar**; e.g. spinal motor neurons, cortical pyramidal cells |
| **Interneuron** | Short axon, or none at all | Integrates activity **within** a single brain structure rather than conducting signals **between** structures |

Note that "interneuron" is defined on a different basis (axon length and function) from the other three (number of processes). An interneuron can also be multipolar; the stellate cells of the cortex in Section 3.4 are a good example.

The book is candid that classifying neurons is **genuinely hard** and that neuroscientists still **do not agree on the best method** (Cembrowski & Menon, 2018; Wichterle, Gifford, & Mazzoni, 2013; Zeng & Sanes, 2017). Number of processes is just one convenient scheme.

> **Trap.** "Most neurons are unipolar" is wrong: most are **multipolar** (more than **two** processes). Also watch the cut-off: two processes is **bipolar**, not multipolar.

### Neurons and neuroanatomical structure: nuclei, ganglia, tracts and nerves

Step back from single cells to the visible structures they form. At the gross level, the nervous system contains two kinds of structure: those made **mainly of cell bodies** and those made **mainly of axons**. Each kind has a different name depending on whether it lies in the CNS or the PNS, which gives a 2 × 2 grid that is one of the most heavily examined facts in the chapter.

| | Central nervous system | Peripheral nervous system |
|---|---|---|
| **Cluster of cell bodies** | **Nucleus** (plural *nuclei*) | **Ganglion** (plural *ganglia*) |
| **Bundle of axons** | **Tract** | **Nerve** |

A way to fix it: the **CNS** words are the ones you will meet throughout the brain chapters ("the red nucleus", "the optic tract"), while the **PNS** words are the ones you know from the body ("the sciatic nerve", "the dorsal root ganglia").

The word **nucleus** therefore carries **two different neuroanatomical meanings**: the DNA-containing organelle inside a single neuron's cell body, and a cluster of many neuronal cell bodies in the CNS. Context tells you which is meant.

> **Trap.** "A bundle of axons in the brain is called a nerve" is wrong; in the CNS it is a **tract**. "A cluster of cell bodies outside the CNS is a nucleus" is wrong; it is a **ganglion**. The one famous exception to the naming rule is not in Pinel but trips up students who know it: the **basal ganglia** (Section 3.4) are clusters of cell bodies **inside** the CNS, so strictly they should be called nuclei. The name is historical. Some texts therefore prefer "basal nuclei"; in Pinel the term is **basal ganglia**.

### Glia: the forgotten cells

Neurons are not the only cells in the nervous system. The other major class is the **glial cells**, or **glia** (pronounced "GLEE-a"). The second Scan Your Brain calls them "the forgotten cells", because for much of the history of neuroscience they were treated as background.

How many are there? The book corrects a claim that circulates widely. It is often said that the human brain contains **ten times as many glia as neurons**. That is **incorrect**. The current estimate is roughly **two glial cells for every three neurons** (Nimmerjahn & Bergles, 2015; von Bartheld, 2017), so the two populations are of the same order of size, with neurons slightly more numerous.

> **Trap.** Older textbooks, popular science writing and many older answer keys state that glia outnumber neurons by about **10 to 1** (and some say 50 to 1). The 11th edition explicitly rejects this: the ratio is about **2 glia : 3 neurons**. If an exam asks for "Pinel's" figure, give the 2:3 ratio, and be ready for an older key that still expects "glia far outnumber neurons".

Pinel describes four kinds of glia.

### Oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells: the myelin makers

In the **central nervous system**, insulation is the job of **oligodendrocytes**. Each one sends out extensions that coil around the axons of nearby neurons. Those extensions are loaded with **myelin**, the **fatty insulating substance** introduced above, and the wrappings they form, the **myelin sheaths**, make axons **conduct signals faster** (Chapter 4 explains how, through saltatory conduction).

**Schwann cells** do the same job in the **peripheral nervous system**.

Figure 3.9 shows two differences between them that examiners exploit.

1. **Segments per cell.** Each **Schwann cell** forms **one** myelin segment on **one** axon; in effect it wraps itself around the axon. Each **oligodendrocyte** sends out several extensions and so supplies **several myelin segments**, often on **more than one axon**.
2. **Regeneration.** Only **Schwann cells** can **guide axonal regeneration** (regrowth) after damage. This is why effective regrowth of damaged axons in the mammalian nervous system is **restricted to the PNS**. A severed peripheral nerve can regrow; a severed spinal cord tract, whose axons are myelinated by oligodendrocytes, essentially cannot.

> **Trap.** CNS = **O**ligodendrocyte; PNS = **S**chwann. A memory hook some students use: "**COPS**" (**C**NS **O**ligodendrocytes, **P**NS **S**chwann cells). And do not conclude that because oligodendrocytes make more segments they are "better" myelinators; the clinically decisive difference is that only Schwann cells support regrowth.

### Microglia

**Microglia** are the third class. As their name says, they are **smaller than the other glial cells**. Their classic role is defensive: when the brain is injured or diseased, microglia **multiply**, **engulf cellular debris or even whole cells** (Brown & Neher, 2014), and **trigger inflammatory responses** (Smith & Dragunow, 2014). They are, in effect, the brain's resident immune cells.

### Astrocytes

**Astrocytes** are the fourth class. They are the **largest** glial cells, and they are named for their **star shape** (*astro* means star). Figure 3.10 shows the feature that gives some astrocytes special importance: their extensions **cover the outer surfaces of the blood vessels** running through the brain, and they **also contact neurons**. Sitting between blood and neuron in this way, these astrocytes appear to:

- **let some chemicals pass** from the blood into CNS neurons while **blocking others** (Paixão & Klein, 2010); and
- **contract or relax blood vessels** according to the **blood-flow demands** of particular brain regions (Howarth, 2014; Mishra et al., 2016; Muoio, Persson, & Sendeski, 2014).

The second of these is worth connecting forward: functional brain imaging (Chapter 5) depends on the fact that active brain regions receive increased blood flow, and astrocytes are among the cells that adjust that flow.

| Glial cell | Size / shape | Location | Main role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oligodendrocyte** | Several myelin-rich extensions | CNS | Myelinates several axon segments |
| **Schwann cell** | Wraps one segment | PNS | Myelinates one segment; guides regeneration |
| **Microglia** | **Smallest** | CNS | Respond to injury and disease: multiply, engulf debris, trigger inflammation |
| **Astrocyte** | **Largest**; star-shaped | CNS | Cover blood vessels and contact neurons; regulate passage of chemicals and local blood flow |

> **Trap.** "Smallest = microglia; largest = astrocytes". Examiners swap them. And do not confuse **astrocytes** (star-shaped **glia**) with **stellate cells** (star-shaped **neurons**, specifically cortical interneurons, Section 3.4). Same shape, same meaning of the name, completely different cell class.

### Beyond support: the new view of glia

For decades the standard assumption was that glia mainly **supported** neurons: feeding them, clearing their waste and forming a physical framework that held neural circuits in place. The name itself, **glia = glue**, records that assumption.

That limited view has been overturned by a series of discoveries. For **astrocytes**, the most studied glial cells, the evidence the book lists is that they:

- **exchange chemical signals** with neurons and with other astrocytes (Araque et al., 2014; Montero & Orellana, 2015; Yoon & Lee, 2014);
- **control the formation and maintenance of synapses** between neurons (Baldwin & Eroglu, 2017);
- **modulate neural activity** (Bouzier-Sore & Pellerin, 2013);
- **form functional networks** with neurons and other astrocytes (Gittis & Brasier, 2015; Haim & Rowitch, 2017; Lee et al., 2014; Perea, Sur, & Araque, 2014);
- **control the blood-brain barrier** (Alvarez, Katayama, & Prat, 2013; Cabezas et al., 2014);
- **respond to brain injury** (Khakh & Sofroniew, 2015); and
- **contribute to some forms of cognition** (e.g. Dallérac & Rouach, 2016; Martin-Fernandez et al., 2017).

**Microglia**, too, turn out to do far more than clean up (Pósfai et al., 2018). They have been implicated in regulating **cell death** (Wake et al., 2013), in **synapse formation** (Parkhurst et al., 2013; Welberg, 2014) and in **synapse elimination** (Wake et al., 2012).

The authors' summary is that research on glial function is still young but exciting, and that glia now look far more numerous in their effects, and far more important, than anyone would have guessed **two decades** earlier. Some researchers have gone so far as to propose that **glial networks may be where thoughts reside** (Verkhratsky, Parpura, & Rodríguez, 2010). Treat that as a speculative proposal the book reports, not an established finding.

### More kinds of glia than four

The four-way classification (oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, microglia, astrocytes) is itself a simplification. Researchers have recently reported a **new type of glial cell** (Fan & Agid, 2018), and astrocytes alone come in **at least fifteen varieties**, which differ in their structure, their physiology and the brain regions where they are found (Chai et al., 2017; Clarke & Liddelow, 2017; Lin et al., 2017). The authors expect that sorting out the function of each type will be difficult.

> **Trap.** Older exam keys list glial functions only as "support, nutrition, insulation, phagocytosis". In the 11th edition, the correct answer to "which of the following has been shown for astrocytes?" can include **chemical signalling with neurons, synapse formation and maintenance, control of the blood-brain barrier, regulation of blood flow, and a role in cognition**. "Glia play no part in information processing" is now a wrong option.

## 3.3 Neuroanatomical Techniques and Directions

This module has two halves. The first describes some of the most widely used **methods for seeing** neural structure. The second sets out the **coordinate system** that neuroanatomists use to say where a structure lies in a vertebrate nervous system.

### Why neural tissue has to be prepared

It is natural to assume that the obstacle to seeing neurons is their small size. Pinel argues that size is not the real problem. The real problem is **crowding**: neurons are packed so closely, and their axons and dendrites are so densely interwoven, that unprepared neural tissue under a microscope shows almost nothing useful. Everything overlaps everything else.

The solution is to **prepare tissue in different ways**, each of which makes **one aspect** of neuronal structure clearly visible while hiding the rest, and then to **combine** what the different preparations reveal. No single method shows everything. The techniques below should be learned as a set of complementary tools, each with a characteristic strength and a characteristic blind spot.

### Golgi stain

Pinel calls the **Golgi stain** the greatest stroke of luck in the early history of neuroscience. It was discovered **by accident** in the **early 1870s** by **Camillo Golgi** (pronounced "GOLE-jee"), an **Italian physician**.

Golgi was not trying to stain neurons at all; he was trying to stain the **meninges**. He exposed a block of neural tissue to **potassium dichromate** and **silver nitrate**. The two chemicals react to form **silver chromate**, and for reasons that are still not understood, the silver chromate entered **only a few neurons in each slice** and turned each of those neurons **completely black**.

That partial, all-or-none uptake is exactly what makes the stain valuable. Because only a small fraction of neurons are stained, they stand out against an unstained background, and because each stained neuron is filled entirely, its **whole shape** shows: cell body, dendrites and axon. For the first time, individual neurons could be seen, though only **in silhouette** (Figure 3.11). The stain reveals nothing of the cell's interior.

**Use:** Golgi stains are commonly used to discover the **overall shape** of neurons.

### Nissl stain

The Golgi stain has an obvious limitation built into its strength: since it stains only a few neurons, it gives **no indication of how many neurons** an area contains. The first staining method to solve that problem was the **Nissl stain**, developed in the **1880s** by **Franz Nissl**, a **German psychiatrist**.

The dye most often used in the Nissl method is **cresyl violet**. Nissl dyes enter **all** the cells on a slide, but they **bind to DNA and RNA**, molecules that are most concentrated in **neuron cell bodies**. The result is that each cell body appears as a stained dot while axons and dendrites stay pale.

**Use:** because each dot is a cell body, Nissl stains are used to **estimate the number of cell bodies** in an area by **counting the stained dots**. Figure 3.12 shows a Nissl-stained section through the **rat hippocampus** at two magnifications, which illustrates the stain's two uses. At **low magnification** it gives a **gross picture of brain structure**, because groups of cell bodies (layers and nuclei) stand out as dense bands; at **high magnification** the individual cell bodies can be told apart and **counted**. Only the layers made up mainly of cell bodies take up the dye densely.

### Golgi versus Nissl

The two stains are opposites in almost every respect, which is why examiners pair them.

| | Golgi stain | Nissl stain |
|---|---|---|
| Discoverer | **Camillo Golgi**, Italian physician | **Franz Nissl**, German psychiatrist |
| Date | **Early 1870s** | **1880s** |
| How discovered | **Accidentally**, while trying to stain the meninges | Developed to overcome the Golgi stain's limitation |
| Chemistry | Potassium dichromate + silver nitrate → **silver chromate** | Commonly **cresyl violet**; binds **DNA and RNA** |
| Which cells stain | **A few** neurons per slice, at random | **All** cells; cell bodies stain most heavily |
| What you see | **Whole neuron in black silhouette**; no internal detail | **Cell bodies as dots**; processes largely unstained |
| Used for | **Overall shape** of neurons | **Counting** cell bodies; mapping layers and nuclei |

> **Trap.** "Golgi = shape, Nissl = number." To count the neurons in a nucleus, use **Nissl**; to see one neuron's dendritic tree, use **Golgi**. Order matters too: Golgi's stain (**1870s**) came first, and Nissl's (**1880s**) was devised to make up for its failure to show cell numbers. The book's second Scan Your Brain pairs "Golgi stain" with **black**.

### Electron microscopy

The Golgi and Nissl stains are viewed through a **light microscope**, and light imposes a ceiling. Because of the physical nature of light, light microscopy can magnify only about **1,500 times**, which is not enough to show the fine anatomical details of neurons such as synaptic vesicles or the structure of a synapse.

**Electron microscopy** gets past that ceiling. In outline:

1. Thin slices of neural tissue are coated with an **electron-absorbing substance**, which different parts of the neurons take up to **different degrees**.
2. A **beam of electrons** is passed **through the tissue** onto **photographic film**.
3. Where the tissue absorbed more of the substance, fewer electrons get through, so the film records the structure. The image is an **electron micrograph**, which shows neuronal structure in extraordinary detail.

A variant, the **scanning electron microscope**, produces striking **three-dimensional** micrographs (Figure 3.13 shows a neuron cell body studded with terminal buttons, illustrating how many synaptic contacts a single neuron receives). The trade-off is that scanning electron microscopy **cannot magnify as much** as conventional electron microscopy.

Pinel also points out that the method's strength is its weakness. Electron micrographs are so detailed that they make it **hard to see the general layout** of a structure: you see the trees in perfect detail and lose the forest.

> **Trap.** Light microscope limit ≈ **1,500×**. Scanning electron microscope = **3-D** images but **less** magnification than conventional (transmission) electron microscopy. An option saying "scanning EM gives the highest magnification" is wrong.

### Neuroanatomical tracing techniques

Stains show what neurons look like where they sit. They do not tell you **where a neuron's axon goes**, or **where the axons arriving in an area come from**. That is the job of **tracing techniques**, and there are two kinds, defined by the direction in which the tracer chemical travels along the axon (Figure 3.14).

**Anterograde (forward) tracing** answers: *where do the neurons in this area send their axons?*

1. The investigator **injects** an anterograde tracer chemical into the area containing the **cell bodies** of interest.
2. The **cell bodies take it up** and **transport it forward** along their axons to the **terminal buttons**.
3. After **a few days**, the brain is removed and sliced, and the slices are treated to reveal where the chemical ended up. Wherever it is found, those cells' axons must terminate.

**Retrograde (backward) tracing** answers the reverse question: *which neurons send axons into this area?*

1. The investigator injects a retrograde tracer into the **target area**.
2. **Terminal buttons** in that area take it up, and it is **transported backward** along the axons to the **cell bodies**.
3. After **a few days**, the brain is removed, sliced and treated. Wherever labelled cell bodies are found, those neurons must project into the injected area.

| | Anterograde | Retrograde |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of transport | Forward: cell body → terminal buttons | Backward: terminal buttons → cell body |
| Injected into | The area of **origin** (cell bodies) | The area of **termination** (target) |
| Taken up by | **Cell bodies** | **Terminal buttons** |
| Question answered | Where do these neurons **project to**? | Which neurons **project into** this area? |

> **Trap.** Students often reverse these because "retrograde" sounds like "looking back to the source". That is actually the right intuition: **retrograde** tracing lets you find the **source** of the inputs to an area, by starting at the terminals and travelling backward to the cell bodies. **Anterograde** starts at the cell bodies and follows the axons **forward** to their destination.

### Directions in the vertebrate nervous system: the three axes

Just as you cannot learn your way around an unfamiliar city without north-south and east-west, you cannot describe the nervous system without a coordinate system. Neuroanatomical directions in vertebrates are defined **relative to the orientation of the spinal cord**, and in a typical four-legged vertebrate (Pinel illustrates with one of the authors' cats, Figure 3.15a) the system is simple. There are **three axes**:

1. **Anterior-posterior.** **Anterior** means toward the **nose end**; **posterior** means toward the **tail end**. The same two directions are also called **rostral** (toward the nose; literally "beak") and **caudal** (toward the tail).
2. **Dorsal-ventral.** **Dorsal** means toward the surface of the **back** or the **top of the head**; **ventral** means toward the surface of the **chest** or the **bottom of the head**.
3. **Medial-lateral.** **Medial** means toward the **midline** of the body; **lateral** means **away from the midline**, toward the body's sides.

In a four-legged animal these line up neatly: the back and the top of the head face the same way (up), so "dorsal" means the same direction everywhere along the nervous system.

### The human complication: an upright posture

Humans break this tidy system by walking on their hind legs. Standing upright **changes the orientation of the cerebral hemispheres relative to the spinal cord and brain stem**: there is a bend of roughly **90 degrees** where the brain stem meets the forebrain. Figure 3.15b shows that the directions within the cerebral hemispheres are **rotated by 90°** compared with the directions in the spinal cord and brain stem.

The key to avoiding confusion is that the terms were adapted to humans by **keeping the names of body surfaces the same** as in a typical non-upright vertebrate. The consequences:

- The **top of the human head** and the **back of the human body** are **both dorsal**, even though in a standing human they point in different directions (up and backward).
- The **bottom of the human head** and the **front of the human body** are **both ventral**, even though they point down and forward.

To get round this ambiguity, the terms **superior** (toward the top of the head) and **inferior** (toward the bottom of the head) are often used for the **primate head**.

> **Trap.** In a standing human, "dorsal" in the **spinal cord** means toward the **back**, but "dorsal" in the **cerebral hemispheres** means toward the **top of the head**. Likewise "anterior" in the brain means toward the face, but in the spinal cord the "anterior" (ventral) surface faces the belly. Questions that ask "which surface of the human brain is dorsal?" want **the top**. The book's second Scan Your Brain pairs **dorsal** with "top of head" and **posterior** with **caudal**.

### Proximal and distal

Two more direction terms. In general **proximal** means **near** and **distal** means **far**. In the **peripheral nervous system** specifically, they are measured from the **CNS**: **proximal** means **closer to the CNS** and **distal** means **farther from it**. So the shoulders are proximal to the elbows, and the elbows are proximal to the fingers.

### Planes of section

The brain drawings in the rest of the book are usually slices, and the slices are cut in one of **three planes** (Figure 3.16):

- **Horizontal sections**, cut parallel to the ground in a standing person, so they separate upper from lower parts of the brain.
- **Frontal sections**, also called **coronal sections**, cut parallel to the face, so they separate front from back.
- **Sagittal sections**, cut vertically from front to back, so they separate left from right.

Two further terms:

- A **midsagittal section** is a sagittal cut made exactly **down the centre**, **between the two hemispheres**. (This is the view used in the book's third Scan Your Brain, a labelled photograph of a real brain.)
- A **cross section** is any cut made **at a right angle** to a **long, narrow structure** such as the **spinal cord** or a **nerve**.

> **Trap.** **Frontal = coronal** (think of a crown, *corona*, sitting across the top of the head from ear to ear). **Sagittal** separates left from right (think of an arrow, *sagitta*, shot straight through the head from front to back). Because a cross section of the spinal cord is cut at right angles to the cord, in an upright human it lies in the horizontal plane; the term "cross section" is defined by the structure, not by the body.

## 3.4 Anatomy of the Central Nervous System

The first three modules covered the divisions of the nervous system, the cells it is built from, and the methods and vocabulary used to study it. This final module concentrates entirely on the **CNS**, and it is organised as an ascent: it starts with the spinal cord, then climbs through the five divisions of the brain from the most posterior (the medulla) to the most anterior (the cerebral hemispheres).

### Spinal cord: grey matter and white matter

Cut the spinal cord across (a cross section, Figure 3.17) and two zones are obvious:

- an inner, **H-shaped** (butterfly-shaped) core of **grey matter**; and
- a surrounding zone of **white matter**.

The colours have an anatomical explanation. **Grey matter** consists largely of **cell bodies** and **unmyelinated interneurons**. **White matter** consists largely of **myelinated axons**, and it is the fatty myelin that gives it its glossy white appearance. So the grey core is where the spinal cord's neurons sit and synapse; the white shell is the set of ascending and descending axon tracts running up and down the cord.

The H has two pairs of arms:

- the two **dorsal horns** (the arms pointing toward the back); and
- the two **ventral horns** (the arms pointing toward the front, the belly side).

> **Trap.** In the **spinal cord**, grey matter is **inside** and white matter is **outside**. In the **cerebral hemispheres** (later in this section) the arrangement is reversed: the grey **cortex** is on the **outside** and the white matter lies **beneath** it. Questions that ask "where is the grey matter?" without saying which part of the CNS are testing exactly this.

### Spinal nerves, dorsal roots and ventral roots

**Pairs of spinal nerves**, one on the left and one on the right, attach to the spinal cord at **31 different levels** of the spine, giving **62 spinal nerves** in all. As each spinal nerve approaches the cord it **splits**, and its axons enter or leave the cord by one of two **roots**: the **dorsal root** or the **ventral root**.

The two roots carry traffic in opposite directions, and they are built from different kinds of neuron:

**Dorsal root: sensory.** Every axon in the dorsal root, whether it belongs to the somatic or the autonomic nervous system, belongs to a **sensory (afferent) unipolar neuron**. The cell bodies of these neurons are clustered together **just outside the cord**, forming the **dorsal root ganglia** (a "ganglion" because it is a cluster of cell bodies outside the CNS). Many of their synaptic terminals end in the **dorsal horns** of the grey matter.

**Ventral root: motor.** The neurons of the ventral root are **motor (efferent) multipolar neurons** whose **cell bodies lie in the ventral horns**. What they connect to depends on which system they belong to:

- **somatic** ventral root neurons project directly to **skeletal muscles**;
- **autonomic** ventral root neurons project to **ganglia**, where they synapse on second-stage neurons that in turn project to **internal organs** (the heart, stomach, liver and so on). This is the two-stage autonomic path from Section 3.1 seen at the level of the cord.

| | Dorsal root | Ventral root |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | **Sensory (afferent)**, into the cord | **Motor (efferent)**, out of the cord |
| Neuron type | **Unipolar** | **Multipolar** |
| Cell bodies | **Dorsal root ganglia**, just outside the cord | **Ventral horns** of the grey matter |
| Terminals / targets | Many terminals in the **dorsal horns** | Skeletal muscles (somatic) or autonomic ganglia (autonomic) |

> **Trap.** **Dorsal = sensory in, ventral = motor out.** The classical name for this rule, which Pinel does not use but many exam papers do, is the **Bell-Magendie law**. Two further catches: the sensory neurons of the dorsal root are **unipolar** (not bipolar), and their cell bodies are **outside** the cord in the dorsal root ganglia, whereas the motor neurons' cell bodies are **inside** the cord in the ventral horns. Finally, the number is **31 pairs**, that is **62** spinal nerves; an option giving "31 spinal nerves" has counted pairs as nerves.

### Five major divisions of the brain: from three swellings to five

To find your way around a new city you first learn its major districts; once you know them, you can locate any address roughly. The brain's five "districts" are its **five major divisions**, and the reason there are five, rather than some other number, lies in **embryonic development** (Figure 3.18).

1. In the vertebrate embryo, the tissue that will become the CNS is first recognisable as a **fluid-filled tube** (the neural tube; its development is covered in Chapter 9).
2. The first sign of a brain is **three swellings** at the **anterior end** of this tube. These become the adult **forebrain**, **midbrain** and **hindbrain**.
3. Before birth, the three swellings become **five**, because the **forebrain swelling divides into two** and so does the **hindbrain swelling**. The midbrain stays single.

From **anterior to posterior**, the five swellings present at birth are:

1. **Telencephalon**
2. **Diencephalon**
3. **Mesencephalon** (the **midbrain**)
4. **Metencephalon**
5. **Myelencephalon**

*Encephalon* means "within the head". These five swellings develop into the **five divisions of the adult brain**.

Pinel passes on the mnemonic the authors used as students: the **telencephalon is on top**, and the other four lie **below it in alphabetical order**: **d**i-, **mes**-, **met**-, **my**el-. (Alphabetically, "mes" comes before "met", which comes before "my".)

| Embryonic three-part brain | Five divisions | Main adult structures (this chapter) |
|---|---|---|
| **Forebrain** | **Telencephalon** | Cerebral hemispheres: cortex, limbic system, basal ganglia, commissures |
| | **Diencephalon** | Thalamus, hypothalamus |
| **Midbrain** | **Mesencephalon** | Tectum (colliculi), tegmentum |
| **Hindbrain** | **Metencephalon** | Pons, cerebellum |
| | **Myelencephalon** (medulla) | Tracts, reticular formation |

### The adult brain and the brain stem

Figure 3.19 shows the five divisions in the adult human brain. As in other higher vertebrates, the **telencephalon**, which forms the left and right **cerebral hemispheres**, grows far more than any other division during development, so much that in a side view it folds back over and hides most of the rest.

The other **four** divisions, from the diencephalon down to the myelencephalon, are often grouped together as the **brain stem**: the stalk on which the cerebral hemispheres sit. The **myelencephalon** is usually called the **medulla**.

> **Trap.** Pinel's **brain stem** = **diencephalon + mesencephalon + metencephalon + myelencephalon** (everything except the telencephalon). Many clinical and anatomy texts define the brainstem more narrowly as **midbrain, pons and medulla** only, leaving out the diencephalon and the cerebellum. If a question quotes Pinel, include the diencephalon; if it is clearly using the clinical definition, do not. A related trap: **metencephalon** (pons and cerebellum) and **myelencephalon** (medulla) look alike; "myel-" is the one next to the spinal cord, and it is the medulla.

Having learned the five districts, the chapter now visits each in turn, starting at the bottom with the myelencephalon and climbing to the telencephalon.

### Myelencephalon

The **myelencephalon**, or **medulla**, is the **most posterior** division of the brain, the part that merges with the spinal cord. As you would expect from that position, it consists largely of **tracts** carrying signals **between the rest of the brain and the body**.

The part of the myelencephalon that interests psychologists most is the **reticular formation** (Figure 3.20).

### The reticular formation

The **reticular formation** is a complex network of about **100 tiny nuclei**. It occupies the **central core of the brain stem**, stretching from the **posterior boundary of the myelencephalon** to the **anterior boundary of the midbrain**. Its name comes from its net-like look under the microscope (*reticulum* means "little net").

Because some parts of it are involved in **arousal**, the reticular formation is sometimes called the **reticular activating system**. Pinel warns against that label. The many nuclei of the reticular formation take part in a wide range of functions, including:

- **sleep**;
- **attention**;
- **movement**;
- **maintenance of muscle tone**; and
- various **cardiac, circulatory and respiratory reflexes**.

Calling such a varied collection of nuclei a single "system", let alone an "activating" one, implies a unity of function that is not there, so the label can mislead.

> **Trap.** The reticular formation is **not confined to the medulla**: it runs through the core of the medulla, pons and midbrain, which is why it appears in Figure 3.29's summary under the myelencephalon, the metencephalon **and** the mesencephalon (in the tegmentum). Also, older texts and exam keys present the "reticular activating system" as **the** arousal system (the classic Moruzzi and Magoun account, which Pinel takes up in the sleep chapter). The 11th edition treats "activating system" as a **misleading** name, because the nuclei serve many functions besides arousal.

### Metencephalon: pons and cerebellum

The **metencephalon**, like the myelencephalon, contains many **ascending and descending tracts** and part of the **reticular formation**. It has **two major divisions**:

- The **pons**: the tracts and reticular nuclei of the metencephalon form a **bulge on the ventral surface** of the brain stem, and that bulge is the pons.
- The **cerebellum** ("little brain"): the large, deeply **convoluted** structure on the brain stem's **dorsal surface**.

The cerebellum is an important **sensorimotor** structure. Damage to it **eliminates the ability to control movements precisely** and to **adapt movements to changing conditions**. However, cerebellar damage also produces a range of **cognitive deficits**, for example in **decision making** and in the **use of language**. The book draws the logical conclusion: the cerebellum's functions are **not limited to sensorimotor control**.

> **Trap.** **Pons = ventral bulge; cerebellum = dorsal.** And the older view that the cerebellum is "purely a motor coordination centre" is out of date in the 11th edition: cerebellar damage also impairs decision making and language. "Cerebellum" is "little brain"; do not confuse it with "cerebrum" (the cerebral hemispheres).

### Mesencephalon: the tectum

The **mesencephalon** (midbrain), like the metencephalon, has **two divisions**: the **tectum** and the **tegmentum** (Figure 3.21).

The **tectum** ("roof") is the **dorsal surface** of the midbrain. In mammals it consists of **two pairs of bumps** called the **colliculi** ("little hills"):

- The **posterior pair**, the **inferior colliculi**, have an **auditory** function.
- The **anterior pair**, the **superior colliculi**, have a **visual-motor** function: more specifically, they direct the body's **orientation toward or away from particular visual stimuli** (Gandhi & Katnani, 2011). Turning your eyes and head toward a sudden flash in your peripheral vision is the kind of response involved.

In **lower vertebrates** the tectum's function is **entirely visual-motor**, and there it is sometimes called the **optic tectum**.

> **Trap.** **Superior colliculi = vision (visual-motor orienting); inferior colliculi = hearing.** One memory hook: the eyes are higher in the head than the ears, and the superior colliculi are above the inferior. Note also that "superior/inferior" in the human brain stem correspond to "anterior/posterior" along the neuraxis, which is why Pinel can describe the superior colliculi as the **anterior** pair and the inferior colliculi as the **posterior** pair. The book's third Scan Your Brain uses "**inferior** colliculus" as one of its labels.

### Mesencephalon: the tegmentum

The **tegmentum** is the division of the mesencephalon that lies **ventral to the tectum** (think of the roof and, under it, the floor). Besides part of the reticular formation and **tracts of passage** (axons simply passing through on their way elsewhere), the tegmentum contains three structures that biopsychologists care about. Pinel calls them "colourful", and two of their names are literally colours.

1. The **periaqueductal gray (PAG)**: the grey matter surrounding the **cerebral aqueduct**, the duct joining the third and fourth ventricles. It is of special interest because it plays a role in mediating the **analgesic (pain-reducing) effects of opioid drugs** such as morphine.
2. The **substantia nigra** ("black substance"): an important component of the **sensorimotor system**. Its pathway to the striatum is the one that degenerates in **Parkinson's disease** (end of this section).
3. The **red nucleus**: also an important **sensorimotor** structure.

| Midbrain division | Position | Contents (this chapter) | Function(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tectum** ("roof") | **Dorsal** | Superior colliculi | Visual-motor orienting |
| | | Inferior colliculi | Auditory |
| **Tegmentum** | **Ventral** to tectum | Reticular formation; tracts of passage | Various |
| | | **Periaqueductal gray** (around the cerebral aqueduct) | Opioid analgesia |
| | | **Substantia nigra** | Sensorimotor |
| | | **Red nucleus** | Sensorimotor |

> **Trap.** **Tectum** (roof, dorsal, colliculi) versus **tegmentum** (floor, ventral, PAG, substantia nigra, red nucleus): the names differ by a few letters, and so do the exam options. "The substantia nigra is part of the tectum" is wrong. "The periaqueductal gray surrounds the fourth ventricle" is wrong; it surrounds the **cerebral aqueduct**.

### Diencephalon: the thalamus

The **diencephalon** has **two structures**: the **thalamus** and the **hypothalamus** (Figure 3.22).

The **thalamus** is the large, **two-lobed** structure that forms the **top of the brain stem**. Its position relative to the ventricles is the key to picturing it:

- one lobe sits on **each side of the third ventricle**; and
- the two lobes are joined by the **massa intermedia**, a bridge of tissue that runs **through** the third ventricle.

On the thalamus's surface you can see white **laminae** (layers), which are made of **myelinated axons**.

Internally, the thalamus consists of **many different pairs of nuclei**, and **most of them project to the cortex**. (Pinel shows the overall layout in an appendix.)

### Thalamic nuclei: the sensory relay nuclei

The best-understood thalamic nuclei are the **sensory relay nuclei**. Each receives signals from **sensory receptors**, **processes** them, and passes them on to the appropriate area of **sensory cortex**. Three are named in this chapter:

| Sensory relay nucleus | Sensory system |
|---|---|
| **Lateral geniculate nuclei** | **Visual** |
| **Medial geniculate nuclei** | **Auditory** |
| **Ventral posterior nuclei** | **Somatosensory** (touch and other bodily sensations) |

A simple hook for the two geniculate nuclei: **L**ateral = **L**ight, **M**edial = **M**usic.

Two qualifications stop "relay" from being taken too literally:

1. Sensory relay nuclei are **not one-way streets**. Every one of them **receives feedback** from the very areas of cortex it projects to (Zembrzycki et al., 2013). The thalamus and cortex are in a loop, not a pipeline.
2. Less is known about the **other** thalamic nuclei, but **most of them receive input from areas of cortex and project to other areas of cortex** (Sherman, 2007). So much of the thalamus is involved in cortex-to-cortex communication rather than in relaying sensory input.

> **Trap.** **Lateral** geniculate = **vision**; **medial** geniculate = **hearing**; ventral posterior = **somatosensory**. Swapping lateral and medial geniculate is the classic error. Also, "the thalamus is a passive relay station" is not the 11th-edition position, because of the cortical feedback and the cortex-to-cortex nuclei. And note the anatomy: the two thalamic lobes flank the **third** ventricle (not the lateral ventricles), joined by the **massa intermedia**.

### Hypothalamus and the pituitary gland

The **hypothalamus** sits **just below the anterior thalamus** (*hypo* means "below") (Figure 3.23). It plays an important role in regulating several **motivated behaviours**, for example **eating**, **sleep** and **sexual behaviour**, which are the subjects of Chapters 12, 13 and 14.

The hypothalamus exerts part of its influence by controlling the **release of hormones from the pituitary gland**, which hangs from the hypothalamus on the **ventral surface of the brain**. The pituitary's name is a historical accident worth knowing: it literally means **"snot gland"**. It was first found in a gelatinous state behind the nose of a cadaver, and anatomists wrongly assumed it was the main source of **nasal mucus**.

### Optic chiasm, decussation and the mammillary bodies

Besides the pituitary, two other structures show on the **inferior surface of the hypothalamus**: the **optic chiasm** and the **mammillary bodies**.

The **optic chiasm** is where the **optic nerves** from the two eyes **meet** and some of their fibres **decussate**, that is, **cross over to the other side of the brain** (the details are in Chapter 6). Two terms describe the result:

- fibres that cross are **contralateral**: they project from one side of the body to the **other** side; and
- fibres that do not cross are **ipsilateral**: they stay on the **same** side.

The **mammillary bodies** are a **pair of spherical nuclei** on the **inferior surface of the hypothalamus**, just **behind the pituitary**. They are often regarded as part of the hypothalamus. (Pinel shows them and the other hypothalamic nuclei in an appendix.) Keep them in mind: they reappear in the limbic system below, and again in Korsakoff's amnesia in Chapter 11.

> **Trap.** **Contra**lateral = **opposite** side; **ipsi**lateral = **same** side. "Decussate" means to **cross the midline**, and the optic chiasm is the textbook example. On position: the optic chiasm, pituitary and mammillary bodies are all on the **inferior (ventral)** surface of the hypothalamus, with the mammillary bodies **posterior** to the pituitary. And the mammillary bodies are counted in **both** the diencephalon (as part of the hypothalamus) **and** the limbic system; an option saying they belong to only one of these is incomplete rather than simply wrong, so read the question carefully.

### Telencephalon

The **telencephalon** is the **largest** division of the human brain, and it carries out the brain's **most complex functions**. The book gives three examples: it **initiates voluntary movement**, it **interprets sensory input**, and it supports **complex cognitive processes** such as **learning, speaking and problem solving**. Its components in this chapter are the **cerebral cortex**, the cortical **fissures, gyri and lobes**, the **cerebral commissures**, the **hippocampus**, and two subcortical groupings, the **limbic system** and the **basal ganglia**.

### Cerebral cortex: grey outside, white beneath

The cerebral hemispheres are covered by a layer of tissue called the **cerebral cortex** ("cerebral bark", because it covers the hemispheres the way bark covers a tree).

- The cortex consists mainly of **small, unmyelinated neurons**, so it looks **grey** and is often called the **grey matter**.
- The layer **beneath** the cortex consists mainly of **large myelinated axons**, which look **white**; this is the **white matter**.

This is the reverse of the spinal cord, where the grey matter forms the inner H and the white matter surrounds it.

### Convolutions: fissures, sulci and gyri

The human cortex is **deeply convoluted** (folded and furrowed) (Figure 3.24). The convolutions have one clear effect: they **increase the amount of cortex without increasing the overall volume of the brain**, much as crumpling a sheet of paper lets it fit into a smaller box.

Not all mammals have a convoluted cortex. **Most mammals are lissencephalic** ("smooth-brained").

Why do some species have folded brains and others not? It was once thought that the **number and size of cortical convolutions** set a species' **intellectual capacity**. The evidence does not support that. The extent of folding seems to be related more to **body size**: **every large mammal** has an extremely convoluted cortex. (The book's journal prompt invites you to think about why only large mammals have highly folded cortex. The book leaves the question open; one line of reasoning, offered here rather than by Pinel, is geometric: as a brain grows larger, the amount of cortex needed grows faster than the space available to hold a smooth sheet, so the sheet must fold.)

The folds have names that depend on size:

- **Fissures**: the **large** furrows.
- **Sulci** (singular **sulcus**): the **small** furrows.
- **Gyri** (singular **gyrus**): the **ridges** between fissures and sulci.

> **Trap.** Pinel's usage: **fissure = large groove, sulcus = small groove, gyrus = ridge.** Many anatomy and clinical texts call the same grooves by different names: Pinel's **central fissure** is the **central sulcus** (fissure of Rolando) elsewhere, and his **lateral fissure** is the **Sylvian fissure** (lateral sulcus). Expect both names in Indian papers. And do not reverse "gyrus" and "sulcus": the ridge is the gyrus, the groove is the sulcus.

### The longitudinal fissure and the cerebral commissures

The **largest** of the fissures is the **longitudinal fissure**, which runs front to back down the midline and **almost completely separates** the two cerebral hemispheres. "Almost", because a small number of **tracts cross the longitudinal fissure** and connect the hemispheres directly. These hemisphere-connecting tracts are the **cerebral commissures**. The **largest cerebral commissure** is the **corpus callosum**, clearly visible in the frontal section of Figure 3.24. (Chapter 16 on the split brain depends on this structure.)

> **Trap.** A **commissure** connects the **two hemispheres** (left with right). The corpus callosum is the largest commissure, but not the only one. Do not confuse "commissure" with "decussation": a decussation is where fibres **cross to the opposite side** on their way somewhere (as at the optic chiasm), not a tract linking matching areas of the two hemispheres.

### The four lobes and their major gyri

On the **lateral** (outer side) surface of each hemisphere there are two major landmarks: the **central fissure** and the **lateral fissure** (Figures 3.24 and 3.25). Between them these two grooves mark off, incompletely, the **four lobes** of the hemisphere: **frontal**, **parietal** ("pa-RYE-e-tal"), **temporal** and **occipital** ("ok-SIP-i-tal").

How the landmarks divide them: the **central fissure** separates the frontal lobe in front from the parietal lobe behind; the **lateral fissure** separates the temporal lobe below from the frontal and parietal lobes above; the occipital lobe forms the back of the hemisphere.

Three of the largest gyri sit at these landmarks:

| Gyrus | Lobe | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Precentral gyrus** | **Frontal** | Just in front of the central fissure |
| **Postcentral gyrus** | **Parietal** | Just behind the central fissure |
| **Superior temporal gyrus** | **Temporal** | Uppermost gyrus of the temporal lobe, just below the lateral fissure |

### Lobes are not functional units

Pinel adds a conceptual warning that matters for every later chapter. The cerebral lobes are **not functional units**. The cortex is best pictured as a **flat sheet of cells** that happens to be divided into "lobes" only because it **folds in on itself** at certain places as it develops. It is therefore a mistake to think of a lobe as a unit with **one set of functions**. Different parts of a single lobe can do very different things, as the next subsection shows.

### What each lobe does: a first sketch

Even so, it is helpful at this stage to have a rough idea of what areas within each lobe do. Later chapters treat each in depth.

**Occipital lobe.** Its main function is simple to state: humans depend heavily on analysing **visual** input to guide behaviour, and the occipital cortex, together with large stretches of neighbouring cortex, performs that analysis.

**Parietal lobe: two large functional areas.**

- The **postcentral gyrus** analyses **sensations from the body**, for example touch (it is the somatosensory cortex).
- The remaining cortex in the **posterior parietal** region is involved in **perceiving where objects are and where our own body is**, and in **directing attention**.

**Temporal lobe: three broad areas.**

- The **superior temporal gyrus** is involved in **hearing and language**.
- The **inferior temporal cortex** is where **complex visual patterns are recognised** (so vision is not confined to the occipital lobe).
- The **medial temporal cortex**, on the inner surface and not visible in the usual side view, is important for **certain kinds of memory**.

**Frontal lobe: two distinct functional areas.**

- The **precentral gyrus** and the adjacent frontal cortex have a **motor** function.
- The frontal cortex **anterior to the motor cortex** (the prefrontal cortex) carries out **complex cognitive functions**, such as **planning sequences of responses**, **evaluating the likely outcomes** of possible courses of action, and **assessing the significance of other people's behaviour** (Euston, Gruber, & McNaughton, 2012; Isoda & Noritake, 2013; Pezzulo et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** **Pre**central gyrus = **motor**, in the **frontal** lobe; **post**central gyrus = **somatosensory**, in the **parietal** lobe. The central fissure lies between them, motor in front and sensory behind. Swapping these is among the most common errors in the whole chapter. Note too that visual pattern recognition is in the **inferior temporal** cortex, not only the occipital lobe.

### Neocortex: six layers

About **90 percent** of human cerebral cortex is **neocortex** ("new cortex"), also called **isocortex**. Neocortex has **six layers**, and by convention they are numbered with Roman numerals **I to VI, starting from the surface** and working inward toward the white matter.

Figure 3.26 shows two adjacent sections of neocortex prepared with the two stains from Section 3.3: one with a **Nissl stain**, which shows the **number and shape of cell bodies** in each layer, and one with a **Golgi stain**, which shows the **silhouettes of a small fraction** of the neurons. Pinel draws **three** characteristics from the figure and adds a **fourth** that the figure does not show.

The figure also summarises the contents of each layer (based on Rakic, 1979). In outline: **layer I** is mostly axons and dendrites with few cell bodies; **layer II** has densely packed stellate cells and a few small pyramidal cells; **layer III** has loosely packed stellate cells and intermediate-sized pyramidal cells; **layer IV** has bands of densely packed stellate cells and **no pyramidal cells**; **layer V** has **very large pyramidal cells** and a few loosely packed stellate cells; **layer VI** has pyramidal cells of various sizes with loosely packed stellate cells; and below the six layers lies the white matter, made of myelinated pyramidal cell axons with few cell bodies.

### Pyramidal and stellate cells

**First characteristic:** many cortical neurons fall into one of **two categories**.

- **Pyramidal cells** are **large multipolar neurons** with **pyramid-shaped cell bodies**, a large dendrite called the **apical dendrite** that runs from the tip of the pyramid **straight up toward the cortical surface**, and a **very long axon** (Lodato, Shetty, & Arlotta, 2015). Their long axons make them the cortex's **output** neurons.
- **Stellate cells** are **small, star-shaped interneurons**, that is, neurons with a **short axon or no axon**. They handle **local** processing within the cortex.

**Second characteristic:** the six layers **differ** from one another in the **size and density** of their cell bodies and in the **relative proportions of pyramidal and stellate cells** they contain. That is why the layers are distinguishable at all.

### Columnar organisation

**Third characteristic:** many long axons and dendrites run **vertically** through the neocortex, at **right angles to the layers**. This vertical flow of information is the basis of the neocortex's **columnar organisation**: the neurons in a given **vertical column** often form a **mini-circuit** that carries out a **single function** (Rowland & Moser, 2014).

So the neocortex is organised in two directions at once: horizontally into **layers** with different cell types, and vertically into **columns** that cut across the layers and work as functional units.

### Layer thickness reveals function

**Fourth characteristic**, not visible in the figure: although all neocortex has six layers, the **thickness of the individual layers varies from area to area** (Zilles & Amunts, 2010). The variation is not random; it follows each area's job.

- **Layer IV** contains **stellate cells specialised to receive sensory signals from the thalamus**, so **layer IV is especially thick in sensory cortex**.
- **Layer V** contains **pyramidal cells that send signals from the neocortex to the brain stem and spinal cord**, so **layer V is especially thick in motor cortex**.

This is why the caption of Figure 3.26 says the thickness of the layers can hint at an area's function: a thick layer IV marks the illustrated section as **sensory** neocortex.

> **Trap.** **Layer IV = input from the thalamus = thick in sensory cortex. Layer V = output to brain stem and spinal cord = thick in motor cortex.** Questions reverse these. Also, layers are numbered **from the surface inward** (I is outermost), so "layer VI is closest to the pial surface" is wrong. And **stellate** cells are **neurons** (interneurons); **astrocytes** are **glia**. Both are star-shaped.

### Hippocampus: cortex that is not neocortex

The **hippocampus** is one important region of cortex that is **not neocortex**: it has only **three major layers** (the book cites Schultz & Engelhardt, 2014). It lies at the **medial edge of the cerebral cortex**, where the cortex **folds back on itself** in the **medial temporal lobe** (it is visible in the frontal section of Figure 3.24). That infolding gives it a cross-sectional shape that reminded early anatomists of a **seahorse** (*hippocampus* means "sea horse"). The hippocampus plays a major role in **some kinds of memory** (Chapter 11).

> **Trap.** Neocortex = **six** layers and about **90 percent** of human cortex; hippocampus = **three** major layers. "All cerebral cortex has six layers" is false. The hippocampus is **cortex** (part of the telencephalon), even though it is tucked away medially and is listed as a limbic structure.

### Limbic system and basal ganglia: a warning about the word "system"

Most of the **subcortical** part of the telencephalon is taken up by **axons** running to and from the neocortex. But there are also several large **subcortical clusters of nuclei**, and some of them are assigned to either the **limbic system** or the **basal ganglia system**.

Pinel cautions against taking the word **system** too literally in either case. It suggests more certainty than the evidence warrants: it is not fully clear **what** these proposed systems do, **which structures** belong in them, or even whether it makes sense to treat each as a **single unified system**. Used loosely, though, both concepts are a convenient way to organise several subcortical structures.

### The limbic system: tracing the ring

The **limbic system** is a **circuit of midline structures** that **encircle the thalamus** (*limbic* means "ring"). It is involved in regulating **motivated behaviours**, including what the book (repeating a very old joke in biopsychology) calls the **four F's of motivation**: **fleeing, feeding, fighting and sexual behaviour**.

Its major structures are the two you have already met, the **mammillary bodies** and the **hippocampus**, plus the **amygdala**, the **fornix**, the **cingulate cortex** and the **septum**. The easiest way to learn them is to travel round the ring, as Pinel does with Figure 3.27:

1. Start at the **amygdala**, an **almond-shaped nucleus** in the **anterior temporal lobe** (*amygdala* means "almond"; pronounced "a-MIG-dah-lah").
2. **Posterior** to the amygdala is the **hippocampus**, which runs **beneath the thalamus** in the **medial temporal lobe**.
3. Next come the **cingulate cortex** and the **fornix**, both of which **curve around the dorsal thalamus**.
   - The **cingulate cortex** is the large strip of cortex in the **cingulate gyrus** on the **medial surface** of each hemisphere, **just above the corpus callosum** (*cingulate* means "encircling").
   - The **fornix** is the **major tract of the limbic system**. It **leaves the dorsal end of the hippocampus**, sweeps **forward in an arc** along the **top of the third ventricle**, and **ends in the septum and the mammillary bodies** (*fornix* means "arc").
4. The **septum** is a **midline nucleus** at the **anterior tip of the cingulate cortex**.
5. Several tracts link the **septum and mammillary bodies** back to the **amygdala and hippocampus**, which **closes the ring**.

| Limbic structure | What it is | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Amygdala** | Almond-shaped nucleus | Anterior temporal lobe |
| **Hippocampus** | Three-layered cortex | Medial temporal lobe, beneath the thalamus, posterior to the amygdala |
| **Cingulate cortex** | Strip of cortex in the cingulate gyrus | Medial surface of the hemisphere, just above the corpus callosum |
| **Fornix** | Major limbic **tract** | Arcs from the hippocampus over the third ventricle to the septum and mammillary bodies |
| **Septum** | Midline nucleus | Anterior tip of the cingulate cortex |
| **Mammillary bodies** | Pair of spherical nuclei | Inferior surface of the hypothalamus, behind the pituitary |

### What the limbic structures do

Of the limbic structures, the **hippocampus**, the **hypothalamus** and the **amygdala** have been studied most:

- the **hippocampus** has a role in **certain forms of memory**;
- the **hypothalamus** is involved in **motivated behaviours** such as eating, sleep and sexual behaviour; and
- the **amygdala** is involved in **emotion**, especially **fear**.

Note that in this sentence Pinel treats the **hypothalamus** as belonging with the limbic structures, even though it is a diencephalic structure and is not in the six-item list. Later chapters develop all three in detail (memory in Chapter 11, hunger in 12, emotion in 17).

> **Trap.** The **fornix** is a **tract** (a bundle of axons), not a nucleus; the **septum**, **amygdala** and **mammillary bodies** are **nuclei**; the **cingulate cortex** and **hippocampus** are **cortex**. Questions asking "which of the following is the major fibre tract of the limbic system?" want the **fornix**. The **four F's** are fleeing, feeding, fighting and **sexual behaviour** (the fourth F is the joke). Older anatomy texts sometimes listed the amygdala among the basal ganglia; in Pinel it is a **limbic** structure.

### The basal ganglia

Figure 3.28 shows the **basal ganglia**. Their components in this chapter:

- The **caudate** (*caudate* means "tail-like"), a long nucleus with a head and a curving tail, and the **putamen** (pronounced "pew-TAY-men"). These two **receive inputs from the neocortex** (Graybiel, 2000). Both have a **striped appearance**, and together they are called the **striatum** ("striped structure").
- The **globus pallidus** ("pale globe"), a pale, rounded structure that is the **striatum's major output target**. It lies **medial to the putamen**, between the putamen and the thalamus.

So the basic flow is: **neocortex → striatum (caudate + putamen) → globus pallidus**.

The basal ganglia play a part in **performing voluntary motor responses** and in **decision making** (Hikosaka et al., 2014).

### Parkinson's disease and the nucleus accumbens

Two parts of the basal ganglia story connect directly to later clinical chapters.

**The nigrostriatal pathway and Parkinson's disease.** A pathway runs **from the substantia nigra of the midbrain to the striatum**. **Parkinson's disease**, a disorder marked by **rigidity**, **tremors** and **poverty of voluntary movement**, is associated with the **deterioration of this pathway**. This is why the substantia nigra, a midbrain structure, keeps appearing in discussions of the basal ganglia: it supplies one of their major inputs. (The chapter-opening case of Chapter 4, Roberto Garcia d'Orta, is a Parkinson's patient.)

**The nucleus accumbens and reward.** The **nucleus accumbens** lies in the **medial part of the ventral striatum**. It is thought to play a role in the **rewarding effects of addictive drugs** and of **other reinforcers** (Chapter 15 builds on this).

> **Trap.** **Striatum = caudate + putamen only**; the **globus pallidus** is a separate structure that receives the striatum's output. (Standard anatomy also groups the putamen and globus pallidus as the **lentiform nucleus**, a term Pinel does not use.) In **Parkinson's disease** the damage is to the **substantia nigra → striatum** pathway; the substantia nigra itself is in the **midbrain tegmentum**, not in the basal ganglia proper. And the **nucleus accumbens** is part of the **ventral striatum**, therefore a **basal ganglia** structure in Pinel's scheme, not a limbic one.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the four modules. Following them turns a list of names into a model.

1. **Everything is defined by a few binary splits.** CNS/PNS, brain/spinal cord, somatic/autonomic, afferent/efferent, sympathetic/parasympathetic; and at the structural level, cell bodies versus axons, CNS names versus PNS names (nucleus/ganglion, tract/nerve). Most definitional MCQs are a test of which side of one of these splits a term falls on.
2. **Grey means cell bodies, white means myelinated axons.** This one rule explains the spinal cord (grey H inside), the cortex (grey outside), the white laminae on the thalamus, and why the periaqueductal *gray* is a cluster of neurons around the aqueduct.
3. **Development explains the layout.** Three embryonic swellings become five; the telencephalon outgrows the rest; the other four become the brain stem; the upright human posture bends the neuraxis by about 90 degrees, which is why "dorsal" changes direction between spinal cord and cerebral hemispheres, and why the cortex folds into "lobes" that are anatomical, not functional, units.
4. **Every method shows one thing and hides another.** Golgi shows shape but not number; Nissl shows number but not shape; electron microscopy shows detail but loses the overall layout; anterograde and retrograde tracing answer opposite questions about connections. Real neuroanatomy combines them, as Figure 3.30's double-stained slide shows.
5. **Protection comes in layers.** Bone, then three meninges, then CSF in a single connected reservoir, then the blood-brain barrier at the vessel walls, with astrocytes helping to police what crosses. Each layer has its own failure mode: hydrocephalus when CSF cannot drain, CNS disorders when the barrier is impaired.
6. **"System" is a warning word.** The reticular "activating system", the limbic system and the basal ganglia system are all useful groupings that the authors explicitly refuse to treat as proven functional units. The same caution applies to lobes. The 11th edition also revises old certainties: glia are no longer mere support cells (nor ten times as numerous as neurons), the cerebellum is not purely motor, and the thalamus is not a one-way relay.

## Worked example

*Part A (clinical localisation).* A 45-year-old woman has had worsening headaches for two months. Imaging shows a small tumour in the midbrain. Her **lateral and third ventricles are enlarged**, but her **fourth ventricle is normal in size**. Before surgery the neurosurgeon drains some fluid, and afterwards she reports sharp pain whenever she turns her head quickly.

**Where is the blockage?** CSF made by the choroid plexuses flows from the lateral ventricles to the third, then through the **cerebral aqueduct** to the fourth, and out into the subarachnoid space. Ventricles upstream of a block swell; those downstream do not. Enlarged lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth ventricle put the block **between the third and fourth ventricles**, at the cerebral aqueduct, exactly Pinel's example of how **hydrocephalus** arises. The midbrain location fits, because the aqueduct runs through the mesencephalon.

**What else might the tumour be pressing on?** The aqueduct is surrounded by the **periaqueductal gray** (opioid analgesia), with the **tectum** dorsal to it (superior colliculi for visual-motor orienting, inferior colliculi for hearing) and the rest of the **tegmentum** ventral to it (substantia nigra and red nucleus, both sensorimotor). Symptoms in any of these domains would be consistent with the location.

**Why the pain after drainage?** CSF **supports and cushions** the brain. Removing some reduces that support, which is why patients who have had CSF drained commonly report headaches and stabbing pain on sudden head movements.

**What is the treatment logic?** Drain the excess fluid and try to remove the obstruction, as the book states. (Clinicians usually do the draining with a shunt.)

*Part B (research design).* A researcher wants to know (1) which brain areas send axons **to** the nucleus accumbens, (2) where the accumbens **sends** its own axons, (3) **how many** neurons it contains, (4) what its neurons' **dendritic trees** look like, and (5) the fine structure of its **synapses**.

1. Inputs **to** an area: inject a **retrograde** tracer into the accumbens; terminal buttons there take it up and carry it **back** to the cell bodies of the projecting neurons; slice the brain after a few days and look for labelled cell bodies.
2. Outputs **from** the area: inject an **anterograde** tracer into the accumbens; its cell bodies carry it **forward** to their terminals; find the labelled terminals.
3. Cell counts: **Nissl** stain (cresyl violet), count the stained cell bodies.
4. Dendritic shape: **Golgi** stain, which fills a few neurons entirely in black silhouette.
5. Synaptic structure: **electron microscopy**, because a light microscope tops out at about 1,500× magnification.

To report the injection site, she would describe the accumbens as lying in the **medial** part of the **ventral striatum**, a **basal ganglia** structure in the **telencephalon**, and she would show it in a **frontal (coronal)** section.

## MCQ traps

1. **SNS** in Pinel = **somatic** nervous system, not sympathetic.
2. The **ANS** has **afferent** fibres too (from internal organs); sympathetic/parasympathetic refers only to its **efferent** nerves.
3. **Afferent = toward CNS (sensory); efferent = away (motor).** a = approach/arrive; e = exit/escape.
4. **Sympathetic** leaves the **thoracic and lumbar** cord; **parasympathetic** leaves the **brain and sacral** cord.
5. Sympathetic synapses are **far** from the target; parasympathetic synapses are **near** the target on **very short** second-stage neurons.
6. **12 pairs** of cranial nerves, numbered **front to back**; **olfactory (I)** and **optic (II)** are purely sensory; most are mixed.
7. **Vagus (X)** is the **longest** cranial nerve, motor and sensory, to and from the **gut**.
8. All **autonomic motor fibres in cranial nerves are parasympathetic**.
9. Meninges outside → in: **dura mater** (tough mother) → **arachnoid** (spider-web) → **subarachnoid space** (CSF, large vessels) → **pia mater** (pious mother). Singular: **meninx**.
10. CSF **made** by the **choroid plexuses** (capillary networks from the pia); **absorbed** into **dural sinuses** → **jugular veins**. 11th ed.: more complex than this (Brinker et al., 2014).
11. **Cerebral aqueduct** links the **third and fourth** ventricles; blockage → **hydrocephalus** ("water head").
12. Blood-brain barrier = **tightly packed cells of cerebral blood vessel walls**, not the meninges; blocks mainly **proteins and other large molecules**; **glucose** is **actively transported**; some regions are leaky.
13. Glia : neurons ≈ **2 : 3** (Nimmerjahn & Bergles, 2015; von Bartheld, 2017). "10 times as many glia" is **wrong** in the 11th ed.
14. **Oligodendrocytes = CNS**, several segments, often several axons. **Schwann cells = PNS**, one segment each, and **only they guide regeneration**.
15. **Microglia = smallest** glia (injury response, engulf debris, inflammation); **astrocytes = largest**, star-shaped.
16. **Nucleus/tract = CNS; ganglion/nerve = PNS.** "Nucleus" has two meanings (organelle; cluster of cell bodies).
17. **Most neurons are multipolar** (>2 processes). Unipolar = 1, bipolar = 2. **Interneurons** = short or no axon, integrate within one structure.
18. **Golgi stain**: Camillo Golgi, Italian physician, **early 1870s**, **accidental** (staining meninges), potassium dichromate + silver nitrate → silver chromate; **few** neurons, **black**, whole silhouette → **shape**.
19. **Nissl stain**: Franz Nissl, German psychiatrist, **1880s**; **cresyl violet**; binds **DNA and RNA** in cell bodies → **counting** cells.
20. Light microscope limit ≈ **1,500×**. **Scanning EM** = **3-D** but **less** magnification than conventional EM.
21. **Anterograde** = inject at cell bodies, transported **forward** to terminals (where does it project?). **Retrograde** = taken up by **terminals**, transported **back** to cell bodies (what projects here?).
22. **Rostral = anterior; caudal = posterior.** In humans, the **top of the head and the back** are both **dorsal**; the **bottom of the head and the front** are both **ventral**; superior/inferior used for the primate head.
23. **Frontal = coronal.** **Sagittal** separates left/right; **midsagittal** = exactly between hemispheres; **cross section** = right angle to a long structure.
24. Spinal cord: **grey inside (H), white outside**; cortex: **grey outside, white beneath**.
25. **31 levels → 62 spinal nerves.** **Dorsal root = sensory, unipolar, cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia. Ventral root = motor, multipolar, cell bodies in ventral horns.**
26. Five divisions anterior → posterior: **tel-, di-, mes-, met-, myelencephalon** (tel on top, rest alphabetical). Forebrain → tel + di; hindbrain → met + myel.
27. Pinel's **brain stem** = the **four** divisions below the telencephalon, **including the diencephalon**.
28. **Reticular formation**: about **100** nuclei, medulla to midbrain; "reticular activating system" is **misleading** because it serves sleep, attention, movement, muscle tone and cardiac/circulatory/respiratory reflexes too.
29. **Tectum** (roof, dorsal): **superior colliculi = visual-motor**, **inferior colliculi = auditory**. **Tegmentum** (ventral): **PAG** (opioid analgesia), **substantia nigra**, **red nucleus**. Lower vertebrates: **optic tectum**.
30. Thalamus: two lobes either side of the **third ventricle**, joined by the **massa intermedia**. **LGN = vision, MGN = hearing, VPN = somatosensory**; relay nuclei receive **cortical feedback**.
31. **Hypothalamus** below the **anterior** thalamus; **pituitary** = "snot gland"; **optic chiasm** and **mammillary bodies** on its inferior surface, mammillary bodies **behind** the pituitary.
32. Pinel's **central fissure** = central sulcus elsewhere; **lateral fissure** = Sylvian fissure. **Precentral = motor (frontal); postcentral = somatosensory (parietal).**
33. **Neocortex** ≈ **90 percent** of human cortex, **six** layers numbered **I to VI from the surface**; **layer IV thick in sensory**, **layer V thick in motor** cortex.
34. **Pyramidal** cells = large multipolar, apical dendrite, long axon; **stellate** cells = small star-shaped **interneurons** (not astrocytes).
35. **Limbic** = "ring" around the thalamus; **four F's**: fleeing, feeding, fighting, sexual behaviour. **Fornix** = the major limbic **tract** (arc), ending in septum and mammillary bodies.
36. **Striatum = caudate + putamen**; **globus pallidus** is its main output target, medial to the putamen. **Nucleus accumbens** = medial **ventral striatum** (reward).

| **Precentral gyri** | Frontal-lobe gyri in front of the central fissure; motor |

| **Superior temporal gyri** | Temporal-lobe gyri; hearing and language |

## Likely exam points

1. The "system of twos" tree from nervous system down to sympathetic/parasympathetic (Figure 3.2).
2. Somatic = external environment; autonomic = internal environment; both contain afferent and efferent nerves.
3. Sympathetic outflow thoracic + lumbar, parasympathetic brain + sacral; both two-stage, relay far from vs near the target.
4. The three conventional sympathetic/parasympathetic principles and the caveat that each has exceptions (Guyenet, 2006).
5. Cranial nerves: 12 pairs numbered front to back; I and II purely sensory; vagus (X) longest; autonomic fibres parasympathetic; used to localise pathology.
6. The three meninges in order with literal meanings; CSF in the subarachnoid space.
7. CSF: four ventricles + central canal + subarachnoid space as one reservoir; choroid plexus → dural sinuses → jugular veins; support and cushioning.
8. Hydrocephalus from a blocked channel such as the cerebral aqueduct; drainage and removal of the obstruction.
9. Blood-brain barrier: tightly packed vessel walls; glucose actively transported; leaky regions; governs drug access; impaired in CNS disorders.
10. External and internal features of a multipolar neuron and the job of each.
11. Cell membrane: lipid bilayer with channel and signal proteins.
12. Unipolar, bipolar, multipolar (most common) and interneurons.
13. Nuclei vs ganglia, tracts vs nerves.
14. Glia : neuron ratio about 2 : 3, not 10 : 1.
15. Oligodendrocytes vs Schwann cells (location, segments, regeneration); microglia smallest, astrocytes largest.
16. Expanded roles of astrocytes and microglia; at least 15 astrocyte types; a new glial type (Fan & Agid, 2018).
17. Golgi (Italian physician, 1870s, accidental, silver chromate, shape) vs Nissl (German psychiatrist, 1880s, cresyl violet, counting).
18. Electron microscopy: the ~1,500× light limit; scanning EM gives 3-D images at lower magnification.
19. Anterograde vs retrograde tracing.
20. Three axes of direction, rostral/caudal, the human dorsal/ventral complication, superior/inferior, proximal/distal.
21. Horizontal, frontal (coronal), sagittal, midsagittal and cross sections.
22. Spinal cord: grey H inside, white outside; 31 pairs (62) spinal nerves; dorsal root sensory unipolar (dorsal root ganglia), ventral root motor multipolar (ventral horns).
23. Three embryonic swellings → five divisions; the tel-then-alphabetical mnemonic; Pinel's brain stem.
24. Reticular formation: about 100 nuclei, many functions, "activating system" misleading.
25. Pons (ventral) and cerebellum (dorsal; movement plus cognition).
26. Tectum (superior colliculi visual-motor, inferior auditory) vs tegmentum (PAG, substantia nigra, red nucleus).
27. Thalamus: massa intermedia; LGN, MGN, VPN; cortical feedback. Hypothalamus: motivated behaviour, pituitary, optic chiasm, mammillary bodies.
28. Cortex: convolutions and body size; fissures, sulci, gyri; corpus callosum; four lobes, their major gyri and functions; lobes not functional units.
29. Neocortex: 90 percent, six layers, pyramidal vs stellate cells, columns, layer IV (sensory) and V (motor); hippocampus three layers.
30. Limbic ring (amygdala, hippocampus, cingulate cortex, fornix, septum, mammillary bodies; four F's) and basal ganglia (striatum → globus pallidus; nigrostriatal pathway and Parkinson's; nucleus accumbens and reward).
