# Chapter 2: Evolution, Genetics, and Experience

**What this chapter is really about.** Most chapters of this book teach you a piece of the nervous system. This one teaches you a way of thinking, and it does so by first taking apart two ways of thinking that you almost certainly already hold. The first is the habit of sorting any human ability into one of two bins, *physiological* or *psychological*, as if some activities (a reflex, a hormone surge) belong to the body and others (self-awareness, love, intelligence) float free of it. The second is the habit of asking whether a trait is *inherited* or *learned*, or, in its more sophisticated-sounding form, *how much* of it is inherited and how much learned. Pinel and Barnes argue that both habits are products of the cultural climate you grew up in, that both are inconsistent with the evidence, and that the correct replacement is a model in which every behaviour is the product of **interactions** among an organism's genes (shaped by evolution), its experience, and its perception of the present situation.

The rest of the chapter fills in the three ingredients of that model. Module 2.2 covers **evolution**: Darwin, natural selection, how behaviour itself drives evolution, the timeline from the first multicellular organisms to *Homo sapiens*, nine common misconceptions about evolution, and how the brain evolved. Module 2.3 covers **genetics**: Mendel's peas, chromosomes, DNA, how a gene becomes a protein, the Human Genome Project, and the field that the 11th edition treats as its headline theme, **epigenetics**. Module 2.4 gives two classic demonstrations that genes and experience interact (selectively bred rats and phenylketonuria). Module 2.5 turns to the most misread number in all of psychology, the **heritability estimate**, and shows with twin research why it tells you nothing about any single person.

**How to read this chapter.** Treat 2.1 and 2.5 as a pair: the musician-and-instrument metaphor introduced in 2.1 is the key that unlocks heritability in 2.5, and examiners test both ends of it. Module 2.2 is fact-heavy (dates, the order of hominin evolution, and a cluster of confusable terms: spandrels, exaptations, homologous, analogous, convergent evolution), so read it for precision. Module 2.3 is vocabulary that every later chapter assumes (allele, homozygous, phenotype, transcription, codon); learn the pairs as contrasts, because that is how they are examined. Where the 11th edition has dropped or revised material that older editions and older answer keys still use (mate bonding, birdsong, mitochondrial DNA, some evolutionary dates, the phrase "identical twins"), the notes say so in a Trap or in a clearly labelled older-edition box.

## Opening: The Problem Is Your Zeitgeist

The chapter does not open with a patient. It opens with a diagnosis of the reader. A **zeitgeist** (German, roughly "spirit of the times"; pronounced *TSYTE-gyste*) is the general intellectual climate of a culture, the set of assumptions so widely shared that nobody notices they are assumptions. Pinel and Barnes claim that the zeitgeist you absorbed encourages you to think about the biological bases of behaviour in ways the facts do not support, and that the main purpose of the chapter is to correct that.

The specific fault they target is a fondness for **simple, mutually exclusive dichotomies**: right or wrong, good or bad, attractive or unattractive. Dichotomies are attractive because they are easy; they let you ignore the subtleties and inconsistencies of real phenomena. In biopsychology the two most damaging dichotomies are the ones named above, physiological versus psychological and inherited versus learned. The authors dwell on them precisely because they are still among the most common questions students ask in biopsychology classes.

> **Trap.** "Zeitgeist" is a glossary term in this chapter (and reappears in history-of-psychology papers). It means the intellectual climate of a culture or era, not a particular school of thought or a particular theorist's world view. A question that defines it as "the dominant theory of a period" is close but slightly off; the book's sense is broader, the general climate within which theories are formed.

## 2.1 Thinking about the Biology of Behaviour: From Dichotomies to Interactions

### Where the physiological-or-psychological split came from

To see why people instinctively separate the physical from the mental, Pinel and Barnes go back to the end of the Middle Ages. For much of Western history, truth was simply whatever the Church declared it to be. From about **1400**, as the famines, plagues and wars of the Dark Ages eased, attention turned to art, trade and learning. This was the **Renaissance** ("rebirth", roughly **1400 to 1700**), and some of its scholars began doing something new: instead of deferring to doctrine, they examined things directly. That habit of direct observation is the root of modern science.

The difficulty was that much of what the new observers found contradicted Church teaching. The French philosopher **René Descartes** (**1596 to 1650**; pronounced *day-CART*) offered a settlement that, in effect, divided the universe between the two authorities. He proposed that reality is made of two quite different kinds of thing:

1. **Physical matter**, which obeys natural laws and can therefore be studied scientifically. The human body, *brain included*, belonged here, and so did non-human animals in their entirety.
2. **The human mind** (also called the soul, self or spirit), which has no physical substance, obeys no natural laws, controls human behaviour, and was therefore the business of the Church.

This philosophy became known as **Cartesian dualism**. Because the Roman Church endorsed it, the idea that mind and brain are separate entities spread even more widely, and it has outlived several centuries of scientific progress. The book's observation about the present day is the important part: most people now accept that behaviour has a physiological basis, yet many still assume, without examining the assumption, that some category of human activity *transcends* the brain.

> **Trap.** Two details are regularly tested. First, under Cartesian dualism the **brain is on the physical side**; it is the *mind* that is non-physical. Students sometimes write "Descartes separated the brain from the body", which is wrong. Second, in Descartes's scheme **non-human animals were entirely physical**, with no mind at all. That is exactly why the chimpanzee self-recognition studies later in this module are such a direct challenge to dualistic thinking.

### Where the nature-or-nurture split came from

The same taste for dichotomies shapes how people think about **development**. For centuries scholars argued about whether humans and other animals inherit their behavioural capacities or acquire them through learning. This argument is the **nature-nurture issue**.

In the early twentieth century the two sides of the issue were, roughly, two continents:

- **North American experimental psychology** was overwhelmingly committed to **nurture**. Its most famous spokesman, **John B. Watson**, the father of behaviourism, denied that there was any real evidence for the inheritance of behavioural traits. He claimed (Watson, 1930) that if he were given **a dozen healthy infants** and complete control over the world they were raised in, he could train any one of them, picked at random, into any kind of specialist he chose, from doctor or lawyer to beggar or thief, and that a baby born into a long line of criminals would turn out well with careful upbringing.
- **European ethology** took the opposite emphasis. **Ethology** is the study of animal behaviour in the wild. The ethologists concentrated on **instinctive behaviours**, behaviours that appear in all like members of a species even when there seems to have been no opportunity to learn them, and they emphasised **nature** (inherited factors). Because these behaviours were not learned, the early ethologists concluded that they must be *entirely* inherited.

Pinel and Barnes's verdict is blunt: the ethologists were wrong, and so were the early experimental psychologists. Both made the same logical error, assuming that if a trait is not the product of one factor it must be wholly the product of the other.

> **Trap (older keys and other textbooks).** The 11th edition does not name individual ethologists. Questions drawn from other sources may ask who founded ethology: the standard answer is **Konrad Lorenz** and **Niko Tinbergen** (who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize with Karl von Frisch). If an MCQ asks which tradition emphasised *nature* and *instinctive* behaviour, the answer is **European ethology**; North American behaviourism (Watson) emphasised *nurture*. Do not reverse the two continents.

### Why the physiological-or-psychological dichotomy fails

Pinel and Barnes note that Cartesian dualism came under attack soon after the Church endorsed it. Their historical example is **Julien Offray de la Mettrie**, who in **1747** anonymously published a pamphlet that scandalised Europe. His offence was to argue that thought is produced by the brain. For this he had to flee to Berlin, where he lived in exile for the rest of his life. The story is a reminder that the claim now taken for granted in every neuroscience class was once considered dangerous.

The modern case against **physiological-or-psychological thinking** (the assumption that some aspects of human psychological functioning are so complex they could not be the product of a physical brain) rests on **two lines of evidence**:

1. **Brain manipulation changes even the most complex psychological processes.** Damage to, or stimulation of, particular parts of the brain can alter self-awareness, memory or emotion. If the "highest" mental functions can be removed or distorted by a lesion, they cannot be independent of the brain.
2. **Non-human species show abilities once thought to be purely psychological and purely human.** Some species, especially primates, are capable of things such as complex problem solving that were assumed to require a uniquely human, non-physical mind.

The book illustrates each line with a case, and it chooses both cases on the same topic, **self-awareness**, because self-awareness is widely regarded as one of the hallmarks of the human mind. If even self-awareness can be broken by brain damage, and if even a chimpanzee can show it, the case for a separate, non-physical mind collapses from both sides.

### The Case of the Man Who Fell Out of Bed

The first case comes from the neurologist **Oliver Sacks (1985)**. His patient, a man in hospital, woke to find what he took to be a severed human leg in the bed beside him. He concluded that a nurse had planted it as a grotesque prank, and he threw it out of the bed, only to find himself landing on the floor with the leg still attached. When Sacks, trying to calm him, asked where his own left leg was if this one was not it, the man could not say; as far as he was concerned his leg had vanished.

The patient was suffering from **asomatognosia**, a deficiency in the awareness of parts of one's own body. Three facts about it are examinable:

- It typically involves the **left side** of the body.
- It usually results from damage to the **right frontal and parietal lobes** (Feinberg et al., 2010).
- It is a disturbance of **self-awareness** produced by **brain damage**.

The point Pinel and Barnes draw is that the man's altered experience was strange and psychologically complex, yet it was unmistakably the result of a lesion. More generally, they argue, the whole range of human experience can be produced by manipulating the brain. A "psychological" phenomenon as rich as the sense that one's body belongs to oneself turns out to be something the brain does.

> **Trap.** Asomatognosia is **left-sided** body unawareness after **right-hemisphere** (frontal and parietal) damage. Expect distractors that swap the side of damage, or that name the temporal or occipital lobe. Note also that Sacks's patient is a *clinical* illustration of the first line of evidence (brain damage changes self-awareness), not the second (animal abilities).

### The Case of the Chimps with Mirrors

The second case comes from **G. G. Gallup**'s research on self-awareness in **chimpanzees** (Gallup, 1983). One way to probe whether an animal is self-aware is to give it a mirror. A chimpanzee's first reaction is always social: it behaves as if it were looking at another chimpanzee. After a day or two, however, its behaviour changes. It begins to use the mirror to groom itself, to inspect parts of its body it cannot otherwise see, and to experiment with its reflection by pulling faces and adopting odd postures while watching the result.

Gallup wanted evidence that was harder to explain away, so he devised the **mark test**:

1. Each chimpanzee was **anaesthetised**.
2. While it was unconscious, its **eyebrow** (eyebrow ridge) was painted with a **red, odourless dye**. Odourless matters: the chimp could not detect the mark by smell, and because it was applied under anaesthesia the chimp had no memory of its application.
3. After the chimp recovered, the **mirror was reintroduced**.

On seeing the marked eyebrow in the mirror, each chimpanzee repeatedly **touched the marked spot on its own face** while watching its reflection. The time spent looking in the mirror rose **more than threefold**, and several chimps kept touching their eyebrows and then **smelling their fingers**. A chimp that treated the reflection as another animal would have no reason to touch its *own* eyebrow; the behaviour only makes sense if the animal recognises the image as itself.

Since Gallup's demonstration, what is now called the **mirror self-recognition test** has been passed by several other species, including **Asian elephants, orangutans and European magpies**. Humans pass it only from about **15 to 24 months of age**.

The point is the mirror image of Sacks's case. Non-human animals, which some people assume have no mind at all, are capable of considerable psychological complexity, here self-awareness, even though their brains are less complex than ours.

> **Trap.** The mark was painted on the **eyebrow (ridge)**, with **red, odourless** dye, under **anaesthesia**; mirror-viewing time increased **over threefold**. Human infants pass the test at **15 to 24 months**, not at birth and not at 6 months. Magpies (a bird) are on the list of species that pass, which is a favourite "which of the following" option.

### Why the nature-or-nurture dichotomy fails

Pinel and Barnes borrow a line in the spirit of Mark Twain: reports of the death of nature-or-nurture thinking have been greatly exaggerated. Each time it has been discredited, it has returned in a slightly altered form. They trace three versions.

**Version 1: "genetics or learning".** The original question asked whether a behaviour was inherited or learned. It was undermined when factors other than genes and learning were shown to shape behavioural development: the **prenatal environment**, **nutrition**, **stress** and **sensory stimulation**. The concept of nurture was widened accordingly.

**Version 2: "genetics or experience".** With the wider concept, the question became whether a behaviour was due to genes or to experience (any experiential factor, not only learning). This version was undermined by the convincing argument that behaviour *always* develops under the **joint control of both** nature and nurture (Johnston, 1987; Rutter, 1997), never under one alone.

**Version 3: "how much genetics, how much experience?"** Faced with that argument, many people simply swapped one dichotomy for another. They stopped asking *whether* a trait is genetic and began asking *what proportion* of it is genetic and what proportion experiential. This sounds more sophisticated, but Pinel and Barnes call it **fundamentally flawed**, because it assumes that genes and experience combine **additively**: that a capacity such as intelligence is assembled by adding a quantity of genetics to a quantity of experience. In reality the two **interact**, and when two factors interact the question "how much of the outcome is due to each?" has no meaningful answer.

### The Case of the Thinking Student

Pinel illustrates the point with an exchange with one of his students. She had read that intelligence is **one-third genetic and two-thirds experience** and asked whether that was true. Instead of answering, he asked her whether it would be sensible, if he wanted to understand music better, to start by asking how much of the music comes from the musician and how much from the instrument. She saw at once that the question was silly: the music is produced by the two together, and the only sensible thing to study is how they interact. Before he could finish drawing the parallel she drew it herself: intelligence comes from the interaction of genes and experience, so asking what fraction comes from each is equally pointless.

The book extends the metaphor with three more interactions where the "how much from each?" question is obviously empty:

- how much of **evaporation** is due to the water and how much to the temperature;
- how much of **reproduction** is due to the male and how much to the female;
- how much of a piece of **music** is due to the musician and how much to the instrument.

In each case the answer lies in understanding the **nature of the interaction**, not in apportioning credit. The same holds for behavioural development.

> **Trap.** This metaphor is used **twice** in the chapter, for **different** purposes, and examiners exploit the confusion. Here in 2.1 it shows that you **cannot** partition a **single individual's** trait into genetic and experiential percentages. In 2.5 the same metaphor is extended to show that you **can** partition the **differences among many individuals** in a sample. Keep "development of an individual" (inseparable) and "development of differences among individuals" (statistically separable) apart.

> **Trap (older keys).** Many older texts and answer keys illustrate the same point with an analogy usually attributed to **Donald Hebb**: asking how much of a behaviour is due to heredity and how much to environment is like asking how much of a field's **area** is due to its **length** and how much to its **width**. The 11th edition uses the musician-and-instrument version instead, but the logic is identical: when two factors combine multiplicatively or interactively, a "percentage due to each" for a single case is meaningless.

### A model of the biology of behaviour

Having shown what is wrong with the two dichotomies, Pinel and Barnes set out the way of thinking adopted by most biopsychologists (their Figure 2.3). The whole model reduces to one premise: **all behaviour is the product of interactions among three factors**:

1. the organism's **genetic endowment**, which is itself a product of its **evolution**;
2. its **experience**;
3. its **perception of the current situation**.

The figure lays this out as a seven-step loop, and it is worth being able to reconstruct it in order, because each step anticipates a later module:

1. **Evolution** influences the pool of behaviour-influencing **genes** available to the members of each species. (Module 2.2.)
2. Each individual's genes initiate a **unique programme of neural development**. (Module 2.3.)
3. **Experience modifies the expression** of the individual's genes. (Epigenetics, end of 2.3, and 2.4.)
4. The development of each individual's nervous system depends on its **interactions with its environment**, that is, on its experience.
5. The individual's **current behavioural capacities and tendencies** are determined by its unique patterns of neural activity, some of which are experienced as **thoughts, feelings and memories**.
6. **Current behaviour** arises from interactions among those ongoing patterns of neural activity and the individual's **perception of the current situation**.
7. The **success** of the individual's behaviour influences the likelihood that its genes will be **passed on** to future generations, which feeds back to step 1.

Two features of the model deserve explicit comment. First, step 5 quietly settles the physiological-or-psychological question: thoughts, feelings and memories are not a separate realm but the way some patterns of neural activity are *experienced*. Second, there are two feedback loops. Behaviour feeds back into **experience** (what you do changes what happens to you, which changes how your genes are expressed and how your brain develops), and behaviour feeds back into **evolution** (what you do affects whether your genes are passed on). Neither genes nor experience sits at the start of a one-way chain.

The book closes the module with a journal prompt worth answering for yourself: if a student asked whether **depression** is physiological or psychological, what would you say? The model's answer is that the question is malformed. Depression is a pattern of neural activity that is experienced as low mood; it is shaped by genes whose expression is modified by experience, and it is triggered and maintained by the person's perception of their current situation. It is both, and it is neither, because "physiological" and "psychological" are not two separate categories of cause.

> **High-yield.** Behaviour = interaction of **(1) genetic endowment (from evolution)**, **(2) experience**, **(3) perception of the current situation**. Seven-step loop: evolution → genes → experience modifies gene expression → experience shapes neural development → neural activity determines capacities (some experienced as thoughts, feelings, memories) → current behaviour from neural activity × perceived situation → behavioural success affects which genes are passed on.

The remaining modules take the three ingredients in turn: evolution (2.2), genetics (2.3), and the interaction of genetics and experience in development (2.4). The final module (2.5) asks a different question, about the genetics of **differences** among people.

## 2.2 Human Evolution

This module does three jobs. It gives the history of evolutionary theory (Darwin and natural selection), it shows that behaviour is not merely a product of evolution but one of its engines (social dominance and courtship displays), and it walks through the course of evolution from the first complex organisms to modern humans, ending with nine common misunderstandings about evolution and a section on how the brain evolved.

### Darwin's theory of evolution

Modern biology is usually dated from **1859**, the year **Charles Darwin** published ***On the Origin of Species***, which set out the theory of evolution, the single most influential theory in the biological sciences. To **evolve** is to undergo systematic change from pre-existing species.

Be precise about Darwin's contribution, because examiners are. He was **not the first** to suggest that species evolve. He was the first to **assemble a large body of supporting evidence**, and the first to propose **how** evolution happens.

Darwin offered **three kinds of evidence** that species evolve:

1. **The fossil record** changes systematically through progressively more recent geological layers. (The book's figure illustrates this with the evolution of the hominin skull.)
2. **Striking structural similarities among living species**, for example among a human's hand, a bird's wing and a cat's paw, which suggest descent from common ancestors.
3. **Selective breeding**: programmes of selective breeding had produced major changes in domestic plants and animals.

Pinel and Barnes add a **fourth** kind of evidence, which they rate as the most convincing of all: **direct observation of rapid evolution in progress**. Their example is **Grant (1991)**, who observed evolution among the **finches of the Galápagos Islands**, the same population Darwin himself studied, after just a **single season of drought**. As the figure explains, an **18-month drought** on one island left only large, hard-to-eat seeds, which improved the chances that a bird with a long beak would survive and breed. Within one drought, the population shifted.

> **Trap.** Count carefully. Darwin presented **three** kinds of evidence (fossils, structural similarities, selective breeding); the book's figure shows **four** because it adds direct observation of evolution in progress (Grant, 1991, Galápagos finches), which the authors call the **most convincing**. A question asking for "Darwin's evidence" expects three; a question asking what the book considers the strongest evidence expects direct observation.

### Natural selection and fitness

Darwin's proposed mechanism was **natural selection**. The argument runs in three steps:

1. Members of every species **vary** a great deal in structure, physiology and behaviour.
2. Heritable traits that go with **high rates of survival and reproduction** are the ones most likely to be passed to the next generation.
3. Repeated over many generations, this leads to species that are increasingly well adapted to surviving and reproducing in their **particular environmental niche**.

Darwin chose the name deliberately, to stress the parallel with **artificial selection** by animal breeders. A horse breeder produces faster horses by mating the fastest animals in the existing stock; nature produces fitter animals by, in effect, "breeding" the fittest. **Fitness**, in Darwin's sense, is the ability of an organism to **survive and contribute its genes to the next generation**.

Darwin's theory clashed with the dogmatic views built into the nineteenth-century zeitgeist and met strong resistance at first. Resistance has not disappeared, but Pinel and Barnes point out that it comes almost entirely from people who do not understand the evidence. They quote the science writer **David Quammen (2004)**, whose point is that the evidence for evolution is abundant, varied, growing and freely available, so that nobody needs to accept evolution as a matter of faith.

> **Trap.** **Fitness** in the Darwinian sense is **not** strength, health or athletic ability. It is reproductive success: survival *and* passing on genes. An animal that lives a long healthy life but leaves no offspring has a Darwinian fitness of zero. This is exactly why the next section (social dominance, courtship) matters: behaviour that increases mating success increases fitness even if it does nothing for survival.

### Social dominance

Some behaviours obviously affect evolution: finding food, avoiding predators and defending one's young all improve an animal's chances of passing on its genes. Pinel and Barnes concentrate on two whose role is less obvious but no less important.

The males of many species settle into a stable **hierarchy of social dominance** through combative encounters with other males. In some species the encounters cause real injury; in others they are mostly posturing and threat until one animal backs down. The dominant male typically wins against all others; the number-two male beats everyone except the dominant male; and so on down the line. Once the hierarchy is in place, fighting declines, because lower-ranking males learn to avoid or quickly submit to those above them. Most of the remaining fighting happens near the top, among males competing for high positions, so the lower levels of the hierarchy are usually only vaguely defined.

Why does dominance matter for evolution? The book gives two reasons.

**Dominant males copulate more.** **McCann (1981)** studied **10 bull elephant seals** sharing the same breeding beach. These enormous animals challenge one another by rearing up to full height and pushing chest to chest; usually the smaller one gives way, and if it does not, a savage neck-biting fight follows. McCann found that the **dominant male accounted for about 37 percent** of all copulations observed during the study, whereas the **lowest-ranking male (number 10) accounted for only about 1 percent**. Whatever traits made the top bull dominant were therefore far more likely to appear in the next generation.

**Dominant females produce more, and healthier, offspring.** **Pusey, Williams and Goodall (1997)** found that **high-ranking female chimpanzees** produced more offspring, and that those offspring were more likely to survive to sexual maturity. The researchers attributed the advantage to the fact that high-ranking females are better able to keep access to productive foraging areas.

> **Trap.** Two numbers and one sex. **McCann (1981)**: **10** bulls, top bull about **37 percent** of copulations, bottom bull about **1 percent**. **Pusey, Williams and Goodall (1997)**: the dominance advantage applies to **female** chimpanzees too (more offspring, better survival), so "social dominance affects only male reproductive success" is false.

### Courtship display and the origin of species

In many species, copulation is preceded by an intricate **courtship display**. The male approaches and signals interest; the signal may be olfactory, visual, auditory or tactile. His signal may elicit a signal from the female, which elicits a further response from him, and so on until copulation occurs. If either partner fails to respond appropriately at any step, copulation is unlikely.

Courtship displays matter because they are thought to **promote the evolution of new species**. To see why, you need the definition of a species. A **species** is a group of organisms **reproductively isolated** from other organisms: members of a species can produce **fertile** offspring only by mating with members of the same species.

A new species begins to split off when some **barrier** discourages breeding between a subpopulation and the rest of the species. Once that reproductive barrier exists, the subpopulation evolves independently until interbreeding with the original population becomes impossible. The barrier can be:

- **Geographic.** A few birds fly together to an isolated island; their descendants breed only among themselves for many generations and evolve into a separate species.
- **Behavioural.** A few members of a species develop **different courtship displays**. Because only the right exchange of displays leads to mating, these animals effectively stop breeding with the rest of their **conspecifics** (members of the same species). The behavioural difference becomes a reproductive barrier, and speciation can begin without any physical separation at all.

> **Trap.** **Conspecifics** are members of the **same** species. The word turns up throughout the book (rats learning food preferences from conspecifics, conspecific aggression), and questions sometimes offer "members of a different species" or "members of the same family" as distractors. Note also that the definition of species turns on **fertile** offspring; animals that can produce only sterile hybrids are still separate species.

### Evolution of vertebrates

By combining fossil evidence with comparisons among living species, scientists have reconstructed the evolutionary history of humans, though some details remain disputed. The book's sequence, with its dates, is the backbone of a lot of MCQs, so learn it as a timeline.

**Complex multicellular, water-dwelling organisms** first appeared about **800 million years ago**. Roughly **250 million years later** (about **550 million years ago**), the first **chordates** evolved. **Chordates** (pronounced *KOR-dates*) are animals with **dorsal nerve cords**, large nerves running along the middle of the back (the dorsum). They are one of the **40 or so phyla** (pronounced *FY-la*), the large categories into which zoologists group animal species.

About **25 million years after that** (about **525 million years ago**), the first chordates with **spinal bones** protecting the dorsal nerve cord evolved. The bones are **vertebrae** (*VERT-eh-bray*), and the chordates that have them are **vertebrates**. The earliest vertebrates were **primitive bony fishes**. Living vertebrates fall into **seven classes**: the **fishes** account for **three** of them, and the other four are **amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals**.

### Evolution of amphibians

The move onto land began about **410 million years ago**, when some bony fishes started making excursions out of the water. The book illustrates this with a recently discovered fossil of a "missing link" creature that had fish-like scales, teeth and gills together with primitive wrist and finger bones like those of land animals.

Fishes that could survive briefly on land had two big advantages: they could escape from **stagnant pools** to nearby fresh water, and they could exploit **terrestrial food sources**. The advantages were large enough that, through natural selection, **fins became legs and gills became lungs**, and the first **amphibians** (frogs, toads, salamanders) evolved about **370 million years ago**. Amphibians are only half-way out of the water: in their **larval** form they must live in water, and only adults can survive on land.

### Evolution of reptiles

About **315 million years ago**, **reptiles** (lizards, snakes, turtles) evolved from a branch of the amphibians. Reptiles were the first vertebrates to lay **shell-covered eggs** and to be covered in **dry scales**, and both adaptations cut their dependence on watery habitats.

The logic is neat. An amphibian has to spend its early life in a pond or lake. A reptile still spends its early life in water, but the water is *inside a shell-covered egg*, so the egg can be laid on land. Once hatched, a reptile can live far from water because its dry scales greatly reduce water loss through the skin.

### Evolution of mammals and primates

About **225 million years ago**, at the height of the age of dinosaurs, a new class of vertebrates evolved from one line of **small reptiles**. The females fed their young with secretions from special glands, the **mammary glands**, and the class is named **mammals** after them. Eventually mammals stopped laying eggs; instead, females nurtured their young inside their own bodies, in a watery internal environment, until the young were mature enough to be born. The **duck-billed platypus** is a surviving mammal that still lays eggs.

Spending the first stage of life inside the mother turned out to have great survival value: it gave the long-term security and environmental stability needed for **complex programmes of development** to unfold. Most classification systems now recognise about **26 orders of mammals**. Ours is the order **primates**, which we named, with characteristic modesty, from the Latin *primus*, "first" or "foremost".

Primates are hard to classify because **no single characteristic is possessed by all primates and by no other animals**. Even so, most experts agree on about **16 groups** of primates. The book's figure shows species from **five** of them:

| Group | Example shown in the figure |
|---|---|
| **Tarsiers** | Tarsier |
| **Cebids** (a New World monkey group) | Squirrel monkey |
| **Old World monkeys** | Hussar monkey |
| **Apes** | Silver-backed lowland gorilla |
| **Hominins** | Human |

The **apes** (gibbons, orangutans, gorillas and chimpanzees) are thought to have evolved from a line of **Old World monkeys**. Like Old World monkeys, apes have **long arms** and **grasping hind feet** specialised for **arboreal** (treetop) travel, and **opposable thumbs** that are not long enough to be much use for precise manipulation (the book's figure compares the hands and feet of a human and a chimpanzee). Unlike Old World monkeys, apes have **no tails** and can **walk upright for short distances**.

**Chimpanzees are the closest living relatives of humans**: almost **99 percent** of genes are identical in the two species. But the actual ape ancestor of humans is probably **long extinct**; humans did not evolve from chimpanzees, and the two share a common ancestor.

> **Trap.** "Humans evolved from chimpanzees" is false. Chimpanzees are our closest *living* relatives (about 99 percent of genes shared), and humans and chimps descend from a common ancestor that is itself long extinct. Similarly, apes evolved from **Old World** monkeys, not New World monkeys; and the platypus is the book's example of an **egg-laying mammal**.

### Emergence of humankind

**Hominins** are the primates of the group that includes humans. In the book's taxonomy of our species, humans are: kingdom **Animal**, phylum **Chordate**, class **Mammal**, order **Primate**, family **Hominin**, genus ***Homo***, species ***sapiens***. Hominins include **six sub-groups**, among them ***Australopithecus*** and ***Homo***. The fossil record suggests that *Homo* contained **at least eight species**, **seven of which are extinct**; the **Neanderthals** (*Homo neanderthalensis*) are one of the extinct ones, and **we (*Homo sapiens*) are the only survivor**.

Human evolution is hard to reconstruct because the evidence is thin: only a few partial hominin fossils from the critical period have been found. Pinel and Barnes single out **three discoveries** that have been particularly illuminating:

1. An unusually complete fossil of a **3-year-old early *Australopithecus* girl** found in **Ethiopia**. Her skull is about **3.3 million years old**.
2. Fossils showing that a population of **tiny hominins** lived on the Indonesian island of **Flores** as recently as **18,000 years ago**.
3. Several early **Australopithecine** fossils with a **mixture of human and non-human characteristics**, found in a pit in **South Africa** (Pickering et al., 2011).

Many experts believe the **Australopithecines** evolved about **4 million years ago in Africa** from a line of apes. The name is descriptive: the Latin and Greek roots mean "southern ape" (*australo*, southern; *pithecus*, ape). The genus seems to have persisted for about **2 million years**, with several species living on the African plains, before dying out. They were only about **1.3 metres (4 feet)** tall and had **small brains**, but their **pelvis and leg bones** show that they walked **upright**. Any remaining doubt was removed by a set of **fossilised footprints**: about **3.6 million years ago**, Australopithecine hominins walked across African **volcanic ash**, leaving a **70-metre trail**. The prints belong to **two adults and a child**, and the child often stepped in the adults' footprints.

> **Trap.** "Southern ape" is the meaning of *Australopithecus*; it has nothing to do with Australia. Note also the pairing the book insists on: Australopithecines had **small brains but upright posture**. Upright walking came long before the big brain, which is the opposite of what many students assume.

### The genus Homo and the arrival of modern humans

The first ***Homo*** species are thought to have evolved from one species of *Australopithecus* about **2 to 2.8 million years ago**. Their most distinctive feature was the size of the **brain cavity**: **larger than that of *Australopithecus* but smaller than that of modern humans**. Early *Homo* species **used fire and tools**, and they **coexisted in Africa with various Australopithecine species for about half a million years**, until the Australopithecines died out. Early *Homo* species also lived **outside Africa** for about **1.85 million years**. Then, about **275,000 years ago**, early *Homo* species were gradually replaced in the fossil record by **modern humans (*Homo sapiens*)**.

Pinel and Barnes then point out a paradox. The **"big three" human attributes**, a **large brain**, **upright posture**, and **free hands with an opposable thumb**, have been present for **hundreds of thousands of years**, yet most of what we count as human accomplishment is very recent:

|---|---|
| Artistic products (wall paintings, carvings) | **40,000** years |
| Ranching and farming | **10,000** years |
| Writing | **7,500** years |

### Thinking about human evolution: nine commonly misunderstood points

The book's summary figure shows the main branches of vertebrate evolution (bony fishes, amphibians, reptiles, mammals) and their emergence dates (vertebrates 525, amphibians 410, reptiles 315, mammals 225, hominins 6 million years ago). Against that background, Pinel and Barnes list **nine** commonly misunderstood points about evolution. These are the "cautions on interpreting evolution", and almost every one of them has produced an exam question.

1. **Evolution does not proceed in a single line.** The familiar image of an evolutionary ladder or scale is misleading; a far better metaphor is a **dense bush**, with many branches growing in many directions at once.
2. **Humans have little reason to claim evolutionary supremacy.** We are simply the **last surviving species** of a group (the hominins) that has existed for only a **blip** of evolutionary time.
3. **Evolution does not always proceed slowly and gradually.** Rapid change, within **a few generations**, can be triggered by sudden changes in the environment or by **adaptive mutations**. Whether *human* evolution occurred gradually or suddenly is still hotly debated among **paleontologists** (scientists who study fossils).
4. **Few products of evolution have survived to the present.** Only the tips of the branches of the evolutionary bush are alive today: **fewer than 1 percent** of all known species still exist.
5. **Evolution does not progress toward preordained perfection.** Evolution is a **tinkerer, not an architect**. Improvements come from modifying existing programmes of development, and the results, though better suited to their particular environment, are never perfect designs. The book's example: mammalian sperm do not develop well at body temperature, and the "solution" was the **scrotum**, which is hardly an elegant answer to any design problem.
6. **Not all existing behaviours or structures are adaptive.** Evolution often works through changes in developmental programmes that produce several related characteristics, only one of which may be adaptive. The incidental, non-adaptive by-products are called **spandrels**; the book's example is the human **belly button**, a functionless by-product of the umbilical cord. In addition, a behaviour or structure that was once adaptive can become non-adaptive, or even **maladaptive**, if the environment changes.
7. **Not all adaptive characteristics evolved to perform their current function.** Characteristics that evolved for one function and were later co-opted for another are **exaptations**. The book's example is **bird wings**: they are limbs that originally evolved for **walking**.
8. **Similarities among species do not necessarily mean common evolutionary origins.** Structures that are similar because they share a common evolutionary origin are **homologous**; structures that are similar without a common origin are **analogous**. Analogous similarities arise through **convergent evolution**, the evolution in unrelated species of similar solutions to the same environmental demands. Telling the two apart requires careful analysis: a **bird's wing and a human's arm** share an underlying skeletal plan that points to a common ancestor (homologous), whereas a **bird's wing and a bee's wing** have few structural similarities and evolved separately because flight was advantageous to both (analogous).
9. ***Homo sapiens* mated with other *Homo* species** it encountered, such as the **Neanderthals**. This changes how we should think about our origins: modern humans are not the descendants of a single ancestral *Homo* population but the combined offspring of **many *Homo* populations** that once coexisted and interacted.

> **Trap (spandrel vs exaptation).** These are the most confused pair in the chapter. A **spandrel** is **non-adaptive**: a by-product that came along with something else and does nothing useful (belly button). An **exaptation** is **adaptive now** but evolved for a **different** original function (bird wings, originally limbs for walking). Quick test: does it currently do something useful? If no, spandrel. If yes but it originally did something else, exaptation.

> **Trap (homologous vs analogous).** **Homologous = same origin** (common ancestor), even if the function now differs: bird wing and human arm. **Analogous = same function or appearance, different origin**, produced by **convergent evolution**: bird wing and bee wing. Mnemonic: *homo-* means "same", and homologous structures have the same ancestry. A question that calls the bird wing and bee wing homologous "because both are used for flight" is the standard trap.

> **Trap.** "Fewer than 1 percent" of known species survive, and the right metaphor is a **bush**, not a ladder. An option saying evolution is "progress toward more complex, more perfect forms" contradicts points 1 and 5.

### Evolution of the human brain

Early research on brain evolution concentrated on **size**, on the assumption that bigger brains mean greater intellect. That assumption quickly hit **two problems**:

1. **Humans do not have the biggest brains.** The human brain weighs about **1,350 grams**; the brains of **whales and elephants** weigh **5,000 to 8,000 grams**.
2. **The brains of acclaimed intellectuals**, for example **Albert Einstein**, turned out to be **unremarkable** in size, nothing like their extraordinary intellects.

It is now clear that healthy adult human brains vary greatly in size, from about **1,000 to 2,000 grams**, and that there is **no clear relationship between overall brain size and intelligence** in humans.

Part of the trouble is that **larger animals tend to have larger brains**, presumably because a larger body needs more brain tissue to control and regulate it. Body size therefore confounds every raw comparison. Elephants out-brain humans, men on average out-brain women, and big men out-brain small men, and none of these differences tells you anything about which group is more intelligent.

That led to a proposed correction: express **brain weight as a percentage of total body weight**. This measure lets humans (**2.33 percent**) move ahead of elephants (**0.20 percent**), but it also puts both behind the **shrew (3.33 percent)**, which nobody regards as the intellectual giant of the animal kingdom. The ratio fixes one problem and creates another.

A more productive approach compares the evolution of **different brain regions**. In particular, it has been useful to consider the **brain stem** separately from the **cerebrum** (the cerebral hemispheres):

- The **brain stem** regulates **reflex activities critical for survival**, such as heart rate, respiration and blood glucose level.
- The **cerebrum** is involved in more complex **adaptive processes**, such as learning, perception and motivation.

The book's figure compares the brain stems and cerebrums of a **bass, rat, cat, chimpanzee and human**, living descendants of species from which humans evolved. It makes **three points** about the evolution of the human brain:

1. The brain has **increased in size** during evolution.
2. **Most of the increase has occurred in the cerebrum.**
3. The cerebral surface has become more and more folded. These folds, **convolutions**, pack a much larger area of **cerebral cortex** (the outermost layer of cerebral tissue) into the skull.

Despite the differences, there are **fundamental similarities** across related species. All brains are made of many **neurons**, and the neural structures found in one species can usually be found in the **same locations** in related species. Human, monkey, rat and mouse brains, for instance, are built from the **same major structures with broadly the same wiring**, and a given structure usually does **much the same job** in each species. The human brain appears to have evolved from the brains of our closest primate relatives. This is the justification for much of the comparative research you met in Chapter 1 and will meet throughout the book.

> **Trap.** Three numbers to keep straight: human brain about **1,350 g** (normal adult range about **1,000 to 2,000 g**); whale and elephant brains **5,000 to 8,000 g**; brain-to-body percentage: shrew **3.33** > human **2.33** > elephant **0.20**. And the conclusion: neither absolute size nor the brain-to-body ratio predicts intelligence. Most evolutionary growth was in the **cerebrum**, not the brain stem, and convolutions increased cortical **surface area**.

### Older editions: evolutionary psychology and mate bonding

The 11th edition has **dropped** the section that earlier editions placed here, "Evolutionary Psychology: Understanding Mate Bonding". The book's own end-of-module "Scan Your Brain" quiz still carries two items from the older material: one asks what evolutionary psychologists think male-female **bonding** accomplishes (answer: **bonding**, which helps ensure that offspring survive and reproduce), and another asks when **birds** emerged (answer: about **160 million years ago**), a date the 11th-edition text and timeline figure no longer give. Because Indian papers often draw on older keys, the essentials of the dropped material are summarised here, from standard textbook knowledge rather than the 11th-edition text.

- **Evolutionary psychology** tries to understand behaviour by working out the evolutionary pressures that shaped it.
- **Mating patterns.** **Polygyny** is one male forming mating bonds with more than one female; it is the most common pattern in mammals. **Polyandry** is one female bonding with more than one male; it is rare and occurs in species in which males contribute more than females to raising the young. **Monogamy** is an enduring bond between one male and one female; it is uncommon in mammals.
- **Parental investment.** The standard explanation, from **Trivers (1972)**, is that the sex that invests more in each offspring (in mammals, the female, through gestation and nursing) can produce relatively few offspring and is therefore more selective about mates, while the sex that invests less can increase its fitness by mating with many partners. That asymmetry favours polygyny in mammals. Monogamy is expected where young survive much better with the sustained help of both parents.
- **Human mate preferences.** Older editions summarised **Buss**'s cross-cultural survey findings that men, on average, place more weight on youth and physical attractiveness in a partner and women more on status and resources, as evolutionary theory predicts.
- **The caution.** Evolutionary-psychology explanations are inferred after the fact and are hard to test experimentally; a behaviour pattern that fits an evolutionary story is not thereby shown to have evolved for that reason. This fits the 11th edition's own cautions above: not every trait is an adaptation (spandrels), and not every adaptation evolved for its current use (exaptations).

> **Trap (older keys).** If a question asks about polygyny, polyandry, monogamy, Trivers or Buss, it comes from an older edition; the definitions above are the expected answers. If a question asks for "the 11th-edition evolutionary topics on behaviour", the answer is **social dominance and courtship displays**, not mate bonding.

## 2.3 Fundamental Genetics

### Darwin's two gaps and Mendel's unread manuscript

Darwin's theory rested on two facts he could not explain. He did not know **why conspecifics differ** from one another, and he did not know **how characteristics are passed from parent to offspring**. Both are essential to natural selection: selection needs variation to act on, and it needs that variation to be heritable.

The irony, which Pinel and Barnes enjoy, is that the answers were sitting unread in Darwin's own files. An obscure **Augustinian monk, Gregor Mendel**, had sent him a manuscript describing his work on inheritance. Neither man lived to see its importance recognised: Darwin lived from **1809 to 1882** and Mendel from **1822 to 1884**, and the significance of Mendel's research was not appreciated until the **early twentieth century**, well after both had died.

### Mendel's experiment on seed colour

Mendel studied inheritance in **pea plants**, and his success came from two wise design decisions:

1. He studied **dichotomous traits**: traits that occur in one form or the other, **never in combination**. Seed colour in his peas was such a trait; every plant had either **brown** seeds or **white** seeds, never something in between.
2. He began his experiments by crossing the offspring of **true-breeding lines**: lines in which interbred members always produce offspring with the same trait (say, brown seeds), generation after generation.

Both decisions removed noise. Dichotomous traits gave him clean categories to count, and true-breeding lines meant he knew exactly what each starting parent could pass on.

**The first cross.** Mendel crossed plants from a line that bred true for **brown** seeds with plants from a line that bred true for **white** seeds. **Every** offspring of this first cross had **brown** seeds. White seemed to have vanished.

**The second cross.** He then bred these first-generation offspring with one another. In the second generation, about **three-quarters** of the plants had **brown** seeds and about **one-quarter** had **white** seeds. White had reappeared.

He repeated the experiment many times with different pairs of dichotomous traits, and the pattern was always the same. One trait, which he called the **dominant trait**, appeared in **all** of the first-generation offspring. The other, the **recessive trait**, disappeared in the first generation and reappeared in about **one-quarter** of the second. The book notes that Mendel would have got the same kind of result if he had used true-breeding lines of **brown-eyed (dominant)** and **blue-eyed (recessive)** humans.

Why was this so important? Every earlier idea about heredity had assumed that offspring inherit the **traits** of their parents. Mendel's result flatly contradicted that. The white-seed trait was passed to a quarter of the second generation by first-generation parents that **did not themselves have it**. So what parents pass on cannot simply be their visible traits. This forces a distinction between two things:

- an organism's **phenotype**: its **observable** traits;
- its **genotype**: the traits it can **pass on** to its offspring through its genetic material.

The first-generation plants had a **brown phenotype** but a genotype that carried **white** as well.

> **Trap (genotype vs phenotype).** **Phenotype = what you can observe** (brown seeds, blue eyes, a behaviour). **Genotype = what the genetic material carries and can transmit.** Two organisms can share a phenotype and differ in genotype (a brown-seeded plant may or may not carry the white factor), which is exactly what Mendel's first-generation plants show. A question that defines phenotype as "the genes that are expressed" or genotype as "the visible traits" is reversing them.

### Mendel's four-part theory

To explain his results, Mendel proposed a theory with **four central ideas**:

1. **There are two kinds of inherited factor for each dichotomous trait**, for example a brown-seed factor and a white-seed factor for seed colour. Today each inherited factor is called a **gene**.
2. **Each organism has two genes for each dichotomous trait.** So any pea plant carries one of three combinations: brown plus brown, white plus white, or brown plus white. The pair of genes governing a single trait are **alleles** (pronounced *a-LEELZ*). An organism with **two identical** alleles for a trait is **homozygous** for that trait; one with **two different** alleles is **heterozygous**.
3. **In heterozygous organisms, one kind of gene dominates the other.** A plant with one brown-seed gene and one white-seed gene always has brown seeds, because the brown-seed gene dominates the white-seed gene.
4. **Each organism randomly inherits one of its "father's" two factors and one of its "mother's" two factors** for each dichotomous trait.

Now run the experiment through the theory, step by step, which is what the book's figure does. Write **B** for the brown-seed gene and **w** for the white-seed gene.

**First cross.** The true-breeding brown parent is **BB** (homozygous); the true-breeding white parent is **ww** (homozygous). Each offspring receives one gene from each parent, so every offspring is **Bw** (heterozygous). Because B is dominant, all have brown seeds. **Result: 100 percent brown.**

**Second cross.** Now cross two **Bw** plants. Each parent passes on either B or w, at random, so there are four equally likely combinations:

| | B from parent 2 | w from parent 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **B from parent 1** | BB (brown) | Bw (brown) |
| **w from parent 1** | wB (brown) | ww (white) |

That gives **25 percent BB**, **50 percent Bw**, and **25 percent ww**. Because B is dominant, the BB and Bw plants (75 percent) have brown seeds and only the ww plants (25 percent) have white seeds. **Result: 3 brown to 1 white**, exactly Mendel's three-quarters and one-quarter.

Notice what the table reveals about genotype and phenotype. The brown phenotype in the second generation hides **two different genotypes**: one-third of brown plants are BB and two-thirds are Bw. Only breeding them (or genetic testing) can tell them apart.

> **Trap (dominant vs recessive).** **Dominant** does **not** mean more common, stronger, better, or more likely to spread through a population. It means only that the trait is **expressed in a heterozygote**. A **recessive** trait is expressed **only in homozygotes**, so it can be carried silently for generations (the white-seed gene in Mendel's first generation; the PKU gene in Module 2.4). Check also the generation: the dominant trait appears in **all** first-generation offspring; the recessive trait reappears in about **one-quarter** of the **second** generation.

> **Trap (homozygous vs heterozygous).** **Homozygous = two identical alleles** (BB or ww). **Heterozygous = two different alleles** (Bw). A homozygous organism can be homozygous **dominant** (BB) or homozygous **recessive** (ww); "homozygous" does not mean "recessive". Alleles are the **two genes controlling the same trait**, sitting at the **same location** on the two chromosomes of a pair; "allele" is not a synonym for "dominant gene".

### Chromosomes: reproduction and recombination

Mendel's "factors" needed a physical home. Not until the **early twentieth century** were genes found to be located on **chromosomes**, the thread-like structures in the **nucleus** of each cell. Chromosomes come in **matched pairs** in virtually all multicellular organisms, and each species has a characteristic number of pairs in each of its body cells; **humans have 23 pairs**. The two alleles that control a given trait sit at the **same location**, one on each chromosome of a particular pair.

There are two kinds of cell division, and confusing them is one of the commonest errors in this module.

**Meiosis** (*my-OH-sis*) is the cell division that produces **gametes** (egg cells and sperm cells). In meiosis the chromosomes divide and **one chromosome of each pair goes to each of the two gametes** produced. Each gamete therefore carries **only half** the usual number of chromosomes (**23** in humans, one from each pair). When a sperm and an egg combine at **fertilisation**, the result is a **zygote** (a fertilised egg cell) with the **full complement** of chromosomes (**23 pairs** in humans). The book's photograph shows sperm cells attached to the surface of an egg; at least one must enter the egg for fertilisation to occur.

Meiosis contributes to genetic diversity in **two** ways, and you should be able to name both:

1. **Random division of the chromosome pairs.** Which member of each pair ends up in a given gamete is a matter of chance, so each gamete receives a different mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
2. **Genetic recombination.** In the **first stage of meiosis**, the chromosomes line up in their pairs. The members of each pair then **cross over** one another at random points, **break apart** at the points of contact, and **exchange sections**. As a result, each chromosome in the gamete is a new, spliced-together combination of segments from the parent's two chromosomes. Each of the gametes that combined to form you therefore contained chromosomes that were unique recombinations of your grandparents' chromosomes, carried by your parents.

**Mitosis** (*my-TOE-sis*) is **all other cell division** in the body. Just before a mitotic division, the number of chromosomes **doubles**, so that when the cell divides **both daughter cells** receive the **full complement** of chromosomes (23 pairs in humans).

| | Meiosis | Mitosis |
|---|---|---|
| Produces | **Gametes** (egg and sperm) | All other body cells |
| Chromosomes in each product | **Half** (23 single chromosomes in humans) | **Full** complement (23 pairs) |
| Source of diversity | Random assortment of pairs **plus** recombination (crossing over) | None; daughter cells are copies |

> **Trap (meiosis vs mitosis).** **Meiosis makes gametes with half the chromosomes; mitosis makes body cells with the full set.** Mnemonic: mei**o**sis makes **o**va (and sperm). Genetic **recombination** (crossing over) happens in **meiosis**, in its **first stage**, not in mitosis. The **zygote** is the fertilised egg, the product of fertilisation, not of meiosis.

### DNA structure and replication

Chemically, a chromosome is one long molecule of **deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)** made of **two strands**. Along each strand runs a backbone of **phosphate and deoxyribose**, and hanging off that backbone is a sequence of **nucleotide bases**. There are **four** nucleotide bases: **adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C)**. The **genetic code** is the **sequence** of these bases along each chromosome, in the same way that a sequence of letters carries the meaning of a written sentence.

The two strands are **coiled around each other** and held together by the attraction of specific bases for one another: **adenine binds to thymine**, and **guanine binds to cytosine**. Because the pairing is so strict, the two strands of each chromosome are **exact complements**. If you know one strand, you know the other. The book's example: the sequence A-G-T-C-G on one strand must be matched by T-C-A-G-C on the other.

This complementarity is what makes **replication** possible. Replication is essential: without it, **mitotic cell division** could not happen, because each daughter cell needs its own full copy of every chromosome. The process is thought to work as follows:

1. The two strands of the DNA molecule begin to **unwind**.
2. As each strand is exposed, its bases attract **free-floating complementary bases** from the surrounding fluid of the nucleus (A attracts T, T attracts A, G attracts C, C attracts G).
3. The newly attracted bases link up along each old strand, building a new partner strand.
4. When unwinding is complete, there are **two double-stranded DNA molecules**, each **identical to the original**.

> **Trap.** The base pairs are **A-T and G-C**. Uracil does not appear in DNA (it replaces thymine in **RNA**, as you will see below). The backbone of DNA is **phosphate and deoxyribose**. A question giving "A-G and C-T" or "A-C and G-T" as the pairing rule is wrong.

### Errors in replication: mutations

Replication does not always go to plan. Some errors are large and clinically serious: in **Down syndrome** (covered in Chapter 10) there is an **extra chromosome** in each cell. Much more commonly, though, replication errors take the form of **mutations**: alterations of **individual genes**.

What happens to a mutation depends on its effect on fitness. **In most cases**, mutations **disappear from the gene pool within a few generations**, because the organisms that inherit them are **less fit** and leave fewer descendants. **Occasionally**, a mutation **increases** fitness, and in doing so it can drive **rapid evolution**. This connects back to point 3 of the nine misunderstood points in Module 2.2: rapid evolutionary change can be triggered by adaptive mutations.

> **Trap.** A **mutation** is an alteration of an **individual gene**. An extra **whole chromosome** (as in Down syndrome) is a different kind of replication error, not a mutation in the book's sense. And most mutations are **eliminated** by selection within a few generations; "mutations usually increase fitness" is false.

### Sex chromosomes and sex-linked traits

There is one exception to the rule that chromosomes come in matched pairs. The ordinary, matched chromosomes are called **autosomal chromosomes**. The exception is the pair of **sex chromosomes**, the pair that determines an individual's sex. There are two types, **X** and **Y**, and they look different and carry different genes. **Females have two X chromosomes; males have one X and one Y.**

Traits influenced by genes on the sex chromosomes are **sex-linked traits**. **Virtually all** sex-linked traits are controlled by genes on the **X chromosome**, because the **Y chromosome is small and carries few genes**.

X-linked traits occur more often in one sex than in the other, and the direction depends on whether the gene is dominant or recessive. Work through the logic rather than memorising the result:

- **Dominant X-linked trait → more common in females.** A dominant trait is expressed if you inherit even one copy. Females have **two** X chromosomes and therefore **twice the chance** of inheriting the dominant gene.
- **Recessive X-linked trait → more common in males.** A female shows a recessive X-linked trait only if she has the recessive gene on **both** of her X chromosomes. A male has only **one** X chromosome, with no second X to mask it, so **every male who carries the gene shows the trait**.

The classic example of a recessive sex-linked trait is **colour blindness**. Because the colour-blindness gene is quite **rare**, females almost never inherit two copies and so almost never have the disorder, whereas **every male with one colour-blindness gene is colour blind**.

> **Trap.** **Recessive** X-linked → more common in **males** (colour blindness). **Dominant** X-linked → more common in **females** (two chances to inherit it). Students who memorise "sex-linked = male disorder" will miss the dominant case. Also: sex-linked traits are almost all **X**-linked, because the **Y is small and carries few genes**; "sex-linked traits are carried on the Y chromosome" is the standard distractor.

### Structural genes, promoters and the puzzle of cell differentiation

**Structural genes** contain the information needed to synthesise **proteins**. Proteins are **long chains of amino acids**; they control the physiological activities of cells and are important components of cell structure.

Here is a puzzle worth pausing on. **Every cell in the body contains exactly the same genes**: a brain cell, a hair cell and a bone cell carry identical DNA. So how do cells become so different from one another? Part of the answer lies in the stretches of DNA that contain **no structural genes**. All genes were once assumed to be structural genes, but structural genes turn out to make up only a **small portion** of each chromosome.

The non-structural stretches are still not well understood, but some of them clearly have a function. Among these are **promoters**: stretches of DNA whose job is to determine **whether or not particular structural genes are converted into proteins**. The conversion itself is a two-phase process called **gene expression**. Control of gene expression by promoters matters enormously, because it strongly influences **how a cell develops** and **how it functions** once mature. Two cells with identical genes become different kinds of cell because different sets of their genes are switched on.

Promoters behave like **switches** that can be adjusted in two directions: **turned up or turned down**. The adjusting is done by proteins that bind to DNA:

- **Activators** are DNA-binding proteins that **increase** gene expression.
- **Repressors** are DNA-binding proteins that **decrease** gene expression.

> **Trap.** **Promoters** are stretches of **DNA** (not proteins) that determine whether a structural gene is expressed. **Activators** and **repressors** are **proteins** that bind to DNA and turn expression up or down. The answer key for the book's own quiz item "stretches of DNA that control the process of gene expression" is **promoters**.

### Transcription: from DNA to messenger RNA

Gene expression has two phases. The first is **transcription**.

1. The small section of the chromosome that contains the gene **unravels**, exposing the structural gene to be expressed.
2. The unravelled section of **one** of the two DNA strands acts as a **template** for building a short strand of **ribonucleic acid (RNA)**, base by complementary base.
3. This transcribed strand is called **messenger RNA (mRNA)**, because it carries the genetic code **out of the nucleus** into the rest of the cell.

RNA is like DNA with two differences that are classic MCQ material:

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Bases | A, **T**, G, C | A, **U (uracil)**, G, C |
| Backbone | Phosphate and **deoxyribose** | Phosphate and **ribose** |

So in RNA, **uracil replaces thymine**, and **ribose replaces deoxyribose**.

### Translation: from messenger RNA to protein

Once the mRNA leaves the nucleus, the second phase, **translation**, begins.

1. The mRNA strand attaches to any one of the many **ribosomes** in the cell's **cytoplasm** (the clear fluid inside the cell).
2. The ribosome moves along the mRNA, reading the code as it goes. The code is read in units of three: each group of **three consecutive bases** on the mRNA is a **codon**.
3. Each codon instructs the ribosome to add one of the **20 kinds of amino acid** to the protein it is building. The book's example: the codon **guanine-guanine-adenine (GGA)** specifies the amino acid **glycine**.
4. Amino acids are delivered to the ribosome by molecules of **transfer RNA (tRNA)**. As the ribosome reads each codon, it attracts a tRNA molecule carrying the matching amino acid, which is added to the growing chain.
5. The ribosome continues codon by codon until it reaches a codon that signals the **end** of the protein. The completed protein is then **released into the cytoplasm**.

In summary: **transcription** converts the **DNA base-sequence code** into an **RNA base-sequence code** (in the nucleus); **translation** converts the **RNA base-sequence code** into a **protein** (at a ribosome in the cytoplasm).

> **Trap (transcription vs translation).** **Transcription = DNA → mRNA, in the nucleus** (same "language" of bases, just copied). **Translation = mRNA → protein, at the ribosome in the cytoplasm** (bases converted into the different "language" of amino acids). A **codon** is **three** bases on **mRNA**, not on tRNA and not a single base. **mRNA** carries the code out of the nucleus; **tRNA** carries the amino acids to the ribosome. There are **20** amino acids, not 4 and not 64.

### The Human Genome Project

One of the most ambitious scientific projects ever undertaken began in **1990**. The **Human Genome Project** was a loosely organised collaboration of major research institutions and individual teams in several countries, and its aim was to map the sequence of all **3 billion nucleotide bases** that make up the human chromosomes.

The motivation was **medical**. The expectation was that, once the genome was described, it would be fairly simple to link genomic variations to particular diseases and then design treatment and prevention programmes tailored to each patient. More than two decades after the genome was first described, those medical breakthroughs have **still not materialised** (Hall, 2010). Even so, the project has transformed our understanding of ourselves and revolutionised genetics. Pinel and Barnes list **three major contributions**:

1. **New techniques.** The project produced many new methods for studying DNA. Things that were impossible before are now routine, and work that used to take months can now be done in a few hours. With these methods, genomes have been established for many species, including many **long-extinct** species, which has yielded important insights into evolution.
2. **Surprisingly few genes.** Humans, the most complex of all species, have relatively **few structural genes**: about **21,000**. **Mice** have about the **same** number, and **corn** has **many more**. **Protein-encoding genes make up only about 1 percent of human DNA.** Researchers have since produced a nearly complete map of the full set of proteins our genes encode, the **human proteome**.
3. **Disease variants, but less useful than hoped.** Many genomic variations related to particular diseases have been identified, but this has proved less useful than expected. **So many genes** have been linked to each disease that sorting out their interactions with one another and with experience is very difficult. Worse, even when many genes have been linked to a disease, all of them together often account for only a **small part of its heritability**. The book's example: **18 gene variants** have been linked to **adult-onset diabetes**, yet together they account for only **6 percent** of the heritability of the disease.

> **Trap.** Numbers to keep: project began **1990**; **3 billion** bases; about **21,000** structural genes in humans (about the same as **mice**; **corn** has more); protein-encoding genes about **1 percent** of human DNA; **18** variants explain only **6 percent** of the heritability of adult-onset diabetes. The book's quiz answer for the percentage of human DNA that is protein-encoding is **1**.

### The rise of epigenetics

For roughly half a century (dated in the book from the **1960s**), genetics research and thinking were dominated by the **structure and expression of protein-encoding genes**. Around the **turn of the twenty-first century**, the field changed direction. Interest shifted from genes and their expression toward **other possible mechanisms of inheritance**, and in particular toward the mechanisms by which **experience** exerts its effects on development. The result was an explosion of interest in an area that had been sitting in the background since **1942**: **epigenetics**.

**Epigenetics** is the study of **all mechanisms of inheritance other than those mediated by changes to the gene sequence of DNA**. Read that definition slowly. It does not say "the study of environmental effects"; it says the study of inheritance that does **not** depend on the base sequence.

Why did epigenetics rise so fast around 2000? The book identifies **four conditions** that set the stage:

1. The Human Genome Project had generated an arsenal of **new research techniques**.
2. **Protein-coding genes** turned out to make up only about **1 percent** of human DNA, and nobody knew what the other **99 percent** was doing; it was widely written off as **"junk DNA"**.
3. Most **RNA molecules** turned out to be **small**: only **1.2 percent** were of the large protein-encoding kind. This suggested that encoding proteins is only a **minor function** of RNA.
4. Although there was general agreement that development is the product of **gene-experience interactions** (the model in Module 2.1), the **mechanisms** by which those interactions happen were unknown.

With these conditions in place, research quickly began to produce important discoveries, and those discoveries challenged the narrow view, held for half a century, that the genetic code is the only mechanism of inheritance.

> **Trap.** The definition of **epigenetics** turns on inheritance **not** mediated by changes in the **DNA base sequence**. An option defining it as "the study of mutations" or "changes in the gene sequence caused by the environment" is wrong on exactly that point. The book dates the field's long background to **1942** without naming its originator; other sources credit the biologist **Conrad Waddington** with coining the term in that year. Note also the two percentages that are easily swapped: protein-coding genes about **1 percent** of DNA; large protein-encoding RNA molecules about **1.2 percent** of RNA.

### Five discoveries of epigenetic research

Despite its youth, epigenetic research has already produced an impressive list of findings. The book presents **five**.

**1. Non-gene DNA is not junk.** Epigenetic investigations of the DNA outside genes have found **many active areas**, and many of them appear to **control the expression of nearby genes**. The belief that non-gene DNA is junk is no longer tenable.

**2. Many mechanisms that modulate gene expression have been found.** Two of the most widely studied are described in the next section.

### The two workhorse mechanisms: DNA methylation and histone remodelling

- **DNA methylation** is the reaction in which a **methyl group attaches to a DNA molecule**, usually at **cytosine** sites in mammals.
- **Histone remodelling** is the reaction in which **histones** (the **proteins around which DNA is coiled**) **change their shape** and thereby alter the shape of the adjacent DNA. There are several different ways in which this can happen.

The crucial point, and a favourite trap: **both DNA methylation and histone remodelling can either decrease or increase gene expression.** Neither is a one-way "off switch". And neither alters the base sequence: the letters of the code stay the same while the accessibility or readability of the code changes.

> **Trap.** Many students learn "methylation silences genes". The 11th edition states explicitly that **both** mechanisms can **increase or decrease** expression. Histones are **proteins**, not stretches of DNA; the quiz answer to "the shape of DNA can be influenced by the change of shape of the adjacent ___" is **histones**. In mammals methylation usually occurs at **cytosine** sites.

### Epigenomes, epitranscriptomes and experience-induced change

**3. Cataloguing the epigenome.** So much interest has been generated that a worldwide effort is under way to catalogue the **epigenome** of every cell type. An **epigenome** is a catalogue of **all the modifications to DNA within a particular cell type other than changes to the nucleotide base sequence**. As of **2018**, the epigenomes of **over 600 cell types** had been characterised.

**4. Epigenetic changes to RNA.** Some epigenetic effects involve **post-transcription alterations to RNA** that leave the RNA base sequence unchanged. Such modifications occur in **every kind of RNA molecule examined so far**, with special attention paid to messenger RNA and transfer RNA. Their prevalence has prompted a new cataloguing effort: the **epitranscriptome** of a cell is the full set of modifications made to its RNA **after transcription** that do **not** involve changes to the RNA base sequence.

**5. Experience can induce epigenetic changes, and they can last a lifetime.** Epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone remodelling can be triggered by particular experiences, for example **neural activity**, **hormonal state** or **changes in the environment**, and the resulting changes can **last a lifetime**. This is the mechanism the interaction model in Module 2.1 was missing: it is how step 3 ("experience modifies the expression of an individual's genes") actually happens.

> **Trap.** **Epigenome** concerns modifications to **DNA** in a cell type; **epitranscriptome** concerns modifications to **RNA** after transcription. Neither involves a change in the base sequence. **Over 600** cell-type epigenomes had been characterised by **2018**.

### Transgenerational epigenetics

Discovery 5 raises an obvious question. If experience can produce lasting epigenetic changes in an individual, can those changes be passed to that individual's **children**? Could your parents' experiences be transmitted to you, and yours to your children?

Biologists first observed such **transgenerational epigenetic effects** in **plants**, and they have now been seen in **mammals** too. **Transgenerational epigenetics** is the subfield of epigenetics that studies the **transmission of experiences across generations via epigenetic mechanisms**. The book's mammalian example: when **mice** experience an **odour paired with a painful shock**, the **memory of that experience is passed on to subsequent generations** through epigenetic mechanisms (Dias & Ressler, 2014; Dias et al., 2015). In the original report (a detail not given in the textbook), the conditioned odour was acetophenone, and the offspring and grand-offspring of the conditioned males, which had never encountered the odour themselves, showed heightened sensitivity to that specific odour. There is also **growing evidence** that transgenerational epigenetic inheritance occurs in **humans** (Yeshurun & Hannan, 2018). The authors note that it would be an **evolutionary advantage** for a species to be able to pass on new adaptations to a changing environment quickly, without waiting for natural selection to act on chance mutations.

Pinel and Barnes ask the reader to pause on the implications. Two conclusions follow. First, your genetic material is probably **not fixed at conception**: it is modified, epigenetically, as your experiences pile up across your lifetime. Second, some of those modifications may be **inherited** by your descendants. They describe these findings as having **revolutionised genetics**, with major implications for how we think about our ancestors, ourselves and our descendants.

> **Trap.** The book's example of transgenerational epigenetics is **mice**, an **odour paired with shock**, the **fear memory** transmitted to later generations (Dias & Ressler, 2014). The effects were found **first in plants**, then in mammals; human evidence is described as **growing**, not established. Do not confuse transgenerational epigenetic inheritance with ordinary genetic inheritance: the DNA base sequence is not changed. (The idea is sometimes compared with Lamarck's inheritance of acquired characteristics, but the mechanism, chemical marks on the genome, is quite different from anything Lamarck proposed.)

> **High-yield.** Epigenetics = inheritance **not** via changes in the base sequence. Four conditions for its rise: HGP techniques; coding genes only about 1 percent of DNA ("junk DNA" puzzle); only 1.2 percent of RNA molecules are large protein-encoding ones; unknown mechanism of gene-experience interaction. Five discoveries: non-gene DNA active; many mechanisms (DNA methylation, histone remodelling, each able to raise or lower expression); epigenome catalogues (600+ cell types by 2018); epitranscriptome (post-transcription RNA changes); experience-induced, lifelong changes. Plus transgenerational transmission (mouse odour-shock).

### Older editions: mitochondrial DNA

Earlier editions included a short section on **mitochondrial DNA** that the 11th edition no longer has. The essentials, from standard textbook knowledge:

- **Mitochondria** are the energy-generating structures in a cell's cytoplasm, and they contain their **own small set of DNA**, separate from the chromosomes in the nucleus.
- Mitochondrial DNA is inherited essentially **only from the mother**, because the mitochondria of the zygote come from the egg.
- Because it is passed down the maternal line without recombination and accumulates mutations at a fairly steady rate, mitochondrial DNA has been used as an **evolutionary clock** to trace maternal lineages and estimate when human populations diverged, work that supported an **African origin** of modern humans.

> **Trap (older keys).** If a question asks which DNA is inherited **only from the mother**, the answer is **mitochondrial DNA**. This topic is absent from the 11th edition, so if it appears it is drawn from an older edition or a different textbook.

## 2.4 Epigenetics of Behavioural Development: Interaction of Genetic Factors and Experience

This module gives **two classic examples** of genes and experience interacting to direct the development of behaviour. Two terms frame it:

- **Ontogeny** is the **development of individuals** over their life span.
- **Phylogeny** is the **evolutionary development of species** through the ages.

Module 2.2 was about phylogeny; this module is about ontogeny. In both of its examples, development turns out to be a product of the **interaction** of genetic and experiential factors, an interaction that Pinel and Barnes say we now know is likely mediated by the **epigenetic mechanisms** described at the end of Module 2.3.

> **Trap.** **Ontogeny = the individual's development** (from conception through the life span). **Phylogeny = the species' evolutionary development.** The old slogan "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" uses both words, which is why they are so easily swapped in MCQs. Note also that the 11th edition gives only **two** examples here, selective breeding and PKU; older editions had a **third**, the development of birdsong (summarised in an older-edition box at the end of this module).

### Tryon's selective breeding of maze-bright and maze-dull rats

Recall from Module 2.1 that most early (North American) psychologists assumed behaviour develops largely through **learning**. **Tryon (1934)** undermined that assumption by showing that a behavioural trait can be **selectively bred**.

Tryon chose, almost provocatively, the very behaviour that the learning theorists of his day had made their own: **maze running in laboratory rats**. The procedure:

1. He trained a **large, heterogeneous** group of laboratory rats to run a **complex maze**, rewarding them with **food** on reaching the goal box.
2. He mated the females and males that **least frequently entered incorrect alleys** during training; he called these rats **maze-bright**.
3. He separately mated the females and males that **most frequently entered incorrect alleys**; he called these **maze-dull**.
4. When the offspring of each strain matured, their maze learning was tested, and again the **brightest of the bright** were mated with one another and the **dullest of the dull** with one another.

He continued this for **21 generations**. By the **eighth generation** there was **almost no overlap** between the two strains: with a few exceptions, the **worst** maze-bright rat made **fewer errors** than the **best** maze-dull rat. The book's figure shows the progression: a single broad distribution of error scores in the original population, two partly overlapping distributions by the fourth generation, and two almost completely separated distributions by the eighth.

### The cross-fostering control

An obvious objection is that good maze running might be passed from parent to offspring not genetically but through **learning**: bright mothers might somehow teach their pups, or rear them in a way that makes them better learners. Tryon controlled for this with a **cross-fostering** procedure. He tested **maze-bright pups reared by maze-dull parents** and **maze-dull pups reared by maze-bright parents**.

The result was clear. The offspring of maze-bright rats made **few errors even when reared by maze-dull rats**, and the offspring of maze-dull rats made **many errors even when reared by maze-bright rats**. The difference travelled with the **biological parents**, not with the rearing parents, which is what a genetic difference predicts.

> **Trap.** Tryon's study ran for **21 generations**; the near-complete separation was achieved by the **8th**. The control that ruled out transmission by learning was **cross-fostering** (pups reared by parents of the other strain). A question that says Tryon "reared all pups in identical cages" as his control, or that gives "8 generations" as the length of the programme, is wrong.

### Searle (1949): what was actually being bred?

Since Tryon, many behavioural traits have been selectively bred; it appears that **any measurable behavioural trait that varies among members of a species** can be selectively bred.

But selective breeding studies carry an important general lesson: **breeding for one behavioural trait usually brings a host of other behavioural traits along with it**. This shows that the trait used as the breeding criterion is **not the only trait influenced by the genes** that the breeding has separated.

**Searle (1949)** demonstrated this with Tryon's strains. He compared maze-bright and maze-dull rats on **30 different behavioural tests** and found that they **differed on many** of them. The pattern of differences suggested that the maze-bright rats were better maze learners **not because they were more intelligent but because they were less fearful**, a trait that is **not adaptive** in many natural environments.

This is a sobering result for anyone who reads "maze-bright" as "intelligent". Tryon selected on a performance measure (errors in a maze), and the genes he concentrated turned out to act, at least in part, through emotionality rather than through learning ability as such.

> **Trap.** **Searle (1949)**: **30** tests; maze-bright rats were **less fearful**, not more intelligent. So the labels "bright" and "dull" describe maze performance, not general intelligence. An option saying "Searle confirmed that maze-bright rats were more intelligent across all tasks" is the standard distractor.

### Cooper and Zubek (1958): enriched and impoverished environments

Selective breeding studies prove that genes influence the development of behaviour. They do **not** show that experience does not. **Cooper and Zubek (1958)** made this point in a classic study using maze-bright and maze-dull rats.

**Design.** Young maze-bright and maze-dull rats were reared in one of two environments:

1. an **impoverished environment**: a **barren wire-mesh group cage**;
2. an **enriched environment**: a wire-mesh group cage containing **tunnels, ramps, visual displays and other objects** designed to stimulate interest.

**Result.** When the rats reached maturity, the **maze-dull rats made significantly more errors than the maze-bright rats only if they had been reared in the impoverished environment**. Reared in the enriched environment, the two strains **did not differ significantly**.

The book's bar chart makes the pattern visible. In the enriched condition the bright and dull bars are close together. In the impoverished condition the dull rats' bar is far higher (many more errors), while the bright rats' errors are not much different from those of the enriched groups.

Why this matters: the genetic difference between the strains, built up over generations of selective breeding, **showed up in behaviour only under certain rearing conditions**. You cannot say how much of a dull rat's maze performance is "due to" its genes, because the size of the genetic effect itself depends on the environment. That is exactly what an **interaction** means, and it is the experimental counterpart of the musician-and-instrument argument in Module 2.1.

> **Trap.** The **genetic difference disappeared** in the **enriched** environment and was **present only** in the **impoverished** environment. Students often reverse this. Remember the direction by the logic: a stimulating environment allowed the "dull" genotype to develop normally, while a barren environment exposed its weakness.

### Phenylketonuria: a single-gene metabolic disorder

It is often **easier to understand the genetics of a behavioural disorder** than the genetics of normal behaviour. The reason: many genes contribute to the development of a typical behavioural trait, but sometimes **a single abnormal gene** is enough to disrupt it. A good example is the neurological disorder **phenylketonuria (PKU)**.

**Discovery (1934).** PKU was discovered in **1934** by **Asbjørn Følling**, a **Norwegian dentist**, who noticed a peculiar **odour in the urine** of his **two intellectually disabled children**. Guessing, correctly, that the odour was related to their disorder, he had their urine analysed, and both samples contained high levels of **phenylpyruvic acid**. Following up, Følling found other intellectually disabled children with abnormally high urinary phenylpyruvic acid and concluded that this subgroup all had the **same disorder**.

**Symptoms.** In addition to **intellectual disability**, the symptoms of PKU include **vomiting, seizures, hyperactivity, irritability and brain damage**.

**Genetics.** The pattern of transmission through affected families shows that PKU is caused by a **single gene mutation**. The key figures:

- About **1 in 100** people of **European descent** **carry** the PKU gene.
- Because the gene is **recessive**, PKU develops only in **homozygous** individuals, those who inherit a PKU gene from **both** mother and father.
- In the **United States**, about **1 in 16,000** infants is born with PKU.
- The incidence is **lower** in **African Americans and Asian Americans** than in Americans of European descent.

**Biochemistry.** The biochemistry turned out to be fairly straightforward. PKU homozygotes **lack phenylalanine hydroxylase**, an enzyme needed to convert the amino acid **phenylalanine** into **tyrosine**. As a result:

1. **Phenylalanine accumulates** in the body.
2. Levels of **dopamine**, a neurotransmitter normally synthesised from **tyrosine**, are **particularly low**.
3. The consequence is **abnormal brain development**.

> **Trap.** The missing enzyme is **phenylalanine hydroxylase**; the blocked conversion is **phenylalanine → tyrosine**; the neurotransmitter singled out as low is **dopamine** (made from tyrosine). The substance Følling found in the **urine** was **phenylpyruvic acid**. Følling was a **Norwegian dentist** (not a physician or a geneticist), and he found it in **two** of his own children in **1934**, the same year Tryon published his selective breeding study.

> **Trap (Mendelian reasoning).** PKU is **autosomal recessive**. Two unaffected parents of an affected child must both be **heterozygous carriers**. For each further child of those parents, the chance of PKU is **1 in 4** (homozygous recessive), the chance of being an unaffected carrier is **2 in 4**, and the chance of being an unaffected non-carrier is **1 in 4**. Among their **unaffected** children, **two-thirds** are carriers. This is just Mendel's second cross with PKU in place of white seeds.

### Treating PKU: diet, timing and the sensitive period

Like other behavioural traits, the behavioural symptoms of PKU result from an **interaction** between genetic and environmental factors, specifically between the **PKU gene** and **diet**. That interaction is what makes PKU treatable.

In most modern hospitals, the **blood of newborn infants is routinely screened** for high phenylalanine. If the level is high, the infant is immediately put on a special **phenylalanine-restricted diet**. The diet **reduces** both the amount of phenylalanine in the blood and the development of **intellectual disability**. It does **not**, however, prevent the development of **subtle cognitive deficits**.

**Timing is critical.** The restricted diet does **not** significantly reduce the development of intellectual disability in PKU homozygotes **unless it is started within the first few weeks of life**. Conversely, the dietary restriction is often **relaxed in adulthood**, with few obvious ill effects. In other words, phenylalanine is toxic to the developing brain during a limited early window, but much less so afterwards.

That pattern illustrates a general concept. The **sensitive period** for a trait is the period, usually early in life, during which a particular experience must occur to have a **major effect** on the development of that trait. For PKU, the relevant "experience" is the diet, and the sensitive period is the first weeks of life.

Now look at what PKU does to the nature-nurture dichotomy. PKU is about as "genetic" as a disorder can be: one recessive gene, inherited in strict Mendelian fashion. Yet whether a child with that genotype develops intellectual disability depends on what the child eats, and when. Saying that PKU intellectual disability is "caused by genes" or "caused by diet" is equally incomplete; it is caused by a particular genotype **meeting** a particular diet **during** a particular developmental window.

> **Trap (11th edition vs older keys).** Older editions and many older notes say the phenylalanine-restricted diet **prevents** (or "completely prevents") the intellectual disability of PKU. The 11th edition is more careful: the diet **reduces** intellectual disability but does **not prevent subtle cognitive deficits**, and it works only if started **within the first few weeks**. It can often be **relaxed in adulthood**. If an option says "lifelong strict diet is essential because any relaxation in adulthood causes intellectual disability", it contradicts the book.

> **Trap (sensitive vs critical period).** The 11th edition uses **sensitive period**: the time, usually early in life, when a particular experience must occur to have a **major** effect on a trait's development. Older texts often say "critical period", which implies an all-or-none window. PKU's diet window is the book's example.

### Older editions: the development of birdsong

Earlier editions of Pinel used a **third** classic example in this module, the development of **birdsong**, which the 11th edition has dropped. It remains common in older keys, so the essentials are summarised here from standard textbook knowledge:

- **Two phases of song learning.** In the **sensory phase**, a young male songbird hears the songs of adult members of its species and forms a memory, or **template**, of the correct song. In the later **sensorimotor phase**, the bird begins to sing, starting with immature, rambling **subsongs**, and gradually matches its own output to the stored template until the song becomes fixed (**crystallised**). Birds raised without hearing their species' song, or deafened before the sensorimotor phase, develop abnormal song. Both a genetic predisposition (the bird learns its own species' song far more readily than others) and experience during a sensitive period are required.
- **Age-limited vs open-ended learners.** **Zebra finches** are **age-limited** learners: they learn their song during a sensitive period early in life and keep it unchanged. **Canaries** are **open-ended** learners: they can add new song elements in later breeding seasons.
- **Neural basis.** Song production depends on a **descending motor pathway** of forebrain nuclei, notably the **high vocal centre (HVC)** and the **robust nucleus of the arcopallium (RA)**, which control the vocal organ (the **syrinx**); a separate anterior forebrain pathway is involved in song learning. In canaries, song control is lateralised to the **left** hemisphere, and the song-control nuclei **enlarge** during the breeding season, a classic early example of seasonal neural plasticity, including the addition of new neurons, in the adult brain.

> **Trap (older keys).** If a question asks about the sensory and sensorimotor phases, templates, subsong, zebra finches versus canaries, or the HVC, it is drawing on older-edition material. The underlying lesson is the same as the 11th edition's two examples: genetic programmes and experience interact, often within a sensitive period.

## 2.5 Genetics of Human Psychological Differences

The first four modules converge on one question: **why are we the way we are?** You have seen that each person is a product of gene-experience interactions and that, in the development of an individual, the effects of genes and experience cannot be separated. The final module keeps the focus on gene-experience interactions but turns to a **fundamentally different** developmental question: not how individuals develop, but how **differences among individuals** develop.

### Development of individuals versus development of differences among individuals

This distinction is the hinge of the whole chapter, and Pinel and Barnes admit that it confuses many people:

- In the **development of individuals**, the effects of genes and experience are **inseparable**.
- In the **development of differences among individuals**, they are **separable**, statistically.

The musician metaphor from Module 2.1 makes the difference concrete. One musician's music comes from the interaction of musician and instrument, and it makes no sense to ask what fraction of it each produced. Now change the question. Suppose you pick **100 people at random** and have each play a **different professional-quality guitar**. You can now ask a sensible statistical question: how much of the **variation** in the quality of the music across the 100 performances comes from **differences among the players**, and how much from **differences among the guitars**? Here you would almost certainly find that most of the variation comes from the players, since some have played for years and some have never touched a guitar, while the guitars are all good. In the same way, researchers can take a group of volunteers and ask what proportion of the **variation among them** in some attribute (intelligence, say) is associated with **genetic differences** as opposed to **experiential differences**.

The method is to study people of **known genetic similarity**. The standard comparison is between two kinds of twins:

- **Monozygotic twins** developed from the **same zygote** and are therefore genetically similar. (The book now avoids calling them "identical", for reasons you will see below.)
- **Dizygotic twins** developed from **two zygotes** and are therefore **no more similar genetically than any pair of siblings**.

Pairs of monozygotic and dizygotic twins who were **separated in infancy by adoption** are especially informative, because members of a separated pair share genes to a known degree but did not share a family environment. The most extensive study of this kind is the **Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart** (Bouchard & Pedersen, 1999).

> **Trap.** **Monozygotic = one zygote** (genetically similar). **Dizygotic = two zygotes** (no more alike genetically than ordinary siblings, merely born at the same time). And keep the two developmental questions apart: the **individual's** development is inseparably genetic and experiential; the **differences among individuals** in a sample can be statistically apportioned. Every heritability question on the paper turns on this distinction.

### The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart

The design, with the numbers examiners like:

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Twins reared apart | **59 pairs of monozygotic** twins and **47 pairs of dizygotic** twins |
| Comparison groups | Many pairs of monozygotic and dizygotic twins **reared together** |
| Ages | **19 to 68** |
| Testing | Each twin brought to the University of Minnesota for about **50 hours** of testing |
| Focus | **Intelligence** and **personality** |

The question was simple: would adult monozygotic twins reared apart turn out similar because they were genetically similar, or different because they had grown up in different environments?

The results were remarkably **consistent**, both **internally** (across the various cognitive and personality dimensions studied) and **externally** (with the findings of other studies). In general, adult **monozygotic twins were substantially more similar to each other on all psychological dimensions than adult dizygotic twins**, **whether or not** the members of a pair had been raised in the same family.

The logic is worth spelling out. If similarity between twins came mainly from sharing a home, then twins reared apart should be much less alike than twins reared together, and the monozygotic advantage should shrink when the home is not shared. Instead, monozygotic pairs were more alike than dizygotic pairs even when reared apart. That points to genetic differences as a major source of the psychological differences among the people in this study.

### What a heritability estimate is

To put a number on the contribution of genetic variation in a study such as the Minnesota study, researchers calculate **heritability estimates**. Here is the definition, which you should be able to reproduce exactly in meaning:

> A **heritability estimate** is a numerical estimate of the **proportion of the variability** in a particular trait, **in a particular study**, that resulted from the **genetic variation in that study**.

Three phrases in that definition do all the work:

1. **"Proportion of the variability"**: heritability is about **differences** among people, not about the trait itself in any one person.
2. **"In a particular study"**: it is a property of a **sample** (a population, under its conditions), not of the trait in general.
3. **"Genetic variation in that study"**: it depends on how much genetic variation the sample happened to contain.

So heritability estimates tell us about the contribution of **genetic differences** to **phenotypic differences** among the participants of a study. They say **nothing** about the relative contributions of genes and experience to the **development of individuals**.

Pinel and Barnes suggest pausing on the definition, and returning to the musicians helps. You cannot ask how much of one performance came from the instrument and how much from the player. But with many players, each with an instrument, you **can** ask how much of the **differences** among their performances is associated with differences in their instruments and how much with differences in their skill. Likewise, with intelligence measured in many people, you can ask how much of the **differences** in intelligence is associated with differences in their genes and how much with differences in their environments.

A worked illustration. Suppose a study reports a heritability estimate of **70 percent** for IQ. The correct reading: in that sample, under its conditions, about 70 percent of the **variance** in IQ scores was associated with genetic differences among the participants. The **incorrect** readings, all common: that 70 percent of each participant's intelligence comes from their genes; that a given child's IQ is 70 percent fixed; that environmental interventions can change IQ by at most 30 percent. None of these follows.

> **Trap (the single most important trap in the chapter).** **Heritability estimates apply to populations (samples), never to individuals.** A heritability of 0.7 for intelligence does **not** mean that 70 percent of a person's intelligence is genetic. It describes the sources of **variation among people in a particular study**. Any option that applies a heritability figure to one person ("70 percent of her IQ is inherited") is wrong, however reasonable it sounds.

### Why heritability estimates are so often misread

The key point follows directly from the definition: **the size of a heritability estimate depends on how much genetic and how much environmental variation existed in the group it was calculated from**, so it **cannot necessarily be generalised** to other groups or other situations.

The Minnesota study is the book's example. Its participants had **relatively little environmental variation**: all were raised in **industrialised countries** (**Great Britain, Canada and the United States**) by parents who had met the **strict standards required for adoption**. With environments that similar, most of the variation in the twins' intelligence and personality was bound to come from genetic variation. If the twins had instead been adopted into radically different homes (the book's contrast is **European royalty** versus a person living in **extreme poverty**), the heritability estimates for IQ and personality would very likely have been **much lower**.

Push the logic to its limits and the point becomes obvious. If everyone in a sample grew up in exactly the same environment, every remaining difference among them would have to be genetic, and heritability would approach 100 percent. If everyone in a sample were genetically identical, every difference would have to be environmental, and heritability would be zero. The trait has not changed in either case; only the variation in the sample has. Heritability is a description of a sample, not a fixed property of a trait.

**How big are heritability estimates?** For complex human traits and behaviours, the answer is surprisingly uniform. Intelligence, personality traits, aggression, divorce, religious beliefs, sports participation, psychiatric disorders and even television watching all show **substantial** heritability estimates, **most between 40 and 80 percent**, in the representative Western samples studied. The figures come out about the same **regardless of the trait** and **regardless of the method** used to calculate them (**twin, adoption or family-tree studies**).

Because genetic variability turns out to contribute substantially to individual differences in virtually **all** human traits, several eminent geneticists have argued that **no more heritability studies should be done** (e.g., **Johnson et al., 2009**; **Petronis, 2010**). Another estimate that "trait X is 50 percent heritable" adds little. What they are excited about instead are two newer kinds of twin study, with which the chapter ends.

> **Trap.** Three linked misreadings, each a common MCQ distractor, and all false. (1) "High heritability means the trait cannot be changed by the environment": heritability describes variation in a sample under existing conditions and says nothing about what a **new** environment could do; PKU (Module 2.4) is a genetic disorder whose outcome depends on diet. (2) "A heritability estimate from one group applies to all groups": it depends on the genetic and environmental variation in the group studied (Minnesota had little environmental variation; in Turkheimer's study, below, heritability changes with socioeconomic status). (3) "Heritability tells us how much of a trait is genetic": a trait can depend entirely on genes for its development and still show low heritability if nearly all the **differences** in a sample are environmental.

> **Trap (beyond the 11th edition).** The **Flynn effect**, the substantial rise in average IQ test scores across successive generations in many countries during the twentieth century, is not mentioned in the 11th edition but is a common exam topic from other sources. It makes the same point as the misreadings above: the rise is far too fast to be genetic, yet it occurred in populations where IQ has high heritability. High within-group heritability does not mean that group averages are immune to environmental change.

### Twin studies of epigenetic effects

The first newer approach uses twins to study **epigenetics** in humans. In plants and non-human animals it is now clear that epigenetic changes can be **triggered by experience**, can **last a lifetime**, and can be **passed on to future generations**. Whether this applies to humans is harder to establish, because **experimental manipulation of human genetic material is unethical**.

Monozygotic twins offer a way round the problem. At conception they are genetically identical, so by assessing their DNA **repeatedly** over time, researchers can document the **emergence and persistence of epigenetic differences** between them. And by comparing **monozygotic with dizygotic** pairs, they can judge how far those changes reflect experience rather than genes. The reasoning: if epigenetic changes were under **genetic** control, the pattern of changes should be **more similar in monozygotic than in dizygotic pairs**.

**Fraga and colleagues (2005)** published the **first systematic demonstration** of epigenetic differences in human twins.

- **Sample:** **40 pairs of monozygotic twins**, aged **3 to 74**.
- **Method:** tissue samples (**blood, skin, muscle**) screened for epigenetic alterations such as **DNA methylation** and **histone remodelling**.
- **Finding:** the twins were **epigenetically indistinguishable early in life**, but **differences between them accumulated with age**, and each type of tissue showed a **different epigenetic profile**.
- **Conclusion:** the old assumption that monozygotic twins are genetically identical was **disproven**, and Pinel and Barnes argue that the habit of calling monozygotic twins **"identical twins" should be curtailed**.

**Wong and colleagues (2010)** examined **DNA methylation** in **buccal cells** (cells scraped from the lining of the mouth).

- **Sample:** **46 pairs of monozygotic** and **45 pairs of dizygotic** twins.
- **Method:** samples taken from the same twins at **age 5** and again at **age 10**, and DNA methylation assessed.
- **Finding:** DNA methylation was **prominent in both groups at both ages**, and the **concordance rates** of methylation were the **same** for monozygotic pairs as for dizygotic pairs.
- **Conclusion:** because sharing more genes did not make twins more alike in methylation, **differences in DNA methylation are mainly a consequence of experiential factors**.

The discovery of epigenetic differences between monozygotic twins raises an exciting clinical possibility: **epigenetic differences may explain why one twin develops a disease and the other does not** (Bell & Spector, 2011; Haque, Gottesman & Wong, 2009). **Bell and Spector (2011)** argue that **disease-discordant monozygotic twin studies** are a particularly powerful approach. The method:

1. Identify monozygotic twin pairs who are **discordant** for a disease of interest (one twin has it, the other does not).
2. Search each pair for **epigenetic differences**, concentrating on the regions of DNA thought to be involved in the disorder.

Because the twins started with the same genome, any consistent epigenetic difference between the affected and unaffected twin is a strong clue to the causes and mechanisms of the disease. Large-scale studies of monozygotic twins across different ages, tissues and epigenetic effects could greatly improve our understanding of human disease.

> **Trap.** **Fraga et al. (2005)**: **40 MZ pairs**, ages **3 to 74**, blood/skin/muscle; indistinguishable when **young**, differences **accumulate with age**; tissue-specific profiles; "identical twins" label should be dropped. **Wong et al. (2010)**: **buccal** cells, **46 MZ and 45 DZ** pairs, ages **5 and 10**; **equal** methylation concordance in MZ and DZ pairs, so methylation differences are mainly **experiential**. Do not swap the two studies' samples or conclusions. And note the reasoning: **equal** MZ and DZ concordance implies **environmental** causation; **higher** MZ concordance would have implied genetic control.

> **Trap (11th edition vs older keys).** Older notes and keys define monozygotic twins as "identical twins who share 100 percent of their genes". The 11th edition says MZ twins are genetically identical **at conception** but become **epigenetically different** as they age, and it recommends **not** calling them identical.

### Twin studies of the effects of experience on heritability

The second newer approach drives home the point that heritability estimates depend on the conditions and participants of a particular study. The influential study is **Turkheimer and colleagues (2003)**.

**Background.** Before this study, **all** published studies of the heritability of intelligence had been conducted on **middle- to upper-class families**, and heritability estimates for intelligence were typically about **75 percent**.

**Design.** Turkheimer and colleagues assessed the heritability of intelligence in two samples of **7-year-old twins**: twins from families of **low socioeconomic status (SES)** and twins from families of **middle to high SES**.

**Results.**

| Sample | Heritability estimate for intelligence |
|---|---|
| Middle- to high-SES twins | about **70 percent** (as expected) |
| Low-SES twins | only **10 percent** |

The effect has since been **replicated** and extended to other age groups: **babies** (**Tucker-Drob et al., 2011**) and **adolescents** (**Harden, Turkheimer & Loehlin, 2007**).

**Why would heritability collapse in poverty?** Apply the logic from the previous section. In comfortable homes, most children receive an environment adequate for their intellectual development, so environmental differences among them are modest and genetic differences account for most of the variation in their intelligence. In poor homes, environments vary enormously in how much they restrict development (nutrition, stimulation, schooling, stress), and those environmental differences swamp the genetic ones. Genetic potential cannot show itself when the environment holds everyone back to different degrees.

**Implications.** Pinel and Barnes draw two.

1. **Theoretical.** The study forces us to think of intelligence as developing from the **interaction** of genes and experience, not from one or the other. It seems that a person can inherit the **potential** for superior intelligence, but that potential is **rarely realised in a poverty-stricken environment** (Nisbett et al., 2012).
2. **Practical and political.** Many politicians have argued against special programmes for the poor on the grounds that heritability estimates for intelligence are high: if intelligence is "largely inherited", they say, such programmes waste money. Turkheimer's findings show the argument is **mistaken**. Reducing poverty would allow all children to reach their intellectual potential.

> **Trap.** **Turkheimer et al. (2003)**: **7-year-old** twins; heritability of intelligence about **70 percent** in **middle- to high-SES** families but only **10 percent** in **low-SES** families; previous studies (all middle- to upper-class) gave about **75 percent**. Replicated in **babies** (Tucker-Drob et al., 2011) and **adolescents** (Harden, Turkheimer & Loehlin, 2007). The direction is the trap: heritability is **lower**, not higher, in poverty. A common wrong answer says "genes matter more for poor children".

> **High-yield.** Heritability = proportion of **variance** in a trait **in a particular study** associated with **genetic variation in that study**; about populations, not individuals; depends on the genetic and environmental variation present; most complex traits 40 to 80 percent in Western samples; Minnesota (59 MZ + 47 DZ reared apart, ages 19 to 68, about 50 hours) had low environmental variation; Turkheimer (2003) shows heritability of IQ falls to 10 percent in low-SES families.

The chapter's closing summary identifies the themes that ran through it.

- **Thinking about epigenetics** was the dominant theme. The chapter introduced the field and argued that it has major implications for understanding behaviour, from experience-induced gene expression to transgenerational effects and epigenetic differences between monozygotic twins.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all five modules. If you can trace each one through the chapter, you understand it rather than just remembering it.

1. **Interaction, not addition.** The musician and the instrument (2.1), maze-dull rats that are dull only in barren cages (Cooper & Zubek, 1958), a single gene whose effect depends on diet and timing (PKU), and heritability that falls from 70 to 10 percent in poverty (Turkheimer et al., 2003) are the same idea four times: when genes and experience interact, "how much of this is genetic?" has no answer for an individual.
2. **The mind is what the brain does.** Cartesian dualism put the mind outside nature. Asomatognosia shows self-awareness breaking with a right frontal-parietal lesion; Gallup's chimpanzees show self-awareness in a species Descartes considered mindless; step 5 of the model describes thoughts and feelings as the experienced side of neural activity. The physiological-or-psychological question dissolves.
3. **Individuals versus populations.** The development of one person is inseparably genetic and experiential; the differences among people in a sample can be statistically apportioned. Heritability belongs to the second category only, which is why it says nothing about any individual and why it changes when the sample's environments change (Minnesota versus Turkheimer).
4. **Genes are read, not just carried.** Promoters, activators and repressors control which genes are expressed; DNA methylation and histone remodelling can raise or lower expression; experience can trigger those changes and they can last a lifetime, diverge between monozygotic twins, and sometimes pass to the next generation. Step 3 of the model ("experience modifies gene expression") is no longer a slogan but a mechanism.
5. **Evolution is a tinkerer, and behaviour is one of its tools.** Natural selection acts on survival *and* reproduction, so social dominance and courtship displays are evolutionary forces; the outcome is a bush, not a ladder, full of imperfect designs, spandrels, exaptations and look-alike (analogous) structures.
6. **Genotype is not phenotype.** Mendel's white seeds reappear from brown parents; PKU is carried silently by unaffected heterozygotes; Tryon's "maze-bright" rats turned out to be less fearful rather than more intelligent. In every case what you can observe is a poor guide to what is being transmitted or selected.

## Worked example

*A healthy couple, both of European descent, are told that their 10-day-old son's newborn screening shows a very high blood phenylalanine level, and PKU is confirmed. The father says that since the problem is "in his genes", there is nothing anyone can do. The mother has read that intelligence is "75 percent genetic" and asks whether their son's intelligence is therefore already largely settled. They are planning another child and want to know the risk. The grandmother asks whether the boy's condition is "physiological or psychological".*

Work through it with the chapter's concepts.

**What are the parents' genotypes?** PKU is caused by a single **recessive** gene and appears only in **homozygotes**. Neither parent has PKU, so each must be a **heterozygous carrier**: same normal **phenotype** as a non-carrier, different **genotype**. This is Mendel's first-generation brown-seeded plant carrying a hidden white-seed factor.

**What is the risk for the next child?** It is Mendel's second cross. Each parent passes on the normal or the PKU allele at random, so each future child has a **1 in 4** chance of PKU, a **2 in 4** chance of being an unaffected carrier, and a **1 in 4** chance of carrying neither. If the next child is unaffected, the chance that he or she is a carrier is **2 in 3**. (If they had dizygotic twins, each twin's risk would be independent; monozygotic twins would share the same genotype.)

**Is the father right that nothing can be done?** No, and this is the chapter's central lesson in miniature. The child lacks **phenylalanine hydroxylase**, so phenylalanine cannot be converted to **tyrosine**, phenylalanine accumulates, and **dopamine** (made from tyrosine) is low, disrupting brain development. But the behavioural outcome depends on the interaction of that gene with **diet**. A **phenylalanine-restricted diet** started immediately greatly reduces the risk of intellectual disability. Timing matters: the diet must begin **within the first few weeks of life**, the **sensitive period**, which is why screening happens at birth and why a 10-day-old diagnosis is good news. Be honest about the limits: the 11th edition says the diet does **not** prevent **subtle cognitive deficits**. The restriction is often **relaxed in adulthood** with few obvious ill effects.

**Is the mother right that his intelligence is "75 percent genetic"?** The figure is a **heritability estimate**, and it has been misread twice. First, heritability describes the **variation among people in a particular study**, not the make-up of any one person's intelligence; it cannot tell her anything about her son. Second, the figure of about 75 percent comes from studies of **middle- to upper-class** families; **Turkheimer et al. (2003)** showed the estimate falls to about **10 percent** in low-SES families, because heritability depends on how much environmental variation a sample contains. Most strikingly, her son's own case refutes the inference she is drawing: his condition has a **purely genetic cause**, yet whether he develops intellectual disability depends overwhelmingly on an **environmental** factor, his diet. Genetic causation and environmental modifiability are not opposites.

**Is it physiological or psychological?** The question assumes the **Cartesian** split that Module 2.1 dismantles. His future intellectual capacities will be patterns of neural activity, shaped by genes whose effects depend on experience (here, food), and experienced by him as thoughts, memories and abilities. In the terms of the interaction model it is both at once, and asking which one it "really" is has no answer.

**What would you not say?** Do not tell the parents that intelligence is fixed by genes, that the diet guarantees completely normal cognition, or that a heritability figure from a published study predicts their son's IQ.

## MCQ traps

1. **Cartesian dualism** (Descartes, 1596 to 1650): **brain physical**, **mind non-physical**; non-human animals **entirely physical**; endorsed by the **Roman Church**.
2. **La Mettrie (1747)** argued thought is produced by the brain and was exiled to **Berlin**.
3. **Watson (1930)**, behaviourism, "a dozen healthy infants": **nurture**. **European ethology** (animal behaviour in the wild, instinctive behaviours): **nature**. Both wrong.
4. **Asomatognosia** = unawareness of own body parts, usually **left** side, after **right frontal and parietal** damage; Sacks (1985), "the man who fell out of bed".
5. **Gallup (1983)** mark test: anaesthetised chimps, **red odourless dye on the eyebrow**, mirror time up **more than threefold**; also passed by **Asian elephants, orangutans, European magpies**; humans at **15 to 24 months**.
6. The "**how much** is genetic?" version of nature-nurture assumes **additive** combination; Pinel calls it fundamentally flawed.
7. **Musician metaphor**: an **individual's** music cannot be apportioned (2.1); **differences** among many musicians can be (2.5).
8. Model of behaviour: **genetic endowment (from evolution) × experience × perception of the current situation**.
9. **Darwin (1859), *On the Origin of Species***: not the first to propose evolution; first to amass evidence and propose a mechanism (**natural selection**).
10. Darwin's evidence = **three** (fossils, structural similarities, selective breeding); the **most convincing** = direct observation (**Grant, 1991**, Galápagos finches after one drought season).
11. **Fitness** = ability to **survive and contribute genes** to the next generation, not physical strength.
12. **McCann (1981)**: **10** bull elephant seals; dominant **about 37 percent** of copulations, number 10 **about 1 percent**.
13. **Pusey, Williams & Goodall (1997)**: high-ranking **female** chimps had more offspring, more surviving to maturity.
14. **Species** = can produce **fertile** offspring only with members of the same species; **conspecifics** = members of the **same** species; courtship differences can form a **behavioural** reproductive barrier.
15. Timeline: multicellular **800**; chordates **550**; vertebrates **525**; fish onto land **410**; amphibians **370**; reptiles **315**; mammals **225**; hominins **6** million years ago.
16. **Chordates** have a **dorsal nerve cord**; about **40** phyla; **seven** vertebrate classes (three fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals); first vertebrates = primitive **bony fishes**.
17. **Reptiles** first had **shell-covered eggs** and **dry scales**; amphibian **larvae** must live in water; the **platypus** is an egg-laying mammal.
18. About **26** orders of mammals; about **16** groups of primates; **apes** evolved from **Old World monkeys**; chimps share **almost 99 percent** of genes with humans but are **not our ancestors**.
19. ***Australopithecus*** = "**southern ape**"; about **4** million years ago in Africa; **1.3 m**; **small brain but upright**; footprints **3.6** million years old, **70 m** trail, two adults and a child.
20. First ***Homo***: **2 to 2.8** million years ago; brain cavity **between** *Australopithecus* and modern humans; **fire and tools**; overlapped Australopithecines **half a million years**; outside Africa for about **1.85 million** years.
21. ***Homo sapiens***: about **275,000** years ago in the 11th edition (older keys: about **200,000**); *Homo* had at least **8** species, **7** extinct (Neanderthals among them).
22. Three fossil finds: **3-year-old girl**, Ethiopia, **3.3** million years; tiny hominins on **Flores** as recently as **18,000** years ago; mixed-feature Australopithecines in a **South African** pit.
23. Recent accomplishments: art **40,000**, farming **10,000**, writing **7,500** years ago.
24. **Spandrel** = non-adaptive by-product (**belly button**). **Exaptation** = evolved for one function, co-opted for another (**bird wings**, originally walking limbs).
25. **Homologous** = common origin (bird wing and human arm). **Analogous** = similar without common origin (bird wing and bee wing), via **convergent evolution**.
26. Fewer than **1 percent** of known species survive; evolution is a **bush**, a **tinkerer not an architect** (the **scrotum** example).
27. Brain weights: human about **1,350 g** (range **1,000 to 2,000 g**); whales and elephants **5,000 to 8,000 g**; brain-to-body percentage: **shrew 3.33** > **human 2.33** > **elephant 0.20**.
28. Brain evolution: most growth in the **cerebrum**; **convolutions** increase cortical **surface area**; brain stem = survival reflexes.
29. **Mendel**: dominant trait in **all** first-generation offspring; recessive reappears in about **one-quarter** of the **second** generation (**3:1**).
30. **Phenotype** = observable; **genotype** = transmissible. **Homozygous** = identical alleles; **heterozygous** = different alleles. **Alleles** = the two genes for one trait at the same location.
31. **Meiosis** makes **gametes** with half the chromosomes (**23**); **mitosis** makes body cells with **23 pairs**; **recombination** (crossing over) occurs in the **first stage of meiosis**.
32. DNA pairs **A-T** and **G-C**; RNA has **uracil** instead of thymine and **ribose** instead of deoxyribose.
33. **Recessive** X-linked traits are commoner in **males** (**colour blindness**); **dominant** X-linked traits commoner in **females**; the **Y** carries **few genes**.
34. **Promoters** = DNA stretches controlling expression; **activators** (increase) and **repressors** (decrease) are DNA-binding **proteins**.
35. **Transcription** = DNA → mRNA (nucleus); **translation** = mRNA → protein (ribosome, cytoplasm); **codon** = **3** bases; **20** amino acids; **GGA = glycine**; **tRNA** carries amino acids.
36. **Human Genome Project**: began **1990**; **3 billion** bases; about **21,000** genes (mice similar, corn more); coding DNA about **1 percent**; **18** diabetes variants explain only **6 percent** of heritability.
37. **Epigenetics** = inheritance **not** mediated by changes in DNA base sequence; background since **1942**; only **1.2 percent** of RNA molecules are large protein-encoding ones.
38. **DNA methylation** (methyl group, usually at **cytosine**) and **histone remodelling** can each **increase or decrease** expression; **over 600** cell-type epigenomes by **2018**; **epitranscriptome** = post-transcription RNA modifications.
39. **Transgenerational epigenetics**: first in **plants**; in **mice**, fear of an **odour paired with shock** passes to later generations (**Dias & Ressler, 2014**).
40. **Tryon (1934)**: **21** generations, near-complete separation by the **8th**, **cross-fostering** control; **Searle (1949)**: **30** tests, maze-bright rats **less fearful**, not smarter.
41. **Cooper & Zubek (1958)**: maze-dull rats worse **only** in the **impoverished** environment; no significant difference when **enriched**.
42. **PKU**: **Følling, 1934**, Norwegian **dentist**, **two** children, **phenylpyruvic acid** in urine; **1 in 100** European-descent carriers; **1 in 16,000** US births; **recessive**.
43. PKU biochemistry: no **phenylalanine hydroxylase** → phenylalanine not converted to **tyrosine** → low **dopamine**; diet must start in the **first few weeks** (**sensitive period**); **subtle deficits** remain; often **relaxed in adulthood**.
44. **Heritability** applies to **populations** (samples), never to individuals, and depends on the genetic and environmental variation in the sample.
45. **Minnesota Study**: **59 MZ** and **47 DZ** pairs reared apart, ages **19 to 68**, about **50 hours** of testing; low environmental variation (**Great Britain, Canada, US**; screened adoptive parents).
46. Most complex traits: heritability **40 to 80 percent**, regardless of trait or method; **Johnson et al. (2009)** and **Petronis (2010)** argue for no more heritability studies.
47. **Fraga et al. (2005)**: **40 MZ** pairs aged **3 to 74**, epigenetic differences grow **with age**; stop saying "identical". **Wong et al. (2010)**: **buccal** cells, **46 MZ + 45 DZ** pairs at **5 and 10**, equal methylation concordance → mainly **experiential**.
48. **Turkheimer et al. (2003)**: **7-year-old** twins; heritability of IQ **70 percent** (middle to high SES) vs **10 percent** (low SES); earlier studies about **75 percent**.
49. The book's quiz still lists **birds at 160 million years ago** and **male-female bonding**, items the 11th-edition text no longer teaches (older-edition material).

## Likely exam points

1. **Cartesian dualism**: Descartes's mind (non-physical) versus matter (physical, including brain and all animals); Church-sanctioned; still survives as the idea that some mental activity transcends the brain.
2. **Nature-nurture history**: North American behaviourism (Watson, nurture) versus European ethology (instinctive behaviours, nature); both wrong.
3. **Two lines of evidence against physiological-or-psychological thinking**: brain damage or stimulation alters complex psychological processes; non-human species show supposedly human abilities.
4. **Asomatognosia** (Sacks, 1985): left-sided body unawareness after right frontal and parietal lesions.
5. **Gallup's (1983) mark test** and the species that pass it; human infants at 15 to 24 months.
6. **Three versions of nature-or-nurture thinking**, ending with the additive "how much" fallacy, refuted by the musician-and-instrument metaphor.
7. **The interaction model**: genes (from evolution), experience, perception of the current situation; the seven-step loop.
8. **Darwin (1859)**: three kinds of evidence plus direct observation (Grant, 1991); natural selection; fitness.
9. **Social dominance**: McCann (1981) elephant seals (37 vs 1 percent); Pusey, Williams and Goodall (1997) female chimpanzees.
10. **Courtship displays and speciation**: species, conspecifics, geographic versus behavioural reproductive barriers.
11. **Evolutionary timeline**: 800, 550, 525, 410, 370, 315, 225 million years; hominins 6 million; key adaptations (legs and lungs; shelled eggs and dry scales; mammary glands and internal gestation).
12. **Primates and apes**: about 26 mammalian orders, 16 primate groups, five shown; apes from Old World monkeys; chimps about 99 percent genetically identical but not ancestral.
13. **Hominin sequence**: *Australopithecus* (4 million years, Africa, 1.3 m, small brain, upright, 3.6-million-year footprints) → *Homo* (2 to 2.8 million years, fire and tools) → *Homo sapiens* (275,000 years); interbreeding with Neanderthals.
14. **Three fossil discoveries**: Ethiopian 3-year-old girl (3.3 million years), Flores (18,000 years), South African pit.
15. **The big-three paradox**: large brain, upright posture, opposable thumb long established; art 40,000, farming 10,000, writing 7,500 years ago.
16. **Nine misunderstood points about evolution**, especially bush not ladder, fewer than 1 percent surviving, tinkerer not architect, spandrels, exaptations, homologous versus analogous.
17. **Brain evolution**: size and brain-to-body ratio both fail to predict intelligence; cerebrum expansion and convolutions; conserved structures across species.
18. **Mendel's experiment** and four-part theory; the 3:1 ratio worked through a Punnett square; genotype versus phenotype.
19. **Meiosis versus mitosis**; genetic recombination; 23 pairs; gametes and zygote.
20. **DNA structure and replication**: A-T and G-C complementarity; mutations usually eliminated.
21. **Sex-linked inheritance**: recessive X-linked traits (colour blindness) in males, dominant in females.
22. **Gene expression**: structural genes, promoters, activators, repressors; transcription (DNA → mRNA) and translation (mRNA → protein); codons, ribosomes, tRNA; RNA's uracil and ribose.
23. **Human Genome Project**: 1990, 3 billion bases, about 21,000 genes, coding DNA 1 percent, the proteome, and the disappointing diabetes example (18 variants, 6 percent).
24. **Epigenetics**: definition, four conditions for its rise, five discoveries, DNA methylation and histone remodelling (both bidirectional), epigenome and epitranscriptome.
25. **Transgenerational epigenetics**: plants, then mice (odour-shock fear memory, Dias & Ressler); growing human evidence.
26. **Tryon (1934)** selective breeding with cross-fostering; **Searle (1949)** less fearful, not smarter; **Cooper and Zubek (1958)** enriched versus impoverished.
27. **PKU**: Følling (1934), phenylpyruvic acid, recessive single gene, carrier and incidence figures, phenylalanine hydroxylase, tyrosine and dopamine, newborn screening, diet in the sensitive period, subtle deficits, adult relaxation.
28. **Development of individuals versus differences among individuals**; MZ versus DZ twins.
29. **Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart**: design figures and the MZ-more-similar finding.
30. **Heritability estimates**: definition, population-not-individual, dependence on sample variation (Minnesota), typical 40 to 80 percent, calls to stop such studies.
31. **Twin studies of epigenetics**: Fraga et al. (2005), Wong et al. (2010), disease-discordant MZ designs (Bell & Spector, 2011); "identical twins" no longer appropriate.
32. **Turkheimer et al. (2003)**: heritability of IQ 70 versus 10 percent by SES; replications; theoretical and policy implications.
