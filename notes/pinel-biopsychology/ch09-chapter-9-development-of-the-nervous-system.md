# Chapter 9: Development of the Nervous System

**What this chapter is really about.** Most students picture the brain as finished wiring: billions of neurons, connected once, working for life. Pinel and Barnes replace that picture. The brain is a **plastic** (changeable) living organ that builds itself from a single fertilised egg and keeps changing with experience for as long as you live. The chapter makes four points that organise everything else: (1) neurodevelopment is an astonishing, precisely sequenced process; (2) **experience** plays a critical role in it, not just genes; (3) the **adult** brain remains plastic, including, contrary to most of twentieth-century teaching, the birth of new neurons; and (4) when neurodevelopment goes wrong, the consequences appear in disorders such as **autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** and **Williams syndrome**.

The idea that ties these together is that development is always an **interaction between genetic programmes and experience**. A cell becomes a neuron rather than a skin cell because of which parts of its DNA it reads, and that depends on its environment; a visual cortex organises properly only if the eyes deliver patterned input at the right time; an adult hippocampus adds more neurons if its owner explores a complex world.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 9.1 is the machinery: the five phases by which a neural tube becomes a wired nervous system, dense with terms and classic experiments (Sperry's rotated frog eyes, the half-retina and half-tectum studies, the extra-limb grafts). Section 9.2 asks how the human brain grows before and after birth, with special attention to the prefrontal cortex. Section 9.3 covers experience and postnatal development, including the most tested distinction in the chapter, **critical versus sensitive period**. Section 9.4 overturns the "no new adult neurons" dogma. Section 9.5 applies everything to ASD and Williams syndrome. Read the experiments slowly: the marks are in knowing *what a result rules out*, not just what it found.

## The Case of Genie

Most of us grow up in broadly similar circumstances: warm, safe, stimulating environments, supportive families, enough to eat and drink. Because there is so little variation in early experience across most people, the role of experience in brain and psychological development is easy to overlook. One way to see it is to look at the rare cases in which a child has been reared in a grossly abnormal environment. Genie is the case Pinel and Barnes use (Curtiss, 1977; Rymer, 1993).

When Genie was admitted to hospital at the age of **13**, she was only **1.35 metres (4 feet 5 inches)** tall and weighed **28.1 kilograms (62 pounds)**. She could not stand erect, could not chew solid food, and could not control her bladder or bowels. From the age of **20 months** she had spent most days **tied to a potty** in a small, dark, closed room, wearing only a cloth harness that prevented her from moving anything but her hands and feet. In the evenings she was moved to a covered crib and put in a **straitjacket**. Her father was intolerant of noise and **beat her if she made any sound at all**. According to her mother, who was almost totally blind, Genie's father and brother rarely spoke to her, although they sometimes **barked at her like dogs**. Her mother was allowed only a few minutes with her each day, during which she fed Genie cereal or baby food; Genie was given no solid food. On admission she made almost no sounds and was **totally incapable of speech**.

After her rescue at 13, a major effort was made to put her development back on track and to document both her problems and her improvements. She received special care and training, but her behaviour **never became typical**. Her continuing problems included the following: she did not react to extremes of warmth and cold; she had **silent tantrums** in which she would flail, spit, scratch, urinate and rub her own mucus on herself; she was easily terrified (for example, of dogs and of men wearing khaki); she **could not chew**; and she could speak only **short, poorly pronounced phrases**. She is believed to be living in a home for intellectually disabled adults.

The authors' conclusion is deliberately modest: Genie's problems were *apparently* the result of her abuse, so the case **suggests** an important role for experience. A single case cannot separate the many things wrong with her environment (isolation, language deprivation, restraint, poor nutrition, violence) or say which produced which deficit; it shows only that something in the ordinary environment is necessary for typical development.

Genie is not an isolated example. Reports of childhood adversity affecting neurodevelopment are widespread (see Teicher et al., 2016), and research has established that **malnutrition associated with poverty** is by itself sufficient to impair neurodevelopment (see Storrs, 2017). That last point is worth holding on to for a clinical exam: you do not need Genie-level abuse to see effects. Neurodevelopment is both important and vulnerable.

## 9.1 Five Phases of Early Neurodevelopment

Development begins with a **zygote**, the single cell formed when an ovum (egg) and a sperm combine. The zygote divides into two daughter cells, those two into four, four into eight, and so on until a mature organism exists. But if cell multiplication were all that happened, Pinel and Barnes point out, each of us would end up like a bowl of rice pudding: an amorphous mass of identical cells.

To avoid that fate, three things other than multiplication must happen:

1. Cells must **differentiate**: some must become muscle cells, some multipolar neurons, some glial cells, and so on.
2. Cells must **make their way to appropriate sites** and align themselves with the cells around them to form particular structures.
3. Cells must **establish appropriate functional relations** with other cells.

The developing nervous system achieves these in **five phases**, which you should memorise in order:

1. **Induction of the neural plate**
2. **Neural proliferation**
3. **Migration and aggregation**
4. **Axon growth and synapse formation**
5. **Neuron death and synapse rearrangement**

Before the phases themselves, the chapter introduces the cells that make them possible.

### Stem cells and the language of potency

A fertilised egg is **totipotent**: it can develop into **any** class of cell in the body, bone, skin, neuron or heart. Very soon, however, cell division produces generations of new cells that are no longer totipotent (see Boroviak & Nichols, 2014; Kohwi & Doe, 2013). The potency of developing cells then narrows in steps, and the terms for the steps are classic MCQ material:

| Term | Can become | Book's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Totipotent** | Any class of cell in the body | The fertilised egg |
| **Pluripotent** | Many, but not all, classes of body cell | Early descendants of the zygote |
| **Multipotent** | Different cells of only **one class** | Different kinds of blood cells |
| **Unipotent** | Only **one type** of cell | Bipolar neurons |

Read the multipotent and unipotent rows carefully, because the distinction is subtle. A multipotent cell is committed to one *class* but can still produce several *kinds* within it (several kinds of blood cell). A unipotent cell can produce only one *type* (a bipolar neuron). As the embryo develops, new cells become more and more specialised, and eventually most developing cells are unipotent.

The **totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent** cells created during early development are all **embryonic stem cells** (Figure 9.1). Two properties of stem cells matter for understanding the nervous system (see Morey, Santanach, & Di Croce, 2015):

1. **An almost unlimited capacity for self-renewal**, if they are kept in an appropriate culture. Cultures of embryonic stem cells can be kept alive and multiplying for **more than a year**.
2. **The ability to develop into many different kinds of cells.**

Both properties come from the way stem cells divide: **asymmetric cell division**, meaning cell division that produces two daughter cells with different characteristics. When a stem cell divides, **one daughter is a stem cell and the other develops into a more specific cell type** (see Ito & Suda, 2014). Think through why this single mechanism gives you both properties. Because one daughter is always another stem cell, the stem-cell population never runs out (self-renewal). Because the other daughter is free to specialise, the population keeps producing diverse cell types. A culture made only of symmetric divisions into specialised cells would use itself up.

> **Trap.** "Asymmetric cell division" does **not** mean the two daughters are different sizes. It means they have different **characteristics**: one remains a stem cell, the other becomes a more specific cell type. It is the basis of self-renewal.

### How identical DNA produces different cells

This raises an obvious puzzle. Every cell in your body has **exactly the same DNA**. What makes one cell become a skin cell and another a neuron? The answer is that cells can **transcribe different sections of their DNA depending on their experience**, that is, depending on the signals in their surroundings. These mechanisms are called **epigenetic** (see Bale, 2015; Yao et al., 2016). Epigenetics was the subject of Chapter 2, and "thinking about epigenetics" is one of the emerging themes of the book; in this chapter it appears here, in the determination of cell fate.

The ability to study development has grown enormously with biochemical tools for controlling the fate of developing cells. A culture of stem cells can now be **induced to develop into one of many different brain cell types** (see Tsunemoto et al., 2018). It is even possible to watch a miniature three-dimensional brain, a **brain organoid**, develop in culture from stem cells (see Di Lullo & Kriegstein, 2017; Kelava & Lancaster, 2016; Pasca, 2018; Shen, 2018). Because stem cells can become different mature cells, their **therapeutic potential** is under intense investigation: will stem cells injected into a damaged part of a mature brain develop into the appropriate structure and improve function? That question is taken up in Chapter 10.

### Induction of the neural plate

**Three weeks after conception**, the tissue destined to become the human nervous system becomes recognisable as the **neural plate**: a small patch of **ectodermal** tissue on the **dorsal** surface of the developing embryo. The embryo at this stage has three layers of cells, the **ectoderm** (outermost), the **mesoderm** (middle) and the **endoderm** (innermost). The development of the neural plate is the **first major stage of neurodevelopment in all vertebrates** (Figure 9.2).

The development of the neural plate is **induced** by chemical signals from an area of the underlying **mesoderm layer**. Because this area organises the development of the tissue above it, it is called an **organizer** (see Araya et al., 2014; Kiecker & Lumsden, 2012).

The evidence for an organizer is a transplantation experiment whose logic is worth spelling out. Tissue taken from the **dorsal mesoderm** of one embryo (the donor) is implanted beneath the **ventral ectoderm** of another embryo (the host). Ventral ectoderm would never normally become nervous tissue. Yet the result is an **extra neural plate on the ventral surface** of the host. Reason through what this shows. The ectoderm on the ventral side clearly *could* become neural tissue; it simply never received the instruction. The instruction must therefore come from the dorsal mesoderm, since moving that mesoderm moved the neural plate with it. The neural plate is not formed because the dorsal ectoderm is special in itself, but because of what lies beneath it.

> **Trap.** The printed caption of Figure 9.2 in the 11th edition says the neural plate develops from tissue in the **endoderm**. That is an error in the caption. The text is clear, and correct: the neural plate is **ectodermal** tissue; its development is **induced by signals from the mesoderm**. If an option says "the neural plate develops from endoderm", it is wrong; if it says "the neural plate is induced by the mesoderm", it is right.

### From neural plate to neural tube

As Figure 9.3 shows, the growing neural plate **folds to form the neural groove**, and then the **lips of the neural groove fuse to form the neural tube**. The figure's timeline for humans is useful to remember: the neural plate at about **18 days**, the neural groove at about **21 days**, and the neural tube at about **24 days**, in other words during the third and fourth weeks of embryological development.

Errors in this folding process produce **neural tube defects**, which develop into severe birth defects of the central nervous system (see Greene & Copp, 2014). The **inside of the neural tube** eventually becomes the **cerebral ventricles and the spinal canal**; the hollow centre of the tube is the origin of the fluid-filled spaces of the adult CNS. By **40 days after conception**, three swellings are visible at the anterior end of the human neural tube.

### Neural proliferation

Once the lips of the neural groove have fused, the cells of the tube begin to **proliferate**, that is, to increase greatly in number. The rate is remarkable: more than **4 million cells per hour** (see Silbereis et al., 2016).

Proliferation is **not simultaneous or equal** throughout the tube. Most cell division occurs in the **ventricular zone** and the **subventricular zone**, the two regions adjacent to the ventricle (the fluid-filled centre of the tube). Remember the subventricular zone, because it returns in 9.4 as one of the sites of adult neurogenesis.

In each species, cells in different parts of the tube proliferate in a **particular sequence**, and that sequence is responsible for the pattern of swelling and folding that gives the brain of that species its characteristic shape. In many animals the swellings become the **forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain**. The complex pattern of proliferation is partly controlled by chemical signals from **two organizer areas** in the neural tube:

- the **floor plate**, which runs along the midline of the **ventral** surface of the tube; and
- the **roof plate**, which runs along the midline of the **dorsal** surface of the tube (see Kanold & Luhmann, 2010).

> **Trap.** There are two different "organizer" ideas in this module and examiners like to mix them. The organizer that **induces the neural plate** is in the **underlying mesoderm**. The two organizer areas that help control **proliferation** are the **floor plate (ventral)** and **roof plate (dorsal)** of the neural tube itself. LO 9.3 asks you to name exactly these two.

### Radial glial cells: the stem cells of the neural tube

Here is a fact that surprises most students. The stem cells created in the developing neural tube are **virtually always radial glial cells** (see Falk & Götz, 2017). A **radial glial cell** has its cell body in the ventricular or subventricular zone and a **long process that extends all the way to the outermost part of the developing neural tube**. Figure 9.4 shows how radial glial cells undergo **asymmetric cell division** to produce, over time, **neurons**, then **glia** (astrocytes and oligodendrocytes), and other cells of the developing nervous system such as **ependymal cells**.

So the cells that later in life we think of as "support" cells are, in development, the source of the neurons themselves. And radial glial cells have a second job: in addition to being stem cells, they play a **key role in cell migration**, which is the next phase.

### Migration: time, location and direction

Once cells have been created by division in the ventricular zone, they **migrate** to their target locations. During migration the cells are still **immature**, lacking the axons and dendrites that characterise mature neurons. Two major factors govern migration: **time** and **location**. In a given region of the tube, subtypes of neurons arise on a precise and predictable schedule and then migrate together to particular destinations (see Itoh, Tyssowski, & Gotoh, 2013; Kohwi & Doe, 2013).

Migration in the neural tube is of two kinds (Figure 9.5):

- **Radial migration** proceeds from the ventricular zone **in a straight line outward** towards the outer wall of the tube.
- **Tangential migration** occurs **at a right angle to radial migration**, that is, **parallel to the walls** of the tube.

Many cells use **both** kinds of migration to get from their point of origin in the ventricular zone to their destination (see Budday, Steinmann, & Kuhl, 2015; Evsyukova, Plestant, & Anton, 2013).

### Two mechanisms of migration

Separate from the *direction* of migration is the *mechanism* by which a cell moves. There are two (Figure 9.6), and the distinction between direction and mechanism is the source of a favourite MCQ.

**Somal translocation.** The developing cell extends a **process** from its cell body that seems to **explore the immediate environment**. Numerous chemicals guide the movement of these processes by **attracting or repelling** them (see Devreotes & Horwitz, 2015; Maeda, 2015). Once the process finds a suitable environment, the **cell body moves into and along the process** (see Cooper, 2013). Because the process can head in any direction, somal translocation allows a cell to migrate **radially or tangentially**.

**Radial-glia-mediated migration.** The developing cell uses the **long process of a radial glial cell as a kind of rope**, pulling itself up and away from the ventricular zone. Because radial glial processes run only from the ventricular zone to the outer surface, this mechanism allows migration in a **radial fashion only** (see Ohshima, 2015).

> **Trap.** Match mechanism to direction. **Somal translocation: radial or tangential.** **Radial-glia-mediated migration: radial only.** An option claiming that radial glia guide tangential migration is wrong, and the reason is anatomical: the glial "rope" points only outward.

### The inside-out pattern of cortical development

Early research on migration focused on the **cortex**, and it suggested an orderly process in which cells migrated from deeper to more superficial layers. Because **each new wave of cortical cells migrates through the already formed lower layers** before reaching its destination, this radial pattern is called the **inside-out pattern** (see Greig et al., 2013). Be careful with the logic of the name: the *deepest* layers are formed *first*, and later-born cells pass them to settle further out. So the youngest neurons end up in the most superficial layers.

The picture has turned out to be much more complex than originally thought. **Many cortical cells engage in long tangential migrations** to reach their final destinations, and the patterns of proliferation and migration **differ between areas** of the developing cortex (see Anderson & Vanderhaeghen, 2014; Silbereis et al., 2016; Sun & Hevner, 2014). The inside-out pattern is a real feature of radial development, not the whole story.

### The neural crest

The **neural crest** is a structure situated **just dorsal to the neural tube** (you can see it in the 24-day panel of Figure 9.3). It is formed from cells that **break off from the neural tube as it is being formed**. Neural crest cells develop into the **neurons and glial cells of the peripheral nervous system**, as well as many other cell types in the body (see Buitrago-Delgado et al., 2015; Kuratani, Kusakabe, & Hirasawa, 2018).

> **Trap.** The neural **tube** gives rise to the **CNS** (and its hollow centre to the ventricles and spinal canal). The neural **crest** gives rise to the **PNS** (and other body cell types). A question that assigns PNS neurons to the neural tube is testing exactly this.

### Aggregation

Once developing neurons have migrated, they must **align themselves with other developing neurons that have migrated to the same area**, to form the structures of the nervous system. This is **aggregation**. It is thought to be mediated by **at least three non-exclusive mechanisms** ("non-exclusive" meaning they can all operate together):

1. **Cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs).** These molecules on the surfaces of neurons and other cells can **recognise molecules on other cells and adhere to them** (see Mori et al., 2014; Sytnyk, Leshchyns'ka, & Schachner, 2017; Weledji & Assob, 2014). Their importance is shown by **knockout mice** (mice lacking a particular gene under investigation; see Chapter 5): eliminating **just one type of CAM** has a **devastating effect on brain development** (DiCicco-Bloom, 2006; Lien et al., 2006).
2. **Gap junctions.** There is evidence that gap junctions contribute to aggregation and other aspects of neurodevelopment (see Belousov & Fontes, 2013; Niculescu & Lohmann, 2014). Recall from Chapter 4 that gap junctions are points of communication between adjacent cells, bridged by narrow tubes called **connexins**.
3. **Glia-neuron interactions**, for example between **microglia** and neurons (see Thion & Garel, 2017).

### Axon growth: growth cones

Once neurons have migrated and aggregated into structures, **axons and dendrites begin to grow** from them (see Yogev & Shen, 2017). For the nervous system to work, these projections must reach appropriate targets, often far away.

At each growing tip of an axon or dendrite is an amoeba-like structure called a **growth cone**. It extends and retracts finger-like cytoplasmic extensions called **filopodia** (Figure 9.7), which behave as though they are **searching for the correct route** (see Kerstein, Nichol, & Gomez, 2015; Kahn & Baas, 2016). Remarkably, **most growth cones reach their correct targets**. The question for the rest of this phase is how.

### Sperry's eye-rotation experiments

The first demonstration that axons are capable of precise growth, and the first suggestion of how, came from a series of studies of **neural regeneration** by **Roger Sperry in the early 1940s**. Sperry worked with **frogs** because, unlike mammals, frogs have **retinal ganglion cells that regenerate**. The axons of the retinal ganglion cells make up the optic nerve and terminate in the **optic tectum**, the structure called the **superior colliculus** in mammals.

> **Key study, Sperry (early 1940s).** Sperry **cut the optic nerves** of frogs, **rotated their eyeballs 180 degrees**, and waited for the retinal ganglion cell axons to regenerate. He then used a convenient behavioural test: when he dangled a lure **behind** a frog, it **struck forward**, indicating that its visual world, like its eye, had been **rotated 180 degrees**. Frogs whose eyes had been rotated but whose optic nerves had **not** been cut responded in **exactly the same way**.

Work through the three conditions in Figure 9.8, because the design is the point.

- A **normal frog** strikes accurately at an insect.
- A frog whose eye is **rotated without cutting the nerve** misdirects its strikes by 180 degrees. That is expected: each retinal ganglion cell is still connected to its original tectal site, but the retina is now upside down, so every retinal location reports the wrong position in space.
- A frog whose nerve is **cut and eye rotated** is at first blind. If regenerating axons simply grew to whatever tectal site was nearest, or reorganised to match the new orientation of the eye, the frog's vision would be correct after regeneration. Instead, once the nerve has regenerated the frog **misdirects its strikes by 180 degrees, exactly like the uncut rotated frog**.

The only interpretation that fits the third result is that **each retinal ganglion cell axon grew back to the same point in the optic tectum to which it had originally been connected**, even though its starting position had been rotated. Behaviour told Sperry what the anatomy must be; later **neuroanatomical investigations confirmed** that this is exactly what happens (see Guo & Udin, 2000).

### The chemoaffinity hypothesis

On the basis of these regeneration studies, Sperry proposed the **chemoaffinity hypothesis** of axonal development (see Sperry, 1963). The hypothesis has two parts:

1. **Each postsynaptic surface in the nervous system releases a specific chemical label.**
2. **Each growing axon is attracted by its label to its postsynaptic target**, during both neural development and regeneration.

The hypothesis was productive. Since Sperry proposed it, **many guidance molecules for axon growth have been identified** (see Dudanova & Klein, 2013; Onishi, Hollis, & Zou, 2014). And as Pinel and Barnes note, it is hard to imagine another mechanism by which an axon growing from a rotated eyeball could find its precise original target on the tectum.

### Beyond chemoaffinity: signals along the route

Despite generating a great deal of research, the chemoaffinity hypothesis **fails to account for one discovery**: some growing axons follow **the same circuitous route** to reach their target **in every member of a species**, rather than growing directly to it. If an axon were simply attracted by a label released from its target, it should head straight there. A route that winds the same way in every individual implies that the axon is being steered at points along the way.

This led to a **revised hypothesis**. A growing axon is **not** attracted to its target by a single specific attractant released by the target, as Sperry thought. Instead, growth cones are influenced by **a series of chemical and physical signals along the route** (see Goodhill, 2016; Koser et al., 2016; Squarzoni, Thion, & Garel, 2015; Tamariz & Varela-Echavarría, 2015). Some of these signals **attract** and others **repel** the growing axon (see Seabrook et al., 2017). Navigation is achieved by a sequence of local cues, not by homing on a single beacon.

### Pioneer growth cones and fasciculation

If every axon had to read every cue for itself, development would be slow and error-prone. Two related ideas explain how many axons travel accurately.

- **Pioneer growth cones** are the **first** growth cones to travel along a particular route in the developing nervous system. They are believed to find the correct trail by **interacting with guidance molecules along the route**.
- Subsequent growth cones making the same journey are presumed to **follow the routes blazed by the pioneers**. The tendency of developing axons to **grow along the paths established by preceding axons** is called **fasciculation**.

> **Trap.** Pioneers navigate by **guidance molecules**; followers navigate by **fasciculation**, following earlier axons. Fasciculation is the *following*, not the pioneering. An option defining fasciculation as "growth of the first axons along a route" is wrong.

### Topographic maps and the topographic gradient hypothesis

Much axonal development in complex nervous systems involves growth **from one topographic array of neurons to another**: the neurons on one array project to the second while **preserving the same topographic relations** they had on the first. The standard example is the **retina mapped onto the optic tectum**: neighbouring points on the retina project to neighbouring points on the tectum.

At first it was assumed that this map was maintained by **point-to-point chemoaffinity**, each retinal ganglion cell growing towards its own specific chemical label. The evidence says the mechanism must be more complex, and there are two lines of it.

**Development.** In most species, the synaptic connections between retina and tectum are established **long before either has reached full size**. Then, as the retina and tectum grow **at different rates**, the initial connections **shift to other tectal neurons** so that each retina remains precisely mapped onto the tectum **whatever their relative sizes**. A fixed label at each tectal point cannot produce connections that slide around as the structures grow.

**Regeneration.** The decisive studies are shown in Figure 9.9. In mature frogs or fish, the optic nerve was cut and the pattern of regeneration was examined after **parts of the retina or the optic tectum had been destroyed**.

- **When half the retina was destroyed**, the retinal ganglion cells of the remaining half did **not** confine themselves to their original half of the tectum. They **projected systematically over the entire optic tectum**.
- **When half the optic tectum was destroyed**, the ganglion cells of the whole retina did **not** search for their lost targets. They **projected systematically over the remaining half of the tectum**, compressed but still in order.

In both cases the axons **did not grow to their original points of connection; they grew out to fill the available space in an orderly fashion**. Point-to-point chemoaffinity predicts the opposite in both experiments: half a retina should map onto half a tectum, and axons whose targets were destroyed should fail to connect. The result rules out strict point-to-point labels while preserving the idea of chemical guidance.

The explanation proposed is the **topographic gradient hypothesis** (see Weth et al., 2014). It holds that axons growing from one topographic surface (for example, the retina) to another (for example, the optic tectum) are guided to targets arranged on the terminal surface **in the same way as the axons' cell bodies are arranged on the original surface** (see Cang & Feldheim, 2013; Klein & Kania, 2014; Triplett, 2014). The key part of the hypothesis is that the growing axons are guided by **two intersecting signal gradients**, for example an **anterior-posterior gradient** and a **medial-lateral gradient** (see Seabrook et al., 2017; but see Goodhill, 2016).

Think about why gradients explain the half-retina and half-tectum results where labels cannot. A gradient tells an axon its *relative* position ("more anterior than my neighbour"), not an absolute address. If half the tectum is gone, the axons still sort themselves in the right order relative to each other, and simply squeeze into the space that remains; if half the retina is gone, the surviving axons spread out in order across the whole tectum. Relative positional information is exactly what produces "orderly filling of available space".

Gradients are not the whole story. Other mechanisms also contribute to accurate topographic mapping (e.g., Quast et al., 2017), including **spontaneous neural activity** (see Seabrook et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017) and **neuron-astrocyte interactions** (see López-Hidalgo & Schummers, 2014). Note too the "but see Goodhill, 2016" in the citation: the gradient account is the leading hypothesis, not a settled fact.

> **Trap.** Sperry's eye-rotation study supports **chemoaffinity**: axons return to their **original** targets. The half-retina and half-tectum studies **challenge point-to-point chemoaffinity** and support the **topographic gradient hypothesis**: axons fill the **available space in order**. A question pairing "half the tectum destroyed" with "chemoaffinity" is the trap.

### Synapse formation

Once axons reach their intended sites, they must establish an appropriate pattern of synapses. During neurodevelopment, synapses form at roughly **700,000 synapses per second** (see Silbereis et al., 2016). (Set that beside the proliferation figure: **more than 4 million cells per hour**. Both numbers come from Silbereis et al., 2016, and both are the kind examiners like to swap.)

Our understanding of how axons connect to their targets has **lagged behind** our understanding of how they reach them. One reason is simple: a single neuron can grow an axon on its own, but it takes **coordinated activity in at least two neurons** to create a synapse between them (see Andreae & Burrone, 2014). Still, three breakthroughs are listed:

1. Some of the **chemical signals** that play a role in the location and formation of synapses have been identified (see Christensen, Shao, & Colón-Ramos, 2013; Inestrosa & Arenas, 2010; Koropouli & Kolodkin, 2014; Krueger et al., 2012).
2. **Spontaneous neurotransmitter release** has been shown to be important for synapse formation (see Andreae & Burrone, 2018).
3. **Cell surfaces interact before synapse formation** (see de Wit & Ghosh, 2016).

Most current research on **synaptogenesis**, the formation of new synapses, focuses on identifying the chemical signals that must be **exchanged between presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons** for a synapse to be created (see Ou & Shen, 2010; Sheffler-Collins & Dalva, 2012). One complication is the **promiscuity** of developing neurons: **in vitro, any type of neuron can form synapses with any other type**. The specificity of the mature nervous system therefore cannot come from synapse formation alone. Part of it comes afterwards: once established, **synapses that do not function appropriately tend to be eliminated** (see Coulthard, Hawksworth, & Woodruff, 2018).

### Glia and synaptogenesis

Perhaps the most exciting recent discovery about synaptogenesis is that it **depends on the presence of glial cells**, particularly **astrocytes** (see Bosworth & Allen, 2017) and **microglia** (see Stogsdill & Eroglu, 2017). Two findings make the case:

- **Retinal ganglion cells maintained in culture formed seven times more synapses when astrocytes were present.**
- **Synapses formed in the presence of astrocytes were quickly lost when the astrocytes were removed.**

The second finding matters as much as the first: astrocytes are needed not only to build synapses but to keep them.

Why? Early theories emphasised a **nutritional** role: developing neurons need high levels of **cholesterol** during synapse formation, and the extra cholesterol is supplied by astrocytes. Current evidence suggests a much more complex role, in which astrocytes **process, transfer and store information** supplied by neurons (see Clarke & Barres, 2013). Glia are participants in building circuits, not merely caterers.

### Neuron death: apoptosis versus necrosis

**Neuron death** is a **normal and important part of neurodevelopment**. Many more neurons are produced than are needed, about **50 percent more**, and waves of large-scale neuron death occur in various parts of the brain throughout development. (Journal Prompt 9.1 asks why a developing nervous system would make 50 percent more neurons than it needs. A good answer follows from the rest of this section: overproduction lets the system keep the neurons that make correct, functional connections and discard the rest, so precision is achieved by selection rather than by getting every neuron right first time.)

Neuron death during development was originally assumed to be **passive**: neurons died when they failed to get adequate nutrition. It is now clear that developmental cell death is usually **active**: genetic programmes inside neurons are triggered and cause them to **commit suicide**. The two kinds of death have names:

| | **Necrosis** | **Apoptosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | **Passive** cell death | **Active**, genetically programmed cell death |
| What happens | The cell **breaks apart and spills its contents** into the extracellular fluid | The cell's **internal structures are cleaved apart and packaged in membranes** before the cell breaks apart |
| Consequence | Potentially harmful **inflammation** | Membrane packages contain molecules that **attract microglia**, which **engulf and consume** them |
| Overall | Messy and dangerous | **Safe, neat and orderly** |

That table is the answer to LO 9.6's question, "Why is apoptosis safer than necrosis?". Necrosis spills; apoptosis packages. The packaging prevents inflammation and allows microglia to clean up.

Apoptosis has a **dark side**. If the genetic programmes for apoptotic cell death are **blocked**, the consequence can be **cancer** (see Bardella et al., 2018), since cells that should die keep dividing. If the programmes are **inappropriately activated**, the consequence can be **neurodegenerative disease**, since cells that should live die.

### What triggers apoptosis: target-derived survival factors

There appear to be **two kinds of trigger** for the apoptotic programmes in developing neurons:

1. **Genetic programming for early death.** Some developing neurons appear to be programmed to die once they have fulfilled their functions; groups of neurons die together in the absence of any obvious external stimulus (see Dekkers & Barde, 2013; Underwood, 2013).
2. **Failure to obtain life-preserving chemicals supplied by their targets** (see Deppmann et al., 2008).

The evidence that **postsynaptic targets** supply life-preserving chemicals comes from two kinds of experiment, and they work as mirror images:

- **Grafting an extra target structure**, for example an **extra limb**, onto an embryo before the period of synaptogenesis **reduces the death** of neurons growing into the area. More target means more survival factor, so more neurons survive.
- **Destroying some of the neurons growing into an area** before the period of cell death **increases the survival rate of the remaining neurons**. Fewer competitors for the same supply means each gets enough.

Both results are explained by one idea: neurons compete for a **limited supply of a survival chemical provided by the target**. Increase supply or decrease demand, and more neurons survive.

Several of these life-preserving chemicals have been identified. The most prominent class is the **neurotrophins**. **Nerve growth factor (NGF)** was the **first** neurotrophin to be isolated (see **Levi-Montalcini, 1952, 1975**); the **second** was **brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)**. Neurotrophins do more than keep neurons alive. They:

- promote the **growth and survival** of neurons;
- function as **axon guidance molecules**; and
- **stimulate synaptogenesis** (Park & Poo, 2013).

> **Trap.** **NGF was first, BDNF second.** NGF is associated with **Levi-Montalcini**. And neurotrophins are not only survival factors: they also guide axons and stimulate synapse formation.

### Synapse rearrangement

During the period of cell death, neurons that have established **incorrect connections are particularly likely to die**. As they die, the space they leave vacant on postsynaptic membranes is **filled by the sprouting axon terminals of surviving neurons**. Cell death therefore produces a **massive rearrangement of synaptic connections**.

This phase has a second, functional consequence, shown in Figure 9.10: it **focuses the output of each neuron on a smaller number of postsynaptic cells**, thereby **increasing the selectivity of transmission**. Early in development the pattern of synaptic contact is **diffuse**, each axon contacting many cells; after rearrangement it is **focused**, each axon concentrating its contacts on a few. There is evidence that **microglia** play a role in synapse rearrangement (see Coulthard, Hawksworth, & Woodruff, 2018; Ueno & Yamashita, 2014).

Put the last three sections together and you have the logic of the module: **overproduce, let neurons compete for target-derived neurotrophins, let the poorly connected die by apoptosis, and let the survivors take over and focus their connections**. A precise circuit is built from imprecise first attempts.

## 9.2 Early Cerebral Development in Humans

### Why the human brain is a special case

Much of what we know about human brain development comes from **other species**, which is why the **evolutionary perspective** is one of the book's recurring themes.

There is, however, one way in which human brain development is unique. The human brain **develops more slowly** than the brains of other species, and does not reach full maturity until **late adolescence or early adulthood** (see Crone & Dahl, 2012; Fuhrmann, Knoll, & Blakemore, 2015). This module covers cerebral development in both the **prenatal period** (the period of development **before birth**) and the **early postnatal period** (the period **after birth**). It gives special emphasis to the **prefrontal cortex**, because it is the **last part of the human brain to reach maturity** (see Giedd, 2015).

Slowness is the bridge to 9.3: a brain that takes two decades to mature gives experience two decades to shape it.

### Prenatal growth of the human brain

Most of our knowledge of **prenatal** brain growth has been **inferred from nonhuman animals**, because the procedures needed to study it directly would be invasive or otherwise unfeasible in developing humans. Journal Prompt 9.2 asks what this implies, given that the human brain develops more slowly: findings about the *sequence* of development transfer from animals more safely than findings about its *timing*.

Three technical advances now allow researchers to examine developing **human** neural tissue more directly:

1. **Brain organoids.** Three-dimensional brain organoids grown in culture (introduced in 9.1) are built from **human cells** and have been used to study human brain development (see Arlotta, 2018). For example, **Quadrato and colleagues (2017)** induced the development of **photosensitive human brain organoids** and used them to study the development of the **visual system**.
2. **Imaging the prenatal brain.** A small number of laboratories have recently imaged the **functional connectivity** of the developing brain **in utero**. Functional connectivity is defined as **correlated activity between different brain regions over time**. This work has already revealed the development of functional connections in **30- to 38-week-old fetuses** (see Thomason et al., 2017).
3. **Cell-level transcriptomes.** A **transcriptome** is, as the book defines it, a catalogue of all the proteins transcribed in a particular cell. Transcriptomes have now been characterised for the developing human **prefrontal cortex** (see Zhong et al., 2018) and even for the **entire human cerebral cortex** (see Fan et al., 2018) in human embryos. Their analysis is likely to give important insights into cell development in prenatal humans (see Bakken et al., 2016).

### Postnatal growth of the human brain

The human brain grows substantially after birth: it **doubles in volume between birth and adulthood** (see Gilmore, Knickmeyer, & Gao, 2018). Much of the growth occurs in the **first year** (Gilmore et al., 2012; Li et al., 2015), and it continues into the **third year** (see Silbereis et al., 2016).

The crucial point is what this growth is *not*. The increase in size **does not result from the development of additional neurons**. It results from three other kinds of growth:

1. **Synaptogenesis**
2. **Myelination of axons**
3. **Increased branching of dendrites**

### Synaptogenesis, myelination and dendritic branching

**Synaptogenesis.** There is a general increase in synaptogenesis in the human cortex shortly after birth, but the timing differs between regions, and the contrast between sensory cortex and prefrontal cortex is the examinable part:

| Region | Pattern of synaptogenesis | Peak synapse density |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary visual and auditory cortex** | A major **burst in the fourth postnatal month** | **150 percent of adult levels**, reached in the **seventh or eighth postnatal month** |
| **Prefrontal cortex** | A **relatively steady rate** | Reached in the **second year** |

Notice the implication of "150 percent of adult levels". The infant visual cortex has *more* synapses than the adult's. Development therefore must include losing synapses as well as gaining them, a point taken up below.

**Myelination.** Myelination **increases the speed of axonal conduction**, and the myelination of different areas of the human brain **roughly parallels their functional development** (see Purger, Gibson, & Monje, 2015). The order is:

- **sensory areas**: myelinated in the **first few months** after birth;
- **motor areas**: soon after that;
- **prefrontal cortex**: myelination **continues into adulthood** (Yap et al., 2013).

Indeed, myelination appears to be an **ongoing process that changes as a function of experience throughout the lifespan** (see Mount & Monje, 2017). Myelin, like everything else in this chapter, is plastic.

**Dendritic branching.** In general, dendritic branching in the cortex **duplicates the inside-out pattern of neural migration**: branching progresses from **deeper to more superficial layers**. Advances in imaging live neurons in culture are revealing how dendrites reconfigure themselves. Most surprising is the **speed with which even mature dendritic spines** (the small protuberances on dendrites, many of which form synapses with other cells) **can change their shape** in response to an animal's experiences and current environment (see Berry & Nedivi, 2017).

### Regressive changes: synapse loss and cortical thinning

Early human brain development is **not a one-way street**: there are **regressive changes** as well as growth (see Jernigan et al., 2011). Once maximum synaptic density and grey matter volume have been reached, there are **periods of decline**. Like periods of synaptogenesis, these periods of synapse and grey-matter loss occur **at different times in different parts of the brain**.

The order of **cortical thinning** is: **primary sensory and motor areas first**, then **secondary areas**, culminating in **association areas** (see Jernigan et al., 2011). And the achievement of the **adult level of grey matter** in a given area is **correlated with that area reaching functional maturity**: sensory and motor areas reach functional maturity **before** association areas (see Purger, Gibson, & Monje, 2015).

Reason through what that correlation means. Thinning is not damage; it marks maturation. An area becomes functionally mature roughly when it has finished pruning down to its adult level, and the areas that support the most complex functions (association cortex, including prefrontal cortex) are the last to finish.

### New tools for studying the postnatal brain

As with the prenatal brain, new technologies have accelerated research on the postnatal human brain. Developments in **infant magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** and **functional MRI** (Chapter 5) now permit surveys of **structural** and **functional** changes, respectively, across development (see Cao, Huang, & He, 2017; Gilmore, Knickmeyer, & Gao, 2018; Vijayakumar et al., 2018). Cognitive neuroscientists can therefore examine the **neural correlates of particular cognitive capacities at different stages of early development** (see Ellis & Turk-Browne, 2018). And methods for cataloguing the proteins in particular cell types have allowed researchers to create a **proteome** (a catalogue of the proteins in different cell types) of the postnatal human brain (see Carlyle et al., 2017).

### Development of the prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex has the **most prolonged period of development of any brain region**, and its development is believed to be **largely responsible for the course of human cognitive development**, which unfolds over the same period (see Giedd, 2015).

Because the prefrontal cortex is large, complex and heterogeneous, **no single theory explains its function**. But four types of cognitive function have often been linked to it in studies of **adults with extensive prefrontal damage**. Various parts of the adult prefrontal cortex seem to play roles in:

1. **Working memory**: keeping relevant information accessible for short periods while a task is being completed;
2. **Planning and carrying out sequences of actions**;
3. **Inhibiting responses** that are inappropriate in the current context but not in others; and
4. **Following rules for social behaviour** (see Fareri & Delgado, 2014; Watanabe & Yamamoto, 2015).

These functions seem to mature during **adolescence**, which the book notes is often defined as **10 to 24 years of age** (Patton et al., 2018). Their maturation appears to be associated with two neurochemical developments:

- the **growth of dopaminergic axons into the prefrontal cortex** (see Hoops & Flores, 2017); and
- the **maturation of the GABAergic system** in the prefrontal cortex (see Caballero & Tseng, 2016).

Look at the third function closely, because its wording is precise: inhibiting responses that are inappropriate **in the current context but not in others**. The prefrontal cortex is not needed to suppress responses that are always wrong; it is needed to suppress responses that were right a moment ago, or are right elsewhere, but are wrong now. That is exactly what the next experiment tests.

### Piaget's hidden-toy task and perseveration

One line of research on prefrontal development builds on **Piaget's** classic studies of psychological development in infants. In studying **7-month-old** children, Piaget noticed an intriguing error. The procedure (often called the "A-not-B" task in developmental psychology, although Pinel and Barnes do not use that name) runs as follows:

1. A small toy is shown to the infant and then, as the infant watches, placed behind **one of two screens**, left or right.
2. After a brief delay, the infant is allowed to reach for it. Almost all 7-month-olds reach for the **correct** screen, the one behind which they saw the toy placed.
3. After the toy has been placed behind the **same** screen on **several consecutive trials**, it is placed behind the **other** screen, again **as the infant watches**.
4. Most 7-month-olds **keep reaching for the previously correct screen** rather than the one that now hides the toy.

Children tend to make this **perseverative error between about 7 and 12 months**, but **not thereafter** (Diamond, 1985). **Perseveration** is the tendency to **continue making a formerly correct response when it is currently incorrect**.

Notice what the error is *not*. It is not a failure to perceive or to remember that the toy exists: on the first trials the infant reaches correctly, and on the switch trial the infant has just watched the toy go behind the new screen. The error is specifically the persistence of a **previously reinforced response** in the face of current information that it is wrong.

### Diamond's hypothesis and the evidence for it

**Diamond (1991)** hypothesised that the perseverative errors made by infants between 7 and 12 months are **due to a lag in prefrontal cortex development**. Current research supports the hypothesis, and the book gives three lines of evidence. Learn them as three separate arguments, because each links the task to the prefrontal cortex in a different way:

1. **Behavioural parallel in patients.** Adults with **prefrontal damage** show **perseveration when switching tasks**: they fail to suppress previously correct responses when the task changes so that those responses are now incorrect (see Shallice & Cipolotti, 2018). The infants' error looks like the patients' error.
2. **Functional requirement.** The mammalian prefrontal cortex is involved in **retaining information in working memory** (see Cavanagh et al., 2018), and working memory is needed to perform well on Piaget's task, since the infant must hold the toy's new location in mind across the delay.
3. **Developmental timing.** The **number of synapses in the prefrontal cortex is not maximal until the second year of life**, which fits the disappearance of the error after about 12 months.

> **Trap.** The perseverative error appears at **7 to 12 months** (Diamond, **1985**, describing the age range). The **hypothesis** that it reflects immature prefrontal cortex is **Diamond (1991)**. The original observation is **Piaget's**. And the key prefrontal functions involved are **working memory** and **inhibiting a previously correct response**, not perception or long-term memory.

## 9.3 Effects of Experience on Postnatal Development of Neural Circuits

Because the human brain develops so slowly, there are **many opportunities for experience to influence its development**, and the effects are many and varied. This module begins with the vocabulary of timing, then moves from crude manipulations of experience (rearing animals in the dark or in enriched cages) to selective ones (depriving one eye, rewiring one sensory system).

### Critical periods versus sensitive periods

An important feature of the effects of experience on development is that they are **time-dependent**: the effect of a given experience depends on **when** it occurs during development (see Makinodan et al., 2012). In most cases there is a **window of opportunity** in which a particular experience can influence development. Two terms describe these windows, and the difference between them is the single most frequently tested distinction in this chapter:

| | **Critical period** | **Sensitive period** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | It is **absolutely essential** for the experience to occur **within** the interval for it to influence development | The experience has a **great** effect within the interval but **can still have weak effects outside it** |
| Outside the window | **No effect** | **Weaker** effect |
| How common | The term is widely used, but true critical periods are rare | The **vast majority** of experiential effects on development occur during **sensitive** periods |

The words themselves carry the definitions. "Critical" means essential: miss the window and the experience cannot do its work. "Sensitive" means the system is especially responsive during the window, but not closed to influence outside it.

> **Trap.** Examiners will describe an effect that is strongest early in life but still occurs, more weakly or more slowly, in adulthood, and ask you to name the period. That is a **sensitive** period, not a critical one, even if the question writer has been loose with the term "critical period" elsewhere. Pinel and Barnes are explicit that **most** developmental effects of experience involve sensitive periods.

### Early studies: deprivation and enrichment

Most research on the effects of experience on brain development has focused on **sensory and motor systems**, because they lend themselves to experiential manipulation. Early research used two general manipulations:

**Sensory deprivation.** The first studies reared animals **in the dark**. **Rats reared from birth in the dark** were found to have **fewer synapses and fewer dendritic spines in their primary visual cortex**, and as adults they had **deficits in depth and pattern vision**. The structural finding and the behavioural finding go together: deprived of visual experience during development, the visual cortex is less richly connected, and vision is impaired.

**Sensory enrichment.** The first studies of early exposure to **enriched environments**, environments containing toys, running wheels and other rats (see Sale, 2018), found **beneficial effects**. Rats raised in enriched (complex) **group** cages rather than alone in **barren** cages had **thicker cortex**, with **more dendritic spines and more synapses per neuron** (see Berardi, Sale, & Maffei, 2015).

Put the two side by side and you can see they are mirror images. Deprivation gives fewer spines and synapses; enrichment gives more spines and synapses and a thicker cortex. The quantity and complexity of experience is reflected in the quantity of connectivity.

### Deprivation in human infants: congenital cataracts

The effects of sensory deprivation have also been studied in **human babies born with cataracts in both eyes**, which make them nearly blind (Vavvas et al., 2018). This is a natural experiment: the cataracts deprive the visual system of patterned input from birth until they are surgically removed.

When the cataracts were removed **between 1 and 6 weeks after birth**, the infants' vision was **comparable to that of a newborn** (Maurer & Lewis, 2018). In other words, development had not progressed during the period of deprivation; their visual systems were starting from where they had been at birth. Thereafter, **some aspects of vision improved quickly**, but **some visual deficits, for example deficits in face processing, persisted into adulthood** (see Maurer, 2017).

The lesson is that even a few weeks of early deprivation can leave lasting, selective deficits. Different visual abilities evidently have different sensitive periods: some can catch up after the deprivation ends, others, like face processing, cannot fully do so.

### Experience and neurodevelopment: the competitive principle

Research has moved beyond general deprivation and enrichment to **more selective manipulations** of early experience. Many of these selective manipulations have revealed a **competitive aspect** to the effects of experience on neurodevelopment: inputs compete for cortical territory, and the input that is used wins. The competitive aspect is most clearly illustrated by two examples, which LO 9.12 asks for: the effects of **monocular deprivation on ocular dominance columns**, and the development of **topographic sensory cortex maps**.

### Ocular dominance columns and monocular deprivation

Recall from Chapter 6 that in layer IV of primary visual cortex, inputs from the two eyes are segregated into alternating **ocular dominance columns**. Here is what monocular deprivation does to them:

- **Depriving one eye of input for a few days early in life** has a **lasting adverse effect on vision in the deprived eye**.
- But this **does not happen if the other eye is also blindfolded**.
- When only one eye is blindfolded, the ability of **that** eye to activate the visual cortex is **reduced**, whereas the ability of the **other** eye is **increased**.

Both effects occur because early monocular deprivation **changes the pattern of synaptic input** into the primary visual cortex (see Jaepel et al., 2017).

The second bullet is the key to the whole idea of competition, so reason it out. If disuse alone damaged the deprived eye's connections, then blindfolding *both* eyes should damage both eyes' connections at least as much. It does not. What harms the deprived eye is not its own inactivity, but its inactivity **relative to the active eye**. The active eye's inputs take over cortical territory the deprived eye would have held. When both eyes are inactive, neither has a competitive advantage, and the balance is preserved.

The anatomical version of the result: in many species, ocular dominance columns in layer IV are **largely developed at birth**. But blindfolding one eye for **just a few days during the first few months of life** reorganises them: the columns of input from the **deprived eye become narrower** and those from the **non-deprived eye become wider** (**Hata & Stryker, 1994; Hubel, Wiesel, & LeVay, 1977**). The exact timing of this window is **specific to each species**.

Pinel and Barnes use this as their worked example of a **sensitive** rather than a critical period, and the reason is explicit: **during adulthood the effect still occurs, but requires longer periods of monocular deprivation** (see Levelt & Hübener, 2012). The window affects how much deprivation is needed, not whether deprivation can work at all.

### Antonini and Stryker (1993): structural change in days

Because the adverse effects of early monocular deprivation appear so **quickly**, within a few days, it was believed that they **could not be mediated by structural changes**; surely axons cannot be rebuilt that fast, so the effect must be a change in the strength of existing synapses.

> **Key study, Antonini & Stryker (1993).** A few days of early monocular deprivation produced a **massive decrease in the branching of the axons** that project from the cell bodies of **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** neurons to **lower layer IV** of the primary visual cortex. Axons carrying information from the **deprived** eye showed **substantially less branching** than those from the non-deprived eye (Figure 9.11).

The result overturned the assumption directly. Large-scale structural remodelling of axonal arbors can happen in days. The rapid functional effect of monocular deprivation has a rapid anatomical basis.

### Topographic sensory cortex maps

The second illustration of competitive, experience-driven organisation comes from research on **sensory topographic maps**. Two demonstrations are given.

> **Key study, Roe and colleagues (1990).** Researchers surgically altered the course of the developing axons of **ferrets' retinal ganglion cells** so that they synapsed in the **medial geniculate nucleus** of the **auditory** system instead of the **lateral geniculate nucleus** of the visual system. Remarkably, the experience of visual input caused the **auditory cortex** of these ferrets to become **organised retinotopically**, laid out like a map of the retina.

Think about what this shows. The auditory cortex did not have an intrinsic, genetically fixed auditory organisation that visual input had to fit into. Given visual input, it organised itself as a visual map. The *kind of input* a cortical area receives during development largely determines how that area is organised. The general conclusion is that surgically attaching the inputs of one sensory system to cortex that would normally become the primary cortex of another system leads that cortex to develop **many, but not all**, of the characteristics typical of the newly attached system (see Majewska & Sur, 2006). Learn "many, but not all": the cortex is not infinitely malleable, and the qualification is a frequent MCQ distractor.

**Early music training.** Several studies have shown that **early music training influences the organisation of the human cortex** (see Miendlarzewska & Trost, 2014). For example, early musical training **expands the area of auditory cortex that responds to complex musical tones**. This is the human, non-surgical version of the same principle: the inputs a cortical map receives often during development claim more of the map.

## 9.4 Neuroplasticity in Adults

Pinel and Barnes flag this module with a road sign: **SLOW, IMPORTANT VIEWPOINT AHEAD**. Neuroplasticity was once thought to be **restricted to the developmental period**; mature brains were considered set in their ways, incapable of substantial reorganisation. The accumulated evidence now makes clear that **mature brains are continually changing and adapting**. Two lines of research are considered here: **adult neurogenesis**, and the **reorganisation of adult cortex by experience**.

### The old dogma, and the songbird challenge

One of the authors (SB) recalls learning two principles of brain development as a student. The first, learned by experience, was that the human brain starts working in the womb and never stops until one stands up to speak in public. The second, learned in a course, was that **neurogenesis (the growth of new neurons) does not occur in adults**. The first, the author jokes, appears correct; the second has been **proven wrong**.

Before the **early 1980s**, post-developmental brain change was seen as a **downhill slope**: neurons die throughout life and are never replaced. The idea persisted in neuroscience **until the late 20th century**, and it **remains the most widely held public misconception about brain function** (see Yoo & Blackshaw, 2018).

The first serious challenge came from **birds**. **Nottebohm and colleagues** (e.g., **Goldman & Nottebohm, 1983**) found that the brain structures involved in **singing** begin to grow in **songbirds just before each mating season**, and that this growth results from an **increase in the number of neurons**. New neurons, seasonally, in an adult brain.

### Adult neurogenesis in mammals

Then, in the **1990s**, researchers armed with **new techniques for labelling newly born neurons** showed that adult neurogenesis occurs in the **rat hippocampus** (e.g., **Cameron et al., 1993**). Shortly afterwards, new neurons were also found to be continually generated in the **subventricular zone**, the same zone where proliferation occurs during early development.

Figure 9.12 shows how such labelling works. In a section of the **dentate gyrus** of the hippocampus, neuronal cell bodies are stained **blue**, mature glial cells **green**, and **new cells red**. At higher magnification some cells have taken up **both blue and red** stain: they are new (red) cells that are neurons (blue), that is, **new neurons**. The logic of double labelling is the evidence: a new-cell marker alone would not tell you the cell is a neuron, and a neuronal marker alone would not tell you it is new.

These reports were not embraced at first by a generation trained to think of the adult brain as fixed, but acceptance grew as confirmations accumulated. Particularly influential were reports that new neurons are added to the hippocampus of **primates** (e.g., **Kornack & Rakic, 1999**), including **humans** (**Eriksson et al., 1998**), and that the number added to the adult human hippocampus is substantial, an estimated **700 per day per hippocampus** (see Kempermann, 2013; Kheirbek & Hen, 2013; Spalding et al., 2013).

### Where adult neurogenesis occurs

The sites differ between nonhuman mammals and humans, and the difference is a trap waiting to happen.

| | **Most nonhuman adult mammals** | **Adult humans** |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial neurogenesis | **Subventricular zone** and **hippocampus** | **Hippocampus** and **striatum** |
| Low levels also reported | Hypothalamus, cortex, striatum, spinal cord | |
| **Not** observed | | **Subventricular zone** (see Bergmann & Frisén, 2013) |

For nonhuman mammals, the low-level sites come with their own citations: hypothalamus (Sousa-Ferreira, de Almeida, & Cavadas, 2014), cortex (Feliciano & Bordey, 2012), striatum and spinal cord (Yoo & Blackshaw, 2018). For humans, striatal neurogenesis is reported by Ernst et al. (2014; see also Inta, Cameron, & Gass, 2015; Welberg, 2014a) and hippocampal neurogenesis by several reviews (Adams & Morshead, 2018; Kuhn, Toda, & Gage, 2018; Snyder, 2019), **"but see Sorrells et al., 2018"**. That "but see" is the book's way of flagging a contested finding: adult human hippocampal neurogenesis is widely accepted but has been challenged.

> **Trap.** In adult **humans**, neurogenesis is reported in the **hippocampus and striatum** but **not in the subventricular zone**. In rodents the subventricular zone is a major site. And human hippocampal neurogenesis is **contested** (Sorrells et al., 2018).

### Effects of experience on adult neurogenesis

From a biopsychological perspective, the most exciting finding is that **experience influences adult neurogenesis**.

> **Key study, Kempermann & Gage (1999).** **Adult rats living in enriched environments produced more new hippocampal neurons** than adult rats in non-enriched environments. (The chapter's Scan Your Brain exercise puts the figure at **60 percent more**.)

Is it the enrichment or the exercise? The positive effect of enrichment depends **in part on the increased exercise** that typically occurs in enriched environments (see DiFeo & Shors, 2017; **van Praag et al., 1999**). However, enriched environments **without increased exercise** can also effectively increase adult hippocampal neurogenesis (see Birch & Kelly, 2019). So both components contribute.

The implication is provocative. Because the hippocampus is involved in some kinds of memory, both **enriching one's environment** (for example, through diverse cognitive activities) and **regular exercise** **may reduce or delay memory problems** (see Ma et al., 2017). Note the "may": this is an implication drawn from animal work, not a demonstrated clinical effect.

### What newly born adult neurons do

It is now **established** that neurons generated in adulthood **survive, become integrated into neural circuits, and begin to conduct neural signals** (see Kelsch, Sim, & Lois, 2010; Toni et al., 2008). Their fates differ by region:

- Adult-generated **olfactory bulb** and **striatal** neurons become **interneurons** (see Brann & Firestein, 2014; Ernst et al., 2014; Sakamoto, Kageyama, & Imayoshi, 2015). (The Scan Your Brain exercise adds that new olfactory bulb and striatal neurons arise from **adult neural stem cells** at sites in the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles.)
- Adult-generated **hippocampal** neurons grow **axons that form synapses on cells in other parts of the hippocampus** (see Gonçalves, Schafer, & Gage, 2016). (The Scan Your Brain exercise identifies them as **granule cells in the dentate gyrus**.)

Anatomy and physiology have been easier to establish than **function**. Most functional research has focused on the hippocampus, and several theories have been proposed over the past 25 years:

1. A role in **memory** (see Gonçalves, Schafer, & Gage, 2016; Miller & Sahay, 2019; Lieberwirth et al., 2016; Yagi & Galea, 2019), including **forgetting** (see Akers et al., 2014; Mongiat & Schinder, 2014; Welberg, 2014b).
2. A popular theory: **pattern separation**, our ability to **separate distinct percepts into individual memories for storage** (see Aimone et al., 2014; Bergmann & Frisén, 2013; **but see Becker, 2017**).
3. **Mood and anxiety regulation** (see Cope & Gould, 2019).
4. **Resilience to stressful situations** (Anacker et al., 2018).

None of these is settled; the honest answer to "what do adult-born hippocampal neurons do?" is that several functions have been proposed and the question remains open.

### Freund et al. (2013): exploration and individuality

> **Key study, Freund et al. (2013).** A naturalistic approach. The researchers monitored the exploratory behaviour of a **large number of genetically identical mice** living in a **complex enriched environment** over **3 months**. **Variation in individual behaviour increased over time.** As expected, the more **physically active** animals had more hippocampal neurogenesis. But **exploration had an even greater influence than physical activity**: the mice that explored **larger areas**, and were therefore exposed to more cognitive challenges, had the **most newly born hippocampal neurons** (see also Bergmann & Frisén, 2013).

Notice why genetically identical mice matter: any individual differences in behaviour and neurogenesis cannot be attributed to genes, so they must arise from differences in experience. The conclusion the authors draw is that one role of adult hippocampal neurogenesis **might be to allow us to adapt flexibly to complex environments**.

### Experience and the reorganisation of adult cortex

The second line of research concerns **reorganisation of adult sensory and motor cortical maps** by experience. LO 9.14 asks for four examples; here they are.

1. **Mühlnickel and colleagues (1998)**: **tinnitus** (ringing in the ears) produces a **major reorganisation of primary auditory cortex**.
2. **Elbert and colleagues (1995)**: adult musicians who finger **stringed instruments** (e.g., the violin) with their **left hand** have an **enlarged hand-representation area in the right somatosensory cortex**. (Contralateral: left hand, right hemisphere.)
3. **Rossini and colleagues (1994)**: **anaesthetising particular fingers reduced their representation in contralateral somatosensory cortex**.
4. **Hofer and colleagues (2005)**, which warrants special attention.

> **Key study, Hofer et al. (2005).** Eliminating visual input to one eye of **adult mice** reduced the size of that eye's **ocular dominance columns in layer IV** of primary visual cortex. More importantly, the reductions occurred **more quickly and were more enduring if the mice had previously experienced visual deprivation in the same eye**.

Two things follow. First, this is direct confirmation of the sensitive-period claim from 9.3: monocular deprivation still reorganises ocular dominance columns in **adults**. Second, and more interesting, the adult brain has a kind of memory for past adaptations: **once the brain has adapted to abnormal environmental conditions, it can adapt more rapidly if it meets the same conditions again**.

The discovery of adult neuroplasticity is changing how we think about ourselves, and, for people with brain damage, it suggests promising new treatments, which Chapter 10 discusses.

### Scan Your Brain: the second checkpoint

The second checkpoint consolidates 9.2 to 9.4. Its answers, in order, are: **inhibition** (the prefrontal function listed alongside executive functions, planning and social rules); **perseverative error** (Piaget's infants); **critical period** (experience absolutely essential within the interval); **sensitive** (the period in which most experiential effects occur); **axons** (from the lateral geniculate nucleus, reduced by early visual deprivation); **stem** (adult neural stem cells in the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles give rise to new olfactory bulb and striatal neurons); **hippocampus** (60 percent more new neurons in enriched adult rats); **granule** (the dentate gyrus cells that adult-born hippocampal neurons become); and **tinnitus** (reorganises primary auditory cortex).

## 9.5 Atypical Neurodevelopment: Autism Spectrum Disorder and Williams Syndrome

Like any complex process, neurodevelopment is easily thrown off track, and because each stage builds on earlier ones, **one tiny misstep can have far-reaching consequences**. Ironically, much of what we know about typical development has come from studying **developmental disorders**, as the case of Genie already showed. This module covers two: **autism spectrum disorder** and **Williams syndrome**. They are informative together because they are **similar in some respects and opposite in others**.

### Autism spectrum disorder: definition and core symptoms

**Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** is a complex **neurodevelopmental disorder**, difficult to define because cases differ so greatly. It is **almost always apparent before age 3** and **typically does not increase in severity after that age**. Two symptoms are **core symptoms**, because they are **required for diagnosis** (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Volkmar & McPartland, 2014):

1. **A reduced capacity for social interaction and communication** (e.g., social avoidance, lack of interest in social interactions); and
2. **Restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities.**

Other characteristics tend to be associated with the disorder:

- About **66 to 75 percent** of those with ASD are **male** (see Lai et al., 2017; Tordjman et al., 2014).
- Many have an **intellectual or learning disability**.
- They are more likely to have **epilepsy** (see Lee, Smith, & Paciorkowski, 2015).
- **Older mothers** are more likely to have a child with ASD, and the probability for a **young mother (under 30)** increases if the **father is over 40** (Shelton, Tancredi, & Hertz-Picciotto, 2010).

### ASD is heterogeneous

ASD is heterogeneous in **two senses**:

1. **Within an individual**: a person may be severely impaired in some respects but typical, or even superior, in others. Individuals with ASD and an accompanying intellectual disability often perform well on tests of **rote memory, jigsaw puzzles, music and art**.
2. **Between individuals**: the **amount of impairment** differs substantially; some have many impairments, others few.

To show the range, the book presents two cases from opposite ends of the spectrum, noting that **most individuals lie somewhere between them**.

### The cases of Alex and S.D.

**Alex** has relatively severe ASD. As a baby he cried so hard he would sometimes vomit, the first sign of his condition. Now 7, he spends much of each day scampering around the house. When he sees the delivery boy he yells "Are you ready to rock?" because the delivery boy once said that to him in passing. He is **obsessed** with spaghetti, chocolate ice cream and **trucks**; he can spot trucks in a magazine so small that most people would miss them, and he keeps his toy trucks **lined up** in the main hallway. Despite **severe intellectual disability** he has little difficulty with **computers**, and recently bought a new toy truck online. He has **echolalia**, repeating almost everything he hears: he recently told his mother he loves her, but she doubts he understands what it means. He loves music and knows the words to many popular songs.

Map Alex onto the definitions: the repetitive, restricted interests (trucks, lined up), the communication deficit disguised as speech (echolalia), and the islands of preserved ability (computers, music) that illustrate within-individual heterogeneity.

**S.D.**, the self-advocate, wrote their own account (used with the permission of Emma Smith). Diagnosed relatively late, at **12**, S.D. had struggled to relate to peers while **excelling academically**, and describes constant **introspection** as a way of living among people with a fundamentally different nervous system. S.D. now gives talks about ASD in educational settings; audiences are often surprised by the diagnosis because S.D.'s challenges (**executive function and self-care**) are invisible while their public-speaking talent is not. University suits S.D. (clear structure, less social pressure, like-minded peers), and ASD and self-advocacy have given them a sense of **community**.

The pairing makes a clinical point: the same diagnosis covers a non-independent 7-year-old with echolalia and a university student who gives public talks.

### Early warning signs, prevalence and treatment

ASD is common enough that everybody should know its **main early warning sign**: **a delay in the development of social interaction**. Specific signs that could be a cause for concern:

- a **decline in eye contact from 2 months** onwards (see Shultz, Klin, & Jones, 2018);
- **no smiles or happy expressions by 9 months**; and
- **no communicative gestures such as pointing or waving by 12 months** (see Constantino et al., 2017; Jones & Klin, 2014; Yoon & Vouloumanos, 2014).

ASD is the **most prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder**. Until the early 1990s, most **epidemiological studies** (studies of the incidence and distribution of disease in the general population) reported that ASD occurred in **fewer than 1 in 1,000** births in the United States; the **Centers for Disease Control and Prevention now estimate 1 in 68** (see Biyani et al., 2015). The increase is a cause for concern, although it may partly reflect **broadened diagnostic criteria, increased public awareness, and improved identification methods** (see Kim & Leventhal, 2015). Be ready to give those three alternative explanations if asked to evaluate the "epidemic".

More severe forms, like Alex's, are **difficult to treat** (see Walsh et al., 2011). **Intensive behavioural therapy** can improve the lives of some individuals (see Lange & McDougle, 2013), but it is still often difficult for them to live independently.

### ASD savants

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of ASD is that some people with it are **savants**: persons with **general intellectual disabilities** who nevertheless display **amazing and specific cognitive or artistic abilities** (see Treffert, 2014a). Savant abilities take many forms: feats of memory, naming the day of the week for any past or future date, identifying prime numbers, drawing, and playing musical instruments (see Bonnel et al., 2003). The book's cases (Ramachandran & Blakeslee, 1998; Sacks, 1985):

- A man who could tell the **time of day to the exact second** without a watch, and would mumble the correct time even when asleep.
- **Tom**, who was **blind** and could not tie his shoes, had no musical training, yet could play the most difficult piano piece **after hearing it once**, even with his back to the piano.
- A pair of **twins** with ASD who struggled with simple addition and subtraction and could not comprehend multiplication or division, yet could name the **day of the week for any date in the last or next 40,000 years**.
- **Stephen Wiltshire**, the "human camera", who can draw a detailed city skyline, down to the correct number of windows on particular buildings, after a **brief helicopter view**.

Savant abilities **remain a mystery**. They **do not appear to develop through learning or practice**; they seem to **emerge spontaneously**. Even savants with good language **cannot explain their own feats**; they seem to recognise patterns and relationships that escape others. Several investigators have **speculated** that atypical development of some brain areas has led to **compensatory responses** in others. Supporting that idea, savant-like abilities can emerge in otherwise healthy people after **damage to the left anterior temporal lobe**, or after **transient inactivation of the same structure by transcranial magnetic stimulation** (see Treffert, 2014b).

### Genetic mechanisms of ASD

**Genetic and epigenetic factors** influence ASD (see Bagni & Zukin, 2019; Iakoucheva, Muotri, & Sebat, 2019; Quesnel-Vallières et al., 2019; Tremblay & Jiang, 2019). The reasoning from the numbers is worth following:

- **Siblings** of people with ASD have about a **20 percent** chance of a diagnosis (see Gliga et al., 2014). That is well **above** the general-population rate, so there is a genetic contribution, but well **below the 50 percent** expected if ASD were caused by a **single dominant gene**.
- If one **monozygotic twin** is diagnosed, the other has a **60 percent** chance. Identical twins share all their genes; if genes alone determined ASD, the figure would be near 100 percent.

Together these findings suggest that ASD is triggered by **several genes interacting with the environment** (see State & Levitt, 2011). **Several dozen genes** have been implicated, but only in about **1 to 5 percent** of cases can ASD be attributed to a **single gene mutation** (see Mullins, Fishell, & Tsien, 2016; Sztainberg & Zoghbi, 2016; Takumi & Tamada, 2018).

Much better predictors are **transcription-related errors**, errors in the creation of RNA molecules from DNA. They are found in the brain cells of a **majority** of individuals with ASD (see Quesnel-Vallières et al., 2019), and their **number is positively correlated with symptom severity** (Wu et al., 2016).

### Neural mechanisms of ASD

The **heterogeneity** of ASD (deficits in some functions but not others) suggests alterations to some neural structures but not others, and the differences between individuals suggest that the neural correlates vary as much as the symptoms (see Robertson & Baron-Cohen, 2017). Large, systematic studies will therefore be needed.

One line of research has focused on **faces**. Individuals with ASD spend **less time looking at faces, especially the eyes**, and **remember faces less well** (see Rigby, Stoesz, & Jakobson, 2018; but see Guillon et al., 2014). The **fusiform face area** of **adolescents** with ASD, **but not adults** with ASD, shows **less fMRI activity than typical** in response to faces (see Nomi & Uddin, 2015; Lynn et al., 2018). Reason through the age difference: if the face area were permanently abnormal, adults would show it too. Its normalisation by adulthood suggests a **delay in the development of face-processing circuitry** rather than a permanent absence (see Lynn et al., 2018).

The mechanisms may **not be entirely neural**. There is substantial interest in **glial** contributions (see Welberg, 2014c; Zeidán-Chuliá et al., 2014), in particular evidence of **decreased synaptic pruning by glial cells** during development, leading to **larger than typical cerebral volumes in the first year of life** (see Neniskyte & Gross, 2017). Connect this back to 9.1 and 9.2: synapse elimination is a normal part of development, glia participate in it, and too little pruning leaves too much brain.

### Williams syndrome: definition and contrasts with ASD

**Williams syndrome**, like ASD, is a **neurodevelopmental disorder associated with intellectual disability** and a **heterogeneous pattern of abilities and disabilities** (see Van Herwegen, 2015). But there are two sharp contrasts:

- **All** individuals with Williams syndrome have intellectual disability, whereas that is **not necessarily** so in ASD.
- Whereas people with ASD often have difficulty with social interaction, people with Williams syndrome are **sociable, empathetic and talkative**.

In many respects ASD and Williams syndrome are **opposites**, which is one reason they can be studied together fruitfully (see Barak & Feng, 2016). (Journal Prompt 9.4: comparing a disorder that impairs a function with one that spares it helps isolate that function's mechanisms.) Williams syndrome occurs in about **1 in 7,500 births** (see Martens, 2013; Van Herwegen, 2015).

### The case of Anne Louise McGarrah

**Anne Louise McGarrah**, 42, has Williams syndrome (Finn, 1991). She **cannot add 13 and 18**, yet she is an enthusiastic **reader** of biographies, fiction and novels. She has difficulty **telling left from right**, and if asked to fetch several items she often returns with only one. But she **plays the piano** and loves classical music, preferring Mozart, Chopin and Bach for being "very light, very airy, and very cheerful". She is **aware of her condition**: she describes being stared at in a shop and wanting to ask the woman whether she understood that Anne Louise has a disability. The case is a compact map of the syndrome: weak arithmetic and spatial ability, strong verbal and musical ability, social awareness.

### Strengths: language, music and face recognition

It is the **language** abilities of people with Williams syndrome that have attracted the most attention. They show both a **delay in language development** and **language deficits in adulthood** (see Niego & Benítez-Burraco, 2019), but their language is **remarkable given their low IQs, which average around 55**. Asked to name as many animals as possible in **60 seconds**, children with Williams syndrome produced answers such as koala, yak, ibex, condor, Chihuahua, brontosaurus and hippopotamus. Asked to tell a story about a picture, they often produced an **animated narrative**, altering pitch, volume, rhythm and vocabulary to engage the audience. Sadly, their verbal and social skills often lead teachers to **overestimate their cognitive abilities**, so they do not always get the academic support they need. That is a point a clinical examiner may value.

They are often **musically gifted** (Niego & Benítez-Burraco, 2019). Most **cannot learn to read music**, but some have **perfect or near-perfect pitch** and an uncanny sense of rhythm; many retain melodies for years; some are professional musicians. As a group they show **more interest in, and emotional reaction to, music** than the general population (see Järvinen et al., 2015). A further strength is **near-typical ability to recognise faces**.

### Deficits: attention and spatial cognition

People with Williams syndrome have **severe attentional problems** (see Lense, Key, & Dykens, 2011). And their **spatial abilities are even worse than those of people with comparable IQs**: they have difficulty remembering the locations of a few blocks placed on a test board, their **space-related speech is poor**, and their **ability to draw objects is almost nonexistent** (see Nagai, Inui, & Iwata, 2011; Rhodes et al., 2011). The comparison "worse than people with comparable IQs" is what makes this a *specific* deficit rather than a consequence of general intellectual disability.

### Face processing and hypersociability

Faces are where the contrast with ASD is clearest. People with Williams syndrome show an **excessive interest in human faces**, in marked contrast to ASD. They also have **difficulty perceiving negative emotion in faces** (for example, identifying **angry** faces), and they **rate unfamiliar faces as more friendly** than people without the syndrome do. Taken together, these **face-processing biases may contribute to the hypersociability** typical of Williams syndrome (see Barak & Feng, 2016). Reason it through: someone drawn to faces, who fails to read anger and who sees strangers as friendly, will approach strangers readily.

> **Trap.** Williams syndrome: **excessive** interest in faces, **near-typical** face recognition, but **poor** recognition of **negative** emotion and over-friendly ratings of strangers. ASD: **less** looking at faces and eyes, poorer face memory. Do not attribute "poor face recognition" to Williams syndrome.

### Genetics: chromosome 7 and the elastin gene

Williams syndrome is associated with several health problems, including **heart** problems. One heart disorder had been traced to a mutation in a gene on **chromosome 7** controlling the synthesis of **elastin**, a protein that gives elasticity to many organs and tissues, including the heart (see Cunniff et al., 2001). Knowing that the same cardiac problem is common in Williams syndrome, investigators checked this gene in people with the syndrome, which is a neat example of reasoning from a physical symptom to a genetic cause. They found the gene was **absent from one of the two copies of chromosome 7 in 95 percent** of individuals with Williams syndrome (see Howald et al., 2006). It was missing because an accident of reproduction had **deleted an entire segment of chromosome 7**, including the elastin gene and **about 26 others** (see Chailangkarn et al., 2016; Järvinen, Korenberg, & Bellugi, 2013).

And here is the striking link back to ASD: individuals with **extra copies of the same segment** of chromosome 7 display **symptoms of ASD**. Deleting the segment produces hypersociability; duplicating it produces social difficulty. That is the opposite-disorders theme at the level of the genome.

> **Trap.** Williams syndrome = **deletion** on **chromosome 7** (elastin plus about 26 other genes; one copy missing in **95 percent**). **Duplication** of the same segment is associated with **ASD** symptoms. Do not confuse with Prader-Willi (chromosome 15, Chapter 12).

### Cortical differences in Williams syndrome

Several brain differences have been reported (e.g., **decreased basal ganglia volume**; see Dennis & Thompson, 2013), but most research has focused on the **cortex**. Williams syndrome is associated with a **general thinning of the cortex and underlying white matter** (see Meyer-Lindenberg et al., 2006; Niego & Benítez-Burraco, 2019; Toga & Thompson, 2005). The thinning is **greatest in two areas** (Figure 9.13):

| Area | Status | Possibly related to |
|---|---|---|
| **Boundary of the parietal and occipital lobes** | Reduced | **Profound impairment of spatial cognition** |
| **Orbitofrontal cortex** (the inferior frontal cortex near the orbits, or eye sockets) | Reduced | **Remarkable hypersociability** |
| **Superior temporal gyrus** (includes primary and secondary auditory cortex) | Often **typical** thickness | Relatively high levels of **language and music** processing (Niego & Benítez-Burraco, 2019) |

The table pairs the regions and symptoms "respectively", as the book does: parietal-occipital with spatial, orbitofrontal with social. The logic is a structure-function mapping: the thinned regions match the deficits, and the spared region matches the strengths.

> **Trap.** Figure 9.13's caption calls the superior temporal gyrus an area of **typical** cortical volume, while its colour key labels it "increased". The text says its thickness is **often typical**. In an exam, describe it as **spared (typical)**, and remember that it is the region linked to the **strengths** (language and music), whereas the parietal-occipital boundary and orbitofrontal cortex are **reduced**.

### Elves, folktales and the epilogue

Many cultures tell of magical little people (pixies, elves, leprechauns) described much like people with Williams syndrome: short, with small upturned noses, oval ears, broad mouths, full lips, puffy eyes and small chins, and behaviourally engaging storytellers, talented musicians, loving, trusting and sensitive to others. The authors **suggest** that such folktales may originally have been based on people with Williams syndrome; treat this as speculation, not a finding.

The epilogue returns to the chapter's opening theme. SB recalls being amazed, as a student, that a brain could construct itself from one cell through the **interaction of genetic programmes and experience**, and later astounded that the adult brain is **still plastic**. The cases of Genie at the start of the chapter and of ASD and Williams syndrome at the end show what can happen when that complex process is disturbed.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all five modules, and the book's "Themes Revisited" names most of them.

1. **Genes and experience always interact.** Cells read different DNA depending on their surroundings (epigenetics); visual cortex organises itself according to the input it gets; adult neurogenesis tracks exploration. Neurodevelopment never proceeds from genes or environment alone, and even "normal" experience fine-tunes normal function.
2. **Overproduce, then select.** About 50 percent too many neurons, synapse density of 150 percent of adult levels, promiscuous synapse formation in vitro: development builds in excess and then prunes by competition for neurotrophins, by apoptosis, and by elimination of non-functional synapses.
3. **Competition shapes circuits.** Neurons compete for target-derived survival factors; the eyes compete for ocular dominance columns; inputs compete for cortical map territory. The active or better-connected input wins.
4. **Timing matters, but windows rarely close completely.** Most effects of experience operate in sensitive, not critical, periods; monocular deprivation still works in adults, only more slowly.
5. **Plasticity continues into adulthood.** New neurons in the adult hippocampus, cortical maps that reorganise with tinnitus, violin playing or anaesthesia, and a brain that adapts faster the second time (Hofer et al., 2005).
6. **Disorders illuminate typical development.** Genie, ASD and Williams syndrome (the clinical implications theme), with ASD and Williams syndrome as near-opposites, down to deletion versus duplication of the same piece of chromosome 7.

## Worked example

*A paediatrician refers a 3-year-old girl. She is unusually friendly with strangers, talks fluently and expressively, and loves music, yet she cannot copy a simple drawing and her non-verbal IQ is in the intellectual-disability range. She also has a heart murmur. Her parents ask whether this is autism, whether early intervention can help, and whether her brain is "fixed" now.*

**Autism or something else?** The profile is the reverse of ASD's core symptoms: she is **hypersocial**, not socially avoidant, and her communication is a strength. The chapter's picture of **Williams syndrome** fits: universal intellectual disability, relatively strong language and music, excessive interest in people, and **spatial cognition worse than IQ alone predicts** (the drawing failure). The heart murmur is the decisive clue, because cardiac problems in Williams syndrome are linked to the **elastin gene on chromosome 7**, missing from one copy in **95 percent** of cases. A genetic test for the chromosome 7 deletion is the logical next step.

**What should the parents watch for?** Her verbal fluency may lead teachers to **overestimate** her ability, so she may miss support she needs. Her friendliness, together with difficulty reading **angry** faces and a tendency to rate strangers as friendly, carries obvious safety implications.

**Can experience help, and is her brain fixed?** Module 9.3 says most developmental effects of experience operate in **sensitive** periods, so early intervention is likely to have its greatest effect now, but sensitive periods do not slam shut. Module 9.4 adds that the adult brain remains plastic: cortical maps reorganise with practice (Elbert et al., 1995), and enrichment and exercise increase hippocampal neurogenesis in animals. Her brain is not "fixed", though the chapter supports measured optimism rather than a promise of a typical outcome.

## MCQ traps

1. **Potency order:** totipotent (any cell) → pluripotent (many, not all) → multipotent (one class, e.g., blood cells) → unipotent (one type, e.g., bipolar neurons). Only the first three are called embryonic stem cells.
2. **Asymmetric cell division** means daughters with different **characteristics** (one stays a stem cell), not different sizes. It underlies self-renewal.
3. **The neural plate is ectoderm**, induced by the **mesoderm** organizer. Figure 9.2's caption saying "endoderm" is a misprint.
4. **Two organizer ideas:** the mesoderm induces the neural plate; the **floor plate (ventral)** and **roof plate (dorsal)** help control proliferation.
5. **Proliferation** happens mainly in the **ventricular and subventricular zones**, at more than **4 million cells per hour**; synapses form at about **700,000 per second**.
6. **Radial glial cells are the stem cells** of the neural tube, and also guide migration.
7. **Somal translocation: radial or tangential. Radial-glia-mediated migration: radial only.**
8. **Neural crest → PNS neurons and glia**; neural tube → CNS.
9. **Sperry's rotated-eye frogs** struck 180 degrees wrong **after regeneration**, as did rotated frogs whose nerves were never cut. Axons returned to their **original** tectal targets, supporting **chemoaffinity**.
10. **Chemoaffinity fails** to explain axons that take the **same circuitous route** in every member of a species; the revision is a **series of attractive and repulsive signals along the route**.
11. **Pioneer growth cones** follow guidance molecules; **fasciculation** is later axons following earlier ones.
12. **Half retina destroyed → remaining half covers the whole tectum; half tectum destroyed → whole retina compresses onto the remaining half.** This supports the **topographic gradient hypothesis** (two intersecting gradients), not point-to-point chemoaffinity.
13. **Astrocytes:** seven times more synapses in culture; synapses lost when astrocytes removed. Their role goes beyond supplying cholesterol.
14. **Apoptosis = active, packaged, cleared by microglia, safe. Necrosis = passive, spills, inflammation.** Blocked apoptosis → cancer; inappropriate apoptosis → neurodegeneration.
15. **Extra limb grafted → less neuron death; some incoming neurons destroyed → better survival of the rest.** Both point to target-derived survival factors.
16. **NGF was the first neurotrophin (Levi-Montalcini), BDNF the second.** Neurotrophins also guide axons and stimulate synaptogenesis.
17. **Postnatal brain growth is not due to new neurons**, but to synaptogenesis, myelination and dendritic branching. Volume doubles from birth to adulthood.
18. **Visual/auditory cortex:** burst at 4 months, peak of 150 percent of adult density at 7 to 8 months. **Prefrontal:** steady synaptogenesis, peak in the second year; myelination continues into adulthood.
19. **Perseverative error, 7 to 12 months (Diamond, 1985); prefrontal-lag hypothesis (Diamond, 1991).** The task is Piaget's.
20. **Critical period = essential; sensitive period = strongest within but still weak effects outside.** Most effects are sensitive-period effects; monocular deprivation in adulthood is the book's example.
21. **Blindfolding both eyes does not produce the monocular-deprivation deficit.** The effect is competitive.
22. **Antonini & Stryker (1993):** a few days of deprivation massively reduced branching of LGN axons in lower layer IV. Rapid effects can be structural.
23. **Roe et al. (1990):** retinal axons to the **medial** geniculate made **auditory cortex retinotopic**; rewired cortex takes on many, but not all, features.
24. **Adult human neurogenesis:** hippocampus (about 700 per day per hippocampus, contested by Sorrells et al., 2018) and striatum, **not** the subventricular zone.
25. **Freund et al. (2013):** in genetically identical mice, **exploration** predicted neurogenesis even more than physical activity.
26. **Hofer et al. (2005):** prior deprivation makes later adult column shrinkage **faster and more enduring**.
27. **ASD numbers:** onset before 3; 66 to 75 percent male; siblings about 20 percent; MZ twins 60 percent; single-gene cause in only 1 to 5 percent; CDC estimate 1 in 68.
28. **ASD faces:** reduced fusiform face area activity in **adolescents, not adults**, read as developmental delay. Glia: **decreased** pruning, **larger** first-year brain volume.
29. **Savant-like abilities** follow damage to, or TMS of, the **left anterior temporal lobe**.
30. **Williams syndrome:** always intellectual disability (mean IQ about 55), 1 in 7,500, hypersociable, chromosome 7 **deletion** including elastin (95 percent); duplication is linked to ASD symptoms. Spatial ability is worse than IQ predicts.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Zygote** | Single cell formed by the fusion of ovum and sperm |
| **Totipotent** | Able to develop into any class of body cell (the fertilised egg) |
| **Pluripotent** | Able to develop into many, but not all, classes of body cell |
| **Multipotent** | Able to develop into different cells of only one class (e.g., blood cells) |
| **Unipotent** | Able to develop into only one type of cell (e.g., bipolar neurons) |
| **Stem cells** | Cells with near-unlimited self-renewal and ability to become many cell types |
| **Asymmetric cell division** | Division producing daughters with different characteristics: one stem cell, one more specific cell |
| **Epigenetic mechanisms** | Mechanisms by which cells transcribe different DNA sections depending on experience |
| **Brain organoid** | Miniature 3-D brain grown in culture from stem cells |
| **Ectoderm / mesoderm / endoderm** | Outer / middle / inner embryonic cell layers |
| **Neural plate** | Patch of dorsal ectoderm, visible at 3 weeks, that becomes the nervous system |
| **Organizer** | Tissue whose chemical signals induce development (e.g., mesoderm inducing the neural plate) |
| **Neural groove / neural tube** | Folded neural plate / tube formed when the groove's lips fuse |
| **Neural tube defects** | Severe CNS birth defects from errors in neural tube folding |
| **Neural proliferation** | Great increase in the number of neural tube cells |
| **Ventricular / subventricular zone** | Regions next to the ventricle where most cell division occurs |
| **Floor plate / roof plate** | Ventral / dorsal midline organizer areas of the neural tube |
| **Radial glial cells** | Stem cells of the neural tube, with a long process to its outer surface; guide migration |
| **Migration** | Movement of immature cells to their target locations |
| **Radial / tangential migration** | Straight outward from the ventricular zone / parallel to the tube walls |
| **Somal translocation** | A process explores, then the cell body moves along it; radial or tangential |
| **Radial-glia-mediated migration** | Cell climbs a radial glial process; radial only |
| **Inside-out pattern** | Cortical layers form deep first, with later cells migrating past earlier ones |
| **Neural crest** | Cells breaking off just dorsal to the neural tube; form PNS neurons and glia |
| **Aggregation** | Alignment of migrated neurons into structures |
| **Cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs)** | Surface molecules that recognise and adhere to molecules on other cells |
| **Growth cone / filopodia** | Amoeba-like growing tip of an axon or dendrite / its finger-like extensions |
| **Retinal ganglion cells** | Retinal neurons whose axons form the optic nerve |
| **Optic tectum** | Amphibian visual target of retinal axons; superior colliculus in mammals |
| **Chemoaffinity hypothesis** | Sperry: each target releases a specific chemical label that attracts its axon |
| **Pioneer growth cones** | First growth cones along a route; follow guidance molecules |
| **Fasciculation** | Tendency of axons to grow along paths of preceding axons |
| **Topographic gradient hypothesis** | Axons mapped by two intersecting signal gradients onto a target surface |
| **Synaptogenesis** | Formation of new synapses |
| **Necrosis** | Passive cell death; contents spill, causing inflammation |
| **Apoptosis** | Active, programmed cell death; contents packaged and removed by microglia |
| **Neurotrophins** | Target-supplied chemicals promoting neuron survival, growth, guidance and synaptogenesis |
| **Nerve growth factor (NGF)** | First neurotrophin isolated (Levi-Montalcini) |
| **Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)** | Second neurotrophin identified |
| **Synapse rearrangement** | Survivors take over vacated sites; output focused on fewer cells |
| **Prenatal / postnatal period** | Development before / after birth |
| **Functional connectivity** | Correlated activity between brain regions over time |
| **Transcriptome** | Catalogue of all proteins transcribed in a particular cell |
| **Proteome** | Catalogue of the proteins in different cell types |
| **Myelination** | Formation of myelin, increasing conduction speed; prefrontal continues into adulthood |
| **Dendritic spines** | Small dendritic protuberances, many forming synapses; reshape rapidly |
| **Prefrontal cortex** | Last brain region to mature; working memory, planning, inhibition, social rules |
| **Perseveration** | Continuing a formerly correct response when it is now incorrect |
| **Critical period** | Interval in which an experience must occur to influence development |
| **Sensitive period** | Interval of strong effect, with weaker effects still possible outside it |
| **Ocular dominance columns** | Layer IV columns of input from one eye or the other |
| **Monocular deprivation** | Depriving one eye of input; competitively shrinks its columns |
| **Neurogenesis** | Growth of new neurons |
| **Dentate gyrus** | Hippocampal region where adult-born granule cells appear |
| **Pattern separation** | Separating distinct percepts into individual memories for storage |
| **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** | Neurodevelopmental disorder: reduced social interaction/communication plus restricted, repetitive behaviour |
| **Epidemiological studies** | Studies of incidence and distribution of disease in a population |
| **Savants** | People with general intellectual disability and specific amazing abilities |
| **Transcription-related errors** | Errors in making RNA from DNA; predict ASD and its severity |
| **Fusiform face area** | Face-responsive cortex; underactive in adolescents with ASD |
| **Williams syndrome** | Chromosome 7 deletion; intellectual disability, hypersociability, language and music strengths, spatial deficits |
| **Elastin** | Protein giving tissues elasticity; its gene lies in the deleted chromosome 7 segment |
| **Orbitofrontal cortex** | Inferior frontal cortex near the orbits; thinned in Williams syndrome |
| **Superior temporal gyrus** | Includes primary and secondary auditory cortex; typical thickness in Williams syndrome |
