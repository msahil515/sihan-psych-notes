# Chapter 13: Hormones and Sex

**What this chapter is really about.** Most people arrive at this topic with a tidy picture already in their heads: there are two sexes, each has its own "sex hormones", the male hormones build male bodies and make men do male things, the female hormones build female bodies and make women do female things, and the two are mirror images of each other. Pinel and Barnes give this picture a deliberately silly name, the **mamawawa** (the men-are-men-and-women-are-women assumption), and then spend the whole chapter taking it apart. Almost every finding in the chapter is chosen because it embarrasses the mamawawa: the testes and ovaries release *the same* hormones; an "androgen" grows pubic hair in women; the brain of a male rat is masculinised by *oestradiol*; a woman can have testes and XY chromosomes; masculinisation and defeminisation are separate processes; and sexual orientation, gender identity and body type can each vary independently of the other two.

Underneath the polemic there is a precise piece of biology to learn. Hormones act on sex in two ways: they **organise** the body and brain during development, and they **activate** sexual behaviour in adults. The developmental story has a clear rule for the body (a female programme runs unless testicular hormones override it at the right time), and the chapter tests that rule against exceptional human cases, then shows that the same simple rule does *not* hold for the brain as a whole.

**How to read this chapter.** The 11th edition prints seven modules without numbers; they are numbered 13.1 to 13.7 here in the book's order. 13.1 is the hormonal machinery (glands, hormone classes, the pituitary and its control by the hypothalamus, feedback and pulsatile release); every later module depends on it, especially the hypothalamus to anterior pituitary to gonad chain. 13.2 is how the body becomes female or male: gonads, internal ducts, external genitals, puberty. 13.3 moves to the brain and behaviour, from Pfeiffer's 1936 rats through the aromatisation hypothesis to the modern view that brain sex differences are mosaic. 13.4 tests everything against three exceptional human cases. 13.5 is about adults: castration, testosterone replacement, the oestrous cycle and anabolic steroids. 13.6 covers the four brain structures linked to sexual activity. 13.7 ends with sexual orientation and gender identity. As you go, keep asking one question: does this finding fit the idea that female and male are opposite, mutually exclusive categories? The answer is almost always no, and seeing *why* is what the examiners are testing.

## Opening: What's Wrong with the Mamawawa?

### The men-are-men-and-women-are-women assumption

The chapter opens not with a patient but with a habit of thought. The **men-are-men-and-women-are-women assumption** is the tendency to treat femaleness and maleness as discrete, mutually exclusive, opposite categories. Applied to hormones, it produces a neat but false symmetry: females have female hormones that give them female bodies and female behaviour; males have male hormones that do the same on the other side.

Why does such a flawed idea survive? The authors point to three attractions: it is simple, it is symmetrical, and it has comfortable social implications (Carothers & Reis, 2013; Jordan-Young & Rumiati, 2012). The evidence, however, does not support it, and the chapter is organised so that you meet that evidence again and again. Treat "mamawawa" as a technical term: it appears in exam questions, and it is the lens for reading every module.

> **Trap.** "Mamawawa" is not a theory anybody proposes; it is the authors' label for an *assumption* they reject. A question asking which view Pinel and Barnes *support* will never have mamawawa as the right answer. The view they support is that maleness and femaleness are multidimensional and often ambiguous variations of each other, not opposites.

### Developmental and activational effects of sex hormones

Before any particular hormone, one distinction that the rest of the chapter is built on. Sex hormones influence sex in two fundamentally different ways (Bale & Epperson, 2015):

1. **Developmental (also called organisational) effects.** Hormones steer the development, from conception to sexual maturity, of the anatomical, physiological and behavioural characteristics that distinguish one as female or male. These effects are typically permanent; they set up the structure.
2. **Activational effects.** Hormones switch on the reproduction-related behaviour of sexually mature adults (Wu & Shah, 2011). These effects depend on the hormone being present now; remove it and the behaviour fades.

The book discusses the two in different modules (13.2 and 13.3 are developmental; 13.5 is activational), but warns that the separation is cleaner on paper than in life. Because the brain continues to develop into the late teens, the hormone surges of adolescence can have **both** effects at once (Bell, 2018): they activate adult-type behaviour and simultaneously reshape a brain that is still being built.

> **Trap.** "Organisational" and "developmental" are synonyms in this chapter. If an option says organisational effects occur only before birth, it is wrong by the book's own example: adolescent surges can be organisational too.

## 13.1 Neuroendocrine System

This module sets out the general principles of neuroendocrine function, using the glands and hormones that matter for sex as the worked case. By convention, only organs whose **primary** function appears to be hormone release are called endocrine glands. Strictly, though, the endocrine system is larger than that list: the stomach, liver and intestine, and body fat itself, also release hormones into the general circulation (Chapter 12's leptin and the gut peptides are examples).

### Exocrine and endocrine glands

There are two kinds of gland, distinguished by where they send their product.

- **Exocrine glands** (sweat glands are the book's example) pass their secretions into **ducts**, and the ducts deliver them to targets that lie mostly on the body surface.
- **Endocrine glands** are **ductless**. They release their chemicals, called **hormones**, directly into the circulatory system, and a hormone then travels in the blood until it reaches the targets on which it normally acts (other endocrine glands, sites in the nervous system, and so on).

The endocrine glands shown in the book's map of the body (Figure 13.1) are the **pineal, hypothalamus, pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, adrenal, pancreas, ovary and testis**. Notice that the hypothalamus is on the list: it is a brain structure, but it also releases hormones, and that dual identity is what lets the brain run the endocrine system.

> **Trap.** "Ductless" means *endocrine*, not exocrine. Students sometimes reverse it because "exo" sounds like "without". The exocrine gland is the one *with* a duct carrying its secretion out to a surface.

### The gonads, the zygote and the sex chromosomes

The **gonads** are the male **testes** and the female **ovaries**, and they are central to everything in this chapter. Their primary function, as Chapter 2 explained, is to produce gametes: sperm cells in the testes, ova in the ovaries. After **copulation** (sexual intercourse), a single sperm may fertilise an ovum, producing one cell, the **zygote**, which carries all the information needed for the typical growth of a complete adult organism in its natural environment.

The chromosome arithmetic is worth stating exactly. Every human body cell except the gametes has **23 pairs** of chromosomes. Ova and sperm carry only half that number, **one member of each pair**. Fertilisation therefore restores the full 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.

One pair matters most here: the **sex chromosomes**, named because they carry the genetic programmes that direct sexual development.

- Female cells have two large sex chromosomes, both **X chromosomes**.
- Male cells have one **X** and one **Y chromosome**.

It follows that **every ovum carries an X**, while **half of all sperm carry an X and half a Y**. Which sperm reaches the ovum first decides the genetic sex of the zygote: an X-bearing sperm produces a genetic female (XX), a Y-bearing sperm a genetic male (XY). The father's gamete, not the mother's, is the deciding one.

A small but testable aside: chromosomes are not shaped like their letters. After a chromosome duplicates, its two copies stay joined at one point, which gives *every* duplicated chromosome, the Y included, an X-like shape. The Y got its name because it is much smaller than the X, and early observers failed to see one of its small arms. The size difference is reflected in gene content: in humans the Y-chromosome genes appear to control the synthesis of only about **66 proteins** (Rengaraj, Kwon, & Pang, 2015), compared with about **615** for the X (Yamamoto et al., 2013).

> **Trap.** Sex is determined by the **sperm** (X or Y), never by the ovum, which always contributes an X. And the "Y shape" is an artefact of early microscopy, not the chromosome's real structure.

### Three classes of hormones

All vertebrate hormones fall into one of three chemical classes:

| Class | How it is made | Example from the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Amino acid derivative hormones** | Synthesised in a few simple steps from a single amino acid molecule | **Epinephrine** (adrenaline), made from **tyrosine** and released by the **adrenal medulla** |
| **Peptide hormones** and **protein hormones** | Chains of amino acids: **short** chains are peptides, **long** chains are proteins | Oxytocin, vasopressin, all the hypothalamic releasing hormones, all tropic hormones |
| **Steroid hormones** | Synthesised from **cholesterol**, a type of fat molecule (Abruzzese et al., 2018) | Testosterone, oestradiol, progesterone, cortisol |

Peptide versus protein is purely a matter of chain length, which is why the book groups them together.

### Why steroid hormones are special

Here is the fact that makes steroids the stars of this chapter: **all the sex hormones, the hormones that influence sexual development and activate adult sexual behaviour, are steroids.**

Most hormones act by binding to receptors in the **cell membrane** and triggering changes from outside the cell. Steroids can do that too, but they have a second route that other hormones mostly lack. Because steroid molecules are **small and fat-soluble**, they pass easily through the lipid cell membrane. Once inside, they can bind to receptors in the **cytoplasm or nucleus** and in doing so **directly influence gene expression**. (Amino acid derivatives and peptides affect gene expression less commonly and only by less direct mechanisms.) A hormone that can switch genes on and off is a hormone that can change what a cell *is*, not just what it is doing at the moment, which is why steroid hormones tend to have the **most diverse and long-lasting effects** on cellular function of all the hormone classes. That property is exactly what the organisational effects of 13.2 and 13.3 require.

### Sex steroids: androgens, oestrogens and progestins

The gonads do more than make gametes; they also produce and release steroid hormones. The surprise most students meet here is that **the testes and the ovaries release the very same hormones**. What differs is the proportion, not the menu.

The two main classes of gonadal hormone are:

- **Androgens**, of which **testosterone** is the most common.
- **Oestrogens** (spelt *estrogens* in the book), of which **oestradiol** (*estradiol*) is the most common.

Adult ovaries tend to release more oestrogens than androgens, and adult testes more androgens than oestrogens. That difference in balance is the origin of the common habit of calling androgens "the male sex hormones" and oestrogens "the female sex hormones". The authors want you to drop that habit, because it smuggles in the mamawawa: it implies that androgens *produce* maleness and oestrogens *produce* femaleness, and the evidence throughout the chapter shows they don't.

The gonads also release a third class of steroid, the **progestins**. The most common is **progesterone**, which in females prepares the uterus and breasts for pregnancy. Whether it does anything in males is unclear, but in both sexes it seems to be involved in various forms of neuroplasticity (González-Orozco & Camacho-Arroyo, 2019).

> **Trap.** "Testosterone is the male hormone, oestradiol the female hormone" is exactly the statement the book tells you to avoid. Both sexes make both; both gonads release androgens, oestrogens *and* progestins. Expect at least one option built on the "male hormone / female hormone" phrasing.

### The adrenal cortex as a minor sex gland

The **adrenal cortex** is the outer layer of each adrenal gland. Its primary function is regulating blood levels of **glucose and salt**, so it is not usually thought of as a sex gland. However, besides its principal steroid hormones it releases **small amounts of all the sex steroids** that the gonads release. Hold on to this; it is the key to three later puzzles: why women grow pubic and axillary hair (13.2), what goes wrong in adrenogenital syndrome (13.4), and why some castrated men may remain sexually active (13.5).

### The pituitary: why it is called the master gland

The **pituitary gland** is often called the **master gland**, because most of its hormones are **tropic hormones**: hormones whose primary function is to influence the release of hormones from *other* glands. (*Tropic* means "able to stimulate or change something".) The example that matters for this chapter is **gonadotropin**, a pituitary tropic hormone that travels in the blood to the gonads and stimulates them to release gonadal hormones.

### Two glands in one: posterior and anterior pituitary

The pituitary is really **two glands**, the **posterior pituitary** and the **anterior pituitary**, which fuse during embryological development. Their origins are completely different, and that difference explains how each is controlled.

- The **posterior pituitary** grows as a small outgrowth of **hypothalamic tissue**, which eventually dangles from the hypothalamus on the end of the **pituitary stalk**. It is, in effect, a piece of brain.
- The **anterior pituitary** starts life as part of the same embryonic tissue that becomes the **roof of the mouth**. During development it pinches off and migrates upward to settle beside the posterior pituitary. It is glandular tissue, not neural tissue.

Because it is the **anterior** pituitary that releases the tropic hormones, it is strictly the anterior pituitary, not the pituitary as a whole, that deserves the title of master gland.

> **Trap.** "Master gland" belongs to the **anterior** pituitary. The posterior pituitary releases no tropic hormones; it releases vasopressin and oxytocin, which act on non-endocrine targets (kidney, uterus, breast).

### Cyclic females, steady males

Females and males release exactly the same hormones, but not at the same levels, and the hormones do not necessarily do the same jobs. The **major** endocrine difference between the sexes is one of **timing**. In human females, the levels of gonadal and gonadotropic hormones run through a cycle that repeats roughly every **28 days**; these more or less regular fluctuations control the **menstrual cycle**. In males, by contrast, gonadal and gonadotropic hormone levels change little from day to day. The authors cheerfully describe men as rather dull creatures from a neuroendocrine point of view.

### Harris's transplant studies: the master gland has a master

Since the anterior pituitary is the master gland, many early scientists reasonably guessed that the female and male anterior pituitaries must be inherently different, one built to cycle and the other built to hold steady.

**Geoffrey Harris** tested that guess in a series of transplant studies in the **1950s** (described by Raisman, 2015). The logic was a clean crossover:

- A **cycling** pituitary taken from a mature **female** rat, transplanted to the appropriate site in a **male**, became a **steady-state** pituitary.
- A **steady-state** pituitary taken from a mature **male** rat, transplanted into a **female**, **began to cycle**.

The pituitary simply adopted the pattern of whatever body it was in. So anterior pituitaries are **not inherently female (cyclical) or male (steady)**; their pattern of release is set by some other part of the body. In the book's phrase, the master gland seemed to have a master of its own. The next question was where it was.

> **Trap.** Harris's transplant studies showed that the *pituitary* is not where the sex difference in hormone pattern lives. They did not, by themselves, identify the hypothalamus; that came from the separate lines of evidence below. Keep the two steps apart in an answer.

### Control of the pituitary: the seasonal-breeding clue

The first hint that the **nervous system** controls the anterior pituitary came from behavioural studies of birds and other animals that breed only at one time of year. In these species, seasonal changes in the **light-dark cycle** triggered many of the breeding-related changes in hormone release. Reverse the lighting conditions, for example by transporting the animals across the **equator**, and their breeding seasons reversed too. Light can only act through the eyes and the brain, so somehow **visual input to the nervous system was controlling the release of tropic hormones** from the anterior pituitary.

The obvious candidate was the **hypothalamus**, the structure from which the pituitary hangs. Stimulation and lesion experiments quickly confirmed that the hypothalamus regulates the anterior pituitary. But they left a real puzzle: the anterior pituitary, unlike the posterior, receives **no neural input whatsoever**, from the hypothalamus or from any other neural structure. How can a brain region control a gland it is not wired to?

### How the hypothalamus controls the posterior pituitary

The hypothalamus uses two different mechanisms, one for each half of the pituitary. The posterior route is the simpler one because it *is* wired.

The two major posterior pituitary hormones, **vasopressin** and **oxytocin**, are **peptide hormones**. Follow them step by step:

1. They are **synthesised in the cell bodies** of neurons in the **paraventricular nuclei** and **supraoptic nuclei**, which lie on each side of the hypothalamus.
2. They are **transported along the axons** of these neurons, down the pituitary stalk, to the axon terminals in the posterior pituitary.
3. They are **stored** in those terminals until **action potentials** arrive, which release them into the bloodstream.

Neurons that release hormones into the general circulation in this way are called **neurosecretory cells**.

The functions:

- **Oxytocin** stimulates **contractions of the uterus during labour** and the **ejection of milk during suckling**.
- **Vasopressin**, also called **antidiuretic hormone**, promotes **reabsorption of water by the kidneys**.
- Both also seem to influence **stress-coping** and **social** responses (Benarroch, 2013; Hammock, 2015; Shen, 2015).

> **Trap.** Oxytocin and vasopressin are **made in the hypothalamus** (paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei) and only **stored and released** by the posterior pituitary. An option saying "synthesised in the posterior pituitary" is wrong. Also: vasopressin = antidiuretic hormone (ADH); do not confuse it with adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), an anterior pituitary hormone.

### How the hypothalamus controls the anterior pituitary: the portal system

The anterior route was the harder problem, since there are no nerves to follow. **Harris (1955)** proposed the answer: the release of anterior pituitary hormones is itself regulated by **hormones released from the hypothalamus**. In other words, the brain talks to the anterior pituitary chemically, by blood, over a very short distance.

Two early findings supported the idea:

1. **The plumbing exists.** Anatomists found a vascular network, the **hypothalamopituitary portal system**, that looked purpose-built to carry hormones from the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary. A network of hypothalamic capillaries drains into a bundle of **portal veins**, which run down the pituitary stalk and open into a second network of capillaries in the anterior pituitary. (A **portal vein** is defined as a vein that connects one capillary network with another, rather than returning blood to the heart.)
2. **Cutting it breaks control.** When the portal veins in the pituitary stalk are cut, the anterior pituitary stops releasing its hormones normally, and release recovers only **once the veins have regenerated** (Harris, 1955). Recovery tracking regeneration is the elegant part: it shows that the blood connection itself, not some incidental damage, carries the signal.

So the two halves of the pituitary are controlled in opposite styles. The posterior pituitary gets its hormones *delivered by axons* from hypothalamic neurons; the anterior pituitary gets *instructions delivered by blood* through the portal system and makes its own hormones in response.

| | Posterior pituitary | Anterior pituitary |
|---|---|---|
| Embryological origin | Outgrowth of hypothalamic (neural) tissue | Same tissue as the roof of the mouth |
| Neural input | Yes: axons from paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei | **None** |
| How the hypothalamus controls it | Neurosecretory cells release hormones made in the hypothalamus | Hypothalamic releasing and release-inhibiting hormones arrive through the portal system |
| Hormones released | Vasopressin, oxytocin | Tropic hormones (including gonadotropins, thyrotropin, ACTH) and growth hormone |

### Discovery of hypothalamic releasing hormones

If Harris was right, there should be a separate hypothalamic hormone controlling each anterior pituitary hormone. Two kinds were predicted:

- A **releasing hormone**: a hypothalamic hormone thought to *stimulate* release of an anterior pituitary hormone.
- A **release-inhibiting hormone**: one thought to *inhibit* release.

For years these were **putative** (hypothesised) substances: nobody had isolated one. The breakthrough came in the **late 1960s**, from two competing teams working independently:

- **Guillemin** and his colleagues isolated **thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH)** from the hypothalamus of **sheep**.
- **Schally** and his colleagues isolated the **same hormone** from the hypothalamus of **pigs**.

TRH triggers the release of **thyrotropin** from the anterior pituitary, and thyrotropin in turn stimulates the **thyroid** gland to release its hormones. For this work Guillemin and Schally received **Nobel Prizes in 1977**.

The isolation of TRH did two things. It **confirmed** Harris's hypothesis that hypothalamic releasing hormones control the anterior pituitary, and it gave the main impetus for isolating and synthesising the others.

> **Trap.** Sheep go with **Guillemin**, pigs with **Schally**. The first releasing hormone isolated was **TRH**, not GnRH. The Nobel year is **1977**, not the year of isolation (late 1960s).

### Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, FSH and LH

Of direct relevance to sex hormones was the next isolation: **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)**, by Schally's group (**Schally, Kastin, & Arimura, 1971**). GnRH stimulates the release of **both** of the anterior pituitary's **gonadotropins**:

- **Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)**
- **Luteinising hormone (LH)** (spelt *luteinizing* in the book)

One general rule closes the story: **all hypothalamic releasing hormones, like all tropic hormones, have proven to be peptides.** Contrast that with the gonadal hormones at the end of the chain, which are steroids.

What the two gonadotropins do is not detailed in the 11th edition, but the standard division is worth knowing for MCQs: in females, FSH drives the growth of ovarian follicles and LH surges to trigger ovulation; in males, FSH supports sperm production and LH stimulates the testes to make testosterone.

> **Trap.** One releasing hormone (GnRH) controls **two** gonadotropins (FSH and LH). There is no separate "LH-releasing hormone" in the book's account. And remember the chemistry: releasing hormones and tropic hormones are **peptides**; gonadal hormones are **steroids**.

### Three kinds of signal that regulate hormone release

Hormone release is regulated by three different kinds of signal.

**1. Neural signals.** **All endocrine glands except the anterior pituitary** are directly regulated by signals from the nervous system. The glands inside the brain (the pituitary and the pineal) are regulated by cerebral neurons. The glands outside the CNS are innervated by the **autonomic nervous system**, usually by both the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches, which often push hormone release in opposite directions.

A conceptual point follows that the book stresses as "extremely important": hormone release can be influenced by **experience**. Many spring-breeding species are readied for reproduction by sex-hormone release triggered by the **lengthening daily duration of daylight**. So explaining a behaviour by a hormonal mechanism does **not** rule out an explanation by an experiential mechanism; the experience may be acting *through* the hormone.

**2. Hormonal signals.** Hormones also influence hormone release. You have already seen the forward direction: anterior pituitary tropic hormones control their target glands. But the traffic is not one-way. Circulating hormones often **feed back** to the very structures that control their release: the pituitary, the hypothalamus and other brain sites. Most hormonal feedback serves to keep blood levels **stable**: **high** gonadal hormone levels usually act on the hypothalamus and pituitary to **decrease** further gonadal hormone release, and **low** levels usually act to **increase** it. That is negative feedback, the same principle as the set-point systems of Chapter 12.

The book's summary figure (13.5) labels this step "positive or negative feedback". The positive case is not explained in the text, but the standard example is the mid-cycle surge of LH in females, in which rising oestradiol *increases* gonadotropin release rather than suppressing it, and the resulting LH surge triggers ovulation.

**3. Non-hormonal chemical signals.** Circulating chemicals that are not hormones can regulate hormone levels too. Blood levels of **glucose, calcium and sodium** all influence release of particular hormones. The example from Chapter 12: a rise in blood glucose increases insulin release from the pancreas, and insulin then lowers blood glucose.

### Pulsatile hormone release

Hormones are not dripped out at a constant rate. They tend to be released in **pulses** (Plant, 2015): discharged several times a day in large surges that usually last no more than a few minutes. The body adjusts blood levels by changing the **frequency** and **duration** of these pulses, not by turning a steady tap up or down.

A consequence is that circulating hormone levels can swing widely from **minute to minute** (Lightman & Conway-Campbell, 2010). This forces a correction to the earlier statement about males. When male gonadal hormone release is called "steady", it means there are **no major systematic changes from day to day**, not that the level never varies. A man's testosterone, sampled minute by minute, fluctuates a great deal; it is the absence of a multi-day cycle that makes it "steady".

> **Trap.** "Steady" (male) versus "cyclic" (female) refers to the **day-to-day pattern**, not to moment-to-moment constancy. Pulsatile release produces large minute-to-minute fluctuations in both sexes.

### The summary model of gonadal endocrine regulation

The whole module compresses into one loop (the book's Figure 13.5):

1. The **brain** sends neural signals to the **hypothalamus**.
2. The hypothalamus releases **gonadotropin-releasing hormone** into the **hypothalamopituitary portal system**.
3. The portal system carries GnRH to the **anterior pituitary**, which releases **gonadotropins** (FSH and LH) into the general circulation.
4. The gonadotropins reach the **gonads**, which release **androgens, oestrogens and progestins**.
5. The gonadal hormones act on **body tissues** and on the **brain** (where they influence behaviour), and they **feed back** on the pituitary and hypothalamus to regulate further gonadal hormone release.

Every arrow in that loop reappears in the chapter: the GnRH step explains why the hypothalamus, not the pituitary, sets the female cycle (13.3); the gonadal step explains castration (13.5); and the feedback step explains why anabolic steroids shrink the testes (13.5).

## 13.2 Hormones and Sexual Development of the Body

Humans are **dimorphic**: most come in one of two forms, female and male. This module explains how hormones direct the development of the bodily differences, from a single cell to a sexually mature adult. The body is the part of the story that is well understood, and it gives you the rule that the brain (13.3) and the exceptional cases (13.4) are later tested against.

### Sexual differentiation: beware the two-programme picture

Sexual differentiation in mammals starts at **fertilisation**, when one of two kinds of zygote is formed: XX (genetic female) or XY (genetic male). The genetic information on the sex chromosomes usually decides whether development will follow female or male lines.

The book immediately adds a caution, and it is the conceptual centre of the module. Do **not** begin by assuming that there are two parallel but opposite genetic programmes, one for building a female and one for building a male. That is the mamawawa at work. What actually happens is asymmetrical: for the reproductive organs, a female course of development proceeds unless something specific, produced by the testes at a specific time, diverts it. The rest of the module shows that asymmetry three times over: in the gonads, in the internal ducts, and in the external genitals.

### The primordial gonads and the Sry gene

**Six weeks after fertilisation**, every fetus, whatever its genetic sex, has the same pair of gonadal structures, the **primordial gonads** (*primordial* means "existing at the beginning"). Each primordial gonad has two parts with two different potentials:

- an outer covering, the **cortex**, which can develop into an **ovary**;
- an internal core, the **medulla**, which can develop into a **testis**.

In the **seventh week** after conception, the **Sry gene** on the **Y chromosome** of a genetic male triggers the synthesis of **Sry protein** (Arnold, 2017; Sekido & Lovell-Badge, 2013; Wu et al., 2012). Sry protein causes the **medulla** of each primordial gonad to grow and become a **testis**. Where there is **no Sry protein**, the **cortical** cells develop into **ovaries** instead (Lin & Capel, 2015).

The decisive demonstrations are manipulations of the protein itself, and they show that it is the protein, not the chromosomes as such, that decides:

- **Sry protein injected** into a **genetic female** fetus **6 weeks** after conception produces a genetic female **with testes**.
- **Drugs that block the effects of Sry protein**, given to a **genetic male** fetus, produce a genetic male **with ovaries**.

> **Trap.** Two timings that examiners mix up: the primordial gonads are present and **identical at 6 weeks**; the Sry gene acts in the **seventh week**. And note which part becomes which: **medulla → testis** (with Sry), **cortex → ovary** (without Sry). "Cortex becomes testis" is a reversal trap.

> **Trap.** The book writes **Sry gene / Sry protein**. Many exam papers write **SRY** (sex-determining region of the Y chromosome), and older sources call its product **testis-determining factor (TDF)**. All refer to the same switch, located on the **Y** chromosome, not the X.

### Intersexed persons and the weakness of mamawawa thinking

The book points out that such manipulations produce **intersexed persons**: individuals whose sexual characteristics are not all typically female or all typically male (here, genetic females with testes or genetic males with ovaries). Their existence exposes, in a dramatic way, the weakness of mamawawa thinking, which treats "male" and "female" as mutually exclusive, opposite categories. If gonadal sex can be dissociated from chromosomal sex by one protein, the categories are clearly not a single all-or-none switch.

> **Trap.** *Intersexed person* is the 11th edition's term. Older texts and older exam keys may use **hermaphrodite** or **pseudohermaphrodite**; current medical usage also speaks of **differences (or disorders) of sex development (DSD)**. If an option uses the older wording, recognise the concept; in your own answers, use the current terms.

### Internal reproductive ducts: Wolffian and Müllerian systems

**Six weeks after fertilisation**, every fetus has **two complete sets** of internal reproductive ducts, side by side:

| System | Potential | Structures it can become |
|---|---|---|
| **Wolffian system** | Male ducts | **Seminal vesicles** (which hold the fluid in which sperm are ejaculated) and the **vas deferens** (through which sperm travel to the seminal vesicles) |
| **Müllerian system** | Female ducts | The **uterus**, the **upper part of the vagina**, and the **fallopian tubes** (through which ova travel from the ovaries to the uterus) |

A simple memory hook: **W**olffian goes **W**ith the testes; **M**üllerian is the **M**other's system (uterus and tubes).

What chooses between them? In the **third month** of male fetal development, the testes secrete **two** substances, each with its own job:

1. **Testosterone** stimulates the **Wolffian system** to develop into male ducts.
2. **Müllerian-inhibiting substance** causes the **Müllerian system to degenerate**, and also causes the testes to **descend into the scrotum** (the sac that holds the testes outside the body cavity) (Sajjad, 2010).

Notice that male development requires both an *add* signal and a *remove* signal. Testosterone builds the Wolffian ducts but does nothing to the Müllerian ducts; without Müllerian-inhibiting substance, the female ducts would persist alongside the male ones.

Because it is **testosterone, not the sex chromosomes**, that triggers Wolffian development, a **genetic female injected with testosterone** during the appropriate fetal period develops **male reproductive ducts along with her female ones**. She keeps her Müllerian ducts because the injection supplied testosterone but no Müllerian-inhibiting substance.

> **Trap.** Testosterone and Müllerian-inhibiting substance do **different** jobs. Testosterone **builds** Wolffian ducts; MIS **removes** Müllerian ducts. MIS does **not** build male ducts, and testosterone does **not** remove female ducts. MIS is also called **anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)** in other sources; same substance.

### What makes the female ducts develop: Jost (1972)

Now the female side, and the key asymmetry. The development of the female internal ducts is **not** controlled by ovarian hormones. The fetal ovaries are almost completely **inactive** during fetal development. Instead, the Müllerian system develops in **any fetus that is not exposed to testicular hormones** during the critical fetal period.

The evidence is the set of fetal gonadectomy findings the book cites from **Jost (1972)**. Three kinds of fetus all develop **female reproductive ducts**:

1. **Female fetuses** (as expected);
2. **Ovariectomised female fetuses**, which shows that the ovaries are not needed for female ducts;
3. **Orchidectomised male fetuses**, which shows that a genetic male deprived of his testes takes the female course.

Reason through the design. Removing the ovaries changes nothing, so ovarian hormones cannot be the cause of female duct development. Removing the testes switches a male to the female pattern, so testicular secretions must be what normally diverts development away from it. The female pathway is what happens *in the absence* of a testicular signal. (Beyond what the 11th edition describes, the historical background is that Alfred Jost's original experiments, in the 1940s, removed the gonads of rabbit fetuses in utero, and that a testosterone implant alone could maintain the Wolffian ducts but could not make the Müllerian ducts regress. That pointed to a second testicular factor, the one now called Müllerian-inhibiting substance.)

Three surgical terms come with this section:

- **Ovariectomy**: removal of the ovaries.
- **Orchidectomy**: removal of the testes (from the Greek *orchis*, "testicle").
- **Gonadectomy**, or **castration**: removal of the gonads, either ovaries or testes.

> **Trap.** Female ducts develop because of the **absence of testicular hormones**, not the **presence of ovarian hormones**. A question asking "which hormone causes the Müllerian system to develop?" expects the answer that none does: it develops when androgens (testosterone) are lacking around the third month. The book's own self-test item puts it exactly this way.

> **Trap.** *Castration* in this book means removal of the gonads in **either** sex (gonadectomy), not only removal of the testes. Orchidectomy is the male-specific term; ovariectomy the female-specific one.

### External reproductive organs: one bipotential precursor

The external genitals differ from the internal organs in one basic way, and examiners love it.

- For the **gonads** and the **internal ducts**, every typical fetus starts with **separate** precursors for each sex (medulla versus cortex; Wolffian versus Müllerian), and **only one set** goes on to develop.
- For the **external genitals**, both the male and the female organs develop from **the very same precursor** (Sajjad, 2010). This **bipotential precursor** can go either way.

By the **end of the third month** of pregnancy, the bipotential precursor consists of **four parts**, and each part has a male fate and a female fate:

| Part of the precursor | Male fate | Female fate |
|---|---|---|
| **Glans** | Head of the penis | Clitoris |
| **Urethral folds** | Fuse (enclosing the urethra) | Enlarge to become the **labia minora** |
| **Lateral bodies** | Shaft of the penis | Hood of the clitoris |
| **Labioscrotal swellings** | Scrotum | **Labia majora** |

The switch is the same hormone that governs the Wolffian ducts: **testosterone**. When the precursor is exposed to testosterone during the critical fetal window, it differentiates into male external genitals; without that exposure, it differentiates along female lines (Matsushita et al., 2018).

> **Trap.** The **scrotum** and the **labia majora** come from the same structure (the labioscrotal swellings); the **penis head** and the **clitoris** from the glans. Questions often pair the scrotum with the labia *minora*; that is wrong. Labia minora come from the **urethral folds**.

> **High-yield.** Gonads: one primordial gonad with two potentials, decided by Sry protein. Internal ducts: two separate systems, decided by testosterone (build Wolffian) and MIS (remove Müllerian). External genitals: one bipotential precursor with four parts, decided by testosterone. In all three, the female outcome follows from the *absence* of a testicular signal.

### Puberty: hormones and secondary sex characteristics

During childhood, circulating gonadal hormone levels are low, the reproductive organs are immature, and girls and boys differ little in general appearance. This quiet period ends abruptly with **puberty**, the transition from childhood to adulthood. Three things happen during it: the individual becomes **fertile**, the **adolescent growth spurt** takes place, and the **secondary sex characteristics** appear. These are the features, other than the reproductive organs themselves, by which sexually mature females and males differ.

Puberty is driven by increased hormone release from the **anterior pituitary**, through two separate routes:

1. **Growth hormone** acts **directly** on bone and muscle tissue to produce the **pubertal growth spurt** (Colvin & Abdullatif, 2013; Russell et al., 2011). It is the **only anterior pituitary hormone that does not have a gland as its primary target**.
2. Increases in **gonadotropic hormone** and **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** make the **gonads** and the **adrenal cortex** step up their release of gonadal and adrenal hormones, and those hormones in turn start the maturation of the genitals and the development of the secondary sex characteristics.

The guiding principle of typical pubertal maturation is simple. In pubertal males, androgen levels exceed oestrogen levels, and **masculinisation** results; in pubertal females, oestrogens predominate, and **feminisation** results (Colvin & Abdullatif, 2013). Individuals castrated **before puberty** do not become sexually mature unless they receive **replacement injections** of androgens or oestrogens.

The typical changes (the book's Figure 13.9):

| Typically in males | Typically in females |
|---|---|
| Growth spurt | Growth spurt |
| Pubic hair (pyramid pattern) | Pubic hair (inverted pyramid pattern) |
| Axillary (underarm) hair | Axillary hair |
| Facial and body hair | Breasts develop |
| Larynx enlarges (voice deepens) | Body contours become rounded |
| Musculature develops | Uterus grows; menstruation begins |
| Reproductive organs grow; hairline recession begins | |
| Acne | Acne |

> **Trap.** **Growth hormone** is the odd one out among anterior pituitary hormones: it has **no gland** as its primary target and acts **directly on bone and muscle**. The other two puberty signals the book names are **gonadotropic hormone** (to the gonads) and **adrenocorticotropic hormone** (to the adrenal cortex).

### Androstenedione and the pubic-hair problem for the mamawawa

Even at puberty, the mamawawa stumbles. **Androstenedione**, an androgen released mainly by the **adrenal cortex**, is typically responsible for the growth of **pubic hair and axillary hair in females**. An "androgen" producing the female pattern of pubic hair makes it very hard to keep calling androgens "male hormones". (The patterns themselves: **pyramid** in males, **inverted pyramid** in females.)

This fact is not a curiosity; it is the clue that explains a detail of the first exceptional case in 13.4. A woman whose body cannot respond to androgens will have sparse pubic and axillary hair, because the androgen that normally grows that hair in women has nothing to act on.

> **Trap.** Female pubic and axillary hair is grown by an **androgen** (**androstenedione**) from the **adrenal cortex**, not by oestrogen and not by the ovaries.

## 13.3 Sexual Development of Brain and Behaviour

The development of the body is well understood. The harder problem, and the focus of current research, is the sexual differentiation of the **brain** and of **behaviour**. This module traces how theories that grew out of seminal experiments in the **1930s** were reshaped by later research into today's view, and the direction of travel is the lesson: from one simple mechanism to many independent ones.

The book also flags a methodological problem that shaped the field. Until recently, neuroscience studies were usually done on **males only**, or lumped males and females into one group (Fischer & Riddle, 2018). With the growing evidence of sex-related brain differences, that practice is changing (Choleris et al., 2018), and many funding agencies now require researchers to assess for possible sex differences in brain and behaviour.

### Sex differences in the brain

Female and male brains may look alike on casual inspection, and it may be politically comfortable to believe they are the same, but they are not. On average, male brains are about **15 percent larger** than female brains. Many other anatomical differences have been documented (Brecht, Lenschow, & Rao, 2018; Choleris et al., 2018; McCarthy, Nugent, & Lenz, 2017, among others):

- average **volumes** of various cortical areas, nuclei and fibre tracts;
- the **numbers and types of neurons and glial cells** in various structures;
- the **plasticity** of certain structures;
- the **numbers and types of synapses** connecting cells within structures.

Hold that list against the qualification that comes later in the module: these are differences between **averages**.

### Pfeiffer (1936): the first functional sex difference

The first attempts to find a sex difference in mammalian brain *function* focused on what controls the development of the **steady** (male) and **cyclic** (female) patterns of gonadotropin release that you met in 13.1. The seminal experiments were done by **Pfeiffer in 1936**, on **neonatal rats**. The design crossed two factors: some neonates of each sex were **gonadectomised** and some were not; some received **gonad transplants** (ovaries or testes) and some did not. The results:

| Treatment of neonatal rat | Adult pattern of gonadotropin release |
|---|---|
| Gonadectomised, **either** genetic sex | **Cyclic** (female pattern) |
| **Testes** transplanted into a gonadectomised **or** intact female | **Steady** (male pattern) |
| **Ovaries** transplanted | **No effect** on the pattern |

Read the table as an argument. Removing the gonads of a male produces the female pattern, so the female pattern does not need ovaries. Adding testes to a female produces the male pattern, so testicular secretions are sufficient to impose it. Adding ovaries does nothing, so ovaries are not an active ingredient. Pfeiffer's conclusion: the **female cyclic pattern develops unless the preprogrammed female cyclicity is overridden by testosterone during perinatal development**. This is the same "female unless overridden" rule you met for the body in 13.2, now found in the control of hormone release.

Pfeiffer's mistake was about *where* this happened. He concluded that perinatal testicular hormones altered the development of the **pituitary**, because nobody yet knew that gonadotropin release from the anterior pituitary is controlled by the **hypothalamus**. Once that was established (13.1: Harris's transplants, the portal system, the releasing hormones), his experiments were reinterpreted. In the **1960s** his theory was modified to say that perinatal androgens act on the **hypothalamus**, overriding its preprogrammed cyclic female pattern and starting the development of the steady male pattern. In other words, Pfeiffer's experiments became the first evidence that **perinatal** (around the time of birth) androgens masculinise a part of the **brain**.

> **Trap.** Pfeiffer (1936) attributed the effect to the **pituitary**; the **1960s** revision relocated it to the **hypothalamus**. Harris's adult pituitary transplants (1950s) are what made the pituitary explanation untenable. An option saying "Pfeiffer showed the hypothalamus is masculinised by testosterone" credits him with the later reinterpretation.

> **Trap.** In Pfeiffer's design, **ovary transplants had no effect**. If an option says neonatal ovary transplants "feminised" gonadotropin release, it contradicts the result, and it contradicts the whole point that the female pattern needs no ovarian signal.

### The aromatisation hypothesis

The 1960s version of Pfeiffer's theory fitted what was known then, but later research forced major revisions, and the first was the **aromatisation hypothesis**.

Start with the chemistry. All gonadal and adrenal sex hormones are steroids, and all steroids are derived from cholesterol, so their structures are similar and they are readily converted into one another. In particular, a slight change to the testosterone molecule, made under the influence of the enzyme **aromatase**, converts **testosterone into oestradiol**. This conversion is **aromatisation**. (An **enzyme** is a protein that influences a biochemical reaction without taking part in it.)

The **aromatisation hypothesis** states that perinatal testosterone does **not** masculinise the brain directly; the brain is masculinised by **oestradiol that has been aromatised from perinatal testosterone**. That sounds backwards: the "female hormone" making a brain male. But in **rats and mice** the evidence for it is strong, and it comes from two directions:

1. **Oestradiol alone works.** Early injections of oestradiol have masculinising effects on the brain.
2. **Testosterone that cannot become oestradiol does not work.** Masculinisation fails when testosterone is given together with agents that **block aromatisation**, and it fails with androgens that **cannot be aromatised**, such as **dihydrotestosterone (DHT)**.

The two lines are complementary: one shows oestradiol is sufficient, the other that conversion to oestradiol is necessary.

### Alpha fetoprotein: how female rodents are protected

The hypothesis raises an obvious problem. If oestradiol masculinises the rodent brain, why are genetic females not masculinised by their **mothers'** oestradiol, which circulates through the fetal blood supply?

The answer is **alpha fetoprotein**. This protein is present in the blood of rodents during the perinatal period, and it **deactivates circulating oestradiol by binding to it** (Yang & Shah, 2014). The mother's oestradiol, and the female fetus's own, is mopped up in the blood before it can act on the brain.

That creates a second puzzle: how then does a **male** rodent's brain get masculinised, if circulating oestradiol is neutralised? The solution depends on two facts:

1. **Testosterone is immune to alpha fetoprotein.** It travels unaffected from the testes, through the blood, to brain cells.
2. **Inside the brain**, aromatase converts the testosterone to oestradiol, and that oestradiol is **not** deactivated, because **alpha fetoprotein does not readily penetrate the blood-brain barrier**.

So oestradiol acts in the male rodent brain because it is *made there*, behind the barrier, from a precursor the protein cannot touch.

How certain is this? The role of alpha fetoprotein in deactivating oestradiol is **firmly established in rodents**, but its function in **humans remains controversial** (Koebele & Bimonte-Nelson, 2015).

> **Trap.** Alpha fetoprotein binds **oestradiol**, not testosterone, and it works **in the blood**, not in the brain. It protects **female** rodent brains from circulating oestradiol. Its role in humans is **controversial**. Each clause is a separate MCQ.

### Does aromatisation masculinise the human brain?

The aromatisation hypothesis was at first assumed to apply to humans. Current research says it does not: in humans, the masculinising effects of testosterone appear to come from its **direct** effects on the brain (Motta-Mena & Puts, 2017; Puts & Motta-Mena, 2018), not from conversion to oestradiol.

> **Trap.** Aromatisation plays a role in masculinising the brains of **rats and mice**, **not humans**. This is a revision older sources may not have; an older key might treat the aromatisation hypothesis as general to mammals. The 11th edition explicitly says it does not apply to humans.

### The modern perspective: slight, variable, statistical

Up to this point one theory has accounted for many sex differences: the reproductive organs, the secondary sex characteristics and the hypothalamus. **The default programme of development is the female programme, which is overridden in genetic males by perinatal exposure to testosterone.** The initial assumption was that this same mechanism would turn out to be the only mechanism behind all the other differences between female and male brains. That has not proved to be the case (Arnold, 2017; Lenz, Nugent, & McCarthy, 2012).

Before looking at mechanisms, get the nature of the differences right. The vast majority of brain sex differences are **not all-or-none** differences of the mamawawa kind. Most are **slight, variable and statistical**: many differences exist between the *average* male and *average* female brain, but there is usually **plenty of overlap** between individuals. In humans the overlap is so large that some researchers argue it would be better not to think in terms of "male brains" and "female brains" at all (Joel & Fausto-Sterling, 2016).

### Why sex differences matter clinically

None of that means the differences should be ignored. Even if each is slight, collectively they are sufficient to have significant **health implications** (Ball, Balthazart, & McCarthy, 2014; Choleris et al., 2018; Geary, 2019). The book's examples:

- **Parkinson's disease** and **autism spectrum disorder** are far more common in **males**.
- **Alzheimer's disease** is far more common in **females**.
- **Males recover less well than females from traumatic brain injury** (Mollayeva, Mollayeva, & Colantonio, 2018).

### Brains are not masculinised or feminised as a whole

Research on how brain sex differences develop is young, but one principle has emerged: **sex differences develop independently in different parts of the brain, at different times, and by different mechanisms** (Joel & Fausto-Sterling, 2016). The brain is not flipped to "male" or "female" in one go.

Two illustrations:

- **Aromatase is found in only a few areas of the rat brain** (the hypothalamus, for example), and it is only in those areas that aromatisation is critical for testosterone's masculinising effects (de Bournonville et al., 2019). So even within the rat, the aromatisation mechanism is local, not brain-wide.
- **Some sex differences do not appear until puberty**, and these are unlikely to be products of perinatal hormones at all (Ingalhalikar et al., 2014).

### Three factors that matter for the brain but not the body

Studying brain differentiation is further complicated because three factors that play **little or no role** in differentiating the reproductive organs **do** play a role in differentiating the brain:

1. **Sex chromosomes act directly.** Sex chromosomes influence brain development **independently of their effect on hormones** (Arnold, 2017). For example, the brains of male and female mice show different patterns of **gene expression even before the gonads become functional** (Wolstenholme, Rissman, & Bekiranov, 2013), which no gonadal hormone could have caused.
2. **Epigenetic effects.** Epigenetic mechanisms can interact with gonadal hormones to produce sex-specific effects on brain development (Forger, 2018; McCarthy, Nugent, & Lenz, 2017).
3. **Oestradiol has an active role in female development.** The female programme for the reproductive organs normally runs without gonadal steroids, but for the brain the evidence suggests oestradiol is actively needed: **knockout mice lacking the gene that forms oestradiol receptors do not show a female pattern of brain development** (Maekawa et al., 2014).

The upshot is the book's verdict on the "default" idea, which you should be able to state precisely. The conventional view that the female programme is the **default** does a good job of explaining differentiation of the **reproductive organs and the hypothalamus**, but it **falters badly** for differentiation of the **brain in general**. Brain mechanisms are more complex and more selective, and they also **differ between mammalian species** (Sekido, 2014): aromatisation matters in rats and mice, but not in humans.

> **Trap.** "Female is the default" is the book's position **only** for the reproductive organs and the hypothalamus. For the brain in general the book says the idea **falters badly**, for three reasons: direct sex-chromosome effects, epigenetic effects, and an **active** role for oestradiol in female brain development. An option stating "the female brain develops passively in the absence of hormones" is the old view.

### Development of reproductive behaviour in laboratory animals: Phoenix and colleagues (1959)

Because experiments on human sexual development are unethical, most research on how sex differences in behaviour develop has been done on the **reproductive behaviour of laboratory animals**, and until recently it concentrated on **perinatal hormones**.

**Phoenix and colleagues (1959)** were among the first to show that perinatal testosterone **masculinises and defeminises** a genetic female's adult reproductive behaviour. The procedure had three stages, each with a purpose:

1. They injected **pregnant guinea pigs** with **testosterone**, so the female fetuses were exposed to it.
2. When the litters were born, they **ovariectomised** the female offspring, so that the animals' own ovarian hormones could not muddy the adult test.
3. When these females reached maturity, the researchers gave them adult hormone treatments and assessed their copulatory behaviour.

Two results:

- Given **testosterone** as adults, the prenatally exposed females showed **more male-like mounting** than females not exposed to perinatal testosterone: they were **masculinised**.
- Given **progesterone and oestradiol** as adults and mounted by males, they showed **less lordosis** than controls: they were **defeminised**. **Lordosis** is the arched-back posture that facilitates intromission and signals receptivity in female rodents.

### Grady, Phoenix and Young (1965)

The complementary study took the opposite approach: remove testosterone from males early. **Grady, Phoenix, and Young (1965)** found that a lack of early testosterone exposure both **feminises and demasculinises** the adult reproductive behaviour of male rats.

- Male rats **castrated shortly after birth**, then given **testosterone** as adults and paired with a sexually receptive female, **failed to show the typical male copulatory pattern** of **mounting, intromission** (insertion of the penis) and **ejaculation** (ejection of sperm): they were **demasculinised**.
- Given **oestrogen and progesterone** as adults, they showed **more lordosis** than uncastrated controls: they were **feminised**.

Note that in both studies the adult hormone was supplied; what differed was only the **early** exposure. That is what makes these organisational effects: the same adult hormone produced different behaviour depending on what the brain had been exposed to around birth.

### Timing and proceptive behaviour

Timing is critical. In rats, the ability of **single injections** of testosterone to masculinise and defeminise reproductive behaviour seems to be confined to the **first 5 days after birth** (McCarthy, Herold, & Stockman, 2018).

Most of this research measures the copulatory act itself, so less is known about the development of **proceptive behaviours** (solicitation behaviours, which serve to attract a partner) and of sex-related behaviours not directly tied to reproduction. One known finding: perinatal testosterone **disrupts** the proceptive **hopping, darting and ear wiggling** of receptive female rats.

> **Trap.** Match the study to the species and direction. **Phoenix et al. (1959)**: **guinea pigs**, testosterone given **prenatally to females** → **masculinised and defeminised**. **Grady, Phoenix & Young (1965)**: **rats**, **males castrated** at birth → **demasculinised and feminised**. The rat window for single injections is the **first 5 days** after birth.

### Masculinisation is not the opposite of feminisation

Why do researchers always measure **masculinisation separately from defeminisation**, and **feminisation separately from demasculinisation**? Because they are not the same thing. If you assume that masculinising an animal automatically defeminises it, you have fallen into the mamawawa: treating maleness and femaleness as two ends of one axis, so that moving towards one must mean moving away from the other.

The four terms, as the book uses them:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Masculinises** | Enhances or induces male reproductive behaviour |
| **Defeminises** | Suppresses or disrupts female reproductive behaviour |
| **Feminises** | Enhances or induces female reproductive behaviour |
| **Demasculinises** | Suppresses or disrupts male reproductive behaviour |

The evidence that these are independent dimensions:

- Male and female behaviours can **coexist** in the same individual. "Male" behaviours such as mounting receptive females have been seen in females of many mammalian species, and "female" behaviours such as lordosis in males (Reznikov et al., 2016).
- They do not necessarily change in opposite directions after a physiological treatment. **Lesions of the medial preoptic area** abolish **male** reproductive behaviours in **both male and female rats** **without affecting female behaviours** (Singer, 1968; Will, Hull, & Dominguez, 2014). The lesion removed one dimension and left the other untouched.

The book asks you to think this idea through carefully because it returns in 13.6 (the medial preoptic area) and in 13.7 (orientation and gender identity varying independently).

> **Trap.** Masculinisation ≠ defeminisation, and feminisation ≠ demasculinisation. If an option treats them as synonyms, it is the mamawawa error the book names explicitly.

### Development of sex differences in human behaviour: three conclusions

Human research on this question is almost entirely **case studies and correlational studies**, because experiments are unethical, and those designs are hard to interpret (Chapter 1). Still, three general conclusions have emerged.

**1. A few human behaviours are true sexual dimorphisms.** **Sexual dimorphisms** are cases where a behaviour (or a structure) typically comes in two distinct classes, female or male, into which most individuals can be assigned unambiguously. In humans, only **reproduction-related behaviours** clearly fall into this category, and the presence or absence of **prenatal testosterone** appears to be a major factor in their development (Balthazart, 2011; Kreukels & Cohen-Kettenis, 2012).

**2. Most behavioural sex differences are small, with large overlap.** Differences between the average man and the average woman are typically small and overlap substantially (Hines, 2011; Miller & Halpern, 2014). They have been reported in **play behaviour, social interaction, reaction to pain, language, cognition, emotionality, drug sensitivity and responses to stress**. Prenatal testosterone exposure contributes to these differences, but in general it accounts for **only a portion** of each.

**3. Females and males differ in susceptibility to behavioural disorders** (McCarthy et al., 2012; ter Horst et al., 2012). The book's figures:

| More common in males | Ratio | More common in females | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dyslexia | about 3 times | Depression | twice |
| Early-onset schizophrenia | about 3 times | Anxiety disorders | twice |
| Stuttering | about 3 times | Alzheimer's disease | twice |
| Autism spectrum disorders | about 3 times | Certain eating disorders | about 10 times |
| Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder | **10 times** | | |

The mechanisms behind these differences in susceptibility are **unclear** (Cahill, 2014).

A final caution closes the module. The frequent finding that prenatal testosterone influences sex differences does **not** exclude other factors (Hines, 2011). **Cultural factors** have been shown to play a major role in many sex differences, perhaps by acting on the very brain mechanisms that prenatal hormones influence. A hormonal cause and a cultural cause are not rivals that cannot both be true.

> **Trap.** The male-excess disorders are "about three times" (dyslexia, early-onset schizophrenia, stuttering, autism spectrum disorders) except **ADHD at 10 times**. The female-excess disorders are "twice" (depression, anxiety disorders, Alzheimer's) except **certain eating disorders at about 10 times**. Examiners swap ADHD and autism figures.

## 13.4 Three Cases of Exceptional Human Sexual Development

### The exception proves the rule

The authors chose these three cases for more than their intrinsic interest. Their main reason is the proverb **"the exception proves the rule"**, which most people misread. If "prove" meant "establish the truth of", the proverb would be nonsense: exceptions *challenge* a rule, they do not confirm it. The word comes from the Latin **probare, "to test"**, the sense that survives in "proving ground". So the proverb really means that **explaining exceptional cases is a testing ground for any theory**. The three cases below are offered as tests of the rules of 13.2 and 13.3: the female programme runs unless testicular hormones override it; testosterone builds the Wolffian ducts and male genitals; Müllerian-inhibiting substance removes the Müllerian ducts.

> **Trap.** In "the exception proves the rule", *prove* means **test** (Latin *probare*), not **confirm**. An option saying exceptions confirm the truth of a rule is the misreading the book warns against.

### The Case of Anne S., the Woman with Testes

**Anne S.** was a **26-year-old** woman who sought treatment for two problems: she had **never menstruated**, and she had **pain during sexual intercourse** (Jones & Park, 1971). What brought her to the clinic was that she and her husband of **4 years** had been unable to conceive, and she rightly suspected that her lack of a menstrual cycle was part of the reason.

On examination she was a healthy young woman. The only obvious oddity was that her **pubic and axillary hair was sparse and fine**. Her external genitals looked entirely typical, but her internal anatomy did not: her **vagina was only 4 centimetres long** and her **uterus was underdeveloped**.

The diagnosis was that Anne was **chromosomally XY**: she had the genes of a genetic male. Her doctors had three independent lines of evidence:

1. **Chromosomes.** Cells scraped from the inside of her mouth contained both an **X and a Y** chromosome.
2. **Gonads.** A tiny abdominal incision let the physicians look inside, and they found a pair of **internal testes and no ovaries**.
3. **Hormones.** Her hormone levels were **more typical of a male**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition titles this case **"Anne S., the Woman with Testes"**. Older editions (and the 11th edition's own end-of-chapter "Themes Revisited", which still calls it "the woman who wasn't") used **"the Woman Who Wasn't"**. Same case, same patient.

### Explaining Anne: complete androgen insensitivity syndrome

Anne has **complete androgen insensitivity syndrome**. All of her features follow from a mutation of the **androgen receptor gene** that left her androgen receptors **totally unresponsive** (Bramble et al., 2017; Mongan et al., 2015). The condition is rare, about **1 in 60,000 genetic male births** (Bramble et al., 2017; Walia et al., 2018).

Now use the rules from 13.2 to explain every feature, one at a time. This is exactly the "test" the proverb describes.

| Feature of Anne | Explanation from the rules |
|---|---|
| Testes, not ovaries | Her Y chromosome carried a working Sry gene, so the medulla of each primordial gonad became a testis. |
| Typical female external genitals | Her testes released male-typical androgen levels, but with no working receptors her tissues behaved as if no androgen were present. The bipotential precursor followed the female course. |
| Female brain and behavioural development | For the same reason: no androgen action to override the female programme. |
| Testes still inside the abdomen | With female external genitals there was no scrotum for the testes to descend into. |
| Short vagina, underdeveloped uterus | Her testes released **Müllerian-inhibiting substance** as any genetic male's do, and MIS acts through a different receptor from androgens, so her Müllerian system regressed. The structures that depend on it (uterus, upper vagina) did not develop typically. |
| Feminised body at puberty | Her testes released enough **oestrogens** to feminise her body, and there were no androgen effects to counteract them. |
| Sparse pubic and axillary hair | In women this hair is grown by adrenal **androstenedione**, an androgen; with unresponsive receptors it had nothing to act on. |

The case fits the rules remarkably well. Look especially at the uterus: a theory in which "no androgens = fully female" would predict a normal uterus. The two-signal account (testosterone for the Wolffian side, MIS for the Müllerian side) predicts precisely the underdeveloped uterus that was found.

> **Trap.** In complete androgen insensitivity syndrome the problem is the **receptor**, not the hormone. Androgen levels are **male-typical**, not low. And the uterus is underdeveloped not because of androgens but because **Müllerian-inhibiting substance** still worked. An older name for the condition is **testicular feminisation syndrome**; some older keys use it.

### The ethics of disclosure

The case raises a question of medical ethics. Many people believe that physicians should always tell patients every relevant finding. Would you tell Anne that she is a genetic male? Would you tell her husband? Her doctor told neither. Anne's vagina was surgically lengthened, she was counselled to consider adoption, and as far as the authors know she remained happily married and unaware of her chromosomal sex.

The authors add a counterweight. They have heard from several people with **partial androgen insensitivity**, living as women, who recommended **full disclosure**: they had faced sexual ambiguities throughout their lives, and learning the cause helped them. The book leaves the question open; the point is that the "right" answer is genuinely contested.

### The Case of the Little Girl Who Grew into a Boy

The second patient, whom the book calls **Elaine**, sought treatment in **1972**. She had been born with **somewhat ambiguous external genitals** but had been raised as a girl without difficulty until puberty, when she quite suddenly began to develop **male secondary sex characteristics**, which distressed her greatly.

Her treatment had two parts:

- **Surgical**: her vagina was enlarged and her clitoris reduced in size.
- **Hormonal**: her androgen release was suppressed so that her **own oestrogen** could feminise her body.

After treatment she developed into a young woman whose only remaining signs of the masculinising episode were **narrow hips and a husky voice** (Money & Ehrhardt, 1972).

### Explaining Elaine: adrenogenital syndrome and congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Elaine had **adrenogenital syndrome**, an atypical form of sexual development affecting about **1 in 10,000** people. Follow the causal chain carefully, because the two names are often confused:

1. The underlying defect is **congenital adrenal hyperplasia**: a **congenital deficiency in the release of cortisol** from the adrenal cortex.
2. Low cortisol produces **compensatory adrenal hyperactivity**. (The usual mechanism, standard physiology rather than something the book spells out, is loss of cortisol's negative feedback: the pituitary keeps releasing ACTH, which keeps driving and enlarging the adrenal cortex.)
3. The overactive adrenal cortex releases **excessive adrenal androgens** (Bramble et al., 2017; Gondim, Teles, & Barroso, 2018).

The condition causes an array of serious health problems (Turcu & Auchus, 2015), but its best-studied effect is on sexual development, and the effect depends on genetic sex:

- **Genetic males**: little effect beyond an **earlier onset of puberty**.
- **Genetic females**: major effects. At birth they typically have an **enlarged clitoris and partially fused labia**, yet their **ovaries and internal ducts are usually typical**: by the time the excess adrenal androgens appear, it is **too late** for them to stimulate the Wolffian system.

That last point is a rule test in itself: the external genitals were still responsive when the extra androgens arrived, but the critical window for Wolffian development had already passed, so the internal ducts stayed female.

> **Trap.** **Adrenogenital syndrome** (the book's name for the clinical picture) is **caused by** **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (CAH), a congenital **cortisol deficiency** with **excess adrenal androgens**. Many exam papers simply say "CAH". Do not confuse the two cases: Anne (CAIS) has **XY** chromosomes and androgens that cannot act; Elaine (adrenogenital syndrome) has **XX** chromosomes and too much androgen. (Beyond the book: the commonest cause of CAH is 21-hydroxylase deficiency.)

### Treated adrenogenital females: behaviour and sexual preference

Most female cases are now identified **at birth**. The external genitals are surgically altered to a more typically female form, and **cortisol** is given to bring circulating adrenal androgens down. After early treatment these girls develop as typical females, except that **menstruation tends to start later** than usual. Because they were exposed to excess androgens before birth but not afterwards, they are useful participants in studies of how **fetal androgen exposure** affects psychosexual development.

What such studies show:

- As teenagers, early-treated adrenogenital girls tend to show **more tomboyishness, greater strength and more aggression** than most girls, and tend to **prefer boys' clothes and toys** and to **play mainly with boys** (Pasterski et al., 2011).
- But many teenage girls without the condition show the same characteristics. The behaviour of treated adrenogenital females, although tilted towards the stereotypically masculine, usually falls **within the typical range for female behaviour**.

The most interesting questions concern adult romantic and sexual preferences:

- They seem to **lag behind** other women in **dating and sex**, perhaps because of their **delayed menarche**.
- **Most are heterosexual**, although a few studies have found an increased tendency to express **interest in other women** and to be less involved in heterosexual relationships (Bramble et al., 2017; Piaggio, 2014).
- These slight differences may not be **direct** effects of early androgens at all. Some adrenogenital girls have ambiguous genitalia and certain male-typical characteristics (body hair, for example), and these may lead to **different experiences**, which could themselves shape development (Jordan-Young, 2012).

That last point is the methodological lesson. A correlation between prenatal androgen and later behaviour does not identify the route: the hormone might act on the brain, or it might act on the body, which then changes how the person is treated.

### Before cortisol therapy (1950)

**Cortisol therapy** was developed in **1950**. Before then, genetic females with adrenogenital syndrome went untreated. Some were raised as boys and some as girls, and the direction their bodies took at puberty was unpredictable. In some, **adrenal androgens** predominated and masculinised the body; in others, **ovarian oestrogens** predominated and feminised it. So some children raised as boys were transformed at puberty into females, and some raised as girls were transformed into males, sometimes with devastating emotional consequences. Elaine's history, a girl suddenly masculinising at puberty, is the pattern this describes.

### The Case of the Twin Who Lost His Penis

The third case is one of the most famous in the literature on sexual development. **David Reimer** was a **monozygotic twin** whose penis was accidentally destroyed during **circumcision at the age of 7 months**. There was then no satisfactory way to surgically replace the penis. **John Money**, an expert in such matters, recommended that the child be **castrated and raised as a girl**, that an **artificial vagina** be created, and that **oestrogen** be given at puberty to feminise the body. After much anguish, the parents followed his advice.

### Money's report and the nature-nurture test

**Money's (1975)** report of this case of **ablatio penis** (loss of the penis) was influential. Many saw it as the ultimate test of the **nature-nurture** question as it applies to **gender identity** and behaviour (Chapter 2). It pitted the masculinising effects of male genes and male prenatal hormones against the effects of being reared as a girl. And the case had a built-in control that no laboratory study of humans could arrange: a **genetically identical twin brother** raised as a boy.

According to Money, the outcome strongly supported his **social-learning theory of gender identity**. In **1975**, when the child was **12**, Money reported that "she" had developed as a typical girl. That seemed to confirm his prediction that gonadectomy, surgical alteration of the genitals and rearing as a girl would **override** the masculinising effects of male genes and early androgens.

### Diamond and Sigmundson (1997): the follow-up

A long-term follow-up by **impartial experts**, **Diamond and Sigmundson (1997)**, told an entirely different story. Despite having female genitalia and being raised as a girl, the child **developed along male lines**. The authors' conclusion, citing **Reiner (1997)**, is that the organ that determines the course of psychosocial development is the **brain, not the genitals**.

The follow-up describes a childhood in which the child consistently gravitated towards boys' games and activities and showed little interest in dolls or other conventionally feminine pursuits. By the age of about **7** the child felt like a boy, and behaved accordingly in everyday ways (for example, trying to urinate standing up). At school there was teasing by other girls and violent retaliation, which led to expulsion. When an **oestrogen regimen** began at **12**, the child resisted being feminised, disliking the developing breasts. At **14** he decided to **live as a male and took the name David**; his father then told him the full story of his infancy, and for the first time his experience made sense to him.

### David's adult life and its clinical lesson

David then requested **androgen treatment**, a **mastectomy** (surgical removal of the breasts) and **phalloplasty** (surgical construction of a penis). He became a popular young man, **married at 25**, and **adopted his wife's children**. The androgen treatment restored his ability to ejaculate and experience orgasm, but his early castration had **permanently removed his reproductive capacity**.

He remained bitter about his early treatment and about being unable to father children. To spare others the same experience, he cooperated in writing his biography, ***As Nature Made Him*** (**Colapinto, 2000**). He never recovered from the emotional damage, and he died by suicide.

The book draws a clinical conclusion from the case: the practice of **surgically modifying a person's sex at birth should be curtailed**. Irrevocable treatments should wait until **early puberty**, when the person's **gender identity** has emerged and a compatible course of treatment can be chosen.

> **Trap.** Get the people right. **John Money** recommended the reassignment and published the favourable **1975** report (social-learning theory of gender identity). **Diamond and Sigmundson (1997)** published the follow-up showing the reassignment failed. **Colapinto (2000)** wrote the biography *As Nature Made Him*. The injury was at **7 months**, during **circumcision**; the twins were **monozygotic**.

> **Trap.** **Ablatio penis** is the name of the *injury* (loss of the penis), not of a syndrome of development. In the book's self-test, the clue "David Reimer, destruction of penis" has the answer *ablatio penis*.

### Do the exceptional cases prove the rule?

Do current theories of hormones and sexual development pass the test of these three cases? The authors' answer is **yes**. The theories do not supply every answer, especially for brain and behaviour, but they have contributed a great deal to understanding exceptional patterns of sexual differentiation of the **body**. Anne's features are explained trait by trait; Elaine's typical internal ducts and atypical external genitals are explained by the timing of her androgen exposure; David's case shows the limits of what rearing can override.

Notice one more thing about the three cases: **each person was male in some respects and female in others**. Anne was chromosomally and gonadally male but anatomically and psychologically female; Elaine was chromosomally and internally female but partly masculinised externally; David was genetically male, reared female, and identified as male. Each case is therefore a serious challenge to the mamawawa: female and male are not opposite, mutually exclusive categories (Ainsworth, 2015; Carothers & Reis, 2013).

## 13.5 Effects of Gonadal Hormones on Adults

Once an individual is sexually mature, gonadal hormones start to **activate** reproductive behaviour. These **activational effects** are the subject of the first two sections of this module, males then females. The third section deals with **anabolic steroids**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition's module on adults has exactly three sections: male behaviour, female behaviour and anabolic steroids. Earlier editions of Pinel also carried a section on the **neuroprotective effects of oestradiol**, which the 11th edition no longer includes; if an older-key question raises it, the general idea was that oestradiol can protect neurons against damage, but it is not part of this chapter's current content. (The nearest 11th-edition point is in 13.3: females recover better than males from traumatic brain injury.)

### Orchidectomy: Bremer's (1959) 215 Norwegian men

The clearest demonstration that gonadal hormones activate male sexual behaviour is what happens when they are removed. **Bremer (1959)** reviewed the cases of **215 orchidectomised Norwegian men**. About **half** of them had committed sex-related offences and had agreed to castration in exchange for shorter prison terms.

Two generalisations come out of Bremer's review:

1. **Orchidectomy reduces sexual interest and behaviour.**
2. **The rate and degree of the loss are variable.** The spread was wide:
   - about **half** the men became **completely asexual within a few weeks** of the operation;
   - others quickly **lost the ability to achieve an erection** but continued to experience some sexual **interest and pleasure**;
   - a **few** continued to **copulate successfully**, though somewhat less enthusiastically, for the whole duration of the study.

There were bodily changes too: **less hair** on the torso, limbs and face; **more fat on the hips and chest**; **softer skin**; and **reduced muscle mass**.

The forensic result is the number most likely to be examined. Of the **102 sex offenders** in Bremer's study, only **three** were reconvicted of sex offences. On that basis Bremer recommended castration as an effective **treatment of last resort** for male sex offenders.

> **Trap.** Keep Bremer's numbers apart: **215** men reviewed; **102** were sex offenders; **3** reoffended; about **half** became completely asexual within **weeks**. The year is **1959**, and the men were **Norwegian**.

### Why some castrated men stay sexually active

Testicular hormones are cleared from the body within **days** of orchidectomy, yet some men in Bremer's sample stayed sexually active for **months**. Why? It has been suggested that **adrenal androgens** (recall from 13.1 that the adrenal cortex releases small amounts of all the sex steroids) may help maintain sexual activity in some castrated men. The book is careful to add that there is **no direct evidence** for this hypothesis.

### Testosterone as the active ingredient

Orchidectomy removes, at a stroke (or, as the authors put it, in two strokes), a pair of glands that release **many** hormones. Because **testosterone** is the major testicular hormone, the major effects of orchidectomy have generally been attributed to losing testosterone, rather than to losing some other testicular hormone or to some non-hormonal effect of the surgery. That attribution needs testing, and the test is **replacement**: if giving back testosterone alone reverses the effects, testosterone was the missing ingredient. The therapeutic effects of **replacement injections** of testosterone have confirmed the assumption.

### The Case of the Man Who Lost and Regained His Manhood

The **first case report of testosterone replacement therapy** (de Kruif, 1945) concerned a **38-year-old World War I veteran**. In **1918**, aged **19**, he had been castrated by a **shell fragment** that destroyed his testes while sparing his penis.

His body had become soft, with little apparent muscle, his hips had widened and his shoulders narrowed. He married even though his doctors had warned him that he would be **impotent** (unable to achieve an erection), and, as they predicted, he could not achieve an erection when he tried to have intercourse.

He was then given **intramuscular injections of testosterone**. After the **fifth injection**, erections came quickly and lasted. After **12 weeks** of treatment he had gained **18 pounds**, mostly muscle, and had outgrown all his clothes. The case report presents testosterone as having restored the "manhood" the shell fragment took away, and since that first trial testosterone replacement has restored sexuality to many men.

One limit: testosterone does **not** cure the **sterility** (inability to reproduce) of men who lack functional testes. It replaces the hormone, not the sperm-producing tissue.

> **Trap.** The veteran was castrated in **1918** at **19**; the report (de Kruif, **1945**) describes him at **38**. Erections returned after the **fifth** injection; **18 lb** gained in **12 weeks**. Replacement restored sexual function but **not fertility**.

### Two widespread assumptions about testosterone, both wrong

Because testosterone is **necessary** for male sexual behaviour, two assumptions have become widespread:

1. that a man's level of sexuality is a function of how much testosterone is in his blood; and
2. that a man's sex drive can be increased by raising his testosterone level.

**Both are incorrect.** In healthy men, **sex drive and testosterone levels are uncorrelated**, and **testosterone injections do not increase their sex drive**. The explanation is that each healthy man has **far more testosterone than is needed** to activate the neural circuits of his sexual behaviour, and having more than that minimum confers no advantage. Necessary is not the same as rate-limiting.

### Grunt and Young (1952): more is not better

A classic experiment by **Grunt and Young (1952)** shows the point directly, in **male guinea pigs**:

1. They first **rated the sexual behaviour** of each male.
2. On the basis of these ratings they divided the males into three groups: **low, medium and high sex drive**.
3. They **castrated** all of them. Sexual behaviour in every group fell to **negligible levels within a few weeks**.
4. They then gave all the animals a series of **testosterone replacement injections**, and every animal received the **same large dose**.

The result is the elegant part. Sexual behaviour recovered, but each animal returned only to **its own previous level**: low-drive animals became low-drive again, high-drive animals high-drive again. The same, very large dose did not make the low-drive animals match the high-drive ones. So the differences in sex drive between individuals were not differences in the amount of testosterone; something else (most plausibly in the neural circuits it acts on) set each animal's level. With respect to testosterone and sexual behaviour, **more is not necessarily better**.

> **Trap.** In Grunt and Young (1952), equal large replacement doses restored each guinea pig to **its own pre-castration level**; they did **not** equalise the groups or raise the low-drive group. The conclusion is "testosterone is necessary but its level does not set sex drive", which is why testosterone injections do not boost the libido of healthy men.

### Dihydrotestosterone: rodents versus primates

**Dihydrotestosterone (DHT)** is an androgen that **cannot be aromatised** to oestradiol. It **restores copulatory behaviour in castrated male primates**, but **fails** to restore it in **castrated male rodents** (Hull & Dominguez, 2007). The interpretation follows from 13.3:

- In **primates**, testosterone's restoration of copulation appears to be a **direct** effect of testosterone (so a non-aromatisable androgen works).
- In **rodents**, it appears to depend on **oestradiol aromatised from testosterone** (so an androgen that cannot be converted fails).

This is the adult, activational counterpart of the developmental finding that aromatisation masculinises the rodent brain but not the human brain.

> **Trap.** DHT is the standard "non-aromatisable androgen" in both the developmental (13.3) and activational (13.5) evidence. DHT restores copulation in castrated **primates**, **not** rodents. If you remember "rodents need aromatisation", both facts follow.

### Female sexual behaviour: the oestrous cycle in rats and guinea pigs

Sexually mature female **rats and guinea pigs** show **4-day cycles** of gonadal hormone release. The sequence:

1. Over the **2 days before ovulation**, secretion of **oestrogens** by the developing **follicle** (the ovarian structure in which eggs mature) gradually increases.
2. As the egg is released, there is a **sudden surge of progesterone**.
3. These surges of oestrogens and progesterone start **oestrus** (spelt *estrus* in the book): a period of **12 to 18 hours** during which the female is:
   - **fertile**;
   - **receptive** (likely to adopt the lordosis posture when mounted);
   - **proceptive** (likely to engage in behaviours that attract the male);
   - **sexually attractive** (smelling of chemicals that attract males).

The cycle of sexual receptivity is the **oestrous cycle**.

### Ovariectomy and hormone replacement in rodents

The tight link between the hormone cycle and the oestrous cycle in rats, guinea pigs and many other mammals suggests that female sexual behaviour in these species is under hormonal control. Two findings confirm it:

- **Ovariectomy** of female rats and guinea pigs produces a **rapid decline** in both **proceptive and receptive** behaviour.
- **Oestrus can be induced** in ovariectomised rats and guinea pigs by an injection of **oestradiol followed about a day and a half later by an injection of progesterone**, mimicking the natural sequence.

> **Trap.** The order of the natural sequence and of the replacement protocol is **oestrogen first, then progesterone** (about 1.5 days later). Rodent oestrus lasts **12 to 18 hours** in a **4-day** cycle; the human cycle in 13.1 is about **28 days**.

### Female primates: desire outside fertility

Female primates, including women, differ from female rats and guinea pigs in how hormones control their sexual behaviour. Female primates are the **only female mammals motivated to copulate during periods of non-fertility** (Ziegler, 2007). Hormonal state still matters, though: women show even **greater sexual desire**, **more sexual fantasies** and a **greater tendency to initiate sex** during their **fertile** periods (Shimoda, Campbell, & Barton, 2018). And **ovariectomy** has been shown to **reduce sexual desire and the frequency of sexual fantasies** in women (Cappelletti & Wallen, 2016).

So in primates the ovarian hormones *modulate* desire rather than gating it completely, as they do in rodents.

### Which ovarian hormone drives female desire?

The obvious next question is which ovarian hormone is responsible. The evidence is mixed, and the book sets it out finding by finding:

- In **ovariectomised women**, **oestradiol** supplements restored sexual desire and pleasure; so did **testosterone** supplements (Motta-Mena & Puts, 2017).
- In **ovariectomised and adrenalectomised rhesus monkeys** (both sources of sex steroids removed), replacement injections of **testosterone, but not oestradiol**, increased **proceptivity** (Everitt & Herbert, 1972).
- In **postmenopausal women**, some studies found that **oestrogen therapy** renewed sexual interest and others did not; likewise, only **some** studies found that **testosterone** did (Cappelletti & Wallen, 2016; Motta-Mena & Puts, 2017).
- Still other studies found that testosterone increased desire in postmenopausal women **only at unnaturally high levels** (Davis & Braunstein, 2012).

The book's summary is deliberately modest: **both oestradiol and testosterone can influence female sexual behaviour, at least in some cases** (Motta-Mena & Puts, 2017). Notice how far this is from the mamawawa. An "androgen" is one of the two candidate hormones for female sexual desire, and in the monkey study it was the *only* one that worked.

> **Trap.** **Everitt and Herbert (1972)**: **rhesus monkeys**, ovariectomised **and adrenalectomised**, **testosterone** (not oestradiol) increased **proceptivity**. Do not confuse this with **Everitt (1990)** in 13.6, which is about **amygdala lesions in male rats**.

### Anabolic steroid abuse

**Anabolic steroids** are steroids with **anabolic** (growth-promoting) effects; testosterone is one of them. Yet testosterone itself makes a poor anabolic drug, for two reasons: it is **broken down soon after injection**, and it has **undesirable side effects**. Chemists have synthesised many potent, **long-acting** anabolic steroids, but **none without side effects**.

### The scale of use

The book describes the current situation as an **epidemic**. Many competitive athletes and bodybuilders take very large doses, and many other people use them for **cosmetic** purposes. Because steroids are illegal in many countries, the estimates are probably **underestimates**.

- **Elite athletes** began using anabolic steroids in the **1950s** to improve performance.
- By the **1980s** they were in widespread use in the **general population**, often by **adolescents** (Calatayud et al., 2019) and often for **cosmetic** reasons (Figueiredo & Silva, 2014).
- Global estimates of **lifetime prevalence** are **6.4 percent for males** and **1.6 percent for females** (Sagoe et al., 2014).

### Health risks

Although the general public has been using anabolic steroids for about **40 years**, their risks are still not fully understood, for three reasons: use is **illegal and therefore concealed**; there are **more than 100 different testosterone derivatives**; and users vary greatly in their **patterns and doses**. Hazardous effects are well documented, but they typically occur in only a **small proportion** of users (van Amsterdam, Opperhuizen, & Hartgens, 2010).

The documented hazards, roughly from most to least serious:

- **Cardiovascular effects** that have been linked to **premature death** (Angell et al., 2012; Kanayama et al., 2010).
- With **oral** anabolic steroids, adverse effects on the **liver**, including **liver tumours**.
- Less dangerous but still troubling (Kraus et al., 2012): **acne**; **fluid retention** causing general swelling; **muscle pain** and **muscle spasms**; **blood in the urine**; **nausea and vomiting**; **bleeding of the tongue**; and episodes of **anger and depression**.

### Do anabolic steroids enhance sexual function?

The main question in the context of this chapter: do the enormous doses many users take enhance their sexual function? The book's answer is an emphatic **no**. The effects appear to be **uniformly disruptive**.

**In males**, the mechanism runs straight through the feedback loop of 13.1:

1. High circulating levels of anabolic steroids exert **negative feedback** on the hypothalamus and pituitary, **reducing gonadotropin release**.
2. Less gonadotropin means **less testicular activity**.
3. The result can be **testicular atrophy** (wasting of the testes) and **sterility**.
4. **Gynaecomastia** (breast growth in males; *gynecomastia* in the book) can also occur, presumably because the anabolic steroids are **aromatised to oestrogens**.

**In females**, anabolic steroids can cause: **amenorrhoea** (cessation of menstruation) and **sterility**; **hirsutism** (excessive growth of body hair); **growth of the clitoris**; a **masculine body shape**; **baldness**; **shrinking of the breasts**; and a **deeper, coarser voice**. Unfortunately, some of these masculinising effects appear to be **irreversible**.

> **Trap.** Steroid users take an **androgen**, yet the male user's **testes shrink** and he may grow **breasts**. Both follow from the chapter's mechanisms: negative feedback suppresses gonadotropins (testicular atrophy, sterility), and aromatisation converts the excess androgen to oestrogen (gynaecomastia). An option saying anabolic steroids "increase testicular size" or "enhance sexual function" is wrong.

### Two major concerns

The book closes the section with two particular concerns:

1. **Use during puberty**, before the developmental programmes of sexual differentiation are complete, is **especially risky**, because it can interfere with organisational effects as well as activational ones.
2. Many adverse effects may take **years** to appear. Users who notice few immediate problems may pay the price later.

## 13.6 Brain Mechanisms of Sexual Behaviour

Human sexual behaviour is complex and varied: practices differ between cultures and between individuals within a culture, and a person's preferences are often changed by experience (Hoffman, Peterson, & Garner, 2012). Even so, four brain structures have well-established roles in sexual behaviour: the **cortex**, the **hypothalamus**, the **amygdala** and the **ventral striatum**. This module takes them in turn.

### Studying the brain and sex: complementary weaknesses

What is known comes from both human volunteers and non-human subjects (Georgiadis, Kringelbach, & Pfaus, 2012), and each source has its own blind spots.

**Human studies** rely mainly on **functional brain imaging**, which brings serious limitations. Studying the neural activity of copulation in humans is virtually impossible with current technology: there are cultural constraints, participants must remain **motionless** during a scan, and it is hard to fit **two active adults** into a conventional scanner. So human research has concentrated on **sexual arousal**, occasionally to orgasm, triggered by **sexually provocative visual images** or by **masturbation**.

**Laboratory animal studies** (most often rats) avoid those problems. Invasive techniques allow brain mechanisms to be studied in detail, and natural patterns of copulation can be observed. But several important aspects of sexual activity are difficult or impossible to study in animals: **sexual imagery**, **delayed orgasm**, **female orgasm**, and **feelings of sexual attraction**.

The important point is that the weaknesses of the two kinds of subject are **complementary**: what one cannot study, the other often can. Knowledge in this field therefore usually depends on **combining** both kinds of research (Georgiadis et al., 2012).

### Cortex and sexual activity

Because sex is so fundamental to reproduction, and so to the survival of the species, it was once assumed to be run by **archaic circuits in the brain stem** of early evolutionary origin. That assumption is **no longer tenable**. Functional imaging studies of volunteers exposed to sexually arousing stimuli routinely record **widespread cortical activation** (Georgiadis, 2012; Stoléru et al., 2012). In **both** females and males, the areas often activated are the **occipitotemporal, inferotemporal, parietal, orbitofrontal, medial prefrontal, insular, cingulate and premotor** cortices.

Two details are especially testable:

- Activity in **secondary visual cortex** (occipitotemporal and inferotemporal cortex) occurs during sexual arousal **even when the eyes are closed** (Georgiadis, 2012), which suggests it reflects imagery rather than simply visual input.
- Activity in **prefrontal cortex is suppressed during orgasm** (Stoléru et al., 2012).

Presumably the cortex mediates the most **complex** aspects of sexual experience: feelings of release and loss of control, changes in self-awareness, disturbances in the awareness of space and time, and feelings of love.

> **Trap.** Prefrontal activity **falls** during orgasm; it does not rise. And secondary visual cortex is active during arousal **with eyes closed**. Both are single-word reversal traps.

### Hypothalamus: the sexually dimorphic nucleus

Interest in the hypothalamus was driven by the discovery of a specific structural difference between the female and male hypothalamus. In **1978**, **Gorski** and his colleagues found a nucleus in the **medial preoptic area** of the **rat** hypothalamus that was **several times larger in males**. They named it the **sexually dimorphic nucleus**.

Its development is a textbook example of an organisational effect with a precise time window:

- **At birth**, the sexually dimorphic nuclei of male and female rats are the **same size**.
- In the **first few days after birth**, the male nuclei **grow rapidly** and the female nuclei do not.
- The growth of the male nucleus is normally triggered by **oestradiol aromatised from testosterone** (**Gorski, 1980**), the aromatisation hypothesis of 13.3 in action.

The book's Figure 13.12 (Gorski, 1980) shows the manipulations, which test the window from both sides:

| Group | Adult nucleus size (relative to a normal male) |
|---|---|
| **Female**, placebo injection on **day 4** | Small, about a fifth |
| **Female**, **testosterone** injection on **day 4** | More than doubled, a little under half |
| **Male**, castrated on **day 1** | About half |
| **Male**, castrated on **day 4** | Almost normal |
| Normal male control | 100 percent |

Read across the male rows: castration on day 1 removes testosterone before most of the growth has happened, so the nucleus stays small; castration on day 4 comes after much of the growth, so the nucleus is almost normal. Read the female rows: testosterone on day 4 enlarges a female nucleus substantially. Both directions show that perinatal testosterone (via oestradiol) drives the size difference.

Since the discovery in rats, many other sex differences in hypothalamic anatomy have been identified in rats and other species: differences in the **volume** of various nuclei, **cell number**, **connectivity**, **cell morphology**, **neural activity** and **neurotransmitter type**, all influenced by **perinatal exposure to oestradiol** (Lenz & McCarthy, 2010).

> **Trap.** The sexually dimorphic nucleus was **discovered in 1978** (Gorski and colleagues) and the testosterone-manipulation data are from **Gorski (1980)**. It lies in the **medial preoptic area** of the **rat** hypothalamus, is **larger in males**, is the **same size at birth**, and its growth depends on **oestradiol aromatised from testosterone**.

### The medial preoptic area and male sexual behaviour

The **medial preoptic area** (which contains the sexually dimorphic nucleus) plays a key role in **male** sexual behaviour (Will, Hull, & Dominguez, 2014). Four findings converge on that conclusion:

1. **Destroying the entire area abolishes sexual behaviour in the males of all mammalian species studied** (Calabrò et al., 2019).
2. In **females**, medial preoptic lesions do **not** eliminate female sexual behaviour, but they **do** eliminate the **male** sexual behaviours (such as mounting) that females sometimes show. This is the finding from 13.3 that shows male and female behaviours are separate dimensions.
3. **Electrical stimulation** of the medial preoptic area **elicits copulatory behaviour** in male rats (Guadarrama-Bazante & Rodríguez-Manzo, 2019).
4. Copulatory behaviour can be **reinstated in castrated male rats** by **testosterone implants placed in the medial preoptic area**.

The logic is the full set: lesion removes the behaviour, stimulation produces it, and a hormone delivered only to this site restores what castration took away. That last finding also connects the activational effects of 13.5 to a specific brain site.

### The ventromedial nucleus and female sexual behaviour

In **female rats**, the **ventromedial nuclei (VMN)** of the hypothalamus contain circuits that appear critical for sexual behaviour.

- Female rats with **bilateral VMN lesions** do **not display lordosis**.
- The effect seems to be mediated by **oestradiol receptors** in the VMN: females whose **VMN oestrogen receptors are selectively inactivated** also fail to show lordosis (Ventura-Aquino & Paredes, 2020).

> **High-yield.** **Medial preoptic area → male** sexual behaviour (lesion abolishes it, stimulation elicits it, testosterone implants restore it). **Ventromedial nucleus → female** sexual behaviour (lesion or VMN oestrogen-receptor inactivation abolishes lordosis).

> **Trap.** The **VMN** here is the **ventromedial nucleus** of the hypothalamus in its **sexual** role (female lordosis). Chapter 12's **VMH** (ventromedial hypothalamus) was the supposed satiety centre. Do not cross the two chapters' functions, and do not swap MPOA (male) and VMN (female).

### Amygdala and sexual activity

The **amygdalas**, in the left and right medial temporal lobes, play a general role in emotion and social cognition (Olson et al., 2013; Rolls, 2015). In sexual behaviour, they seem to help **identify potential mating partners** on the basis of **sensory social signals**, which are mainly **visual in humans** and **olfactory in rats**. Three lines of research support this.

1. **Bilateral amygdala lesions in primates, including humans.** These produce a cluster of effects known as the **Klüver-Bucy syndrome** (Chapter 17), and the sexual effects are striking (Clay et al., 2019). Humans show **flat affect**, **hypersexuality**, and a complete inability to **direct their sexual advances to appropriate partners or locations** (Calabró et al., 2019).
2. **Everitt's (1990) classic study** of **bilateral amygdalar lesions in male rats**. The lesions disrupted copulatory behaviour, but **not** because the rats had trouble copulating. The deficit arose because they could **not restrict their advances to receptive females**. The motor act was intact; partner selection was not.
3. **Human responses to erotic images.** Men are more likely than women to be sexually aroused by erotic images (Stark et al., 2019), and in several functional imaging studies, erotic images produced **greater amygdalar activation in men** (Stoléru, 2012; Calabró et al., 2019).

> **Trap.** Everitt's (1990) amygdala-lesioned rats **could** copulate; their problem was **choosing receptive partners**. If an option says amygdala lesions abolish the ability to copulate, it misses the point of the study.

### Ventral striatum and sexual activity

Sexual activity is among the most pleasurable human activities, so it is not surprising that it acts as a powerful **reinforcer**. **Pfaus and colleagues (2012)** showed that **both male and female rats** learn to **prefer partners, locations and odours** associated with copulation, a form of conditioned preference.

Because orgasm is associated with pleasure, it is also unsurprising that the **ventral striatum** (the region containing the nucleus accumbens, part of the reward circuitry of Chapter 15) is activated in human volunteers by sexually provocative images (Stoléru et al., 2012). Research on rats links activity in this area to both the **anticipation** and the **experience** of sex and of other forms of pleasure (Matsumoto et al., 2012).

|---|---|
| **Cortex** | Mediates the most complex aspects of sexual experience |
| **Hypothalamus** | Medial preoptic area: male sexual behaviour. Ventromedial nucleus: female sexual behaviour |
| **Amygdala** | Identifying potential mating partners |

## 13.7 Sexual Orientation and Gender Identity

### Terms: sexual orientation and gender identity

Up to here the chapter has not systematically addressed **sexual orientation**. People differ in it:

- **Heterosexual**: women attracted to men, or men attracted to women.
- **Gay** (men attracted to men) or **lesbian** (women attracted to women).
- **Bisexual**: sexually attracted to both men and women.
- **Asexual**: not sexually attracted to others.

Other sorts of orientation exist too, for example a preference for two or more simultaneous partners rather than one (Fausto-Sterling, 2019).

People also vary in **gender identity**: the gender a person most identifies with, which may be **woman**, **man**, **transgender** (a person who identifies as a man, a woman, or some intersection of these), or another gender category.

Ending the chapter on these topics is fitting, the authors say, because they underline its **anti-mamawawa** theme more strongly than anything else.

> **Trap.** Sexual orientation is about **whom one is attracted to**; gender identity is about **which gender one identifies as**. They are separate dimensions, and the final section of the chapter is built on their independence.

### Sexual orientation and genes: Bailey and Pillard

For a variety of historical reasons, research on sexual orientation has been dominated by heterosexual, gay and lesbian participants (Bogaert & Skorska, 2020), so this section deals only with research on those groups.

Research shows that differences in sexual orientation are **influenced by genes**. The twin studies:

| Study | Probands | Monozygotic co-twin also gay/lesbian | Dizygotic co-twin also gay/lesbian |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bailey & Pillard (1991)** | Gay men with twin brothers | **52 percent** | **22 percent** |
| **Bailey et al. (1993)** | Lesbian women with twin sisters | **48 percent** | **16 percent** |

The reasoning: monozygotic twins share all their genes and dizygotic twins about half, while both kinds of twin pair share a family environment to a similar degree. The much higher concordance in monozygotic pairs therefore points to a genetic influence. Notice also what the figures rule out: concordance in identical twins is only about half, so genes are clearly **not** the whole story.

> **Trap.** **52 / 22** for men (Bailey & Pillard, **1991**); **48 / 16** for women (Bailey et al., **1993**). Monozygotic concordance is around 50 percent, which shows a genetic influence but also shows that orientation is **not** genetically determined.

### Sexual orientation and early hormones

Many people mistakenly assume that gay men and lesbian women have **lower levels of sex hormones**. They do not. Heterosexual and gay men **do not differ in circulating hormone levels**. Moreover, **orchidectomy reduces the sexual behaviour of both heterosexual and gay men but does not redirect it**, and **replacement injections simply reactivate the preferences that existed before surgery**. Adult hormones activate sexual behaviour; they do not set its direction.

### Orientation is discovered, not chosen

Another common assumption is that sexual orientation is a **choice**. The book is direct: it is not. People **discover** their sexual orientation rather than choose it. It seems to develop **very early**, and a child's first indication of their orientation usually does not change with maturity. That raises the obvious question: could **perinatal hormone exposure** be the early event that shapes it?

### Animal evidence on perinatal hormones and partner preference

Experiments manipulating perinatal hormone levels are not feasible in humans, so the work has been done on non-human species, and a consistent pattern has emerged. In species whose early sex-hormone exposure has been altered (**rats, hamsters, ferrets, pigs, zebra finches and dogs**), it is not unusual to see males showing female-typical sexual behaviour (such as being mounted by other males) and females showing male-typical behaviour (such as mounting other females).

But the defining feature of orientation is **sexual preference**, so the key studies looked at the **sex of the preferred partner**. In general:

- **perinatal castration of males** increased their adult preference for sex with **males**;
- **perinatal testosterone exposure in females** increased their adult preference for sex with **females** (Henley, Nunez, & Clemens, 2011).

The book weighs this evidence both ways. Caution is needed in extending laboratory results to humans: human sexuality has profound cognitive and emotional components with no counterpart in laboratory animals. Yet a pattern that runs so consistently through so many species would be a mistake to dismiss as irrelevant to humans.

### Ehrhardt and colleagues (1985): diethylstilbestrol

There are indications that perinatal hormones influence human sexual orientation, but the evidence is **controversial** (Fausto-Sterling, 2019; Bogaert & Skorska, 2020). The best-known example is the **quasiexperimental** study of **Ehrhardt and her colleagues (1985)**.

- They interviewed **adult women whose mothers had been exposed to diethylstilbestrol** (a **synthetic oestrogen**) during pregnancy.
- These women reported being **significantly more sexually attracted to women** than a group of **matched controls**.
- Ehrhardt and colleagues concluded that perinatal oestrogen exposure does encourage attraction to women, but that the effect is **weak**: the sexual behaviour of **all but 1 of the 30** participants was primarily **heterosexual**.

The effect **could not be replicated** (Puts & Motta-Mena, 2018), yet the study is still widely cited as evidence that perinatal hormones influence sexual orientation.

It is "quasiexperimental" because the researchers did not assign the hormone exposure; it happened for medical reasons, and they compared the exposed group with matched controls. That is why the result cannot establish causation as firmly as a true experiment would.

> **Trap.** Diethylstilbestrol is a synthetic **oestrogen**, not an androgen. The study is **Ehrhardt et al. (1985)**; **29 of 30** exposed women were primarily heterosexual; the effect was **not replicated**. Do not confuse this Ehrhardt with **Money and Ehrhardt (1972)**, the source of Elaine's adrenogenital case.

### The fraternal birth order effect

A more promising line of research is the **fraternal birth order effect**: the reliable finding that the probability of a man being gay **increases with the number of older brothers** he has (Alexander et al., 2011; Blanchard, 2018; Bogaert & Skorska, 2020).

Two refinements make the effect informative:

- A study of **blended families** (biologically related siblings raised together with adopted or step-siblings) found that the effect depends on the **number of males previously born to the same mother**, **not** on the number of older males a boy is **reared with** (Bogaert & Skorska, 2020). That rules out an explanation in terms of growing up with older boys and points instead to something about the mother's previous pregnancies.
- The effect is relatively **large**: the probability of a man being gay rises by **33.3 percent for every older brother** he has (Balthazart, 2018).

### The maternal immune hypothesis

The **maternal immune hypothesis** has been proposed to explain the fraternal birth order effect. The current evidence favours the idea that mothers develop an **immune response to masculinising hormones from male fetuses** (Balthazart, 2018). With each male pregnancy the response grows, so the mother's immune system increasingly **suppresses the effects of masculinising hormones** in her **younger sons** (Bogaert & Skorska, 2020).

Supporting evidence comes from a recent study of mothers (Bogaert et al., 2018; see Balthazart, 2018): the **mothers of gay men who had older brothers** showed a **stronger immune reaction** against a particular **protein with an important role in brain development**.

Notice how well the hypothesis fits the blended-family result: an immune response that builds up across pregnancies would depend on how many sons the mother has *carried*, not on how many boys are in the household.

> **Trap.** The fraternal birth order effect concerns **older brothers** (not sisters, not younger brothers) and **men's** orientation. It depends on the **biological mother's** previous sons, not on boys one is **raised with**. The figure is **33.3 percent per older brother**. The explanation is the **maternal immune** hypothesis.

### What triggers sexual attraction? Adrenal maturation at about age 10

Most people in Western countries, **whatever their orientation** (heterosexual, bisexual, gay or lesbian), experience their **first intense feelings of sexual attraction at about 10 years of age** (Fausto-Sterling, 2019). That does not fit the usual assumption that sexual interest is triggered by **puberty**, which currently tends to occur at about **10.5 years in females** and **11.5 years in males**.

**McClintock and Herdt (1996)** suggested that the emergence of sexual attraction may be stimulated by **adrenal cortex steroids**. Unlike gonadal maturation, **adrenal maturation** (often called adrenarche in other sources) occurs at about **age 10**, which matches the timing of first attraction better than gonadal puberty does.

> **Trap.** First sexual attraction at about **10**; puberty at **10.5 (females)** and **11.5 (males)**; the proposed trigger (McClintock & Herdt, **1996**) is **adrenal**, not gonadal, steroids.

### Brain differences: LeVay (1991) and its two problems

The brains of gay, lesbian, bisexual and heterosexual people must differ in some way, but how? Many studies have looked for neuroanatomical, neuropsychological, neurophysiological and hormonal-response differences, almost all of them comparing **gay men with heterosexual men**. There has been little research on other orientations (lesbian, bisexual), mostly for **political and cultural** reasons (Byne, 2017), and that gap has major implications for providing appropriate **health care** to lesbian, gay and bisexual people (Boehmer & Elk, 2016; O'Hanlan, Gordon, & Sullivan, 2018).

In a highly publicised study, **LeVay (1991)** found that **one hypothalamic nucleus** in gay men was **intermediate in size** between that of heterosexual women and heterosexual men. (The book does not name the nucleus; it was INAH-3, the third interstitial nucleus of the anterior hypothalamus.) The book identifies **two major problems**:

1. **Replication.** The finding has **not been consistently replicated**, and interpretations of it have been controversial (Byne et al., 2001).
2. **Misinterpretation.** A common reading has been that gay men's brains lie near the **middle of a continuum** running from "female-like" to "male-like" brains (Fausto-Sterling, 2019; Roselli, 2018). That way of thinking is a **prime example of the mamawawa**: it assumes femaleness and maleness are two ends of a single line.

> **Trap.** LeVay (1991): **one hypothalamic nucleus**, gay men **intermediate** between heterosexual women and heterosexual men. The two problems: **not consistently replicated**, and **misinterpreted** as placing gay men midway on a female-to-male brain continuum, which is mamawawa thinking.

### Gender identity, transgender, and gender dysphoria

For most people, **gender identity** matches anatomical sex, but for some it does not. A person whose gender identity is **inconsistent with their anatomical sex** is described as **transgender** (Kreukels & Cohen-Kettenis, 2012).

Many transgender people experience a strong conflict between identity and body known as **gender dysphoria**. The book stresses that this is not a mild preference or a passing thought that life might be better in a different gender; it can be intensely distressing. The depth of that distress is shown by what some people did before surgical sex reassignment was available: some people assigned male at birth who had female gender identities attempted **self-castration**, and others consumed large quantities of **oestrogen-containing face creams** in an attempt to feminise their bodies.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition uses **gender dysphoria** (the DSM-5 term, 2013) for the distress, and **transgender** for the identity. Older exam keys use **gender identity disorder** (DSM-IV), and older texts use **transsexualism**; ICD-11 uses **gender incongruence**. Note also that gender dysphoria names the *distress*; being transgender is not itself defined as a disorder.

### Independence of sexual orientation, gender identity and body type

To finish, the authors bring two of the chapter's main themes together:

1. **The exception proves the rule**: a powerful test of any theory is whether it can explain exceptional cases.
2. **The mamawawa is seriously flawed**: females and males are similar in some ways and different in others, but they are not opposites; their developmental programmes are neither parallel nor opposite; and some people do not fall unambiguously into either category.

The puzzle they apply these to is that **sexual orientation, gender identity and body type are sometimes unrelated** (Fausto-Sterling, 2019). Transgender people are the clearest example. By definition their body type is inconsistent with their gender identity, but their **sexual orientation is a separate matter**: some are attracted to women, some to men, some to neither, and **sexual reassignment does not change this**.

The mere existence of people with non-heterosexual orientations, or with a gender that is neither man nor woman, challenges the mamawawa. Many people retreat to a softer version: femaleness and maleness as the **two ends of a single continuum**, with a few atypical cases in between. The independence of body type, orientation and gender identity is a serious attack on that version too. If the three can vary independently, there is no single line along which a person can be placed. Femaleness or maleness is a **combination of many attributes**, each of which can develop quite independently.

The solution comes from 13.3. It used to be assumed that the brain was differentiated into typical female and male forms by a **single testosterone-based mechanism**. The current view is that female and male brains differ in many ways, and that these differences **develop at different times and by different mechanisms**. With that developmental principle in mind, it is not hard to see how someone could be female-like in some respects, male-like in others, and neither in still others.

The authors close with the point they make throughout the book: searching for the neural basis of a behaviour often deepens our understanding of it, and here, they hope, leads to greater understanding and acceptance of differences in human sexuality.

> **Trap.** A transgender woman is not, by definition, attracted to men (the book's journal prompt asks you to rebut exactly that claim). Orientation is independent of gender identity, and it is **not changed by sexual reassignment**.

### Themes revisited

Three of the book's four major themes run through this chapter:

- **Evolutionary perspective**: most experimental work on hormones and sex has been done on non-human species.
- **Clinical implications**: the other main source of evidence is human clinical cases: the woman with testes (Anne S.), the little girl who grew into a boy (Elaine), the twin who lost his penis (David Reimer), and the man who lost and regained his manhood.
- **Thinking creatively**: conventional thinking about hormones and sex has often clashed with the research findings; resisting the mamawawa is the creative step.

The fourth major theme, **neuroplasticity**, appeared in the effects of hormones on the development of brain sex differences. Of the two emerging themes, only **epigenetics** appeared, as one of the factors contributing to brain sex differences.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all seven modules. If you can follow each one from start to finish, you understand the chapter rather than just remembering it.

1. **The mamawawa fails at every level.** Same hormones in both sexes (13.1); an androgen grows women's pubic hair (13.2); oestradiol masculinises the rodent brain (13.3); masculinisation and defeminisation are separate (13.3); each exceptional case is male in some respects and female in others (13.4); an androgen is a candidate driver of female desire (13.5); medial preoptic lesions remove male behaviour in females without touching female behaviour (13.6); orientation, gender identity and body type vary independently (13.7). Every one is the same lesson: female and male are not opposite, mutually exclusive categories, nor two ends of one continuum.
2. **Organisational versus activational effects.** Early hormones build structures and set up later responses (Jost's ducts, Pfeiffer's cycling, Phoenix's guinea pigs, Gorski's nucleus); adult hormones switch on behaviour that the structures allow (Bremer's castrates, the veteran's replacement therapy, the oestrous cycle). Grady, Phoenix and Young show the two interacting: the same adult hormone produced different behaviour depending on early exposure.
3. **"Female unless overridden", and its limits.** For the gonads (Sry), internal ducts (testosterone plus MIS), external genitals (testosterone) and hypothalamic cyclicity (Pfeiffer), the female course follows from the *absence* of a testicular signal. For the brain in general, the rule falters: sex chromosomes act directly, epigenetic mechanisms interact with hormones, and oestradiol is actively needed for the female pattern.
4. **Timing is everything.** Sry acts in week 7; testes act in month 3; the rat window for single testosterone injections is the first 5 days; Gorski's day-1 versus day-4 castrations; adrenal androgens in adrenogenital syndrome arrive too late for the Wolffian ducts but in time for the external genitals; adrenal maturation at 10 precedes gonadal puberty.
5. **Species differences matter.** Aromatisation masculinises the rodent brain but not the human brain; DHT restores copulation in castrated primates but not rodents; alpha fetoprotein's role is established in rodents but controversial in humans; female primates, unlike rodents, are motivated to copulate when not fertile.
6. **Exceptions test rules, and correlations are weak evidence.** The three cases are tests (*probare*), and the body rules pass. The human behavioural evidence, by contrast, is case-based or correlational: treated adrenogenital girls may differ because of experience, not hormones; the diethylstilbestrol study did not replicate; LeVay's finding did not consistently replicate. The book is confident about bodies and cautious about brains and behaviour, and you should be too.

## Worked example

*A 17-year-old who has always lived as a girl is referred because she has never menstruated. She is tall, with typical breast development and female external genitals, but very sparse pubic and underarm hair. Ultrasound shows no uterus that can be clearly seen and a short vagina; two small gonads are found in the groin. Her karyotype is 46,XY. Testosterone levels are in the typical male range. Her mother asks four questions: Is my daughter "really a boy"? Why did nothing show up at birth? Should we tell her? And will she be attracted to girls?*

Work it through with the chapter's rules rather than by pattern-matching.

**Which condition is this?** Every feature matches Anne S. and **complete androgen insensitivity syndrome**: XY chromosomes, testes, male-typical androgen levels, but a female body. The decisive clue is the combination of *high* androgens with *no* androgen effects, which points to the **receptor**, not the hormone. It is not **adrenogenital syndrome**: that would be an XX person with too much androgen and an enlarged clitoris, with a typical uterus and ovaries.

**Why the female body if testosterone is high?** Because development of the external genitals depends on testosterone *acting*. With unresponsive receptors, the bipotential precursor followed the female course, and the Wolffian ducts were not built. At puberty, oestrogens from her testes, unopposed by androgen action, feminised her body: hence breast development.

**Why no uterus, if this is "female development"?** This is the question that separates a good answer from a weak one. The testes released **Müllerian-inhibiting substance**, which does not act through androgen receptors and so worked normally. The Müllerian system regressed, so the uterus and upper vagina did not develop. That is why her vagina is short and the uterus underdeveloped or absent, exactly as in Anne's case.

**Why sparse pubic hair?** Female pubic and axillary hair depends on adrenal **androstenedione**, an androgen. With no working androgen receptors it cannot act.

**Why was nothing noticed at birth?** Her external genitals were typically female; the only atypical features were internal or appeared only at puberty (absent menstruation, sparse hair). Note the contrast with adrenogenital syndrome, where most female cases are identified at birth from the external genitals.

**Is she "really a boy"?** This is the mamawawa question, and the chapter's answer is that the question assumes a single male-female switch that does not exist. Chromosomally and gonadally she is male-typical; anatomically and in identity she is female, and the only sex hormones her body has been able to respond to are oestrogens. Like each of the three cases, she is male in some respects and female in others.

**Should she be told?** The chapter presents this as a genuine ethical dilemma: Anne's doctors withheld the information, while people with partial androgen insensitivity told the authors that full disclosure had helped them make sense of their lives. A good answer states both positions and notes the book does not settle it.

**Will she be attracted to girls?** Nothing in her diagnosis predicts that. Orientation is discovered, develops early, and in 13.7 is independent of body type and gender identity. Her body developed along female lines because androgens could not act, and her brain and behaviour likewise developed without androgen effects. There is no basis in the chapter for predicting her orientation from her karyotype.

**A follow-on exam twist.** *Suppose instead the patient had been an XX newborn with an enlarged clitoris and partially fused labia, and the parents wanted immediate surgery to make the genitals "definitely female".* Diagnose **adrenogenital syndrome** (congenital adrenal hyperplasia). Treat the hormone problem with **cortisol**, which lowers adrenal androgens. On surgery, cite the lesson the book draws from **David Reimer**: irreversible genital surgery at birth should be curtailed where possible and major decisions deferred until **gender identity** has emerged around early puberty, because the brain, not the genitals, determines psychosocial development.

## MCQ traps

1. **Mamawawa** is the assumption the authors **reject**: maleness and femaleness as discrete, mutually exclusive, opposite categories.
2. **Developmental = organisational** effects; **activational** effects are in adults; adolescent surges can be **both** (Bell, 2018).
3. **Sex is decided by the sperm** (X or Y); every ovum carries an X. The Y is not Y-shaped; Y genes code about **66** proteins, X about **615**.
4. **All sex hormones are steroids** (from cholesterol); **all releasing hormones and tropic hormones are peptides**. Epinephrine is an **amino acid derivative** (from tyrosine).
5. **Testes and ovaries release the same hormones** (androgens, oestrogens, progestins) in different proportions; "male/female sex hormone" is a label the book rejects.
6. **The anterior pituitary** is the true master gland; it receives **no neural input**.
7. **Oxytocin and vasopressin** are **made** in the **paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei** and only **stored/released** by the posterior pituitary; vasopressin = **antidiuretic hormone**.
8. **Harris's 1950s transplants**: female pituitary in a male became **steady**; male pituitary in a female **cycled**. The pituitary is not inherently male or female.
9. **Harris (1955)**: hypothalamic hormones reach the anterior pituitary through the **hypothalamopituitary portal system**; cutting portal veins disrupts release **until they regenerate**.
10. **TRH first**: **Guillemin (sheep)** and **Schally (pigs)**, late **1960s**; Nobel **1977**. **GnRH**: Schally, Kastin & Arimura (**1971**); one GnRH releases **both FSH and LH**.
11. **Every endocrine gland except the anterior pituitary** is directly regulated by neural signals.
12. **"Steady" male hormone levels** = no systematic **day-to-day** change; **pulsatile** release gives large **minute-to-minute** swings.
13. **Primordial gonads identical at 6 weeks**; **Sry acts in week 7**; **medulla → testis**, **cortex → ovary**.
14. **Testosterone builds Wolffian ducts; Müllerian-inhibiting substance removes Müllerian ducts** (and helps the testes descend). A testosterone-injected XX fetus gets **both** duct systems.
15. **Female ducts develop from the absence of testicular hormones**, not from ovarian hormones (female, ovariectomised female and orchidectomised male fetuses all get female ducts: **Jost, 1972**).
16. **External genitals: one bipotential precursor**, four parts. **Labioscrotal swellings → scrotum / labia majora**; **urethral folds → (fuse) / labia minora**; **glans → penis head / clitoris**; **lateral bodies → penis shaft / clitoral hood**.
17. **Growth hormone** is the only anterior pituitary hormone **without a gland** as its primary target; **female pubic and axillary hair** is grown by **androstenedione**, an **adrenal androgen** (male pattern pyramid, female inverted pyramid).
18. **Pfeiffer (1936)**: neonatal gonadectomy → **cyclic** pattern in either sex; testes into females → **steady**; ovary transplants → **no effect**. He wrongly blamed the **pituitary**; the 1960s revision named the **hypothalamus**.
19. **Aromatisation** = testosterone → **oestradiol** via **aromatase**; masculinises **rat and mouse** brains, **not human** brains (human masculinisation is a direct testosterone effect).
20. **DHT** is **non-aromatisable**: fails to masculinise the rodent brain; restores copulation in castrated **primates but not rodents**.
21. **Alpha fetoprotein** binds and deactivates **circulating oestradiol** in **rodents**; testosterone is immune; it does **not** cross the blood-brain barrier; its role in **humans is controversial**.
22. **"Female default"** works for reproductive organs and hypothalamus but **falters badly** for the brain: direct **sex-chromosome** effects, **epigenetics**, and an **active role for oestradiol** (receptor-knockout mice lack the female brain pattern).
23. **Phoenix et al. (1959)**: **guinea pigs**, prenatal testosterone → females **masculinised and defeminised**. **Grady, Phoenix & Young (1965)**: **rats**, males castrated at birth → **demasculinised and feminised**. Window for single injections in rats: **first 5 days**.
24. **Masculinisation ≠ defeminisation**; medial preoptic lesions abolish **male** behaviour in **both sexes** without affecting female behaviour.
25. **Disorder ratios**: dyslexia, early-onset schizophrenia, stuttering, autism ~**3×** male; **ADHD 10×** male; depression, anxiety, Alzheimer's **2×** female; certain eating disorders ~**10×** female.
26. **Anne S.** (Jones & Park, 1971): **26**, married **4** years, vagina **4 cm**, **XY**, internal testes; **complete androgen insensitivity** (receptor mutation, ~**1 in 60,000** genetic male births). Uterus underdeveloped because **MIS** still worked. Older title: "the Woman Who Wasn't".
27. **Elaine** (Money & Ehrhardt, **1972**): **adrenogenital syndrome** (~**1 in 10,000**) caused by **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (cortisol deficiency → excess adrenal androgens); internal ducts typical because androgens came **too late**. Cortisol therapy from **1950**.
28. **David Reimer**: monozygotic twin, penis destroyed in **circumcision at 7 months**; **Money (1975)** claimed success (social-learning theory); **Diamond & Sigmundson (1997)** showed failure; biography *As Nature Made Him* (**Colapinto, 2000**); married at **25**.
29. **Bremer (1959)**: **215** castrated Norwegians; about **half** asexual within weeks; of **102** sex offenders only **3** reoffended.
30. **First testosterone replacement case** (de Kruif, **1945**): veteran castrated in **1918** at **19**; erections after **5th** injection; **+18 lb** in **12 weeks**; sterility not cured.
31. **Grunt & Young (1952)**: equal large testosterone doses returned each castrated guinea pig to **its own previous level**; testosterone level does not set sex drive in healthy males.
32. **Rodent oestrus**: **4-day** cycle, **12 to 18 hours** of oestrus; induced by **oestradiol then progesterone ~1.5 days later**. **Everitt & Herbert (1972)**: rhesus monkeys, **testosterone not oestradiol** increased proceptivity.
33. **Anabolic steroids**: lifetime prevalence **6.4% males, 1.6% females** (Sagoe et al., 2014); **>100** testosterone derivatives; in males cause **testicular atrophy, sterility** (negative feedback) and **gynaecomastia** (aromatisation); **no** sexual enhancement.
34. **Sexually dimorphic nucleus**: **Gorski 1978**, rat **medial preoptic area**, several times larger in males, same size at birth; growth driven by **aromatised oestradiol** (Gorski, **1980**).
35. **MPOA → male**, **VMN → female** (lordosis, via VMN oestrogen receptors). **Everitt (1990)**: amygdala-lesioned male rats could copulate but **could not limit advances to receptive females**.
36. **Bailey & Pillard (1991)**: gay men, **52% MZ / 22% DZ**; **Bailey et al. (1993)**: lesbians, **48% MZ / 16% DZ**.
37. **Fraternal birth order effect**: **+33.3%** per **older brother**, depends on **biological mother's** prior sons not boys one is reared with; explained by the **maternal immune hypothesis**.
38. **Ehrhardt et al. (1985)**: **diethylstilbestrol** (synthetic **oestrogen**); **29 of 30** primarily heterosexual; **not replicated**.
39. **First sexual attraction ~age 10**, before puberty (10.5 F, 11.5 M); **McClintock & Herdt (1996)**: **adrenal** steroids.
40. **LeVay (1991)**: one hypothalamic nucleus (INAH-3) **intermediate** in gay men; problems: **not consistently replicated**; **misread** as a female-to-male continuum (mamawawa).
41. **Gender dysphoria** (DSM-5) replaced **gender identity disorder** (DSM-IV) in older keys; **transgender** = gender identity inconsistent with anatomical sex; orientation is independent and **not changed by reassignment**.

## Likely exam points

1. **Mamawawa**: definition, why it appeals (simplicity, symmetry, social comfort), and how each module refutes it.
2. **Organisational versus activational effects**, with adolescence as the period showing both.
3. **Exocrine versus endocrine glands**; the endocrine gland list; other hormone-releasing organs (stomach, liver, intestine, fat).
4. **Sex chromosomes**: 23 pairs; gametes carry one of each; sperm decides sex; Y not Y-shaped; 66 versus 615 proteins.
5. **Three hormone classes** and why steroids have the most diverse and long-lasting effects (fat-soluble, intracellular receptors, gene expression).
6. **Same hormones in both sexes**: androgens, oestrogens, progestins; adrenal cortex releases small amounts of all.
7. **Posterior versus anterior pituitary**: origins, neural input, hormones; why the anterior pituitary is the master gland.
8. **Harris**: 1950s pituitary transplants (cyclic versus steady pattern follows the host); 1955 portal-system hypothesis and portal-vein cutting with recovery on regeneration.
9. **Seasonal breeding and equator transport** as evidence that the nervous system controls the anterior pituitary.
10. **Releasing hormones**: TRH (Guillemin, sheep; Schally, pigs; late 1960s; Nobel 1977); GnRH (Schally, Kastin & Arimura, 1971) releasing both FSH and LH; all releasing and tropic hormones are peptides.
11. **Three regulators of hormone release** (neural, hormonal feedback, non-hormonal chemicals) and **pulsatile release**; meaning of "steady".
12. **Summary model of gonadal regulation**: brain → hypothalamus (GnRH) → portal system → anterior pituitary (gonadotropins) → gonads (androgens, oestrogens, progestins) → feedback.
13. **Sry**: primordial gonads at 6 weeks, Sry protein in week 7, medulla to testis; injection and blocking experiments; intersexed persons.
14. **Wolffian and Müllerian systems**: structures, the two testicular signals (testosterone, MIS), third month; the testosterone-injected XX fetus with both duct sets.
15. **Jost (1972)**: female, ovariectomised female and orchidectomised male fetuses all develop female ducts; ovaries inactive in fetal life.
16. **External genitals from one bipotential precursor**: four parts and their male and female fates; controlled by testosterone.
17. **Puberty**: growth hormone (no gland target), gonadotropic hormone and ACTH; androgen versus oestrogen predominance; androstenedione and female body hair; pre-pubertal castration.
18. **Pfeiffer (1936)** design and results; pituitary error; 1960s hypothalamic revision.
19. **Aromatisation hypothesis**: two lines of rodent evidence (oestradiol injections; aromatase blockers and DHT); alpha fetoprotein and the blood-brain barrier; does not apply to humans.
20. **Modern view of brain sex differences**: slight, variable, statistical; mosaic development; clinical sex differences (Parkinson's, autism, Alzheimer's, TBI recovery); three brain-specific factors; where the female-default idea holds and where it falters.
21. **Phoenix et al. (1959)** and **Grady, Phoenix & Young (1965)**; the 5-day window; proceptive behaviours; why masculinisation and defeminisation are measured separately.
22. **Three conclusions about human behavioural sex differences**, with the disorder ratios.
23. **"The exception proves the rule"**: *probare*, to test.
24. **Anne S.**: presentation, three lines of diagnostic evidence, CAIS mechanism trait by trait, 1 in 60,000, disclosure ethics.
25. **Elaine and adrenogenital syndrome**: CAH mechanism, 1 in 10,000, sex-specific effects, timing and the Wolffian ducts, treatment, behavioural findings and the experience confound, pre-1950 outcomes.
26. **David Reimer**: Money (1975) versus Diamond & Sigmundson (1997); brain not genitals; the clinical lesson about delaying irreversible surgery.
27. **Bremer (1959)**, the veteran's replacement therapy (de Kruif, 1945), and **Grunt & Young (1952)**: testosterone necessary but its level does not set sex drive.
28. **DHT in primates versus rodents**; the rodent oestrous cycle and replacement protocol; female primates and non-fertile copulation; the mixed evidence on oestradiol versus testosterone in female desire (Everitt & Herbert, 1972).
29. **Anabolic steroids**: prevalence (6.4% / 1.6%), why risks are poorly known, health effects, testicular atrophy and gynaecomastia mechanisms, effects in females, the two major concerns.
30. **Four brain structures**: cortex (eyes-closed visual activity, prefrontal suppression at orgasm); sexually dimorphic nucleus (Gorski 1978/1980); MPOA for male and VMN for female behaviour; amygdala (Klüver-Bucy, Everitt 1990, erotic images); ventral striatum (Pfaus et al., 2012).
31. **Sexual orientation evidence**: twin studies (52/22, 48/16), no adult hormone difference, castration does not redirect, cross-species perinatal hormone findings, Ehrhardt et al. (1985), fraternal birth order effect (33.3 percent), maternal immune hypothesis, adrenal steroids at age 10, LeVay (1991) and its two problems.
32. **Gender identity, transgender, gender dysphoria**, terminology changes, and the independence of orientation, gender identity and body type as the final argument against both the mamawawa and the single-continuum view.
