# Chapter 1: Biopsychology as a Neuroscience

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 1 contains almost no anatomy and very little physiology. What it teaches instead is a way of handling evidence. Pinel and Barnes use it to define the field (biopsychology as the study of the biology of behaviour, one discipline in the larger family of neuroscience), to lay out the kinds of research that biopsychologists do and the research traditions, or "divisions", they work in, and above all to show how claims about the brain ought to be judged. Every later chapter asks you to accept a conclusion on the strength of an experiment, a patient, or a comparison between groups; this chapter gives you the tools for deciding whether the conclusion is earned. An experiment can establish cause; a quasiexperimental study only association; a case study suggests hypotheses but generalises poorly; a single dramatic demonstration can mislead millions. The chapter ends with two historical cautionary tales, Delgado's "tamed" bull and Moniz's prefrontal lobotomy, in which those rules were ignored, the second at terrible cost to tens of thousands of patients. Its distinctions are crisp, which is why examiners mine it so heavily: which division uses which subjects and methods, what a confounded variable is, why the Coolidge effect has that name, what really causes Korsakoff's syndrome, which Nobel prize went to whom.

**How to read this chapter.** The book opens with a short passage on the brain as an object of study, the case of Jimmie G., and a statement of the four major themes and two emerging themes that run through the whole text. It then has five modules, numbered here 1.1 to 1.5. Section 1.1 defines biopsychology, dates its emergence (Hebb, 1949), and places it among six neighbouring neuroscience disciplines. Section 1.2 sets out the three dimensions along which biopsychological research varies: human versus nonhuman subjects, experiments versus nonexperiments, and pure versus applied research. Section 1.3 describes the six divisions of biopsychology and shows how each has approached a single problem, memory. Section 1.4 covers the two working habits of the field, converging operations (illustrated with Korsakoff's syndrome) and scientific inference (illustrated with the perception of motion). Section 1.5 applies critical thinking to the two historical cases.

## Opening: An Unimpressive-Looking Organ

Pinel and Barnes begin with the gap between how the brain looks and what it does. To the eye, the human brain is unremarkable: a soft, creased, walnut-shaped lump of tissue weighing about **1.3 kilograms**, more like beach debris than one of the wonders of nature. Inside, it is an extraordinarily intricate network of **neurons** (cells specialised to receive and transmit electrochemical signals) together with many other cell types. The scale figures the book gives are:

- about **90 billion neurons** (Walløe, Pakkenberg & Fabricius, 2014);
- an estimated **100 trillion connections** among them;
- and, as a consequence, a nearly unlimited number of routes that signals can take through the network (Zimmer, 2011).

Given what brains produce (paintings, prosthetic limbs, supersonic aircraft, journeys to the moon), that complexity is no surprise. **Neuroscience** is the scientific study of the nervous system, and the authors point to the paradox built into it: the brain is being asked to understand something as complex as itself, and whether it has the capacity to do so is an open question (Gazzaniga, 2010).

> **Trap.** The neuron count is an edition-sensitive number. The 11th edition gives **90 billion** (Walløe, Pakkenberg & Fabricius, 2014). Older editions of Pinel, and many question banks built from them, give **100 billion**. If both appear as options in a question tied to the current edition, 90 billion is the book's figure. Keep the three scale numbers apart: **1.3 kg** is the weight, **90 billion** is the neurons, **100 trillion** is the connections.

## The Case of Jimmie G., the Man Frozen in Time

The chapter's opening case comes from the neurologist **Oliver Sacks (1985)**; he is the subject of the essay "The Lost Mariner" in *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat*. Jimmie G. was a friendly, intelligent **49-year-old** with superior ability in mathematics and science, who enjoyed talking about his schooldays and his years in the navy and could recall both in vivid detail. Yet he was a patient on a neurological ward.

The first clue was his grammar: school was described in the past tense, the navy in the present, as though he were still serving, and nothing after the navy was ever mentioned. A few simple questions from Sacks showed why: Jimmie believed he was **19 years old**. Shown a mirror and asked to describe what he saw, he was so alarmed by the middle-aged face in it that Sacks took the mirror away at once. When Sacks returned a few minutes later, Jimmie greeted him cheerfully as a complete stranger and insisted they had never met. Asked where he was, he worked out from the beds and the patients around him that it must be a hospital, but he could not see why he would be in one, and he worried that he might be ill without knowing it.

Further testing confirmed what Sacks suspected. Jimmie's **sensory, motor and cognitive abilities were good**. His one catastrophic problem was that anything said or shown to him was **gone within a few seconds**. He had no memory of his life from his **early 20s** onwards, and he would never again form a lasting memory. He was, in the book's phrase, a man **frozen in time**, stuck in a present without context.

The chapter deliberately withholds Jimmie's diagnosis and returns to him twice. In 1.2, straight after a quasiexperimental study of heavy drinkers, it reveals that his condition was a product of **heavy alcohol consumption**. In 1.4 it names the disorder, **Korsakoff's syndrome**, and uses it as the worked example of converging operations, ending with what that research has meant for patients like him.

> **Trap.** Jimmie G. is the chapter's **opening** case, and his disorder is **Korsakoff's syndrome**, a memory disorder associated with heavy drinking but caused mainly by **thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency** (section 1.4). Do not confuse him with **H.M.** (bilateral medial temporal lobe surgery, Chapter 11) or with **Mr. R.** in this chapter (a left-handed student with a head injury). Keep the numbers straight: Jimmie was **49**, believed he was **19**, and his memory stopped in his **early 20s**.

## Four Major Themes of This Text

Before the modules begin, the authors name four ways of thinking that they want students to keep long after the individual facts have faded: **Thinking Creatively**, **Clinical Implications**, **the Evolutionary Perspective** and **Neuroplasticity**. Each is flagged in the text wherever it appears, and every chapter closes with a "Themes Revisited" section that points back to them. The one that dominates this chapter is thinking creatively.

### Thinking creatively

Television, newspapers, the internet, friends and teachers feed everyone a mixture of biopsychological information, misinformation and opinion, so students arrive with firm views. Researchers share many of the same preconceptions, which have often held science back; several of the field's biggest advances came from people who escaped conventional thinking. **Thinking creatively** is defined as **thinking in productive, unconventional ways**, and the book calls it the cornerstone of any science. The theme appears in three forms: descriptions of research that broke with conventional ideas ("thinking outside the box"), the authors' own attempts to analyse research creatively, and repeated encouragement to let evidence, rather than widely accepted views, decide what you believe.

### Clinical implications

**Clinical** means **pertaining to illness or treatment**. The clinical theme has two aspects, and both are examinable:

1. Much of what biopsychologists know about the **healthy** brain has been learned by studying **dysfunctional** brains.
2. Many of biopsychology's discoveries are relevant to the **treatment** of brain dysfunction.

The book's focus within this theme is that two-way traffic between brain dysfunction and research.

### The evolutionary perspective

The events that produced the human species can never be reconstructed with certainty, yet asking which environmental pressures probably shaped the evolution of our brains and behaviour often yields important insights. That way of thinking is the **evolutionary perspective**. An important component of it is the **comparative approach**: trying to understand a biological phenomenon by comparing it in different species. A great deal of what humans know about themselves has come from studying species related to us through evolution, and the authors count the evolutionary approach among the cornerstones of modern biopsychology.

> **Trap.** Three terms are nested here, and examiners like to blur them. The **evolutionary perspective** is a *theme*: a way of thinking about how environmental pressures shaped brains and behaviour. The **comparative approach** is a *method*, and a component of that perspective: compare a phenomenon across species. **Comparative psychology** (section 1.3) is a *division* of biopsychology whose practitioners compare the behaviour of different species to understand its evolution, genetics and adaptiveness. The chapter's closing summary links them: the evolutionary perspective is **a defining feature of comparative psychology**.

### Neuroplasticity

Until the early **1990s**, most neuroscientists pictured the brain as a fixed three-dimensional array of neural elements wired together into an enormous network of circuits. Research over the past four decades has established that the adult brain is **plastic** (changeable): it keeps growing and changing in response to the individual's environment and experiences. This is **neuroplasticity**, and the authors describe its discovery as arguably the **single most influential discovery in modern neuroscience**, a component of many areas of biopsychological research.

The book adds a corrective to the popular picture. In the media, neuroplasticity is often held up as a cure-all, a way to boost brain function or recover from brain damage. But plasticity is not always beneficial: it also contributes to various forms of brain dysfunction (the book cites Tomaszczyk et al., 2014). Later chapters show both the helpful and the harmful sides.

> **Trap.** Neuroplasticity is a property of the **adult** brain, not only of the developing brain, and it is **not always beneficial**. An option claiming that plasticity is "always adaptive" or "confined to childhood" is wrong on the 11th edition's account. The Scan Your Brain exercise (end of 1.3) phrases it as adult brain connections changing in response to the individual's **genes and experiences**.

## Emerging Themes of This Text

Alongside the four major themes, the authors highlight two "emerging" themes: ideas that recur in this edition and that could become major themes in future editions.

### Thinking about epigenetics

Most people assume that their genes (Chapter 2) determine the characteristics they are born with, the person they grow into, and the traits of their children and grandchildren. The book's position is that genes are only a small part of the story. A person is the product of **continuing interactions between genes and experience**, and those interactions are the core of the field called **epigenetics**. The striking extension is that experiences during one's lifetime **can be passed on to future generations**, which is a fundamentally different way of thinking about how each of us is tied to ancestors and descendants. Epigenetics is currently having a major influence on biopsychological research.

### Consciousness

**Consciousness** is defined as **the perception or awareness of some aspect of one's self or the world**. One major goal of biopsychological research is to understand the **neural correlates of consciousness** better (Ward, 2013; Blackmore, 2018). The book previews three claims that later chapters develop:

1. we are **not consciously aware** of much of the information we receive from our environment;
2. there are **many different states** of consciousness;
3. brain dysfunction can produce **dramatic alterations** in consciousness.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition has **four major themes** (thinking creatively, clinical implications, the evolutionary perspective, neuroplasticity) and **two emerging themes** (thinking about epigenetics, consciousness). Notes built from older editions, such as the 8th, list only the four major themes, so a key that says "four themes" is not wrong, but a question about the "emerging" themes is testing newer material. Epigenetics and consciousness are **not** among the four major themes.

## 1.1 What Is Biopsychology?

The first module does three things: it defines the field, says when and how it emerged, and locates it among the other neuroscience disciplines. Each of the three produces at least one standard exam question.

### Defining biopsychology

**Biopsychology** is the scientific study of the **biology of behaviour** (Dewsbury, 1991). The field goes under several other names, **psychobiology**, **behavioural biology** and **behavioural neuroscience**, but Pinel and Barnes prefer "biopsychology" for a stated reason: the word puts psychology in the centre. It signals a *biological approach to the study of psychology*, not a *psychological approach to the study of biology*. Psychology is the subject matter; biology supplies the explanations.

That makes the book's definition of psychology part of the definition of biopsychology. **Psychology** is the scientific study of behaviour, and "behaviour" is meant broadly: **all the overt activities of the organism** together with **all the internal processes presumed to underlie them**, such as learning, memory, motivation, perception and emotion. Biopsychology is therefore not limited to observable movement. The biology of remembering a face or feeling afraid falls inside it just as much as the biology of walking.

> **Trap.** The list of synonyms (psychobiology, behavioural biology, behavioural neuroscience) does **not** include **physiological psychology** or **neuropsychology**. In this book both of those are **divisions** of biopsychology (section 1.3), not alternative names for the whole field. Historically "physiological psychology" was used for the entire enterprise (Wundt's founding textbook of 1874 used it as its title), which is why some older questions treat it as a synonym; on the 11th edition's scheme it is one of six divisions.

### What are the origins of biopsychology?

The biology of behaviour has been studied for a long time, but biopsychology did not grow into a major neuroscientific discipline until the **20th century**. Its exact birth date cannot be fixed, but one event is singled out as pivotal: the publication in **1949** of ***The Organization of Behavior*** by **Donald Hebb** (see Brown & Milner, 2003).

What made the book so important was its ambition. Hebb offered the **first comprehensive theory** of how complex psychological phenomena, such as perceptions, emotions, thoughts and memories, could be produced by the activity of the brain. It did much to **discredit the view** that mental life is too complex to be rooted in brain physiology and chemistry, simply by showing what such an explanation could look like. (His proposal that connections between neurons strengthen when the neurons are active together reappears in the book's treatment of learning and memory.)

Just as important for the field's later character was **how** Hebb built the theory. He drew on:

1. **experiments with both human and nonhuman animals**;
2. **clinical case studies**;
3. **logical arguments** developed from his own careful observations of everyday life.

This **eclectic approach**, combining several very different kinds of evidence on one question, became a **hallmark of biopsychological inquiry**. Notice that it is an early version of what section 1.4 calls converging operations: no single kind of evidence is trusted to settle a question on its own.

Next to physics, chemistry and biology, biopsychology is an **infant**, healthy and fast-growing; its short history lets a textbook reach current research quickly.

> **Trap.** **Hebb, 1949, *The Organization of Behavior***. The year 1949 appears twice in this chapter for unrelated reasons: it is also the year **Egas Moniz** and **Walter Hess** received their Nobel prizes (Table 1.1). A question pairing "1949" with "first comprehensive theory of how brain activity produces psychological phenomena" wants **Hebb**; pairing it with "prefrontal lobotomy" wants **Moniz**.

### How is biopsychology related to the other disciplines of neuroscience?

Neuroscience is a team effort, and biopsychologists are important members of the team (Albright, Kandel & Posner, 2000; Kandel & Squire, 2000). What biopsychologists bring that the others do not is **knowledge of behaviour and of the methods of behavioural research**. Their behavioural orientation is what makes their contribution distinctive (Cacioppo & Decety, 2009).

The contribution is central, because the **ultimate purpose of the nervous system is to produce and control behaviour** (Grillner & Dickinson, 2002).

Biopsychology is also an **integrative discipline**: biopsychologists gather knowledge from the other neuroscience disciplines and apply it to the study of behaviour. The book lists six neighbouring disciplines that are particularly relevant (it calls them "a few" of the relevant disciplines, so the list is not meant to be exhaustive):

| Discipline | What it studies | Where it returns in the book |
|---|---|---|
| **Neuroanatomy** | The **structure** of the nervous system | Chapter 3 |
| **Neurochemistry** | The **chemical bases** of neural activity | Chapters 4 and 15 |
| **Neuroendocrinology** | **Interactions between the nervous system and the endocrine system** | Chapters 13 and 17 |
| **Neuropathology** | Nervous system **dysfunction** | Chapters 10 and 18 |
| **Neuropharmacology** | The effects of **drugs on neural activity** | Chapters 4, 15 and 18 |
| **Neurophysiology** | The **functions and activities** of the nervous system | Chapter 4 |

The useful rule for remembering the difference between these six and the six divisions of biopsychology in 1.3 is the prefix. The **neuro-** disciplines are defined by the nervous system alone: its structure, its chemistry, its disorders, its response to drugs. The biopsychological divisions always bring **behaviour or psychological processes** into the definition.

> **Trap.** The chapter contains **two different lists of six**, and mixing them is the commonest error on this material. The six **neuroscience disciplines relevant to biopsychology** are neuroanatomy, neurochemistry, neuroendocrinology, neuropathology, neuropharmacology and neurophysiology. The six **divisions of biopsychology** are physiological psychology, psychopharmacology, neuropsychology, psychophysiology, cognitive neuroscience and comparative psychology. Watch the look-alike pairs: **neuropharmacology** (drugs and *neural activity*) versus **psychopharmacology** (drugs and *brain and behaviour*); **neurophysiology** (functions of the nervous system) versus **physiological psychology** (neural mechanisms of behaviour, by direct manipulation of the brain) versus **psychophysiology** (physiological activity and psychological processes in humans, recorded noninvasively); **neuropathology** (nervous system dysfunction) versus **neuropsychology** (the *psychological* effects of brain dysfunction in human patients).

## 1.2 What Types of Research Characterize the Biopsychological Approach?

Biopsychologists study a great many phenomena and go about it in a great many ways. Rather than list topics, this module sorts the research along **three dimensions**, each a pair of alternatives:

1. **human or nonhuman subjects**;
2. **formal experiments or nonexperimental studies** (the two nonexperimental types the book covers are quasiexperimental studies and case studies);
3. **pure or applied** research.

Any study sits on all three at once: Lester and Gorzalka's hamster study below is nonhuman, experimental and pure.

### Human and nonhuman subjects

Both humans and nonhuman animals serve as subjects. Among nonhumans, **mice and rats are the most common**, but **cats, dogs and nonhuman primates** are also widely studied.

**Humans have several advantages as subjects.** They can **follow instructions** and **report their subjective experiences**; and, the authors joke, their cages are easier to clean. The serious point behind the joke is that humans are **often cheaper**, because meeting the highest standards of animal care makes laboratories prohibitively expensive for all but well-funded researchers. The **greatest** advantage is that human participants **have human brains**.

That raises the obvious question of why anyone studies nonhumans. The answer is the **evolutionary continuity of the brain**. Human brains resemble the brains of other mammals in fundamental ways; they differ mainly in **overall size** and in the **extent of cortical development**. The differences between human brains and those of related species are therefore **more quantitative than qualitative**, which means that many principles of human brain function can be clarified by studying nonhumans (Hofman, 2014; Katzner & Weigelt, 2013; Krubitzer & Stolzenberg, 2014).

Because humans **volunteer**, they are usually called **participants** or **volunteers** rather than subjects.

**Nonhuman animals have three advantages over humans as subjects:**

1. **Simplicity.** Their brains and behaviour are simpler than ours, so studying them is often more likely to expose **fundamental brain-behaviour interactions** that are obscured by complexity in humans.
2. **The comparative approach.** Insights often come from comparing species. For example, comparing the behaviour of species that **lack a cerebral cortex** with that of species that **have one** can give valuable clues about what the cortex does.
3. **Ethics.** It is possible to do research on laboratory animals that, for ethical reasons, could not be done on human participants. This does **not** mean that animal research is unregulated (it is governed by a strict ethical code; Blakemore et al., 2012), only that there are **fewer** ethical constraints on laboratory species than on humans.

> **Trap.** "Cheaper" is listed as an advantage of **human** participants, not of animals, because of the cost of meeting animal-care standards. And the ethical advantage of animals is **fewer** constraints, not **no** constraints. An option stating that research on nonhuman animals "is not subject to ethical review" is false.

### The ethics of research on nonhuman animals

Ethics is **not left to the individual researcher's discretion**. **All** biopsychological research, with human participants or with nonhuman subjects, is regulated by **independent committees** working to strict ethical guidelines. The book quotes Ulrich (1991) to the effect that if animals are good enough models of our own most complex behaviour to be worth studying, the same logic obliges researchers to treat them with the respect they would want for themselves.

The authors admit they wrestle with whether a study's benefits outweigh the stress it causes. Public opinion mostly falls into **two camps**:

1. those who **support** animal research, but **only if** animal suffering is minimised **and** the potential benefits to humankind cannot be obtained in any other way;
2. those who **oppose** it, because the stress it causes is not outweighed by its potential benefits.

Every study on nonhuman subjects must first be approved by a **nonhuman animal ethics committee**, whose members come from varied backgrounds and world views, and which commonly asks for more information or justification before approving.

The committees emphasise the **"three R's"**:

| R | Meaning | Example given |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduction** | Reduce the **number** of animals used | |
| **Refinement** | Refine studies, or the way animals are cared for, to **reduce suffering** | Better living conditions |
| **Replacement** | Replace animal studies with **alternative techniques** | Cell cultures; computer models |

The book's illustration of replacement is the **crash-test dummy**: live **pigs** were once used as passengers in car crash tests, and now that dummies exist nobody would defend that. The lesson is that ethical acceptability **keeps shifting** as alternatives appear; detailed **computer models of brains** (Frackowiak & Markram, 2015) may do the same for biopsychology.

(Background, not in the chapter: the three R's framework was proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959.)

> **Trap.** Know the three R's **by name and definition**, because options swap them. **Reduction** is about *numbers* of animals, **refinement** is about *suffering* (procedures and care), **replacement** is about *substituting non-animal methods*. Better housing is **refinement**, not reduction; computer modelling and cell cultures are **replacement**.

### Experiments

The **experiment** is the method scientists use to study **causation**, to find out what causes what; the authors credit it with most of the knowledge on which modern life rests, and note how simple it is. The logic, step by step:

1. The experimenter designs **two or more conditions** under which subjects will be tested.
2. Usually a **different group** of subjects is tested in each condition; this is a **between-subjects design**. Sometimes the **same group** can be tested in every condition; this is a **within-subjects design**.
3. The experimenter **assigns** subjects to conditions, **administers** the treatments and **measures** the outcome, arranging everything so that there is **only one relevant difference** between the conditions being compared.
4. That one difference is the **independent variable**.
5. The variable the experimenter measures to assess the effect of the independent variable is the **dependent variable**.
6. If the experiment has been done correctly, any difference in the dependent variable between conditions **must have been caused** by the independent variable.

Step 3 is where everything can go wrong. If the conditions differ in **more than one** way that could affect the dependent variable, it becomes impossible to tell whether the observed effect came from the independent variable or from the unintended difference. An unintended difference of this kind is a **confounded variable**. Removing every confound can be hard, and readers must watch for confounds the experimenters missed.

> **Trap.** The independent variable is the one the experimenter **controls** (the difference built into the conditions); the dependent variable is the one the experimenter **measures**. A confounded variable is an **unintended** second difference between conditions, not just any extraneous variable: what makes it a confound is that it differs systematically between the conditions being compared, so it offers a rival explanation of the result. Between-subjects = **different** groups per condition; within-subjects = the **same** subjects in every condition.

### The Coolidge effect and Lester and Gorzalka (1988)

The book's model of an experiment designed to eliminate a confound is **Lester and Gorzalka (1988)**, a demonstration of the **Coolidge effect** in females.

The **Coolidge effect** is the fact that a copulating male who can no longer continue copulating with one sex partner can often **start again with a new partner**. The name catches students out. It does **not** honour a researcher called Coolidge; it comes from **President Calvin Coolidge** of the United States, through an anecdote that the authors admit may not be true. On a farm visit, Mrs. (Grace) Coolidge pointedly asks that her husband be told how often each rooster mates; the President, learning that each rooster has many different hens, asks that Mrs. Coolidge be told that. Lester and Gorzalka's subjects, the authors add, were **hamsters**.

**The problem.** The Coolidge effect had not been demonstrated in **females**. Lester and Gorzalka argued that this was not because females lack the effect but because clean experiments are much harder to run with females. The reason is a confound. Males of most mammalian species become **sexually fatigued more readily** than females. So if a female mid-copulation is given a new partner and becomes more receptive, two explanations compete: a genuine Coolidge effect (novelty of the partner), or simply the **greater vigour of a fresh male** compared with the tired one he replaced. In tests of males the confound barely matters, because female mammals show little sexual fatigue.

**The design.** The solution was to make sure the familiar male and the unfamiliar male were **equally fatigued** when they met the test female. Using the labels in Figure 1.3:

1. **First test.** The female subject copulates with **Male 1**. At the same time, the male who will later serve as the *unfamiliar* male is copulating with **a different female**. Both males are now equally tired.
2. **Second test.** Both of those males rest while the female copulates with another male, **Male 2**.
3. **Third test.** The female is tested either with **Male 1 again** (the **familiar group**) or with the male who had been copulating elsewhere during the first test, who is new to her (the **unfamiliar group**, labelled **Male 3** in the figure).

The **independent variable** was the familiarity of the third-test male. The **dependent variable** was the amount of time the female showed **lordosis**, the arched-back, rump-up, tail-diverted posture that signals sexual receptivity in female rodents.

**The result.** On the third test the females were **much more receptive to the unfamiliar male** than to the familiar one, even though the two males were **equally fatigued** and **mounted with equal vigour**. Read from Figure 1.3, mean lordosis duration was roughly 30 minutes with Male 1 and about 16 minutes with Male 2 in both groups; on the third test it was around 6 to 7 minutes with the unfamiliar male but close to 1 minute with the familiar male.

> **Key study, Lester & Gorzalka (1988).** Female **hamsters**; three successive sex tests. The unfamiliar male had been copulating with another female while the test female copulated with the familiar male, so **both males were equally fatigued**. On test 3, lordosis duration was far greater with the **unfamiliar** male. Conclusion: females **do** show a Coolidge effect, and the earlier failure to find it reflected the **male-fatigue confound**, not an absence of the effect.

**What it shows.** Good design eliminates confounds: with fatigue equalised, novelty was the only remaining difference on test 3, so the difference in receptivity can be attributed to it. "Themes Revisited" also offers this study as the chapter's example of **thinking creatively**.

> **Trap.** Named after **President Calvin Coolidge**, not a scientist. Classically **male**; Lester and Gorzalka's contribution was showing it in **females**, using **hamsters**. IV = **familiar versus unfamiliar male**; DV = **lordosis duration**; controlled confound = **male sexual fatigue**. Different females met familiar and unfamiliar males on test 3, so the critical comparison is **between subjects**.

### Quasiexperimental studies

**Physical or ethical obstacles** often prevent researchers from assigning subjects to conditions or administering them; nobody could ethically assign people to years of frequent marijuana use to see whether it damages the brain.

In such situations biopsychologists may run **quasiexperimental studies**: studies of **groups of subjects who have already been exposed to the conditions of interest in the real world**. They look like experiments but are not, because **potential confounded variables have not been controlled**, for example by **random assignment** of subjects to conditions.

The distinction matters because of what each design can conclude:

| | Experiment | Quasiexperiment |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides the condition? | The experimenter | The participants' own lives |
| Confounds controlled? | Yes, if done correctly | No |
| What it can show | That the independent variable **causes** a change in the dependent variable | Only that two variables are **correlated** |
| How to phrase the alcohol finding | "Frequent alcohol consumption **causes** brain damage" | "Frequent alcohol use **is associated with** brain damage" |

The popular press routinely reports quasiexperiments as "experiments".

**Acker et al. (1984).** The book's worked example compared **100 detoxified men who had been heavy drinkers** with **50 men who did not drink**. Overall, the former heavy drinkers performed **worse on tests of perceptual, motor and cognitive ability**, and their brain scans showed **extensive brain damage**. It looks like an experiment but is not: the participants **chose their own group** by drinking or not, so alcohol need not be the only difference between the groups. The book lists four plausible others: as a group, heavy drinkers tend to be **less well educated**, **more prone to accidental head injury**, **more likely to use other drugs**, and **more likely to have poor diets**. The conclusion the study supports is only that heavy drinkers **tend to have more brain damage** than abstainers, not that alcohol **caused** it.

> **Key study, Acker et al. (1984).** **100** detoxified male former heavy drinkers versus **50** male nondrinkers. Drinkers: poorer perceptual, motor and cognitive performance and extensive brain damage on scans. Because participants **self-selected** into groups, alternative causes (education, head injury, other drugs, **diet**) were not excluded, so causation cannot be inferred.

Right after this study, the book springs its reminder: **Jimmie G.'s condition was a product of heavy alcohol consumption.** Hold the list of confounds in mind, because section 1.4 shows that one of them, **poor diet**, turns out to be the main cause of Jimmie's disorder. The confound in the quasiexperiment was not a technicality; it was the real explanation.

### Case studies

**Case studies** are studies that focus on a **single subject or a very small number of subjects**. They are rarely concerned with control subjects. Their value lies elsewhere: they give a **more in-depth picture** than an experiment or a quasiexperiment can, and they are an **excellent source of testable hypotheses**.

Their great weakness is **generalisability** (the book's **generalizability**): the degree to which their results apply to other cases. Because individuals differ in brain function and behaviour, a theory built entirely on a few case studies deserves scepticism; section 1.5 shows what happens when a clinical programme is built on two animals.

> **Trap.** Quasiexperimental studies and case studies are both **nonexperimental**, but they fail in different ways. A **quasiexperimental study** compares **groups** that were exposed to conditions in the real world; its weakness is **uncontrolled confounds**, so it yields **correlation, not causation**. A **case study** examines **one or a few individuals** in depth, usually **without controls**; its weakness is **generalisability**, and its strength is **generating hypotheses**. "Groups exposed in real life, no random assignment" signals a quasiexperiment; "single patient, in depth" signals a case study.

### Pure and applied research

Pure and applied research differ in several respects, but the book's key point is that they are distinguished **less by their own attributes than by the motives of the researchers** who pursue them.

- **Pure research** is motivated mainly by the **researcher's curiosity**; it is done solely to **acquire knowledge**.
- **Applied research** is intended to bring about some **direct benefit to humankind**.

Three qualifications follow.

1. **Many scientists believe that pure research will in the end be of more practical benefit than applied research.** Their reasoning is that applications flow easily once basic principles are understood, and that trying to jump straight to applications without that understanding is short-sighted.
2. **A project need not be entirely one or the other.** Many research programmes mix the two.
3. **Pure findings are often carried forward by translational research**, research that aims to turn the findings of pure research into useful applications for humankind (Howells, Sena & Macleod, 2014).

Pure research is also **more vulnerable to political regulation**, because politicians and voters struggle to see why work without immediate payoff deserves funding. The authors ask which of five projects you would fund:

- motor neurons of the **squid**;
- learning in **recently hatched geese**;
- the activity of **single nerve cells in the visual system of monkeys**;
- **hormones released by the hypothalamus** (a small neural structure at the base of the brain) in **pigs and sheep**;
- the functions of the **corpus callosum** (the large pathway connecting the left and right halves of the brain).

Every one of them was funded, and **every one earned a Nobel prize**. Matched against Table 1.1, they correspond to Hodgkin and Huxley's share of the 1963 prize (with Eccles, the ionic basis of neural transmission), Lorenz with von Frisch and Tinbergen (1973, animal behaviour, including imprinting in goslings), Hubel and Wiesel (1981, neurons of the visual system), Guillemin and Schally (1977, hormone production by the brain), and Sperry (1981, separation of the cerebral hemispheres).

> **Trap.** Pure versus applied is defined by **motive**, not by subject matter, species or setting. Research on squid neurons can be applied if its aim is a treatment; a clinical study can be pure if its aim is understanding. **Translational** research is the bridge that carries pure findings toward application; it is not a third motive in the definition. And remember which way the vulnerability runs: **pure** research is the more exposed to political interference.

## 1.3 What Are the Divisions of Biopsychology?

Researchers who share an approach tend to publish in the same journals, attend the same meetings and join the same societies, and the approaches that flourished became recognised **divisions** of biopsychological research. The book describes **six**:

1. **physiological psychology**
2. **psychopharmacology**
3. **neuropsychology**
4. **psychophysiology**
5. **cognitive neuroscience**
6. **comparative psychology**

They are presented as distinct but **overlap considerably**, and many biopsychologists work in more than one. Learn each with four tags: **what it studies**, **whose brains**, **how**, and whether it leans **pure or applied**.

### Physiological psychology

**Physiological psychology** studies the **neural mechanisms of behaviour** through the **direct manipulation and recording of the brain in controlled experiments**. **Surgical and electrical methods** are the most common.

Two consequences follow from that definition, and both are examinable.

- **Subjects are almost always laboratory animals.** Direct manipulation of the brain in controlled experiments rules out human participants in most instances, for obvious ethical reasons.
- **It has a tradition of pure research.** The emphasis is usually on work that contributes to **theories of the neural control of behaviour**, rather than on research with immediate practical benefit.

It pairs the strongest causal method with the species least like us, which is why section 1.4 gives it a partner.

### Psychopharmacology

**Psychopharmacology** is like physiological psychology except that it manipulates neural activity and behaviour with **drugs**. The overlap is historical as well as conceptual: many early psychopharmacologists were simply physiological psychologists who moved into drug research, and many present-day biopsychologists identify closely with both approaches. But the study of drug effects on brain and behaviour has become so specialised that psychopharmacology is now regarded as a **separate discipline**.

Its orientation differs from physiological psychology's in one key respect: **a substantial portion of psychopharmacological research is applied**. Drugs are sometimes used as tools for studying basic principles of brain-behaviour interaction, but the purpose of many psychopharmacological experiments is to **develop therapeutic drugs** (Chapter 18) or to **reduce drug abuse** (Chapter 15). Psychopharmacologists study drug effects in laboratory species and, **where the ethics of the situation permit**, in humans.

> **Trap.** Physiological psychology and psychopharmacology share the same logic (manipulate the brain, observe behaviour) and differ in the **tool** (surgery and electricity versus drugs) and the **orientation** (a tradition of **pure** research versus a **substantially applied** one). Psychopharmacology can use **human** participants where ethics allow; physiological psychology almost never does.

### Neuropsychology

**Neuropsychology** is the study of the **psychological effects of brain dysfunction in human patients**. Because volunteers cannot ethically be exposed to experimental treatments that endanger normal brain function, neuropsychology works almost entirely with **case studies and quasiexperimental studies** of patients whose brain dysfunction resulted from **disease, accident or neurosurgery**.

The **cerebral cortex**, the outer layer of the cerebral hemispheres, is the part of the brain most likely to be damaged by accident or surgery, and that is one reason neuropsychology has concentrated on it.

Neuropsychology is the **most applied** of the biopsychological subdisciplines. Even when a neuropsychological assessment forms part of a programme of pure research, it is always carried out with an eye to **benefiting the patient** in some way. Neuropsychological tests **aid diagnosis** and thereby help the attending physician choose effective treatment (Benton, 1994). They can also be an important basis for **patient care and counselling**, which is the point of the case that follows, described by **Kolb and Whishaw (1990)**.

### The Case of Mr. R., the Student with a Brain Injury Who Switched to Architecture

Mr. R. was a **21-year-old** university **honours student** who hit his head on the dashboard in a **car accident**. Afterwards his grades slid from exceptional to merely average, and term papers gave him particular difficulty. After a year of struggling he went for a **neuropsychological assessment**, and three findings stood out:

1. He was one of the roughly **one-third of left-handers** whose **language functions are represented in the right hemisphere** rather than the left.
2. His **IQ was superior**, but his **verbal memory** and **reading speed** were **below average**, a very unusual combination in someone with his academic record.
3. Putting those together, the neuropsychologists concluded that he may have suffered damage to his **right temporal lobe** in the accident, which would account for his weakened language skills.

Their practical recommendation was that he pursue a field that did not depend on superior verbal memory. He changed his major to **architecture** and did very well in it.

The case shows the **applied, counselling** side of neuropsychology (it explained a decline and redirected a career), and a lesson in lateralisation: right temporal damage would not normally impair verbal skills, but Mr. R.'s language was on the right.

> **Trap.** "Roughly **one-third** of left-handers have right-hemisphere language" means the **majority** of left-handers still have it on the **left**. Mr. R.'s **IQ stayed superior**; **verbal memory and reading speed** fell. The case (**Kolb & Whishaw, 1990**) illustrates **neuropsychology**.

### Psychophysiology

**Psychophysiology** studies the **relation between physiological activity and psychological processes in humans**. Because its subjects are human, its recording procedures are typically **noninvasive**: physiological activity is recorded **from the surface of the body**.

- The usual measure of brain activity is the **scalp electroencephalogram (EEG)** (Chapter 5).
- Other common measures are **muscle tension**, **eye movement**, and several indicators of **autonomic nervous system** activity: **heart rate**, **blood pressure**, **pupil dilation**, and the **electrical conductance of the skin**.

The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** is the part of the nervous system that **regulates the body's inner environment** (Chapter 3).

Most psychophysiological research aims at understanding the physiology of psychological processes such as **attention, emotion and information processing**, but the method has also produced clinical findings. The book's example is that people with **schizophrenia have difficulty tracking a moving object smoothly with their eyes** (Meyhöfer et al., 2014). Figure 1.5, based on **Iacono and Koenig (1983)**, shows eye-position records made while participants followed a swinging **pendulum**. The healthy control's trace is a smooth wave that follows the pendulum's motion; the traces of the three participants with schizophrenia are irregular, one of them broken into abrupt step-like jumps and the other two ragged and jittery, showing that the eyes kept losing the target and catching up.

The book's journal prompt asks what this might mean for diagnosis. A cheap, objective, noninvasive measure is attractive, but it helps diagnosis only if it is reasonably **specific** to schizophrenia and present in most patients; the chapter reports a group difference, not those properties.

> **Trap.** **Psychophysiology** and **physiological psychology** are the most confused pair in the chapter, and the word order is the clue. **Physiological psychology** explains psychology (behaviour) by physiology, using **direct, invasive** manipulation in **laboratory animals**. **Psychophysiology** records physiology during psychological processes in **humans**, **noninvasively** from the body surface (EEG, skin conductance, heart rate, pupil size, eye movements). The schizophrenia **smooth-pursuit eye-tracking** finding belongs to **psychophysiology**.

### Cognitive neuroscience

**Cognitive neuroscience** is the **youngest** division of biopsychology. Its subject is the brain basis of **cognition**, meaning the **higher intellectual processes**: thinking, memory, attention, and the more complex kinds of perception (Gutchess, 2014; Raichle, 2008).

The rest of its profile follows as a chain: a focus on cognition means mostly **human participants**, and human participants mean **noninvasive** methods rather than penetrating or manipulating the brain. Its **major method** is **functional brain imaging**: recording images of the activity of the **living human brain** while the participant engages in a particular mental activity (Chapter 5). Figure 1.6 is an example: viewed from the top of the head, with the participant lying on her back, the visual areas at the back of both cerebral hemispheres light up when she looks at a **flashing light** (image courtesy of Todd Handy, University of British Columbia).

Because its theory and methods are complex and widely relevant, cognitive neuroscience is typically **interdisciplinary**. Contributors commonly include biopsychologists, cognitive psychologists, social psychologists, **economists**, computing and mathematics experts, and many kinds of neuroscientists. Its borders are porous in two directions:

- when it uses **noninvasive electrophysiological recording**, it shades into **psychophysiology**;
- when it studies **patients with brain dysfunction**, it shades into **neuropsychology**.

> **Trap.** **Youngest** division = **cognitive neuroscience**. **Most applied** division = **neuropsychology**. Major method of cognitive neuroscience = **functional brain imaging**; usual brain measure in psychophysiology = **scalp EEG**. Do not attach "youngest" to comparative psychology because it sounds like a newcomer; it is the reverse.

### Comparative psychology

Biopsychology is more than neural mechanisms; its aim is to understand the **integrated behaviour of the whole animal**. **Comparative psychology** keeps that aim in view, focusing on animal behaviour, including behaviour in **natural environments**.

Comparative psychologists **compare the behaviour of different species** in order to understand the **evolution, genetics and adaptiveness of behaviour**. Some do this in the **laboratory**; others engage in **ethological research**, the study of behaviour in an animal's **natural environment**.

> **Trap.** Comparative psychology is the one division **not defined by a neural method**: its defining tool is the **comparison of species**, and its defining question is **evolution, genetics and adaptiveness** of behaviour. Its link to the four major themes is the **evolutionary perspective**. Notes from older editions list **evolutionary psychology** and **behavioural genetics** as further subfields of comparative psychology; the 11th edition's text names only **laboratory** research and **ethological** research (behaviour in the natural environment).

### Six divisions, one problem: memory

To show the six divisions in action, Table 1.2 takes a single topic, memory, and gives one example of how each division has approached it. Each example also previews a later chapter.

| Division | Short definition | How it has studied memory |
|---|---|---|
| **Physiological psychology** | Neural mechanisms of behaviour, by manipulating the nervous systems of nonhuman animals in controlled experiments | **Surgically removing the hippocampus in rats** and testing them on a range of memory tasks |
| **Psychopharmacology** | Effects of drugs on brain and behaviour | Trying to **improve memory in Alzheimer's patients** with drugs that alter brain chemistry |
| **Neuropsychology** | Psychological effects of brain dysfunction in human patients | Showing that patients with damage to the **hippocampus and surrounding structures cannot form new long-term memories** |
| **Psychophysiology** | Relation between physiological activity and psychological processes in human volunteers, by noninvasive recording | Showing that **familiar faces still trigger the usual autonomic responses** in brain-damaged patients who say they **do not recognise** the face |
| **Cognitive neuroscience** | Neural mechanisms of human cognition, largely through functional brain imaging | **Imaging brain activity** while healthy volunteers perform memory tasks |
| **Comparative psychology** | Evolution, genetics and adaptiveness of behaviour, largely by comparing species | Showing that bird species that **cache seeds have larger hippocampi**, consistent with the hippocampus's role in **memory for location** |

Read the table as a map of what each method can do. The rat lesion study shows *causation*, but in rats; the patient studies show the deficit in humans without control over the damage; the face example shows an autonomic measure revealing **recognition the patient cannot report**; the bird comparison involves no intervention at all. Different kinds of evidence pointing to one structure is converging operations, made explicit in 1.4.

> **Trap.** The example of the seed-caching birds belongs to **comparative psychology**, not physiological psychology, even though it involves the hippocampus. Hippocampal **removal in rats** is **physiological psychology**; hippocampal **damage in patients** is **neuropsychology**; autonomic responses to faces **not consciously recognised** is **psychophysiology**; Alzheimer's **drug** trials are **psychopharmacology**; **imaging** healthy volunteers is **cognitive neuroscience**.

## 1.4 How Do Biopsychologists Conduct Their Work?

This module describes two working habits: **how biopsychologists collaborate** (converging operations) and **how they reason about what they cannot see** (scientific inference). Module 1.5 then shows what happens when both are abandoned.

### Converging operations: how do biopsychologists work together?

Every one of the six approaches has blind spots, and the brain's control of behaviour is enormously complex, so big questions are **rarely settled by one experiment**, or even by a run of experiments using one approach. The field advances fastest when **several different approaches are aimed at the same question**, arranged so that what one does well **covers what another does badly**. That strategy is **converging operations** (Thompson, 2005).

The book's illustration pairs the two divisions whose profiles are most nearly mirror images, applied to the question of what damage to the human cerebral cortex does psychologically:

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Neuropsychology** | Deals **directly with human patients** | Its focus on human patients **rules out experiments** |
| **Physiological psychology** | Can use the **power of controlled experiments** on nonhuman animals | The **relevance of animal research to human brain damage** is always open to question (Couzin-Frankel, 2013; Reardon, 2016) |

The neuropsychologist has the right species but cannot control the damage; the physiological psychologist controls the damage in the wrong species. Together they answer questions neither can answer alone.

### Korsakoff's syndrome: converging operations in action

To show the idea at work, the chapter returns to **Jimmie G.** His disorder was first described in the **late 19th century** by **Sergei Korsakoff**, a **Russian physician**, and became known as **Korsakoff's syndrome**. Its **primary symptom is severe memory loss**, made all the more painful, as Jimmie's case shows, because the people who have it are often otherwise quite capable.

**The first, wrong conclusion.** Because the syndrome is common in **heavy drinkers**, it was first blamed on **alcohol's direct toxic effect on the brain**, an inference from association alone, which the book uses to show why causal conclusions should not be drawn from quasiexperimental studies.

**The correction.** Later research showed that Korsakoff's syndrome is **largely caused by brain damage associated with thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency**. The evidence came from different directions, which is the whole point:

1. **Case observations of non-drinkers.** The first support for the thiamine interpretation was the discovery of the syndrome in **malnourished people who drank little or no alcohol**. If the syndrome can occur without alcohol, alcohol cannot be its necessary cause, and malnutrition becomes the obvious candidate.
2. **Controlled experiments in rats.** **Thiamine-deficient rats** were compared with otherwise identical control rats. The deficient rats developed **memory deficits and patterns of brain damage** similar to those seen in many former heavy drinkers (Mumby, Cameli & Glenn, 1999). Here the experimenter assigned the deficiency, so it must be the cause.
3. **Explaining the association.** Heavy drinkers are vulnerable for two reasons: most of their calories come from **alcohol, which contains no vitamins**, and **alcohol interferes with the metabolism** of what little thiamine they do take in.
4. **A residual role for alcohol.** The story does not end with alcohol cleared. Alcohol has been shown to **accelerate the development of brain damage in thiamine-deficient rats**, so it may have a **direct toxic effect** on the brain as well (Ridley, Draper & Withall, 2013).

Note how Acker et al.'s confound list pays off: **poor diet**, one of the uncontrolled differences between drinkers and abstainers, turned out to be the main cause. The conclusion rests on three kinds of evidence, none sufficient alone:

| Line of evidence | Kind of study | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Studies of Korsakoff patients | **Neuropsychological case studies** | The syndrome itself, in humans, including in malnourished non-drinkers |
| Heavy drinkers compared with abstainers | **Quasiexperiments with human participants** | The association with alcohol and brain damage, but not its cause |
| Thiamine-deficient versus control rats | **Controlled experiments on laboratory animals** | Causation: thiamine deficiency produces memory loss and brain damage |

The moral: biopsychology's **strength lies in the diversity of its methods**, so judging a claim on **one study**, or one line of same-method experiments, is rarely enough.

**What this has meant for patients.** Today heavy drinkers are counselled to **stop drinking** and are treated with **large doses of thiamine**. Thiamine limits further damage and often brings **slight improvement**, but existing damage is **mostly irreversible**.

The book's journal prompt adds one number: Korsakoff's syndrome accounts for roughly **10 percent of adult dementias in the United States**, yet few people have heard of it.

(Background, not in this chapter: in clinical usage the acute neurological phase of thiamine deficiency is called **Wernicke's encephalopathy**, and the combined picture is often termed **Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome**.)

> **Trap.** Korsakoff's syndrome is **largely caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency**, not by alcohol's direct toxicity. But do not overcorrect: alcohol **accelerates** damage in thiamine-deficient rats and **may** also be directly toxic, so "alcohol plays no role" is also wrong. **Sergei Korsakoff** was a **Russian physician** of the **late 19th century**.

> **Trap.** Converging operations means **different methods** aimed at **one problem**, so that their **strengths and weaknesses complement each other**. It is not the same as **replication** (repeating the same study) and not the same as running a **series of similar experiments**. The three methods that converged on Korsakoff's syndrome were **case studies, quasiexperiments and animal experiments**.

### Scientific inference: how do biopsychologists study the unobservable workings of the brain?

**Scientific inference** is the **fundamental method of biopsychology** and of most sciences. The **scientific method** finds things out by **careful observation**, yet many processes **cannot be observed directly**: ice ages, gravity, evaporation, electricity and nuclear fission are studied **empirically** (by observation) through their **effects** only. Biopsychology is in the same position: one of its main goals is to characterise, by empirical methods, the **unobservable processes** by which the nervous system controls behaviour.

The method for doing that is **scientific inference**: scientists carefully **measure the key events they can observe** and use those measurements as the basis for **logically inferring the nature of events they cannot observe**. The book's analogy is a **detective** reconstructing a crime nobody witnessed from the clues left behind. The biopsychologist's clues are measures of behaviour and neural activity; the "crime" is the neural process that produced them.

### The perception of motion: an inference you can make yourself

The book's demonstration is one you can run on yourself, and it reveals how the brain turns **retinal image movement** into **perceived movement**.

**Step 1: retinal motion is not the same as perceived motion.** Hold a hand in front of your face and make its image sweep across your retinas in three ways: by moving your **eyes**, by moving your **hand**, or by moving **both**. Only the retinal movement produced by the **hand** is experienced as motion; the retinal movement produced by your own **eye movements** is not. The inference: some part of the brain must monitor how the retinal image moves and **subtract** the portion caused by your own eye movements, so that only the remainder is perceived as movement in the world.

**Step 2: what information about the eyes does the subtraction use?** Close one eye. With a fingertip, press gently on the **lower eyelid** of the open eye so that the eye rotates slightly **upward**. The whole visual scene appears to **move downward**. So the mechanism does **not** take account of eye movement as such. It takes account only of eye movements that are **actively produced** by neural signals sent from the brain to the eye muscles, not those produced passively by some other means, such as a finger. When the eye was pushed, the brain "assumed" it had stayed still, and so it attributed the movement of the retinal image to movement of the objects in view.

Figure 1.7 sets out the four conditions that make the inference airtight:

| Condition | Eye | Object | Retinal image | What is seen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stationary | Stationary | Stationary | No movement |
| 2 | **Actively** rotates upward | Stationary | Moves up | **No movement** |
| 3 | Stationary | **Moves down** | Moves up | Object moves down |
| 4 | **Passively** rotated upward by a finger | Stationary | Moves up | Object **appears to move down** |

The power of the comparison lies in conditions 2, 3 and 4. In all three, the retinal image does exactly the same thing (it moves up), yet the perceptions differ. Retinal input alone therefore cannot determine perceived motion; something else must be entering the calculation. Condition 2 shows that an active eye movement is cancelled; condition 4 shows that a passive one of the same size is not. The only thing that distinguishes 2 from 4 is whether the brain **commanded** the movement. Hence the figure's conclusion: the brain perceives as movement the **total movement of an object's retinal image minus the portion produced by active movement of the eyes**; it does **not** subtract passive eye movement.

### Hammond, Merton and Sutton (1956): holding the eye still

The opposite trick is to keep the eyes **still** while the brain **tries** to move them, which requires paralysing the eye muscles.

> **Key study, Hammond, Merton & Sutton (1956).** A **paralytic** (movement-inhibiting) substance, the active ingredient of **curare** (a drug some Indigenous peoples of South America use on blow darts), was injected into the eye muscles of the participant, who was **Merton himself**. When he then **tried** to move his eyes, he saw the **stationary visual world move in the same direction as his attempted eye movement**.

The reasoning is a direct prediction from the model inferred above. Normally, if an object's image stays on the same part of the retina even though your eyes have moved to the right, the object must have moved to the right with them. When Merton sent the command to move his eyes right, his brain treated the movement as done; but the paralysed eyes stayed put, so the retinal image did not shift. The only interpretation consistent with "eyes moved right, image did not move" is that the world moved right, and that is what he saw.

The point: much can be learned about brain activity **through inference, without observing it directly**. Neuroscientists have since refined these feedback mechanisms with modern neural recording (Joiner et al., 2013; Wurtz et al., 2011). The chapter does not name the mechanism, but the standard textbook terms for the copy of the motor command that the visual system uses are **efference copy** or **corollary discharge**.

> **Trap.** Get the **directions** right, because they are opposite in the two demonstrations. **Passive** upward push of the eye (finger on lower eyelid): the world appears to move **down**, opposite to the eye's rotation, because the brain did not command the movement and so does not subtract it. **Paralysed** eye plus an **attempted** movement to the right: the world appears to move **right**, the **same** direction as the attempted movement, because the brain subtracts a movement that never happened. The participant injected with curare was **Merton** (of Hammond, Merton and Sutton, **1956**). Curare is a **paralytic**, not an anaesthetic.

## 1.5 Thinking Critically about Biopsychological Claims

The final module opens with popular claims many people believe: that we use only a small part of our brains, that eating three meals a day is important, that intelligence is inherited, that everyone needs at least 8 hours of sleep a night, that there is a gene for schizophrenia, that heroin is an especially dangerous ("hard") drug, and that neurological diseases can now be cured by genetic engineering (Howard-Jones, 2014). Are they true, how would you find out, and why are they believed? The module begins the book's training in telling **flawed claims** from **exciting discoveries**; several of these claims return later (eating in Chapter 12, sleep in Chapter 14, drugs in Chapter 15, schizophrenia in Chapter 18).

### Evaluating biopsychological claims

One of the book's major aims is to teach you to **think creatively** about biopsychological information. The first step in creative thinking is often to **spot the weaknesses** of existing ideas and of the evidence behind them, and the process of recognising those weaknesses is called **critical thinking**. Spotting weaknesses in established beliefs is a major spur to creative new approaches, so critical and creative thinking are two halves of one skill.

The module analyses **two historically important claims** whose evidence proved **grossly flawed**; spotting the flaws needs no expertise, only alertness.

**The first check: was it published in a reputable scientific journal?** A reputable journal sends each manuscript to **experts in the field, usually three or four**, and prints it only if they rate it as sound work; the best journals accept just a **small fraction** of submissions. A claim that has skipped this scrutiny deserves extra scepticism.

**Case 1** is an **unpublished** claim spread through the **news media**; **Case 2** was initially supported by **published research**. Both can be judged with hindsight.

> **Trap.** Peer review is the **first** step in evaluating a claim, not the last. Case 1 fails it (a media demonstration, never peer reviewed). Case 2 passes it (published research, and even a Nobel prize) and is still wrong. An option saying that publication in a reputable journal **guarantees** a claim's validity is false; publication is a minimum, not proof. Reviewers number **usually three or four**.

### Case 1: José and the Bull

**José Delgado**, a charismatic neuroscientist, staged a demonstration of a supposed new method of controlling aggression for an audience of newspaper reporters. He walked into a **Spanish bullring** carrying nothing but a red cape and a small **radio transmitter**. The transmitter could switch on a battery-powered stimulator mounted on the **horns of the bull**, which sent a weak current through an electrode implanted in the bull's **caudate nucleus**, a structure deep in the brain (Chapter 3). Each time the bull charged, Delgado triggered the stimulator and the bull **broke off its charge and veered away**; after a few such interrupted charges it stood quietly while Delgado strutted about the ring.

Delgado presented this as a major scientific breakthrough: the discovery of a **caudate "taming centre"**, stimulation of which could abolish aggression even in animals bred for ferocity. To the people present at this carefully orchestrated event, and to the millions who later read about it, the conclusion seemed compelling. It was even suggested that caudate stimulation through implanted electrodes might be a treatment for **human psychopathy**.

### Analysis of Case 1

The demonstration gave **little or no support** to the conclusion. Stimulation could stop a charging bull in many ways, most of them **simpler, and therefore more probable**, than Delgado's. The stimulation might have made the bull **confused, dizzy, nauseous, sleepy or temporarily blind**, rather than less aggressive, or it might simply have been **painful**. An observation that can be explained in so many ways provides little support for any one of the explanations.

The rule for such situations is **Morgan's Canon**: when several interpretations of a behavioural observation are possible, **give precedence to the simplest one**.

The expert verdict came from **Valenstein (1973)**, who examined the film of the event. In his account, there was no good reason to think the stimulation affected the bull's aggressive tendencies at all: while the current was on, the bull was **forced to keep turning in the same direction**, and that is why the charge stopped. Anyone knowledgeable in the field who watched the film, he argued, could conclude only that the electrode had been activating a **neural pathway that controls movement**.

In 1.2's terms, Delgado ran a **demonstration**, not an experiment: **no control condition**, aggression **never measured independently** of movement, and the obvious **confound** (anything that disrupts movement stops a charge) never ruled out. The claim also skipped peer review.

(Background, not in this chapter: the canon is named after the British comparative psychologist **C. Lloyd Morgan** (1894), whose original formulation warned against explaining an animal's behaviour by a higher mental faculty when a lower one would do. It is a close relative of the general law of parsimony, often called Occam's razor.)

> **Trap.** Delgado's structure was the **caudate nucleus** (a "taming centre"), not the prefrontal lobes; the critic was **Valenstein (1973)** (a **motor pathway** forced the bull to **turn**); the principle was **Morgan's Canon** (simplest interpretation first). Do not attach the caudate or Morgan's Canon to the lobotomy case.

### Case 2: Two Chimpanzees, Moniz, and the Prefrontal Lobotomy

In **1949**, **Egas Moniz** received the Nobel prize in physiology and medicine for developing the **prefrontal lobotomy**: an operation that treats mental illness by **severing the connections between the prefrontal lobes and the rest of the brain**. The **prefrontal lobes** are the two large regions, one in each hemisphere, at the front of the brain (Figure 1.8).

The discovery rested on a report about **two chimpanzees**. Both often became upset when they made errors on a **food-rewarded task**, and both stopped reacting that way after a large **bilateral lesion** (an area of damage on both sides of the brain) of the prefrontal lobes. Moniz saw this result demonstrated at a **scientific meeting in 1935**, and persuaded the neurosurgeon **Almeida Lima** to operate on a series of psychiatric patients (Heller et al., 2006). Lima removed **six large cores** of prefrontal tissue using a surgical instrument called a **leucotome**. As Figure 1.9 shows, the leucotome was inserted into the brain **six times** with its cutting wire retracted; after each insertion the wire was extended and the instrument rotated, cutting out a core of tissue.

(Background, not in this chapter: Moniz, a Portuguese neurologist, called his operation a "leucotomy", hence the name of the instrument; the chimpanzee findings he heard in 1935 were those of Fulton and Jacobsen.)

> **Trap.** The 11th edition says Moniz's programme was based on **two chimpanzees** and that he **witnessed a demonstration at a scientific meeting in 1935**. Older editions, and notes and keys built from them, describe **one chimpanzee named Becky**. For a question keyed to the current edition, "two chimpanzees" is the book's answer; if an older-style option says "one chimpanzee (Becky)", recognise it as the earlier version of the same story. Either way, the critique is identical: a tiny animal sample generalised to humans.

### Transorbital lobotomy and Walter Freeman

After Moniz's claims that prefrontal surgery was therapeutically useful, many variants of **prefrontal psychosurgery** spread rapidly. One was the **transorbital lobotomy**, which was **developed in Italy** and then **popularised in the United States by Walter Freeman in the late 1940s**. The surgeon slid an **ice-pick-like instrument** under the patient's eyelid, drove it through the **orbit** (the eye socket) with a few taps of a **mallet**, pushed it into the prefrontal lobes and swept it **back and forth** to sever their connections with the rest of the brain (Figure 1.10). The operation was **frequently performed in doctors' offices**.

> **Trap.** Two procedures, two pairs of names. **Moniz and Lima**: the **leucotome**, **six cores** of prefrontal tissue, 1935 onward. **Freeman**: the **transorbital** route through the **eye socket** with an **ice-pick-like** instrument and mallet, popularised in the **US** in the **late 1940s**, often done in **doctors' offices**. Freeman **popularised** the transorbital procedure; the book says it was **developed in Italy**. Moniz won the Nobel; Freeman did not.

### Analysis of Case 2

**Psychosurgery** is any brain surgery, such as prefrontal lobotomy, performed to treat a **psychological problem**. The analysis identifies two major weaknesses in the scientific case for it, and each maps onto a concept from 1.2.

**Weakness 1: the evidence base was two chimpanzees.** That shows a failure to appreciate how **diverse brains and behaviour are, both within and between species**. The authors' rule is that **no psychosurgery programme should ever begin without a thorough assessment of the surgery's effects on a large sample of subjects from various nonhuman mammalian species**, and that starting without one is **unwise and unethical**. In 1.2's terms, it is the **generalisability** problem in its extreme form.

**Weakness 2: the first patients were not properly evaluated** (Mashour, Walker & Martuza, 2005; Singh, Hallmayer & Illes, 2007). The early reports of therapeutic success came from the **least objective** observers available, the **physicians who had prescribed the surgery and their colleagues**. Patients were often judged "improved" simply because they had become **more manageable**, while little effort went into assessing more important aspects of their psychological adjustment or documenting **adverse side effects**. In 1.2's terms: a poor outcome measure, raters with a stake in the result, and no comparison group.

**The eventual verdict.** It became clear that prefrontal lobotomies are of **little therapeutic benefit** and can produce a wide range of undesirable side effects:

- **socially inappropriate behaviour** (older editions say "amorality")
- **lack of foresight**
- **emotional unresponsiveness**
- **epilepsy**
- **urinary incontinence**

The procedure was abandoned in many parts of the world, but **not before more than 40,000 patients had been lobotomised in the United States alone**, and prefrontal lobotomies **still continue to be performed in some countries**.

### The case of Howard Dully

The most disturbing part of the story is that lobotomies were not confined to informed, consenting adults. **Howard Dully** described in his memoir (*My Lobotomy*; **Dully & Fleming, 2007**) how he was lobotomised at the age of **12**. The operation was arranged by his **stepmother**, agreed to by his **father**, and performed by **Walter Freeman** in about **10 minutes**. Dully spent most of the rest of his life in **asylums, jails and halfway houses**, asking himself what he had done to deserve it and how much of his troubled life it had caused. Later investigation of the case indicated that he had been a **normal child** and that his stepmother was consumed by hatred of him. Neither his father nor the medical profession stepped in to protect him.

### Science is not an obstacle to treatment

Some see scientific method as an obstacle between patients and treatment. The lobotomy is the authors' answer: a warning against **abandoning science for expediency**, since only the rules of science **protect the public from bogus claims** (Rousseau & Gunia, 2016). Biopsychology has learned from **Delgado, Moniz, Freeman** and others, and sound method and inference are now nearly universal in the field.

## Themes Revisited

The book's own end-of-chapter summary says that the seeds of **three** of the four major themes were planted in this chapter, with **thinking creatively** predominating.

- **Thinking creatively.** It appeared first in the creative design Lester and Gorzalka used to study the Coolidge effect in females. It continued with three ideas that help in thinking about biopsychological claims: **(1) the experimental method, (2) converging operations and (3) scientific inference**. And it ended with two once widely believed claims (the caudate taming centre and the prefrontal lobotomy), whose weaknesses critical thinking exposed and replaced with creative new interpretations.
- **Clinical implications** and **the evolutionary perspective** tend to be tied to particular divisions: clinical implications emerge most often from **neuropsychological, psychopharmacological and psychophysiological** research, while the evolutionary perspective is a **defining feature of comparative psychology**.
- The two emerging themes, **thinking about epigenetics** and **consciousness**, are introduced here and taken up in later chapters.

> **Trap.** The **neuroplasticity** theme is the one major theme **not** among the three whose seeds the chapter says it planted. The three ideas the summary lists for thinking about claims are the **experimental method, converging operations and scientific inference**; Morgan's Canon and peer review are part of the critical-thinking module but are not in that list of three.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the five modules. Trace each one and the chapter becomes a single argument about evidence rather than a list of definitions.

1. **Cause requires control.** The experiment is the only design that licenses a causal conclusion, because only the experimenter's control of conditions removes confounded variables. Lester and Gorzalka earned their conclusion by equalising male fatigue; Acker and colleagues could not earn theirs because drinkers chose their own group; the original "alcohol is toxic" account of Korsakoff's syndrome failed for exactly that reason.
2. **Every method has a blind spot, so evidence must converge.** Hebb's eclectic approach in 1949, the six divisions with their different subjects and methods, Table 1.2's six routes to the hippocampus, and the three-way convergence on thiamine deficiency are all the same idea: the strength of one method covers the weakness of another.
3. **The brain is studied by inference.** Neural mechanisms are unobservable, so they are reconstructed from what can be measured. The eye-movement demonstrations show how far careful inference can go with no recording equipment at all.
4. **Prefer the simplest explanation.** Morgan's Canon disposes of Delgado's taming centre; the male-fatigue explanation was the simpler rival that Lester and Gorzalka had to rule out before claiming a Coolidge effect in females.
5. **Who reports the result, and how, matters.** A media demonstration is not a peer-reviewed study; a newspaper "experiment" is often a quasiexperiment; a surgeon's judgement that his patients are "more manageable" is not an outcome measure.
6. **The stakes are clinical.** Jimmie G. benefits from the thiamine research; Mr. R. was redirected by a neuropsychological assessment; more than 40,000 Americans, Howard Dully among them, paid for the abandonment of these rules.

## Worked example

*A newspaper reports that "an experiment" has shown that adults over 60 who regularly play a particular brain-training app have better memory scores and larger hippocampi on MRI than adults who do not. A private clinic now advertises a "hippocampal stimulation" treatment for memory loss. Its founder appeared on television with one patient who, after a session, recalled a word list perfectly, and he says the treatment has transformed hundreds of his patients. A relative of a 58-year-old man with severe memory loss and a long history of heavy drinking asks you whether to enrol him.*

Work through it with the chapter's tools rather than with opinion.

**Is the app study an experiment?** Almost certainly not. The participants chose whether to use the app, so this is a **quasiexperimental study**. It shows only an **association** between app use and memory or hippocampal size. People who choose such apps may be more educated, healthier or more motivated, and any of these confounds could explain the difference; the press has called a quasiexperiment an experiment.

**What would establish causation?** A true **experiment**: assign older adults at random to the app or to a comparison activity (a **between-subjects design**), with app use as the **independent variable** and memory scores and hippocampal volume as **dependent variables**, and keep everything else the same so that no **confounded variable** remains.

**What does the television demonstration show?** Very little. It is one patient, a **case observation**, with no control condition, presented through the **media** rather than in a **peer-reviewed journal**. That is the profile of **Delgado's bull**. By **Morgan's Canon**, simpler explanations come first: practice with the word list, coaching, an unrepresentative patient, or ordinary variation in performance.

**What about "hundreds of patients transformed"?** That is the **lobotomy** error. The judges of improvement are the clinic's own staff, the least objective observers available, and "transformed" is undefined. Ask what was measured, by whom, against which comparison group, and whether **side effects** were recorded.

**What would convince?** **Converging operations**: controlled animal experiments (physiological psychology), randomised trials in patients, and imaging evidence of a mechanism (cognitive neuroscience), all pointing the same way.

**And the man himself?** His history suggests **Korsakoff's syndrome** must be considered. The chapter's message there is concrete: it is **largely caused by thiamine deficiency**; the established response is to **stop drinking** and give **large doses of thiamine**, which limits further damage and may bring slight improvement; but damage already done is **mostly irreversible**. A neuropsychological assessment (the **most applied** division) would characterise his deficits and guide care and counselling, as it did for **Mr. R.**

## MCQ traps

1. **Neuron count**: the 11th edition says **90 billion** (Walløe, Pakkenberg & Fabricius, 2014); older editions say 100 billion. **100 trillion** is the number of **connections**; the brain weighs about **1.3 kg**.
2. **Biopsychology** = scientific study of the **biology of behaviour** (Dewsbury, 1991); synonyms psychobiology, behavioural biology, behavioural neuroscience. **Physiological psychology** is a **division**, not a synonym, in this book.
3. Pinel prefers "biopsychology" because it denotes a **biological approach to psychology**, not a psychological approach to biology.
4. **Hebb, 1949, *The Organization of Behavior***: first comprehensive theory of how brain activity could produce complex psychological phenomena; **eclectic approach** became the field's hallmark. Do not confuse 1949 (Hebb) with 1949 (Moniz and Hess Nobel).
5. **Four major themes**: thinking creatively, clinical implications, evolutionary perspective, neuroplasticity. **Two emerging themes**: thinking about epigenetics, consciousness.
6. **Neuroplasticity**: adult brain is plastic; called arguably the **single most influential discovery in modern neuroscience**; **not always beneficial**; the old fixed "wiring diagram" view held until the early **1990s**.
7. **Evolutionary perspective** (theme) contains the **comparative approach** (method: compare species); **comparative psychology** is the division.
8. **Two lists of six**: neuroscience disciplines (neuroanatomy, neurochemistry, neuroendocrinology, neuropathology, neuropharmacology, neurophysiology) versus biopsychology's divisions (physiological psychology, psychopharmacology, neuropsychology, psychophysiology, cognitive neuroscience, comparative psychology).
9. **Most common nonhuman subjects** = **mice and rats**.
10. Human advantages include being **cheaper**; nonhuman advantages are **simpler** systems, the **comparative approach**, and **fewer** (not no) ethical constraints.
11. **Three R's**: **reduction** (fewer animals), **refinement** (less suffering, e.g. better housing), **replacement** (cell cultures, computer models; the **crash-test dummy** replaced pigs).
12. **Independent variable** = manipulated; **dependent variable** = measured; **confounded variable** = an unintended second difference between conditions.
13. **Between-subjects** = different groups per condition (the usual design); **within-subjects** = the same subjects in all conditions.
14. **Coolidge effect** is named after **President Calvin Coolidge**, not a researcher.
15. **Lester & Gorzalka (1988)**: **hamsters**; demonstrated the Coolidge effect in **females**; controlled **male fatigue**; DV = **lordosis duration**; females more receptive to the **unfamiliar** male on test 3.
16. **Quasiexperimental** = groups exposed in the real world, **no random assignment**, **correlation only**; the press often calls them experiments.
17. **Acker et al. (1984)**: **100** detoxified male former heavy drinkers versus **50** male nondrinkers; confounds were education, head injury, other drugs and **diet**.
18. **Case studies**: one or a few subjects, rarely controls, in-depth, **hypothesis-generating**; main problem **generalisability**.
19. **Pure vs applied** is defined by the researcher's **motive**; **pure** research is more vulnerable to politics; **translational** research carries pure findings to application.
20. **Pavlov's 1904 Nobel** was for **digestion**, not conditioning. **1981** = Sperry (split hemispheres) **and** Hubel and Wiesel (visual neurons). **1973** = von Frisch, Lorenz, Tinbergen (animal behaviour).
21. **Moniz, 1949**, prefrontal lobotomy, is the book's evidence that the Nobel committees are **not infallible**.
22. **Physiological psychology**: direct surgical/electrical manipulation, almost always **lab animals**, tradition of **pure** research.
23. **Psychopharmacology**: drugs; **substantially applied**; humans only where ethics permit.
24. **Neuropsychology**: human patients, **case studies and quasiexperiments**, cortex-focused, **most applied** division.
25. **Mr. R. (Kolb & Whishaw, 1990)**: 21-year-old left-handed honours student, **right-hemisphere language** (about one-third of left-handers), suspected **right temporal** damage, superior IQ but poor verbal memory and reading speed, switched to **architecture**.
26. **Psychophysiology**: humans, **noninvasive**, scalp **EEG** plus ANS measures (heart rate, blood pressure, pupil dilation, skin conductance), muscle tension, eye movements; schizophrenia **smooth-pursuit** deficit (Iacono & Koenig, 1983, in Fig 1.5; Meyhöfer et al., 2014).
27. **Cognitive neuroscience**: the **youngest** division; human cognition; **functional brain imaging**; interdisciplinary (economists included).
28. **Comparative psychology**: evolution, genetics and adaptiveness of behaviour; **ethological research** = behaviour in the natural environment. **Seed-caching birds have larger hippocampi** is its memory example.
29. **Scan Your Brain item 6** prints "evolutionary behavioral genetics", but the description matches **comparative psychology**.
30. **Converging operations** (Thompson, 2005) = different approaches on one problem with complementary strengths; not replication.
31. **Korsakoff's syndrome**: **Sergei Korsakoff**, Russian physician, late 19th century; primary symptom **severe memory loss**; largely caused by **thiamine (B1) deficiency**; alcohol may also be directly toxic (accelerates damage in thiamine-deficient rats); about **10 percent** of US adult dementias; treated with **thiamine**, damage mostly irreversible.
32. **Perception of motion**: brain subtracts only **active** eye movements. Passive upward push: world moves **down**. **Hammond, Merton & Sutton (1956)**, **curare**, participant **Merton**: attempted movement with paralysed eyes, world moves in the **same** direction as the attempt.
33. **Peer review**: usually **three or four** expert reviewers; a first check, not a guarantee.
34. **Delgado**: bull, **caudate nucleus**, radio stimulation, media event, "taming centre"; **Valenstein (1973)**: the bull was forced to turn, a **motor pathway** was activated; **Morgan's Canon** = simplest interpretation first.
35. **Lobotomy**: Moniz + **Almeida Lima**, **leucotome**, **six cores**; based on **two chimpanzees** (older editions: one, Becky) shown at a **1935** meeting; **transorbital** version developed in **Italy**, popularised by **Walter Freeman**, late **1940s**, through the **orbit**; **40,000+** in the US; **Howard Dully** at age **12**, **10 minutes**.
36. Lobotomy side effects: **socially inappropriate behaviour**, lack of foresight, emotional unresponsiveness, **epilepsy**, **urinary incontinence**; early "improvement" meant **manageability**, judged by the **prescribing physicians**.

| **Neuroendocrinology** | Study of interactions between the nervous system and the endocrine system |
| **Neuropathology** | Study of nervous system dysfunction |
| **Neuropharmacology** | Study of the effects of drugs on neural activity |
| **Neurophysiology** | Study of the functions and activities of the nervous system |
| **Participants / volunteers** | The usual terms for human subjects, marking that they volunteer |
| **Three R's** | Reduction, refinement and replacement: the principles stressed by nonhuman animal ethics committees |
| **Between-subjects design** | A different group of subjects is tested under each condition |
| **Within-subjects design** | The same subjects are tested under every condition |
| **Independent variable** | The single intended difference between experimental conditions |
| **Dependent variable** | The variable measured to assess the effect of the independent variable |
| **Confounded variable** | An unintended difference between conditions that could account for the effect on the dependent variable |
| **Coolidge effect** | A male unable to continue copulating with one partner can often resume with a new partner; named after President Calvin Coolidge |
| **Lordosis** | The arched-back, rump-up, tail-diverted receptive posture of female rodents; Lester and Gorzalka's dependent variable |
| **Quasiexperimental studies** | Studies of groups exposed to conditions of interest in the real world, without random assignment; show correlation only |
| **Case studies** | Studies focusing on one subject or a very small number; in depth, hypothesis-generating, usually without controls |
| **Generalizability (generalisability)** | The degree to which a study's results apply to other cases; the main weakness of case studies |
| **Pure research** | Research motivated by curiosity, done solely to acquire knowledge |
| **Applied research** | Research intended to bring direct benefit to humankind |
| **Translational research** | Research that aims to turn the findings of pure research into useful applications |
| **Physiological psychology** | Division studying neural mechanisms of behaviour by direct manipulation and recording of the brain in controlled experiments, usually on lab animals |
| **Psychopharmacology** | Division studying the effects of drugs on brain and behaviour; substantially applied |
| **Neuropsychology** | Division studying the psychological effects of brain dysfunction in human patients; the most applied |
| **Cerebral cortex** | The outer layer of the cerebral hemispheres; most often damaged by accident or surgery, hence neuropsychology's focus |
| **Psychophysiology** | Division studying the relation between physiological activity and psychological processes in humans by noninvasive recording |
| **Electroencephalogram (EEG)** | Recording of brain electrical activity from the scalp; the usual brain measure in psychophysiology |
| **Autonomic nervous system (ANS)** | The part of the nervous system that regulates the body's inner environment |
| **Cognitive neuroscience** | The youngest division; studies the neural bases of cognition, mainly by functional brain imaging |
| **Cognition** | Higher intellectual processes such as thought, memory, attention and complex perception |
| **Functional brain imaging** | Recording images of the activity of the living human brain during a mental task; cognitive neuroscience's major method |
| **Comparative psychology** | Division comparing the behaviour of different species to understand the evolution, genetics and adaptiveness of behaviour |
| **Ethological research** | The study of behaviour in an animal's natural environment |
| **Converging operations** | Focusing different approaches on one problem so that the strengths of each offset the weaknesses of the others |
| **Korsakoff's syndrome** | Severe memory loss, common in heavy drinkers, largely caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency |
| **Thiamine (vitamin B1)** | The vitamin whose deficiency largely causes Korsakoff's syndrome; given in large doses as treatment |
| **Scientific method** | A system for finding things out by careful observation |
| **Empirical** | Observational; based on observation and measurement |
| **Scientific inference** | Measuring observable events and using them to infer logically the nature of unobservable processes |
| **Curare** | Paralytic substance whose active ingredient Hammond, Merton and Sutton injected into Merton's eye muscles |
| **Critical thinking** | The process of recognising the weaknesses of existing ideas and the evidence on which they rest |
| **Peer review** | Evaluation of a manuscript by experts in the field (usually three or four) before publication |
| **Morgan's Canon** | When several interpretations of a behavioural observation are possible, give precedence to the simplest |
| **Caudate nucleus** | Deep brain structure in which Delgado implanted the bull's stimulating electrode |
| **Prefrontal lobotomy** | Surgery cutting the connections between the prefrontal lobes and the rest of the brain to treat mental illness |
| **Prefrontal lobes** | The large left and right areas at the very front of the brain |
| **Lesion** | An area of damage; a bilateral lesion damages both sides of the brain |
| **Leucotome** | The surgical instrument Lima used to cut six cores of prefrontal tissue |
| **Transorbital lobotomy** | Lobotomy performed by driving an ice-pick-like instrument through the eye socket; developed in Italy, popularised in the US by Freeman |
| **Orbit** | The eye socket |
| **Psychosurgery** | Any brain surgery performed to treat a psychological problem |
