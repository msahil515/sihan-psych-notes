# Chapter 11: Learning, Memory, and Amnesia

**What this chapter is really about.** Pinel opens with a sentence worth taking literally: **learning** and **memory** are two ways of thinking about the same thing. Both are neuroplastic processes, both are about the brain changing its functioning in response to experience. **Learning** is how experience changes the brain; **memory** is how those changes are stored and later reactivated. Without them, Pinel says, every moment would feel like waking from a lifelong sleep: every person a stranger, every act a new challenge, every word incomprehensible. The chapter then asks the obvious biological question, *where* and *how* in the brain does this happen, and it answers it mainly by studying people in whom it has gone wrong. **Amnesia** is any pathological loss of memory, and most of what we know about the neural basis of memory comes from neuropsychological patients with brain-damage-produced amnesia and from animal models of those same patients.

The chapter has a plot, and you should see it before you start. It begins with one patient, H.M., whose surgery seemed to show that a single structure, the hippocampus, was the place where new memories are made. It then spends five modules progressively *complicating* that conclusion: memory turns out to be several systems, not one; the hippocampus turns out to matter less for object recognition than the cortex beside it; consolidation turns out to last years rather than seconds; and memories turn out to be stored diffusely, in the same circuits that first processed the experience. Finally it drops down to the level of single synapses (long-term potentiation) and ends by asking what any of this means for a person with a healthy brain. If an exam question assumes the 1950s view ("the hippocampus stores all new memories"), the chapter's whole job is to teach you why that is wrong.

**How to read this chapter.** Module 11.1 is the foundation: H.M., the five tests, explicit versus implicit and semantic versus episodic memory. Do not move on until you can state every one of those distinctions in one sentence. 11.2 and 11.3 add the other human amnesias (Korsakoff's, Alzheimer's, traumatic brain injury) and use them to introduce consolidation. 11.4 is the animal-model module and is the most reasoning-heavy part of the chapter; read the R.B. puzzle slowly. 11.5 and 11.6 move from lesions to neurons and to the five other brain areas. 11.7 is cellular (LTP, NMDA receptors), and 11.8 brings it back to everyday life. The MCQ-heavy content is concentrated in 11.1, 11.2, 11.3 and 11.7.

## 11.1 Amnesic Effects of Bilateral Medial Temporal Lobectomy

### H.M., the man who changed the study of memory

Pinel calls it ironic that the person who contributed more than anyone else to the neuropsychology of memory was not a neuropsychologist. He collaborated on dozens of memory studies, but he had no research training and not a single degree. He was **H.M.**, a man who in **1953, at the age of 27**, had the medial portions of both temporal lobes removed to treat a severe epilepsy. Pinel's analogy is the **Rosetta Stone**: just as that stone gave archaeologists the key to Egyptian hieroglyphics, H.M.'s deficits gave researchers the key to the neural bases of memory. H.M.'s real name was **Henry Molaison**. He died in **2008**, having donated his brain to science; it was sliced a few years after his death and images of the slices are archived online, so, as Pinel puts it, H.M. continues to contribute to science long after his death.

### Why the surgery was done

In the **11 years** before the operation H.M. averaged **one generalised seizure each week** and **many focal seizures each day**, despite massive doses of anticonvulsant medication. Electroencephalography (EEG) suggested that the seizures arose from foci in the medial portions of **both** his left and right temporal lobes.

The surgical logic followed directly. Removing **one** medial temporal lobe had already proved an effective treatment for patients with a **unilateral** temporal lobe focus. H.M. had a focus on both sides, so the decision was made to perform a **bilateral medial temporal lobectomy**: removal of the medial portions of both temporal lobes, including **most of the hippocampus, the amygdala, and adjacent cortex**.

Learn the two terms Pinel defines in passing, because they are a classic MCQ pair. A **lobectomy** is an operation in which a lobe, or a major part of one, is **removed** from the brain. A **lobotomy** is an operation in which a lobe, or a major part of one, is **separated** from the rest of the brain by a large cut but is **not removed**.

> **Trap.** H.M. had a lob**ectomy** (tissue taken out), not a lob**otomy** (tissue disconnected but left in place). The suffix *-ectomy* means excision in all of medicine (appendectomy, tonsillectomy). If an option says "H.M. underwent a bilateral temporal lobotomy", it is wrong.

### The outcome: a medical success and a memory catastrophe

In several respects the operation was an **unqualified success**. His generalised seizures were all but eliminated, and his focal seizures fell to **one or two per day**, even though his anticonvulsant dose was substantially **reduced**. He entered surgery a reasonably well-adjusted person with normal perceptual and motor abilities and **normal intelligence**, and he left it in nearly the same condition. Nevertheless, **H.M. was the last patient ever to receive a bilateral medial temporal lobectomy**, because of its devastating amnesic effects.

### Retrograde versus anterograde, short-term versus long-term

To assess the amnesic effects of brain surgery, clinicians give two kinds of test. Tests of memory for things learned **before** the surgery detect **retrograde amnesia** (backward-acting amnesia). Tests of memory for things learned **after** the surgery detect **anterograde amnesia** (forward-acting amnesia). Picture the moment of injury as a point on a timeline: retrograde amnesia reaches backwards from it, anterograde amnesia reaches forwards.

If a patient has anterograde amnesia, the next question is which store is affected. **Short-term memory** is the storage of new information for brief periods of time *while a person attends to it*. **Long-term memory** is the storage of new information *once the person stops attending to it*. Notice that the dividing line Pinel uses is **attention**, not a stopwatch. That definition will explain H.M.'s whole behavioural profile.

H.M.'s results on each dimension:

| Memory type | H.M.'s status |
|---|---|
| Intelligence | Normal, unchanged by surgery |
| Remote memory (childhood, distant past) | Reasonably normal |
| Retrograde memory | **Mild** retrograde amnesia for the **2 years** before surgery |
| Short-term memory | **Normal**: digit span of **6** (Wickelgren, 1968) |
| Long-term memory for new experiences | Almost total inability to form it |

The **digit span**, the classic test of short-term memory, is the longest list of digits a person can repeat back correctly after hearing it once. H.M.'s span of six meant that if six digits were read to him he could usually repeat them, but he struggled with longer lists. That is within the normal range.

What he could not do was hold on to anything **once he stopped thinking about it**. A new experience, once it left his attention, was lost forever. Pinel says he became suspended in time on the day in 1953 when he regained his health but lost his future. His family moved shortly after the surgery, and he never learned his new address or where commonly used items were kept in the new house. He never learned to recognise doctors and nurses he met after the operation. He read the same magazines over and over without finding them familiar. If you met him at a party, he could chat normally until he was distracted (by the phone, say); then he would remember neither you, nor the conversation, nor where he was. It was, Pinel writes, as if he was continually regaining consciousness.

> **Trap.** H.M.'s short-term memory was **normal**. Questions often offer "H.M. had a severely reduced digit span" as a distractor. His problem was transfer into long-term storage, not holding information in mind while attending to it. Equally, his retrograde amnesia was **mild and limited to about 2 years**; it is wrong to say he lost all memories from before the surgery.

### Formal assessment of H.M.'s anterograde amnesia: the five tests

To characterise H.M.'s problem objectively, researchers measured his performance on five tests of long-term memory. The pattern of results is the single most important thing in this module, so learn it as a pattern. The **first two tests** documented a severe long-term memory deficit. The **last three** showed that H.M.'s brain *could* store long-term memories, but that he had **no conscious awareness** of them. That finding changed the way biopsychologists think about the brain and memory.

| # | Test | What it measures | H.M.'s result | Conscious recollection? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Digit-span + 1 (Drachman & Arbit, 1966) | Verbal long-term memory | Failed to reach 8 digits in 25 trials (normal is about 15) | No |
| 2 | Block-tapping (Milner, 1971) | Non-verbal (visuospatial) long-term memory | Could not learn a 6-block sequence even after 12 repetitions | No |
| 3 | Mirror drawing (Milner, 1965) | Sensorimotor learning | Clear improvement across 3 days | **No** |
| 4 | Incomplete pictures (Milner et al., 1968) | Non-sensorimotor perceptual learning | Improved on retest 1 hour later | **No** |
| 5 | Pavlovian eyeblink conditioning (Woodruff-Pak, 1993) | Classical conditioning | Learned (slowly); retained almost perfectly 2 years later | **No** |

### Test 1: The digit-span + 1 test

The **digit-span + 1 test** is a classic test of **verbal long-term memory**. H.M. was read **5 digits at 1-second intervals** and asked to repeat them; he did so correctly. On the next trial the same 5 digits were presented in the same order with **one new digit added at the end**. That 6-digit sequence was repeated a few times until he got it right, then another digit was added, and so on.

Why is this a long-term memory test when digit span itself is a short-term memory test? Because the same sequence recurs across trials. A normal person carries the earlier part of the list over from trial to trial in long-term storage, so the list grows well beyond short-term capacity. **After 25 trials, H.M. had not managed to repeat an 8-digit sequence.** Most people can correctly repeat about **15 digits** after 25 trials (Drachman & Arbit, 1966). H.M.'s performance hovered just above his ordinary short-term span, which is exactly what you would expect from someone whose short-term store works and whose long-term store does not.

### Test 2: The block-tapping test

A critic might have argued that H.M.'s deficit was purely verbal. **Milner (1971)** ruled that out with the **block-tapping test**. An array of **9 blocks** was spread on a board in front of him; he watched the neuropsychologist touch a sequence of them and then had to repeat the sequence. A typical person has a **block-tapping span of 6**. H.M. could not learn to touch a sequence of 6 blocks correctly **even when the same sequence was repeated 12 times**.

The result established that H.M. had **global amnesia**: amnesia for information presented in **all sensory modalities**, not just for words. Hold on to that term; "global" in Pinel always means "across modalities", which is not the same thing as "complete".

### Test 3: The mirror-drawing test

The first hint that H.M.'s amnesia did **not** cover all long-term memory came from the **mirror-drawing test** (Milner, 1965). His task was to draw a line within the boundaries of a **star-shaped target** while watching his hand only in a mirror, which reverses the usual relation between what you see and how you must move. He traced the star **10 times on each of 3 consecutive days**, and the number of times he strayed outside the boundaries was recorded on every trial.

His errors fell within day 1, started day 2 lower than they had started day 1, and were low and flat by day 3. That is ordinary skill learning **retained across days**, which by definition is long-term memory. Yet despite his improved performance, **H.M. could not recall ever having done the task before**. Every day the apparatus was new to him; every day his hand behaved as if it had practised.

### Test 4: The incomplete-pictures test

The obvious first interpretation of the mirror-drawing result was that **sensorimotor** tasks were the one exception to H.M.'s inability to form long-term memories. The **incomplete-pictures test** challenged that, because it has no sensorimotor component.

The test uses **five sets of fragmented drawings** of the **same 20 objects**. The sets differ only in completeness: **set 1 is the most fragmented**, **set 5 is the complete drawings**. The subject first tries to identify all 20 objects from set 1; any objects not recognised are then shown in their set 2 versions, and so on until all 20 have been identified. The score is the number of errors at each level.

When H.M. was retested **1 hour later** (Milner et al., 1968), he made far fewer errors and identified the objects from more fragmented versions than on the first test. His visual system had retained something from the first session. Yet, again, **he could not recall previously performing the task**. So the preserved learning was not a special exception for motor skills; it was something more general.

### Test 5: Pavlovian eyeblink conditioning

The final test was classical conditioning. H.M. learned an **eyeblink Pavlovian conditioning** task, **albeit at a slower rate** than normal (Woodruff-Pak, 1993). A **tone** was sounded just before a **puff of air** was delivered to his eye, and the pairings were repeated until the tone alone elicited a blink. **Two years later**, H.M. retained the conditioned response **almost perfectly**, although he had **no conscious recollection** of the training.

Note the two qualifiers, because examiners use them. His acquisition was **slower**, so conditioning was not wholly unaffected. And the retention interval was **two years**, so this is unquestionably long-term memory.

> **Trap.** Which of H.M.'s tests showed preserved learning? **Mirror drawing, incomplete pictures, and eyeblink conditioning.** Which showed impairment? **Digit-span + 1 and block-tapping.** The impaired pair are both tests that require conscious recall of a specific sequence; the preserved three are all demonstrated by improved performance. Do not confuse plain digit span (normal, a short-term test) with digit-span + 1 (impaired, a long-term test).

### Three major scientific contributions of H.M.'s case

H.M.'s case is a personal tragedy, but Pinel names three contributions that proved especially influential. Learn them in order; "three contributions of H.M." is a natural exam question.

**1. Localisation of memory.** By showing that the **medial temporal lobes** play an especially important role in memory, H.M.'s case **challenged the then-prevalent view that memory functions are diffusely and equivalently distributed throughout the brain**. It renewed efforts to relate individual brain structures to specific **mnemonic** (memory-related) processes, and in particular it spawned a massive research effort to clarify the mnemonic functions of the hippocampus and other medial temporal lobe structures.

**2. Separate modes of storage, and the idea of consolidation.** The surgery abolished H.M.'s ability to form certain long-term memories **without** disrupting his short-term memory or his **remote memory** (memory for experiences in the distant past). That dissociation supported the theory that there are **different modes of storage for short-term, long-term, and remote memory**. H.M.'s specific problem appeared to be a difficulty in **memory consolidation**, the translation of short-term memories into long-term memories.

**3. Conscious and unconscious memory.** H.M. was the **first case to reveal** that an amnesic patient might claim **no recollection** of a previous experience while **demonstrating memory for it by improved performance**, as on mirror drawing and incomplete pictures. This led to two categories of long-term memory. Conscious long-term memories became known as **explicit memories**; long-term memories demonstrated by improved test performance **without conscious awareness** became known as **implicit memories**. The distinction is of general relevance: many people with amnesia lose the ability to form explicit memories while keeping the ability to form implicit ones.

### Medial temporal lobe amnesia

Patients with a profile of memory deficits similar to H.M.'s, with **preserved intellectual functioning**, and with evidence of **medial temporal lobe damage** are said to suffer from **medial temporal lobe amnesia**. Research on such patients has shown that H.M.'s pattern, difficulty forming **explicit** long-term memories while retaining the ability to form **implicit** long-term memories of the *same* experiences, was not unique to him. It is a symptom of medial temporal lobe amnesia and of many other amnesic disorders. As a result, the assessment of implicit memory now plays an important role in the study of human memory (Reber, 2013).

### Repetition priming tests

Tests that assess implicit memory are called **repetition priming tests**. The incomplete-pictures test and the mirror-drawing task are two examples, but the most common versions use **words**. The procedure has two stages:

1. Participants examine a list of words. They are **not** asked to learn or remember anything.
2. Later they are shown **fragments** of words from the list (Pinel's example is **_ O B _ _ E R**) and simply asked to complete them.

Controls who saw the original list perform well. Surprisingly, **participants with amnesia often perform equally well**, even though they have **no explicit memory** of having seen the list. (The answer to the example is "lobster".) The design is clever because the instruction never mentions memory: the patient is just completing a puzzle, so whatever memory is shown has to be implicit.

### Why have two memory systems at all?

If there are two parallel systems, one conscious and one unconscious, why? Pinel reasons that the **implicit system probably evolved first**, because it is simpler and does not involve consciousness. So the real question is: what is the advantage of adding a **second, conscious** system?

Two experiments point to the same answer, **flexibility**. One was with amnesic patients (**Reber, Knowlton, & Squire, 1996**), the other with amnesic **monkeys** with medial temporal lobe lesions (**Buckley & Gaffan, 1998**). In both, the amnesic subjects learned an implicit learning task **as well as controls**; but when asked to use their implicit knowledge **in a different way or in a different context**, they **failed miserably**. Presumably, then, explicit memory evolved to allow the **flexible use of information**: implicit knowledge is locked to the situation in which it was acquired, whereas explicit knowledge can be carried elsewhere and applied in new ways.

### Semantic and episodic memories

H.M. formed very few new explicit memories of any kind. Most people with medial temporal lobe amnesia have **less complete** deficits, and studying them showed that explicit memory itself splits in two, with many patients far more impaired on one half than the other.

Explicit long-term memories come in two varieties (Squire et al., 2015):

- **Semantic memories** are explicit memories for **general facts or information**.
- **Episodic memories** are explicit memories for **specific moments (episodes) in one's life** (Rugg & Vilberg, 2013). Episodic memory is also called **autobiographical memory**, and has been likened to **travelling back in time mentally** and re-experiencing one's past.

People with medial temporal lobe amnesia have **particular difficulty with episodic memories**. They cannot remember having breakfast with an old friend this morning, or going to a new film this afternoon, but they often remember a language they learned, world events, and the things they learned at school. **Endel Tulving** has been a major force in research on the semantic-episodic dichotomy (Tulving, 2002).

### The case of K.C., the man who can't time travel

**K.C.**, Tulving's patient, had a **motorcycle accident in 1981** and suffered **diffuse brain damage, including damage to the medial temporal lobes**. Despite severe amnesia, his other cognitive abilities remained remarkably normal. His general intelligence and language were normal; he could concentrate; he played the organ, chess and various card games; his reasoning was good; and his knowledge of mathematics, history, science, geography and other school subjects was good.

He also retained many **facts** about his own early life: his birth date, where he lived as a youth, where his parents' summer cottage was, the names of his schools, the makes and colours of cars he had owned. But amid all this he had severe amnesia for **personal experiences**. He could not recall **a single personal event for more than a minute or two**, and this inability to recall any episode at which he was present covered **his entire life**. Remarkably, he had no difficulty holding a conversation, and his deficit was far less obvious to others than you would expect. He got by, Pinel says, using only semantic memory.

The final detail is the most theoretically important: K.C. understood the concept of time but could not "time travel" **into either the past or the future**. He could no more **imagine future events** (what he would do for the rest of the day, the week, or his life) than recall past ones. Episodic memory, on this evidence, is not only a record of the past but also the machinery for projecting oneself into the future.

> **Trap.** K.C. knew facts **about** his own life (birth date, cars, schools) but could not recall any **event** from it. Facts about yourself are **semantic**, not episodic. An option saying "K.C. had lost all autobiographical knowledge" is too strong; he lost autobiographical *episodes*. And remember the future-imagination deficit; it is the detail examiners like to test.

### Early-life medial temporal lobe damage: Vargha-Khadem and colleagues

**Vargha-Khadem and colleagues (1997)** followed the development of **three patients** who had suffered bilateral medial temporal lobe damage **early in life**. Although they remembered few of the experiences of their daily lives (episodic memory), they **progressed through mainstream schools** and acquired reasonable levels of language and factual knowledge (semantic memory). Despite this academic success, **their episodic memory did not improve** (de Haan et al., 2006).

This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the module that semantic and episodic memory are separable: you can *build* a store of semantic knowledge across years of schooling without any working episodic system to record the lessons themselves.

### The case of the clever neuropsychologist: spotting episodic memory deficits

Episodic deficits are hard to detect, even when extreme, for two reasons: the neuropsychologist usually has **no way of knowing the true events** of the patient's life, and patients become **very effective at giving semantic answers to episodic questions**. Pinel illustrates with a paraphrased exchange.

A neuropsychologist asks a former history teacher to recall one specific lecture. The patient answers with generalities ("I have given thousands of lectures", "I especially liked Greek history", "sometimes I was quite funny", "all my students were there watching and smiling") and, pushed for one unique event, admits "That's hard." The neuropsychologist then tells him a piece of news he will enjoy, that his team, the **Toronto Maple Leafs**, beat New York **6-0** the night before, and asks him to remember the score. An hour later the patient has no idea what he was asked to remember and cannot say when the last meeting was. But asked a general question about the Maple Leafs, he talks happily about going to games with his father as a child and then volunteers: "Did you know that they beat the New York Rangers 6-0?"

Read the logic carefully. The *fact* (the score) was retained and came out embedded in general knowledge about the team, which is semantic retrieval. The *episode* of being told it, by this person, an hour ago, was gone. That is the medial temporal lobe pattern in miniature, and it shows why a clinician must ask for a **specific, unique, datable event**, not general recollections.

### Effects of global cerebral ischaemia on the hippocampus and memory: the case of R.B.

H.M.'s lesion took out the hippocampus, the amygdala and adjacent cortex together, so it could not show which structure was responsible. Pinel gives **two pieces of evidence** that **selective hippocampal dysfunction** can by itself cause medial temporal lobe amnesia. The first comes from **global cerebral ischaemia**, an interruption of the blood supply to the **entire** brain, which often produces medial temporal lobe amnesia.

**R.B.** (Zola-Morgan, Squire, & Amaral, 1986) underwent **cardiac bypass surgery at the age of 52**. The surgery was bungled: the pump that circulated his blood while his heart was disconnected **broke down**, and it was **several minutes** before a replacement arrived from another part of the hospital. R.B. survived, but the resulting ischaemic brain damage left him amnesic. His amnesia was **not as severe as H.M.'s** but was comparable in many respects.

R.B. died in **1983**, and with his family's permission a detailed post-mortem examination was carried out. **Obvious damage was restricted largely to the pyramidal cell layer of just one part of the hippocampus, the CA1 subfield.**

You need the anatomy to read that sentence. The hippocampus has **CA1, CA2, CA3 and CA4 subfields** plus the **dentate gyrus**. **CA** stands for **cornu ammonis**, another name for the hippocampus. The **pyramidal cell layer** is the band of large pyramidal neurons running through the CA fields.

R.B.'s case therefore suggested that **hippocampal damage by itself can produce medial temporal lobe amnesia**. Pinel immediately adds the caveat that will become crucial in 11.4: in cases of cerebral ischaemia **it is difficult to rule out subtle dysfunction in other areas of the brain**.

### Transient global amnesia

The second, and arguably the **strongest**, evidence that selective hippocampal damage can cause medial temporal lobe amnesia comes from **transient global amnesia**. It is defined by **sudden onset in the absence of any obvious cause in otherwise normal adults**. As in other medial temporal lobe amnesias, there is **severe anterograde amnesia** and **moderate retrograde amnesia** for **explicit episodic memories** (Arena & Rabinstein, 2015; Bartsch & Butler, 2013). The difference is that it is **transient**, typically lasting only **4 to 6 hours**. Pinel asks you to imagine the distress of an otherwise healthy person suddenly developing H.M.'s symptoms for an afternoon.

The sudden onset suggested a stroke, but until recently no brain pathology could be linked to it. In recent years, investigators have identified **abnormalities in the CA1 subfield of the hippocampus**. Their **time course** is the clue to their cause: they are usually **not apparent for several hours** after the attack begins and have usually **cleared up 10 days later**, a pattern suggestive of **ischaemia-induced damage** (Hunter, 2011).

> **Trap.** Two ischaemic stories, one subfield. R.B. (permanent, cardiac surgery) and transient global amnesia (temporary, no obvious cause) both point to **CA1**. Do not write CA3 or the dentate gyrus. And "global" here again means **all modalities**, not total: transient global amnesia spares semantic memory, short-term memory and identity.

## 11.2 Amnesias of Korsakoff's Syndrome and Alzheimer's Disease

The amnesias of 11.1 came from brain dysfunction that occurred over a short period and was **localised**. This module turns to two syndromes in which dysfunction develops **slowly** and is **diffuse**.

### Korsakoff's syndrome: aetiology and course

**Korsakoff's syndrome** is a disorder of memory most common in people who have consumed **large amounts of alcohol**. It is largely attributable to brain dysfunction associated with the **thiamine deficiency** that often accompanies heavy drinking (Scalzo et al., 2015). Note the causal chain: alcohol is the context; **thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency** is the proximate cause of the brain damage.

In its advanced stages it is characterised by a variety of **sensory and motor problems**, **extreme confusion**, **personality changes**, and a **risk of death from liver, gastrointestinal, or heart disorders**.

Post-mortem examination typically reveals:

- lesions of the **medial diencephalon**, meaning the **medial thalamus** and **medial hypothalamus**, and
- **diffuse damage** to several other structures, most notably the **neocortex, hippocampus and cerebellum** (Fama, Pitel, & Sullivan, 2012; Kril & Harper, 2012; Savage, Hall, & Resende, 2012).

### Korsakoff's amnesia compared with medial temporal lobe amnesia

The amnesia resembles medial temporal lobe amnesia in some respects. In the **early stages**, the most prominent symptom is **anterograde amnesia for explicit episodic memories**. It differs in its progression: as the disorder advances, **retrograde amnesia** also develops, and it can **eventually extend back into childhood**. Deficits in **implicit** memory depend on the particular test but are in general **less severe** than the explicit deficits (Oudman et al., 2011; Van Tilborg et al., 2011).

Because the damage is diffuse, it has been difficult to pin the amnesia on any one structure. Much attention has gone to one pair of medial diencephalic nuclei, the **mediodorsal nuclei of the thalamus**, because they are **almost always damaged** in Korsakoff patients. Pinel's verdict, however, is that it is **unlikely that the memory deficits are attributable to damage of any single structure**. Amnesia associated with damage to the medial diencephalon, of which Korsakoff amnesia is the main example, is called **medial diencephalic amnesia**.

> **Trap.** Korsakoff's is the **medial diencephalon** (mediodorsal thalamic nuclei, plus mammillary bodies in N.A.'s case), not the medial temporal lobe. Its retrograde amnesia **grows** over the course of the disease and can reach childhood, unlike H.M.'s mild two-year gradient. And the cause is **thiamine deficiency**, not alcohol toxicity as such.

### The up-your-nose case of N.A.

**N.A.** is a particularly well-known patient with medial diencephalic amnesia. His memory deficits were conventional; their cause was not (Teuber, Milner, & Vaughan, 1968).

N.A. joined the US Air Force after a year of college and served as a **radar technician**. One day his roommate was playing with a **fencing foil** behind his chair. N.A. turned unexpectedly and was stabbed **up the right nostril**. The foil punctured the **cribriform plate** (the thin bone around the base of the frontal lobes) and took an upward course into his brain.

His memory profile, in order:

- **Retrograde amnesia that shrank.** Tested a few weeks after the accident, he could not recall significant personal, national or international events from the **2 years** before it. Retested **3 years later**, the retrograde amnesia covered only the **2 weeks** before the accident.
- **Severe anterograde amnesia.** His recall of day-to-day events after the accident was extremely poor. On initial testing he could not remember what he ate for breakfast, people he had recently met, or family visits. Unpredictably, he would sometimes recall specific experiences of **no particular significance**.
- **Limited recovery.** His ability to remember new experiences improved somewhat, but never enough for him to **gain employment**.

An **MRI in the late 1980s** (Squire et al., 1989) revealed extensive medial diencephalic damage, including damage to the **mediodorsal nuclei** and the **mammillary bodies**.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, N.A. is the clean, **focal** counterpart to Korsakoff's diffuse damage: a single penetrating wound produced the same kind of amnesia, which strengthens the case that the medial diencephalon matters. Second, his **shrinking retrograde amnesia** is an important clinical observation: the lost period was not erased but was temporarily inaccessible, and much of it came back.

### The amnesia of Alzheimer's disease

**Alzheimer's disease** is another major cause of amnesia. Its first sign is often a **mild deterioration of memory**. It is **progressive**: eventually **dementia** develops and becomes so severe that the patient cannot perform even simple activities such as eating, speaking, recognising a spouse, or controlling the bladder. Alzheimer's disease is **terminal**.

Efforts to understand the neural basis of Alzheimer's amnesia have focused on **predementia Alzheimer's patients**, those who have not yet developed dementia. Their memory deficits are **more general** than those associated with medial temporal lobe damage, medial diencephalic damage, or Korsakoff's syndrome:

- **major anterograde and retrograde deficits** on tests of explicit memory;
- deficits in **short-term memory** (unlike H.M.);
- deficits in **some types of implicit memory**: implicit memory for **verbal and perceptual** material is often deficient, whereas implicit memory for **sensorimotor learning is not** (Postle, Corkin, & Growdon, 1996).

### The acetylcholine hypothesis and its limits

The level of **acetylcholine** is greatly reduced in the brains of Alzheimer's patients. This results from degeneration of the **basal forebrain**, a midline area located just above the hypothalamus, which is the **brain's main source of acetylcholine**. That finding, together with the finding that **strokes in the basal forebrain can cause amnesia**, led to the view that **acetylcholine depletion is the cause of Alzheimer's amnesia**.

Pinel's assessment is measured. Acetylcholine depletion from basal forebrain damage **may contribute** to Alzheimer's amnesia, but it is **clearly not the only factor**. The brain damage in Alzheimer's disease is **extremely diffuse**, involving many areas including the **medial temporal lobes** and the **prefrontal cortex**, both of which play major roles in memory (Braskie & Thompson, 2013).

| | Medial temporal lobe amnesia | Korsakoff's (medial diencephalic) | Predementia Alzheimer's |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key damage | Hippocampus and medial temporal cortex | Mediodorsal thalamus (plus diffuse) | Basal forebrain, medial temporal lobes, prefrontal (diffuse) |
| Onset | Sudden (surgery, ischaemia) | Slow | Slow |
| Anterograde explicit | Severe | Severe, earliest sign | Major |
| Retrograde | Mild to moderate, graded | Grows, can reach childhood | Major |
| Short-term memory | Normal | Not the main deficit | **Deficient** |
| Implicit memory | Largely spared | Less impaired than explicit; test-dependent | Verbal/perceptual impaired; sensorimotor spared |

> **Trap.** "The cause of Alzheimer's amnesia is acetylcholine depletion" is the textbook distractor. Pinel says depletion from **basal forebrain** degeneration may **contribute** but is **not the only factor**. Also note that predementia Alzheimer's patients, unlike H.M., show **short-term memory** and **some implicit memory** deficits, while their **sensorimotor** implicit learning is spared.

## 11.3 Amnesia after Traumatic Brain Injury: Evidence for Consolidation

### Posttraumatic amnesia

**Closed-head traumatic brain injuries (TBIs)**, brain injuries produced by blows to the head that do **not penetrate the skull**, are the **most common cause of amnesia**. The amnesia that follows a closed-head TBI is called **posttraumatic amnesia (PTA)**.

The sequence of events is the core of this section, and the figure in the book lays it out as a timeline:

1. A blow to the head produces a **coma** (a pathological state of unconsciousness). It usually lasts a few seconds or minutes, but in severe cases can last **weeks**.
2. On regaining consciousness, the patient goes through a **period of confusion**.
3. When the confusion ends, the patient has **retrograde amnesia** for events in the period **just before the blow**, and **anterograde amnesia** for events during the **period of confusion**.

Victims are usually not tested by a neuropsychologist until after the confusion has cleared, if they are tested at all. Testing typically reveals **permanent retrograde amnesia for the events leading up to the blow** and **permanent anterograde amnesia for many of the events of the subsequent period of confusion**.

The anterograde deficit often puzzles friends and relatives who talked to the patient during the confusion, for example during a hospital visit. The patient may have seemed reasonably **lucid** at the time, because **short-term memory is normal**, yet later has **no recollection whatsoever** of the conversation. This is H.M.'s party conversation in another form: the patient can converse in the moment, but nothing is consolidated.

Three relative durations to learn exactly, because they are routinely tested:

- The period of **confusion and anterograde amnesia** is typically **longer** than the **coma**,
- which is typically **longer** than the period of **retrograde amnesia**.
- **More severe blows** produce **longer comas, longer periods of confusion, and longer periods of retrograde amnesia**.

Not shown on the timeline are **islands of memory**: surviving memories for **isolated events** that occurred during periods for which other memories have been wiped out.

> **Trap.** Order of durations after closed-head TBI: **confusion/anterograde amnesia > coma > retrograde amnesia**. A common distractor reverses the last two. Also, "closed-head" means the skull is **not penetrated**; N.A.'s foil injury, which did penetrate (through the cribriform plate), is not a closed-head TBI.

### Gradients of retrograde amnesia and Hebb's consolidation theory

The **gradient** of retrograde amnesia after closed-head TBI seems to provide evidence for **memory consolidation**. If the blow preferentially wipes out **recent** memories while sparing **older** ones, then the storage of older memories must somehow have been **strengthened**, that is, consolidated.

The classic theory of consolidation is **Hebb's**. He argued that the memory of an experience is stored in the **short term** by neural activity **reverberating** (circulating) in **closed circuits** (Hanslmayr, Staresina, & Bowman, 2016). These reverberating patterns are **susceptible to disruption**, for example by a blow to the head, but eventually they induce **structural changes in the involved synapses**, which provide **stable long-term storage**. Notice what the theory predicts about time: reverberating activity cannot plausibly last long, so consolidation on Hebb's account should be **relatively brief**, a few seconds or minutes.

### Electroconvulsive shock as a tool for studying consolidation

**Electroconvulsive shock (ECS)** seemed to offer a controlled way to study consolidation. ECS is an **intense, brief, diffuse, seizure-inducing current** administered to the brain through **large electrodes attached to the scalp**.

The rationale is elegant. If ECS disrupts ongoing neural activity, it should erase from storage **only** those memories not yet converted into structural synaptic changes. So the **length of the retrograde amnesia** an ECS produces gives an **estimate of how long consolidation takes**. Some studies used human patients receiving ECS for **depression**; others used laboratory animals. Since the **1950s**, hundreds of studies have measured ECS-produced gradients of retrograde amnesia for this purpose.

Hebb's theory implies short gradients, of seconds or minutes. But **many studies found evidence for much longer gradients**.

### Squire, Slater and Chace (1975): a three-year gradient

The classic example is **Squire, Slater, and Chace (1975)**. Read the design closely, because its cleverness is in the choice of material.

> **Key study, Squire, Slater, & Chace (1975).** ECS-treated patients were tested on their memory for **television shows that had played for only one season**, in different years before their electroconvulsive therapy. Each patient was tested **twice**, on different forms of the test: **once before** a series of **five** electroconvulsive shocks and **once after**. The difference between before and after scores estimated memory loss for each year. The five shocks disrupted retention of shows that had played in the **3 years** before treatment, but **not** of shows that had played earlier.

Why one-season shows? Because a show that ran for only one season was learned about in one particular year and not rehearsed afterwards, so it dates the memory precisely. And why test each patient before and after? Because each patient serves as his or her own control, which removes individual differences in television-watching.

The conclusion is that consolidation, at least in the sense of becoming resistant to ECS, takes **years**, not seconds. That is incompatible with Hebb's reverberating-circuit timescale.

### The current view: consolidation without end

The current view is that consolidation continues for a **very long time, if not indefinitely** (Dudai, Karni, & Born, 2015; Dudai & Morris, 2013). Lasting memories become **more and more resistant to disruption throughout a person's life**. The proposed reason is that **each time a memory is activated, it is updated and linked to additional memories** (Sandrini, Cohen, & Censor, 2015), and those additional links increase its resistance to disruption by cerebral trauma such as concussion or ECS.

### The hippocampus and consolidation: standard consolidation theory

H.M.'s case provided evidence of consolidation and seemed to point to a special role for the hippocampus. To explain why his surgery disrupted only the retrograde memories from the few years before it, **Scoville and Milner (1957)** suggested that memories are **temporarily stored in the hippocampus until they can be transferred to a more stable cortical storage system**. This has become known as the **standard consolidation theory** or **dual-trace theory** (Clark & Maguire, 2016; Dudai, Karni, & Born, 2015; Moscovitch et al., 2016).

Today, Pinel says, there are **few adherents** to standard consolidation theory. The argument against it is short and worth reproducing exactly. **Temporally graded retrograde amnesia is a feature of many forms of human amnesia**, including Alzheimer's and Korsakoff's amnesia, which involve damage well beyond the hippocampus. If many different kinds of damage produce the same gradient, the gradient cannot be the signature of a special hippocampal transfer mechanism. So it seems unlikely that the hippocampus plays a **special** role in consolidation.

What replaces it? When a conscious experience occurs, it appears to be **rapidly and sparsely encoded in a distributed fashion throughout the hippocampus and other involved structures**.

### Moscovitch's account: new engrams and semanticisation

According to **Moscovitch and colleagues** (Nadel & Moscovitch, 1997; Winocur & Moscovitch, 2011), retained memories become progressively more resistant to disruption by hippocampal dysfunction because **each time a similar experience occurs, or the original memory is recalled, a new engram is established and linked to the original engram**. An **engram** is a change in the brain that stores a memory.

With each new engram, aspects of the original memory are progressively **transformed into a semantic memory**, whose storage is **less dependent on the hippocampus** and **more dependent on cortical structures** (Clark & Maguire, 2016; Moscovitch et al., 2016). This makes the memory **easier to recall** and the original engram **harder to disrupt**.

This account explains the gradient without a transfer box. Old memories survive hippocampal damage not because they have been shipped to the cortex on a schedule, but because they have been recalled and re-encoded many times, becoming more semantic and more widely linked.

> **Trap.** In Pinel, **standard consolidation theory** and **dual-trace theory** are two names for the **same** idea (Scoville & Milner, 1957: temporary hippocampal storage, then transfer to cortex). Other textbooks often call the Moscovitch-style account "multiple trace theory"; Pinel does not use that label, so do not attribute it to him. And Pinel says standard consolidation theory now has **few adherents**.

### Reconsolidation

**Reconsolidation** has attracted significant attention (Bonin & De Koninck, 2015). The hypothesis is that **each time a memory is retrieved from long-term storage, it is temporarily held in labile (changeable or unstable) short-term memory**, where it is once again **susceptible to posttraumatic amnesia** until it is reconsolidated.

Interest began with several studies in the **1960s**, faded, and was rekindled by a key study.

> **Key study, Nader, Schafe, & LeDoux (2000).** Rats were required to **recall** a fear-conditioning trial. Shortly afterwards, the **protein-synthesis inhibitor anisomycin** was infused into their **amygdalae**. The infusion produced **retrograde amnesia for the fear conditioning**, even though the original conditioning trial had occurred **many days before**.

The logic: a memory that was days old should have been long since consolidated and immune to a protein-synthesis inhibitor. It was not, **because it had just been retrieved**. Retrieval had returned it to a labile state that required new protein synthesis to re-stabilise. Most reconsolidation research has involved **fear conditioning**, but some evidence suggests it may be a **general phenomenon** in the nervous system (Bonin & De Koninck, 2015).

> **Trap.** In Nader et al. (2000), the drug was **anisomycin**, a **protein-synthesis inhibitor**, infused into the **amygdala** after **recall** (not after training). The amnesia was **retrograde**. Any option that places the infusion after the original conditioning trial misses the point of the study.

## 11.4 Evolving Perspective of the Role of the Hippocampus in Memory

### Why animal models were needed

As informative as amnesic patients are, they have **major limitations**. Many questions about the neural bases of amnesia can be answered only by **controlled experiments**. To identify which structures participate in which kinds of memory, you must make **precise lesions** in specific structures and control **what and when** subjects learn and **how and when** retention is tested. None of that is feasible in humans, so a major effort went into developing **animal models** of human brain-damage-produced amnesia.

### Why the first attempts failed

The first reports of H.M. in the 1950s triggered a massive effort to model his disorder in animals. In its early years the effort was a **dismal failure**: medial temporal lobe lesions did **not** produce severe anterograde amnesia in rats, monkeys or other species. In retrospect, Pinel gives **two reasons**, and this is one of the chapter's three "thinking creatively" points, so learn both:

1. **The wrong kind of test.** It was not initially apparent that H.M.'s anterograde amnesia was **specific to explicit long-term memory**. Yet most animal memory tests in the 1950s and 1960s, such as **Pavlovian and operant conditioning**, were tests of **implicit** memory, which H.M. himself had preserved. The animals were being tested on exactly the kind of memory the lesion spares.
2. **The wrong lesion.** It was **incorrectly assumed** that the amnesic effects of medial temporal lobe lesions were largely, if not entirely, due to **hippocampal** damage, so most modelling efforts focused on hippocampal lesions alone.

### The monkey version of the delayed nonmatching-to-sample test

Finally, in the **mid-1970s**, more than two decades after H.M., an animal model was developed. It was hailed as a breakthrough because it opened the neuroanatomy of medial temporal lobe amnesia to experimental investigation. In separate laboratories, **Gaffan (1974)** and **Mishkin and Delacour (1975)** showed that monkeys with bilateral medial temporal lobectomies have major problems forming long-term memories for objects in the **delayed nonmatching-to-sample test**.

The trial runs in four steps:

1. The monkey is shown a distinctive object, the **sample object**, under which it finds food (for example, a banana pellet).
2. A screen is lowered during a **delay**.
3. The monkey is then confronted with **two** objects: the sample object and an **unfamiliar** object.
4. To get the food, the monkey must **remember the sample and choose the unfamiliar one**.

Why *non*matching? Because monkeys have a natural preference for novelty, so choosing the new object comes easily once the animal remembers which object is old. The test therefore measures **object-recognition memory**: the only way to succeed is to recognise the sample as already seen.

### How the lesioned monkeys performed

Intact, well-trained monkeys chose correctly on about **90 percent** of trials when the retention intervals were a few minutes or less. Monkeys with bilateral medial temporal lobe lesions had major **object-recognition deficits**, and those deficits modelled H.M.'s in key respects:

- performance was **normal at delays of a few seconds** (short-term memory spared) but fell to **near chance at delays of several minutes**;
- performance was **extremely susceptible to distraction** (Squire & Zola-Morgan, 1985), just as H.M. lost a conversation when the phone rang.

The parallel is not only qualitative. **Humans with medial temporal lobe amnesia** have been tested on the same task, with **coins** as rewards instead of banana pellets, and their performance **mirrored** that of monkeys with similar damage. That cross-species match is what made the test a valid model.

### The three major structures of the medial temporal lobe

The monkey test made it possible to test the assumption that medial temporal lobe amnesia is entirely due to hippocampal damage. The medial temporal lobe has **three major structures**: the **hippocampus**, the **amygdala**, and the adjacent **medial temporal cortex**. The question for the next twenty years was which of the three, alone or in combination, is responsible for the object-recognition deficit. Before answering it, Pinel introduces a methodological development without which the answer could not have been reached: the rat version of the test.

### Why the rat version mattered: hippocampal lesions in monkeys and rats

The issue is surgical. Because of the **size and location** of the monkey hippocampus, almost all studies of hippocampal lesions in monkeys removed it by **aspiration (suction)** via the **inferior surface** of the brain, which destroys **large portions of the medial temporal cortex** as well. A "hippocampal" lesion in a monkey is therefore, in practice, a hippocampus-plus-cortex lesion, and it cannot tell you which component mattered.

In rats the anatomy is kinder. Aspiration of the hippocampus is usually performed via the **dorsal surface**, so the extraneous damage is typically limited to a **small area of parietal neocortex**, which is not thought to be involved in object recognition. Moreover, the rat hippocampus is small enough to be lesioned **electrolytically** or with **intracerebral neurotoxin injections**, methods that produce even less extraneous damage. The rat therefore allows a genuinely selective hippocampal lesion.

### The rat version: David Mumby and the Mumby box

The rat version that most closely resembles the monkey test was developed by **David Mumby**, using an apparatus now called the **Mumby box**. It is a long box with a compartment at each end and a central area, separated by sliding doors. A trial runs as follows:

1. The **sample object** is placed over a food cup at one end. At the other end, an object **identical to the sample** and a **novel object** are placed over two food cups.
2. A sliding door is raised; a trained, food-deprived rat runs to the sample and pushes it aside, and a food-delivery mechanism drops food into the exposed cup.
3. The experimenter immediately removes the sample, and the rat stays at that end for the **delay** (for example, 1 minute).
4. The other door is raised. A trained rat, remembering the sample, runs to the **novel object**, pushes it aside, and is rewarded.
5. The rat runs back to the centre, the door is closed behind it, and new objects are arranged for the next trial.

One practical advantage Pinel notes: the rats **do not have to be handled** either during or between trials, which removes a source of disturbance.

### How well do intact rats perform?

It was once assumed that rats could not perform a task this complex. They can: rats perform **almost as well as monkeys with delays of up to 1 minute** (Mumby, Pinel, & Wood, 1989). The comparison in the book plots intact monkeys (Zola-Morgan, Squire, & Mishkin, 1982) against intact rats; both are near 90 percent at short delays, and the rats fall off more steeply at the longest delay.

The **validity** of the rat version was established by lesion studies. As in humans and monkeys, combined bilateral lesions of the rat's **hippocampus, amygdala and medial temporal cortex** produce major deficits **at all but the shortest retention intervals** (Mumby, Wood, & Pinel, 1992). Same task, same lesion, same pattern across three species.

### Neuroanatomical basis of the object-recognition deficits

With both versions of the test available, researchers in the **early 1990s** began comparing lesions of individual medial temporal lobe structures in monkeys and rats. Challenges to the hippocampus-centred view accumulated quickly. Most reviewers (Bussey & Saksida, 2005; Duva, Kornecook, & Pinel, 2000; Mumby, 2001) reached the same three-part conclusion:

| Bilateral lesion of | Effect on delayed nonmatching-to-sample and other object-recognition tests |
|---|---|
| **Medial temporal cortex** | **Severe and permanent** deficits |
| **Hippocampus** | Only **modest** deficits |
| **Amygdala** | **No** deficits |

The rat data make the point graphically (Mumby & Pinel, 1994; Mumby, Wood, & Pinel, 1992): rats with **medial temporal cortex** lesions dropped well below their presurgery scores at every delay beyond the shortest, whereas rats with **hippocampus-plus-amygdala** lesions performed essentially as they had before surgery. Two of the three structures removed together did almost nothing; the third alone did a great deal.

> **Trap.** For **object-recognition** memory, the critical medial temporal structure is the **medial temporal cortex** (the rhinal cortices), **not the hippocampus**. Hippocampal lesions give modest deficits; amygdala lesions give none. This reverses the 1950s assumption, and it is precisely the assumption examiners will offer you as the "obvious" answer.

### The R.B. puzzle: why ischaemia misled

The finding that medial temporal **cortex** lesions are devastating while **hippocampal** lesions are only modestly harmful revived interest in **R.B.** Recall that his ischaemic damage was restricted largely to the **CA1 pyramidal cell layer**. The result was replicated in **monkeys** (Zola-Morgan et al., 1992) and **rats** (Wood et al., 1993): in both, global cerebral ischaemia caused loss of **CA1 pyramidal cells** and **severe** deficits on delayed nonmatching-to-sample.

Taken alone, that convergence across three species looks like strong support for a key hippocampal role in object recognition. Pinel calls the problem with it "gnawing", and the reasoning is the thing to learn:

1. Ischaemia damages **one small part** of the hippocampus (CA1) and produces **severe** deficits.
2. **Total removal** of the hippocampus produces only **modest** deficits.
3. A small part of a structure cannot do more damage than the whole structure. Therefore the severe deficits after ischaemia must come from damage **outside** the hippocampus (Mumby et al., 1996).

The independent evidence agrees. Although the most **obvious** damage after cerebral ischaemia is in CA1, there is **substantial damage elsewhere** that is more diffuse and therefore harder to quantify (Katsumata et al., 2006; van Groen et al., 2005). **Allen and colleagues (2006)** found that ischaemic patients with greatly reduced **hippocampal volume** were much more likely to have anterograde amnesia, but the same patients also tended to have **extensive neocortical damage**.

This is Pinel's second "thinking creatively" point and his Journal Prompt 11.3. The general lesson is about inference from lesions: the **most visible** damage is not necessarily the **responsible** damage, and diffuse damage is under-counted precisely because it is diffuse.

### The changing view of the hippocampus

Pinel summarises the trajectory, and it is a good paragraph to be able to reproduce:

1. **Initially**, the hippocampus was thought to be the site of temporary storage for **all** newly formed memories.
2. It was soon found that medial temporal lobe structures play a major role only in **explicit** (particularly **episodic**) memories.
3. It was then found that the hippocampus's role in **object-recognition** memory is **minor** compared with that of the adjacent **medial temporal cortex**.
4. **Today**, the hippocampus is considered **just one of several brain structures** that play important roles in memory.

The next module asks what its particular function is.

## 11.5 Neurons of the Medial Temporal Lobes and Memory

The first evidence about the medial temporal lobes came from lesions, but recording the **responses of single neurons** has also been enlightening. The first major studies recorded from **hippocampal neurons in rats**, and they were stimulated by one robust finding: bilateral hippocampal lesions **invariably disrupt** tasks that involve **memory for spatial location**. Two tasks illustrate it.

### The Morris water maze test

In the **Morris water maze test**, intact rats placed at various locations in a **circular pool of murky water** rapidly learn to swim to a **stationary platform hidden just below the surface**. Because the water is opaque and the start point varies, the rat cannot simply follow a fixed route or see the platform; it has to learn where the platform is in relation to cues around the room. Rats with hippocampal lesions learn this simple task **with great difficulty**.

### The radial arm maze: reference memory and working memory

In the **radial arm maze test**, several arms (for example, eight) radiate from a central start chamber, and **the same few arms are baited with food each day**. Intact rats learn to visit **only the baited arms** and **not to visit the same arm more than once** each day. Those two abilities measure two different kinds of memory, a distinction worth memorising:

- **Reference memory** is memory for the **general principles and skills** required to perform a task. Visiting only the baited arms measures it, because which arms are baited is constant across days.
- **Working memory** is **temporary memory** necessary for successful performance of the task one is **currently working on**. Not revisiting an arm today measures it, because which arms have already been visited changes every day.

Rats with hippocampal lesions show **major deficits on both** the reference and working memory measures.

> **Trap.** In the radial arm maze, entering an **unbaited** arm is a **reference** memory error; re-entering an arm **already visited today** is a **working** memory error. Hippocampal lesions impair **both**. Pinel's "working memory" here is the animal-learning definition (temporary memory for the current task), which is related to but not identical with Baddeley's model you may meet elsewhere.

### Hippocampal place cells

Consistent with a spatial role, many hippocampal neurons are **place cells** (Moser & Moser, 2016; Moser, Moser, & McNaughton, 2017): neurons that respond **only when the subject is in a specific location**, the cell's **place field**.

Place fields are **learned**. When a rat is first put into an **unfamiliar** environment, **none** of its hippocampal neurons has a place field there. As the rat familiarises itself with the environment, many neurons **acquire** a place field in it (Silva, Feng, & Foster, 2015), each cell firing only when the rat is in a particular part of the environment, so that different cells cover different parts. Place cells have been found in a variety of species (Finkelstein, Las, & Ulanovsky, 2016; Geva-Sagiv et al., 2016; Moser et al., 2014), **including human patients** (Miller et al., 2013).

The most striking finding is that place cells encode **belief**, not just location. By placing a rat in an **ambiguous situation** in a familiar environment, researchers can determine where the rat thinks it is along the route to a previously rewarded location. Using this strategy, they have shown that the firing of a rat's place cells indicates **where the rat "thinks" it is**, not necessarily where it actually is (Moser, Moser, & McNaughton, 2017). Place cells are therefore part of a representation the animal is using, not a simple GPS read-out.

### Entorhinal grid cells

One line of spatial research has focused on the **entorhinal cortex**, an area of the medial temporal cortex that is a **major source of neural signals to the hippocampus**. A possible answer to the question of how place cells get their spatial information came from the discovery of **grid cells** in the entorhinal cortex.

**Grid cells** are entorhinal neurons that each have a **repeating pattern of evenly spaced, hexagon-shaped place fields that tile the surface of an environment** (Moser & Moser, 2013; Rowland & Moser, 2014; Underwood, 2014). Compare that with a place cell, which has one field; a grid cell has many, laid out like the vertices of a honeycomb across the whole floor. Further properties:

- Grid cells fire when an animal **physically traverses** an intersection point of its grid (Hardcastle, Ganguli, & Giocomo, 2017), **or when its gaze traverses** such a point while surveying the environment (Killian & Buffalo, 2018; Nau et al., 2018).
- The grid is **flexible**: when spatial cues are **rotated or sheared**, the grid pattern is rotated or sheared correspondingly (Krupic et al., 2015; Rowland et al., 2016; Stensola et al., 2015).
- The **even spacing** of the fields could enable **spatial computations** in hippocampal place cells.
- Grid cells have been identified in other species **including humans** (Nau et al., 2018).

The entorhinal cortex contains other spatial neurons too: **head-direction cells**, tuned to the direction the head is facing, and **border cells**, which fire when the subject is near the **borders** of its immediate environment (Rowland et al., 2016; Sanders et al., 2015; Winter, Clark, & Taube, 2015).

The **medial temporal cortex** itself has three parts you need to name: **entorhinal**, **perirhinal**, and **parahippocampal** cortex. In humans these areas are largely hidden from view.

### The relationship between grid cells and place cells

How do grid cells and place cells relate? Pinel presents this as an **ongoing debate** (Rowland et al., 2016; Sanders et al., 2015), and it is a good example of the chapter's habit of setting an attractive simple model against awkward data.

**Two lines of evidence for a one-way relationship (grid cells drive place cells)** (but see Bush, Barry, & Burgess, 2014):

1. There is a **major pathway from the entorhinal cortex to the hippocampus**.
2. Entorhinal grid cells respond in an **ongoing fashion** to an animal's location, whereas hippocampal place cells are active **only in particular locations** (Mallory et al., 2018). It looks as if the general grid signal is being converted into specific place signals.

**Three lines of evidence that the relationship is more complex:**

1. In developing rat pups, **place-cell properties emerge before stable grid-cell firing** (Derdikman & Moser, 2010; Langston et al., 2010; Wills & Cacucci, 2014). An output cannot precede its necessary input.
2. **Place cells can still function after grid cells have been eliminated** (Sanders et al., 2015).
3. **Intact inputs from place cells are necessary for reliable grid-cell firing** (Rowland et al., 2016). Information flows the other way too.

> **Trap.** Place cells are in the **hippocampus** and have **one** place field each; grid cells are in the **entorhinal cortex** and have **many** evenly spaced **hexagonal** fields. The relationship is **not** simply grid-to-place: place cells appear first in development, survive grid-cell loss, and are needed for reliable grid firing.

### The hippocampus as a cognitive map

Although the hippocampus clearly plays a role in spatial learning, its cells respond to more than space. Some code the **temporal** aspects of an experience, so-called **"time cells"** (Eichenbaum, 2014; Giacomo, 2015; Navratilova & Battaglia, 2015). The hippocampus plays a role in learning about **social organisation ("social space")** in humans (Eichenbaum, 2015; Tavares et al., 2015) and in mice (Hitti & Siegelbaum, 2014). And, as the next section shows, hippocampal and surrounding neurons code **concepts**. Accordingly, it has been argued that the hippocampus is better thought of as generating a **"cognitive map"** rather than just a **"spatial map"** (Lisman et al., 2017; Epstein et al., 2017).

### Jennifer Aniston neurons: concept cells

Recording electrodes are sometimes implanted in the brains of patients with **severe epilepsy**, usually as a precursor to surgery, and many are placed in the medial temporal lobes because those structures are **particularly susceptible to epileptic discharges**. This gives a rare chance to record single neurons in awake humans performing tasks.

In one such recording, a neuron fired to images of the actor **Jennifer Aniston** but **not to 80 other images** (Quiroga, 2012). Other medial temporal lobe neurons were found that responded to other individuals (politicians, celebrities) or objects (famous buildings) known to the patients, but because the first responded to Jennifer Aniston, they were initially called **Jennifer Aniston neurons** (Quiroga, 2016).

Their properties:

- **Highly selective.** Each neuron responded to only a **small number** of test objects or individuals, often only **one** could be found.
- **Highly invariant.** If a neuron responded to an object on the first test, it tended to respond to it on all later tests.
- **Hippocampal cells are the most selective and invariant.** The Jennifer Aniston cells of the **hippocampus** were more selective and invariant than those of the other medial temporal structures (parahippocampal, perirhinal and entorhinal cortex, and amygdala).
- **They respond to concepts, not particulars.** This is "without question" their most remarkable feature, and it is why they are now called **concept cells** (Quiroga, Fried, & Koch, 2013).

The evidence for the last point is worth knowing in detail:

- A **Halle Berry neuron** responded to all photos of the actor, **even in her Catwoman costume**, to her **printed name**, and to the **sound of her name** (Quiroga, 2016). Three different sensory forms, one concept.
- A neuron that invariably responded to the **Sydney Opera House** also responded to photos of the **Bahá'í temple in India**. Questioned later, the patient said she had thought the temple photos **were** photos of the Sydney Opera House. The neuron tracked what she believed she was seeing.
- With **ambiguous faces** morphed from two celebrities (for example, **Whoopi Goldberg** and **Bob Marley**), concept cells responded **only when the viewer perceived** the concept to which they were tuned (Quiroga et al., 2014; Reddy & Thorpe, 2014). Same stimulus, different percept, different response.

When concept cells respond to **more than one** concept, there is usually an obvious relationship. On a second day of testing, the original Jennifer Aniston cell also responded to **Lisa Kudrow**, her co-star in *Friends*. Another neuron responded to both **Luke Skywalker and Yoda** from *Star Wars*. It has been suggested that **related concepts trigger activity in circuits of concept cells** in the medial temporal lobes (Quiroga, 2012; De Falco et al., 2016).

Pinel's own question is a good self-test: if researchers found a "Harry Potter neuron", what else might it fire to? On the evidence above, to the printed and spoken name, to the actor in other photos, and possibly to closely associated characters.

Pinel is careful about the conclusion: it is **not yet clear how concept cells contribute to memory storage**, but it is clear that they play a role, and their discovery is a major step forward (De Falco et al., 2016).

> **Trap.** "Jennifer Aniston neuron" and "concept cell" are the **same** thing. They were found in **human epilepsy patients** with implanted electrodes, not in rats. The defining property is response to the **concept** (across photo, written name and spoken name), not to a particular image. And the most selective, invariant ones are in the **hippocampus**.

### Engram cells and optogenetics

**Optogenetics** (introduced in Chapter 5) involves inserting an **opsin gene** into particular neurons, after which **light** can be used to **hyperpolarise or depolarise** them. It has been used extensively in studies of learning and memory in **mice** (Goshen, 2014). One line of research is especially interesting because it can locate the neurons that maintain an engram, so-called **engram cells** (Josselyn, Köhler, & Frankland, 2015; Tonegawa, Morrissey, & Kitamura, 2018).

**Engram cells** are neurons that undergo a **persistent change as the result of experience** such that, when they are later **activated**, retrieval of the original experience is **triggered**, and when they are later **inhibited**, retrieval is **suppressed** (Bliss et al., 2018; Tonegawa, Morrissey, & Kitamura, 2018).

Identifying an engram cell is typically a **two-stage process**:

1. **Tagging stage.** While the animal performs a particular learning task, the neurons that are **active during learning** are induced to **express opsins**.
2. **Manipulate stage.** Those previously active, now opsin-tagged neurons are **inhibited or excited with light**, and the effect on memory is observed (Josselyn, Köhler, & Frankland, 2015; Redondo et al., 2014; Ryan et al., 2015; Takeuchi & Morris, 2014).

The logic is causal rather than correlational. Earlier techniques could show that certain cells were active during learning; this one shows that turning **those specific cells** on recalls the memory and turning them off blocks it. Researchers can now **observe, suppress or activate** engram cells in different parts of the nervous system. Two applications:

- Researchers **reversed depressive-like behaviour in mice** by optogenetically reactivating hippocampal cells that had been active during encoding of a **positive experience** (Ramirez et al., 2015).
- In **mouse models of Alzheimer's disease**, activating engram cells led to retrieval of memories that were otherwise **inaccessible**, suggesting that the memory deficits of Alzheimer's disease are **retrieval deficits rather than encoding deficits** (Roy et al., 2016; Shrestha & Klann, 2016).

> **Trap.** The Alzheimer's engram finding was in **mouse models**, and it suggests the deficit is one of **retrieval**, not **encoding**: the memories were stored but could not be accessed naturally. The two stages are **tagging** then **manipulate**, in that order.

## 11.6 Where Are Memories Stored?

### Lashley's search for the engram

In the mid-20th century there was a major push to find where in the brain memories are stored. It was championed above all by **Karl Lashley**, who wrote a famous review paper, **"In Search of the Engram"**, describing his **fruitless** efforts. Lashley and his successors used the **lesion method**, and the logic was simple: if a structure is the storage site for all memories of a particular type, destroying it should **eliminate all memories of that type acquired before the lesion**.

**No brain structure has shown this result.** Lesions of particular structures tend to produce either **no retrograde amnesia at all**, or retrograde amnesia only for experiences in the **days or weeks just before** the surgery. Two major conclusions follow:

1. **Memories are stored diffusely** in the brain and therefore survive destruction of any single structure.
2. **Memories become more resistant to disruption over time.**

### The structures met so far

The chapter has already implicated **four** structures in memory: the **hippocampus** and the **medial temporal cortex**, which play roles in **episodic** memory; the **mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus**, implicated in **Korsakoff's** amnesia; and the **basal forebrain**, implicated in **Alzheimer's** amnesia. This module adds five more: **inferotemporal cortex, amygdala, prefrontal cortex, cerebellum and striatum**. (The striatum is left out of the book's summary brain diagram only because it would hide the other structures.)

| Structure | Type of memory implicated |
|---|---|
| Hippocampus | Explicit episodic memory; spatial memory; cognitive map |
| Medial temporal cortex | Object recognition; episodic memory |
| Mediodorsal nucleus of thalamus | Implicated in Korsakoff's amnesia |
| Basal forebrain | Implicated in Alzheimer's amnesia (acetylcholine) |
| **Inferotemporal cortex** | Storage of **visual** memories (with perirhinal cortex) |
| **Amygdala** | **Emotional significance** of experiences; strengthens other memories |
| **Prefrontal cortex** | **Temporal order** of events; **working memory**; sequencing |
| **Cerebellum** | **Sensorimotor skills**; eyeblink conditioning |
| **Striatum** | **Habit formation**: consistent stimulus-response relations learned incrementally |

### Inferotemporal cortex

Numerous electrophysiological and functional brain-imaging studies have reached the same important conclusion: **areas of the brain active during the retention of an experience tend to be the same ones that were active during the original experience**. That focused attention on the mnemonic functions of **sensory and motor** areas, in particular the **inferotemporal cortex** (cortex of the inferior temporal lobe), which has complex **visual** functions (Lehky & Tanaka, 2016; Naya & Suzuki, 2011; Suzuki, 2010).

**Bussey and Saksida (2005)** argued that the inferotemporal cortex, together with the adjacent **perirhinal cortex** (Miyashita, 2019; Suzuki & Naya, 2014), plays an important role in **storing memories of visual input**.

> **Key study, Naya, Yoshida, & Miyashita (2001).** Neurons in inferotemporal and perirhinal cortex were recorded while monkeys learned the relation between the two items in **pairs of visual images**. When a pair was **presented**, activity appeared **first in inferotemporal** neurons and **then in perirhinal** neurons. When the monkeys had to **recall** the pair, activity appeared **first in perirhinal** neurons and **then in inferotemporal** neurons.

Naya and colleagues concluded that this **reversed order** reflected the **retrieval of visual memories from inferotemporal cortex**. Think of it as a signal travelling inward during perception (sensory cortex to medial temporal cortex) and outward during recall (medial temporal cortex reaching back to the sensory store).

### Amygdala

The amygdala is thought to play a special role in memory for the **emotional significance** of experiences (Herry & Johansen, 2014; Paz & Pare, 2013). Two lines of evidence:

- **Rats** with amygdalar lesions, unlike intact rats, **do not respond with fear** to a neutral stimulus that has previously been followed by **electric foot shock** (Maren, 2015; Nader, 2015).
- **Bechara and colleagues (1995)** reported a patient with **bilateral amygdala damage** who **could not acquire conditioned autonomic startle responses** to visual or auditory stimuli but had **good explicit memory for the training**. The patient knew which stimulus had been followed by the startling event but did not react to it.

However, there is **little evidence that the amygdala stores memories**. It appears instead to be involved in **strengthening emotionally significant memories stored in other structures** (Do-Monte, Quiñones-Laracuente, & Quirk, 2015; Likhtik & Paz, 2015). The amygdala might be the reason **emotion-provoking events are remembered better than neutral ones** (Dunsmoor et al., 2015; McGaugh, 2015; Yonelinas & Ritchey, 2015).

> **Trap.** Bechara et al.'s (1995) amygdala patient is the mirror image of H.M.: **explicit memory of the training intact, conditioned emotional response absent**. And the amygdala **strengthens** emotional memories stored elsewhere rather than **storing** them. Recall also that amygdala lesions caused **no** object-recognition deficit in 11.4.

### Prefrontal cortex

Patients with damage to the **prefrontal cortex** (the area of frontal cortex anterior to motor cortex) are **not grossly amnesic**, and often show **no deficits at all** on conventional memory tests. Part of the reason for this lack of reliable deficits may be that **different parts of the prefrontal cortex play different roles**, and patients with damage to different areas are often **combined for analysis**, which averages away the effects.

Even so, **two episodic memory abilities** are often lost after **large** prefrontal lesions:

1. **Memory for the temporal order of events**, both anterograde and retrograde, **even when the events themselves are remembered**.
2. **Working memory**, the ability to maintain relevant memories while a task is being completed (D'Esposito & Postle, 2015; Constantinidis & Klingberg, 2016).

As a result of these two deficits, patients with prefrontal damage often have difficulty performing tasks that involve **a series of responses** (Colvin, Dunbar, & Grafman, 2001).

The prefrontal cortex is a large structure made up of many anatomically distinct areas with different connections and, presumably, different functions. Functional imaging finds specific complex patterns of prefrontal activity associated with different memory functions. Some regions appear to perform fundamental cognitive processes such as **attention and task management** during working-memory tasks; others participate in other memory processes (Lehky & Tanaka, 2016; Morici, Bekinschtein, & Weisstaub, 2015). Recent animal studies have highlighted an important role for **hippocampal-prefrontal connections in episodic memory** (Barker et al., 2017; Eichenbaum, 2017).

### The case of the cook who couldn't

One patient with prefrontal damage is well known because she was the **sister of Wilder Penfield**, the famous Montreal neurosurgeon (Penfield & Evans, 1935). Before her brain damage she had been an **excellent cook**. Afterwards she **still remembered her favourite recipes** and **how to perform each individual cooking technique**, yet she was **incapable of preparing even simple meals**, because she **could not carry out the steps in the proper sequence**.

### Cerebellum and striatum

Just as **explicit** memories are presumed to be stored in the circuits that mediated their original perception, **implicit memories of sensorimotor learning** are presumed to be stored in **sensorimotor circuits** (Graybiel & Grafton, 2015). Most research has focused on two structures.

**The cerebellum** is thought to participate in storing memories of **learned sensorimotor skills** through its various neuroplastic mechanisms (Gao, van Beugen, & De Zeeuw, 2012). Its role has been studied most intensively in **Pavlovian conditioning of the eyeblink response in rabbits** (Freeman, 2015). A **tone** (conditional stimulus) is sounded just before a **puff of air** (unconditional stimulus) is delivered to the eye; after several trials the tone alone elicits a blink. Converging evidence from **stimulation, recording and lesion** studies suggests that the effects of this conditioning are **stored as changes in the way cerebellar neurons respond to the tone** (De Zeeuw & Ten Brinke, 2015; Freeman, 2015). Note the link back to H.M.: this is the same paradigm he learned without awareness, which is consistent with its storage outside the medial temporal lobe.

**The striatum** is thought to store memories for **consistent relationships between stimuli and responses**, the kind of memories that **develop incrementally over many trials** (Graybiel & Grafton, 2015). This striatum-based learning is sometimes called **habit formation** (O'Tousa & Grahame, 2014).

### Beyond motor memory: the weather-forecasting task

Few would dispute that the cerebellum and striatum serve sensorimotor memory, but there is growing evidence that they also serve forms of memory with **no obvious motor component**.

> **Key study, Knowlton, Mangels, & Squire (1996).** Participants played a computer **"weather forecasting" game**: predict rain or shine by pressing one of two keys, on the basis of **stimulus cards** shown on screen. Each card had a **different probability** of leading to sunshine, which participants had to learn from feedback, a **probabilistic discrimination** problem. **Parkinson's patients with striatal damage did not improve over 50 trials**, although they had **normal explicit memory** for the training episodes. **Amnesic patients** with medial temporal lobe or medial diencephalic damage **improved markedly** but had **no explicit memory** of the training.

This is a **double dissociation**, the strongest kind of neuropsychological evidence: striatal damage impairs the incremental habit-like learning and spares explicit memory of the episode; medial temporal or diencephalic damage does the reverse. The two kinds of memory depend on separate systems.

> **Trap.** In the weather-prediction task, it is the **Parkinson's (striatal)** patients who **fail to learn** while **remembering the sessions**, and the **amnesics** who **learn** while **not remembering the sessions**. Reversing the two groups is the standard distractor.

## 11.7 Cellular Mechanisms of Learning and Memory

The level of analysis now changes, from **which structures** are involved to the **neuroplastic mechanisms within them**. Most modern thinking here began with **Hebb (1949)**, who argued so convincingly that **enduring changes in the efficiency of synaptic transmission** are the basis of long-term memory that the search for the neural bases of learning and memory has focused **almost exclusively on the synapse**.

### Long-term potentiation: the discovery

Because Hebb's hypothesis was so influential, there was great excitement when an effect of exactly that kind was found. In **1973, Bliss and Lømo** showed that **high-frequency electrical stimulation of presynaptic neurons** produces a lasting **facilitation of synaptic transmission**. This is **long-term potentiation (LTP)**.

LTP has been shown in many species and many brain areas, but it has been studied most often in the **rodent hippocampus**, at three synapses in particular:

1. the **dentate granule cell** synapse (input from the **perforant path**),
2. the **CA3 pyramidal cell** synapse, and
3. the **CA1 pyramidal cell** synapse.

### The classic LTP experiment in the dentate gyrus

The standard demonstration, recorded in the **granule cell layer of the rat hippocampal dentate gyrus**, has three steps:

1. **Baseline.** A single **low-intensity** pulse is delivered to the **perforant path** (the major input to the dentate gyrus), and the response is recorded through an **extracellular multiple-unit electrode** in the granule cell layer.
2. **Induction.** **High-frequency stimulation lasting 10 seconds** (several trains) is delivered to the perforant path.
3. **Test.** Responses to single low-intensity pulses are measured again after delays, **1 day** and **1 week** later.

Transmission at the granule cell synapses was **still potentiated 1 week later**. One measure of LTP is the increased amplitude of the **population spike**, the spike produced by the near-simultaneous firing of many granule cells; after induction, the same test pulse makes **more** granule cells fire.

### Why LTP became the favoured model of memory

LTP is among the most widely studied phenomena in neuroscience, and the reason goes back to Hebb: the synaptic changes he proposed as the basis of long-term memory seemed to be **the same kind of changes that underlie LTP** (Bliss et al., 2018; Nicoll, 2017). LTP has the **two key properties** Hebb proposed:

1. **It lasts a long time**: several **months to a year** after multiple high-frequency stimulations (Abraham, 2006).
2. **Many forms require co-occurrence** of activity in the **presynaptic and postsynaptic** neurons: for example, presynaptic neurotransmitter release co-occurring with postsynaptic depolarisation, or release of a **retrograde transmitter** from the postsynaptic neuron co-occurring with presynaptic activity (Monday & Castillo, 2017; Monday, Younts, & Castillo, 2018; Nicoll, 2017).

Co-occurrence is now recognised as the **critical factor** in LTP, and the assumption that co-occurrence is a physiological necessity for learning and memory is called **Hebb's postulate for learning**.

Pinel lists **seven observations** supporting a link between LTP and learning and memory:

1. LTP can be elicited by **low levels of stimulation that mimic normal neural activity**.
2. LTP effects are **most prominent in structures implicated in learning and memory**, such as the hippocampus.
3. **Learning can produce LTP-like changes** in the hippocampus.
4. Many **drugs that influence learning and memory have parallel effects on LTP**.
5. **Disrupting LTP impairs** memory on many behavioural tasks, whereas **enhancing LTP improves** it (Ricciarelli & Fedele, 2018).
6. Behavioural changes that appear to be **memories can be induced in mice via LTP** (Nabavi et al., 2014).
7. LTP occurs at specific synapses shown to participate in learning and memory in **simple invertebrate nervous systems**.

Pinel's caveat is essential: much of this evidence is **indirect**, and LTP induced by electrical stimulation in the laboratory is **at best a caricature** of the subtle cellular events underlying learning and memory.

Researchers treat LTP as a **three-part process** that maps onto memory:

| Phase of LTP | What it is | Memory analogue |
|---|---|---|
| **Induction** | How high-frequency stimulation induces LTP | **Learning** |
| **Maintenance** | The changes that keep LTP in place | **Memory (storage)** |
| **Expression** | The changes that allow LTP to show up at test | **Recall** |

### Induction of LTP: the NMDA receptor

The **NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptor** is prominent at the synapses where LTP is usually studied. It is a receptor for **glutamate**, the brain's main **excitatory** neurotransmitter. The key fact is that an NMDA receptor does **not respond maximally unless two events occur simultaneously**:

1. **Glutamate must bind** to it, and
2. the **postsynaptic neuron must already be partially depolarised**.

The dual requirement arises because the **calcium channels** associated with NMDA receptors allow a **large influx of calcium ions** only if the postsynaptic neuron is already depolarised when glutamate binds. It is that **calcium influx** that triggers the cascade of postsynaptic events that **induces LTP**.

| Situation | Calcium entry | LTP? |
|---|---|---|
| Glutamate binds, postsynaptic neuron **not** depolarised | Only a few calcium ions | **No** |
| Glutamate binds, postsynaptic neuron **already** depolarised | Many calcium ions | **Yes** |

This requirement is hidden in the usual experiment. High-intensity, high-frequency stimulation always depolarises the postsynaptic neuron through massive **temporal and spatial summation**, so the second condition is met automatically. But with more natural, **low-intensity** stimulation, LTP is **not** induced unless the postsynaptic neuron is **already partially depolarised** (Bliss et al., 2018; Lisman, 2017).

### Why co-occurrence lets LTP learn associations

Pinel calls this requirement an **extremely important characteristic** of conventional LTP, because it lets neural networks learn **associations**. Walk through it:

- If one glutamatergic neuron fires and releases glutamate **without** co-occurring depolarisation of the postsynaptic neuron, there is **no LTP**.
- If the postsynaptic neuron is **depolarised by input from other neurons** at the moment the presynaptic neuron fires, glutamate binding opens the calcium channels wide, calcium flows in, and transmission at that synapse is **potentiated**.

So under natural conditions LTP **"records" that there has been simultaneous activity in at least two converging inputs** to the postsynaptic neuron, which is exactly what the simultaneous presentation of a **conditional stimulus and an unconditional stimulus** would produce. The NMDA receptor is a molecular coincidence detector, and coincidence is what associative learning is made of.

> **Trap.** The NMDA receptor needs **both** glutamate binding **and** prior postsynaptic depolarisation; the ion whose influx triggers LTP is **calcium**. Do not answer sodium, and do not say glutamate alone is sufficient. The requirement is invisible with the usual high-intensity stimulation because that stimulation depolarises the cell anyway.

### Other forms of LTP

NMDA-receptor-mediated LTP, which is **largely postsynaptic**, is **not the only type**. There are **postsynaptic forms that do not rely on the NMDA receptor** (Petrovic et al., 2017), **presynaptic forms** (Bouvier et al., 2018; Monday & Castillo, 2017; Monday, Younts, & Castillo, 2018), and **LTP that relies on glial-cell activation** (Kronschläger et al., 2016).

### Maintenance and expression of LTP: storage and recall

The search for the mechanisms of maintenance and expression began by asking whether they are **presynaptic or postsynaptic**. The answer is **both**, which showed that they are complex; after more than **four decades** of research there are still no satisfactory answers. The quest has nevertheless produced **four important discoveries**:

1. **Dendritic spines give LTP its synapse specificity.** LTP occurs only at **specific synapses** on a postsynaptic neuron, so something must stop events at one set of synapses affecting others on the same neuron. That something is the **dendritic spine**: calcium ions that enter a spine **do not readily pass out of it**, so they act **locally** (Abraham, Jones, & Glanzman, 2019; Attardo, Fitzgerald, & Schnitzer, 2015; Colgan & Yasuda, 2014).
2. **Maintenance involves structural changes that depend on protein synthesis** (Abraham, Jones, & Glanzman, 2019; Bliss et al., 2018; Sweatt, 2016). This was exciting because neuronal structure had been assumed to be **static**. Described changes include increases in the **number and size of synapses**, increases in the **number and size of dendritic spines**, changes in **presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes**, and changes in **dendritic branching**, and they occur **far more rapidly and frequently** than once assumed.
3. **Activity-dependent transcription factors.** The search for how activity could change structure led to the discovery of numerous **transcription factors**, intracellular proteins that **bind to DNA and influence the operation of particular genes**, activated by neural activity (Ryan et al., 2015; Sweatt, 2016).
4. **Epigenetic mechanisms.** **Francis Crick** (co-discoverer of the DNA molecule) proposed in **1984** that **DNA methylation**, an epigenetic mechanism, might play an important role in memory storage. It is now abundantly clear that **many epigenetic mechanisms** play a role in maintaining both LTP and memory (Asok et al., 2019; Kim & Kaang, 2017; Leighton et al., 2017; Sweatt, 2016).

### Variability of LTP: long-term depression and metaplasticity

Pinel and Barnes write that when they first learned about LTP they were both excited: it seemed a good model of memory, and its simplicity suggested its mechanisms would soon be found. A generation of neuroscientists shared that view, yet researchers seem **further from ultimate answers** than they naively thought about a quarter of a century ago. The discoveries have revealed that LTP is **far more complex** than first thought:

- **NMDA-receptor-mediated LTP in the hippocampus**, the most studied form, involves a complex array of changes that are hard to sort out.
- LTP occurs in **many other parts of the CNS**, where it tends to be mediated by **different mechanisms** (Grau, 2014; Gruart et al., 2015).
- **Long-term depression (LTD)** is the **"flip side" of LTP**: a lasting decrease in synaptic transmission that occurs in response to **prolonged low-frequency stimulation** of presynaptic neurons (Atwood, Lovinger, & Mathur, 2014; Bliss et al., 2018; Connor & Wang, 2015).
- **Metaplasticity** refers to the fact that LTP and/or LTD induction **can be modulated by prior synaptic activity** (Abraham, Jones, & Glanzman, 2019; Hulme, Jones, & Abraham, 2013; Müller-Dahlhaus & Ziemann, 2015). It is plasticity of plasticity.

A full understanding of LTP's role in memory will presumably require understanding LTD and metaplasticity too. Pinel's closing judgement is balanced: the dream of finding *the* neural basis of memory has not been fulfilled, but the study of LTP has produced many important discoveries about neural function and plasticity, and **by that criterion it has been a great success**.

> **Trap.** **High**-frequency stimulation produces **LTP**; **prolonged low**-frequency stimulation produces **LTD**. Metaplasticity is **not** a third type of change in synaptic strength; it is the modulation of LTP/LTD **induction** by **prior** activity.

### Nonsynaptic mechanisms of learning and memory

LTP is synaptic, but there are neuroplastic changes **outside the synapse** that may also matter. Pinel names two: **epigenetic mechanisms in the cell nucleus** (Abraham, Jones, & Glanzman, 2019), and **changes to the structure of axons**, specifically their myelination.

**Oligodendrocytes**, the CNS glial cells that myelinate axons, **modulate their myelination in response to experience** (Chang, Redmond, & Chan, 2016). Contrary to popular belief, the myelin sheath is **not static**: myelination is **ongoing throughout the lifespan**, and changes in it alter the **speed of transmission** within and between neurons. Myelination changes through at least **five mechanisms** (Chang, Redmond, & Chan, 2016; Forbes & Gallo, 2017):

1. **Unmyelinated axons can be myelinated** by oligodendrocytes.
2. Previously myelinated axons can have their **myelin removed**.
3. Myelin can be **replaced** when old sheaths degrade.
4. Sheaths can be **thinned or thickened**.
5. Sheaths can be **lengthened or shortened**.

Myelination changes follow learning. When rats learn a spatial task such as the **Morris water maze**, myelination **increases**. In humans, both **motor-skill learning** and **working-memory training** increase myelination in the brain areas associated with that kind of learning (Sampaio-Baptista & Johansen-Berg, 2017). In short, the plasticity of myelin seems to play an important role in learning and memory (Kaller et al., 2017; Monje, 2018; Mount & Monje, 2017).

## 11.8 Conclusion: Biopsychology of Memory and You

Because the chapter has dwelt on brain damage, you might think the biopsychology of memory has little to say to people with healthy brains. The final module disputes this with two lines of research and one case.

### Infantile amnesia

We all experience **infantile amnesia**: we remember virtually nothing of the events of our infancy (Callaghan, Li, & Richardson, 2014; Sneed, 2014; Travaglia et al., 2016). **Newcombe and colleagues (2000)** asked whether normal children who fail to **explicitly** recall or recognise things from early childhood nevertheless show preserved **implicit** memories for them. Two experiments say **yes**.

> **Key study, Newcombe & Fox (1994).** Children were shown photographs of preschool-aged children, some of whom had been their **preschool classmates**. They explicitly recognised only a few. But **whether or not they explicitly remembered a former classmate**, they consistently showed a **large skin conductance response** to photographs of former classmates and **not** to control photographs.

> **Key study, Drummey & Newcombe (1995).** Using a version of the **incomplete-pictures test**, they showed drawings to **3-year-olds, 5-year-olds and adults**. **Three months later**, implicit memory was assessed by asking each participant to identify the drawings, plus control drawings, as quickly as possible as they sharpened from **badly out of focus**. Explicit memory was then assessed by asking which drawings they remembered seeing. The **5-year-olds and adults had better explicit memory than the 3-year-olds**, but **all three groups showed substantial implicit memory**, identifying previously seen drawings sooner even without conscious recollection.

The parallel with H.M. is deliberate. In infantile amnesia, as in medial temporal lobe amnesia, **explicit** memory is absent while **implicit** memory is preserved; the same incomplete-pictures logic reveals it.

### Smart drugs: do they work?

**Nootropics**, or **smart drugs**, are substances (drugs, supplements, herbal extracts and so on) thought to **improve memory**. Pinel's list includes **ginkgo biloba** and **ginseng** extracts, **multivitamins**, **glucose**, **cholinergic agonists**, **Piracetam**, **antioxidants**, **phospholipids**, and **stimulants** such as **amphetamine** and **methylphenidate**. Vendors claim scientific proof that they improve memory in healthy children and adults and block the effects of ageing.

Independent reviews (Hussain & Mehta, 2011; Partridge et al., 2012) have reached three conclusions:

1. Although nootropics are often **marketed to healthy adults**, most research has been done on **non-humans** or on **humans with memory difficulties**, such as the elderly (Onaolapo, Obelawo, & Onaolapo, 2019; Uddin et al., 2019).
2. The relevant human research tends to be of **low quality**, with **few participants and poor controls**.
3. For each nootropic there are typically **a few positive findings**, on which vendors focus, but these are often **hard to replicate** or represent **very small effect sizes** (Farah, 2015).

The bottom line: **no purported nootropic has been convincingly shown to have memory-enhancing effects**. There may be enough positive evidence to justify further investigation of some (Hinnebusch, 2015), but nowhere near enough to justify the advertised claims. Pinel's closing question, why the gulf between evidence and claims is so large, is his third "thinking creatively" point.

### The case of R.M., the biopsychologist who remembered H.M.

The chapter begins with H.M. and ends with **R.M.**, a **biopsychologist**. R.M. **fell on his head while skiing**. On regaining consciousness he had both **retrograde and anterograde amnesia**. For **several hours** he could recall few events of his previous life: not whether he was married, where he lived, or where he worked. He had lost most of his **episodic** memory.

Many things that happened in the hours after the accident were **forgotten as soon as his attention was diverted**. In the car to the hospital he chatted with the person next to him, a friend of a friend with whom he had skied all day; but each time his attention was drawn elsewhere, for example by the mountain scenery, he **completely forgot this person and reintroduced himself**. This was a classic case of **posttraumatic amnesia**.

The irony is the point of the case. During those hours, when R.M. could recall few events of his own life, his thoughts kept drifting to one **semantic memory**: a person he remembered learning about somewhere in his muddled past, **H.M.**, his "fellow prisoner of the present", and he wondered whether the same fate awaited him. R.M. **recovered fully**. Unlike H.M., he received a reprieve, and he was left with greater empathy for those who, like H.M., served a life sentence.

### Themes revisited

All four of the book's major themes appear in this chapter:

- **Neuroplasticity** runs throughout, because memory *is* the change in neural function that stores experience.
- **Clinical implications** played a large role, but the traffic has been **one-way**: we have learned much about memory from amnesic patients, but not yet enough to **treat** their memory problems.
- **Animal models** were essential, because questions of causation must be addressed with them; the study of medial temporal lobe amnesia shows the **comparative approach** at its best.
- **Thinking creatively** appeared at three points: (1) why it was so hard to develop an animal model of medial temporal lobe amnesia; (2) why cerebral ischaemia cases do not provide strong evidence for hippocampal involvement in object recognition; and (3) the gulf between the evidence for smart drugs and the claims.

Both **emerging themes** appear too: **epigenetics** (in both synaptic and nonsynaptic mechanisms) and **consciousness** (the explicit/implicit distinction, which is fundamental to current memory research).

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Memory is several systems, not one.** H.M.'s five tests split long-term memory into explicit and implicit; K.C. and Vargha-Khadem's patients split explicit into semantic and episodic; the weather-prediction task split medial temporal from striatal learning.
2. **The hippocampus was demoted, not dismissed.** From "store for all new memories" (1950s) to "explicit episodic memory", to "minor role in object recognition compared with medial temporal cortex", to "one of several structures", with a distinctive role in spatial and conceptual (cognitive-map) coding.
3. **The most visible lesion is not always the responsible lesion.** Monkey aspiration lesions, R.B.'s CA1 damage and diffuse ischaemic damage all teach the same caution.
4. **Consolidation is long and repeated.** Hebb's seconds became Squire's years; standard consolidation theory gave way to repeated re-encoding (Moscovitch) and reconsolidation (Nader et al., 2000).
5. **Memories live where they were processed.** Lashley found no single store; retrieval reactivates the original sensory circuits (inferotemporal cortex), motor learning lives in motor circuits (cerebellum, striatum), and the amygdala and prefrontal cortex modulate and organise.
6. **At the cellular level, coincidence is the key.** Hebb's postulate, the NMDA receptor's dual requirement and calcium influx explain how a synapse can record an association.

## Worked example

*A 24-year-old motorcyclist is seen three weeks after a crash. He was unconscious for about 20 minutes and confused for two days; his family says he chatted sensibly during their hospital visits, but he now remembers none of them. He cannot remember the ride that day or the hour before it. He still knows his address, job and friends, and recalls one vivid moment in the ambulance. Over two sessions of mirror tracing his errors fall, although at the second session he insists he has never seen the apparatus. His mother has bought him a ginkgo "memory booster" and asks if it will help.*

**Diagnosis.** A non-penetrating blow, coma, then confusion: **closed-head TBI with posttraumatic amnesia**, the commonest cause of amnesia.

**Why the family is puzzled.** During confusion **short-term memory is normal**, so he seemed lucid, but nothing was consolidated; hence **anterograde amnesia for the period of confusion**.

**Do the durations fit?** Confusion (two days) > coma (20 minutes) > retrograde amnesia (about an hour): exactly the expected order.

**The ambulance memory** is an **island of memory**.

**The mirror tracing** shows preserved **implicit** learning with absent **explicit** recollection, the dissociation first shown in H.M. It suggests skill-based rehabilitation can work even when he does not remember the practice.

**Why old memories survived.** His retrograde loss is short and **graded**; older memories, repeatedly recalled and linked (Dudai and colleagues; Moscovitch), resist disruption.

**The ginkgo question.** Reviews (Hussain & Mehta, 2011; Partridge et al., 2012) find no convincingly memory-enhancing nootropic: low-quality studies, poorly replicated or tiny effects, mostly in non-humans or the elderly.

**What to watch.** If anterograde problems persisted beyond the confusion, the H.M./R.B. pattern would raise concern about medial temporal (or, with a penetrating wound such as N.A.'s, medial diencephalic) damage.

## MCQ traps

1. **Lobectomy vs lobotomy.** H.M. had a bilateral medial temporal **lobectomy** (tissue removed), at **27**, in **1953**.
2. **H.M.'s digit span was normal (6)**; his deficit was long-term. **Digit-span + 1** (8 digits not reached in 25 trials; normal about 15) was impaired.
3. **H.M.'s retrograde amnesia was mild**, about **2 years**; remote memory was reasonably normal.
4. **Block-tapping (Milner, 1971)** showed **global** amnesia, meaning **all sensory modalities**, not "total".
5. **Preserved tests:** mirror drawing (Milner, 1965), incomplete pictures (Milner et al., 1968), eyeblink conditioning (Woodruff-Pak, 1993; slower, retained 2 years). All **without awareness**.
6. **Repetition priming tests** measure **implicit** memory; amnesics often perform as well as controls.
7. **Why explicit memory evolved:** **flexibility** (Reber, Knowlton, & Squire, 1996; Buckley & Gaffan, 1998).
8. **Semantic vs episodic.** K.C. knew **facts** about his life but recalled no **episode**, and could not **imagine the future**.
9. **Vargha-Khadem et al. (1997):** early medial temporal damage, mainstream schooling achieved, **episodic memory did not improve**.
10. **R.B.:** cardiac bypass at 52; damage largely confined to **CA1 pyramidal cells**.
11. **Transient global amnesia:** lasts **4 to 6 hours**; **CA1** abnormalities appear hours later and clear in about **10 days**, suggesting ischaemia.
12. **Korsakoff's** = **thiamine deficiency**; **medial diencephalon**, especially **mediodorsal thalamic nuclei**; retrograde amnesia **grows** and may reach childhood.
13. **N.A.:** fencing foil through the **cribriform plate**; **mediodorsal nuclei and mammillary bodies**; retrograde amnesia shrank from **2 years to 2 weeks**.
14. **Alzheimer's** acetylcholine loss is from the **basal forebrain** and **contributes** but is **not the only factor**.
15. **Predementia Alzheimer's** impairs **short-term** and **verbal/perceptual implicit** memory but spares **sensorimotor implicit** learning.
16. **TBI durations:** confusion/anterograde > coma > retrograde.
17. **Squire, Slater, & Chace (1975):** five ECS; amnesia for one-season TV shows of the previous **3 years**, not earlier. Consolidation takes years, contradicting Hebb's brief estimate.
18. **Standard consolidation theory = dual-trace theory** (Scoville & Milner, 1957); now **few adherents**.
19. **Reconsolidation (Nader, Schafe, & LeDoux, 2000):** **anisomycin** into the **amygdala** after **recall**.
20. **Early animal models failed** because they used **implicit** tests and **hippocampus-only** lesions.
21. **Delayed nonmatching-to-sample:** choose the **unfamiliar** object (Gaffan, 1974; Mishkin & Delacour, 1975).
22. **Object recognition:** medial temporal **cortex** lesions severe; hippocampus modest; amygdala **none**.
23. **Monkey hippocampal aspiration** damages **medial temporal cortex**; in rats, only some **parietal neocortex**.
24. **Radial arm maze:** unbaited arm = **reference** error; revisited arm = **working** error; hippocampal lesions impair both.
25. **Grid cells** (entorhinal, many hexagonal fields) vs **place cells** (hippocampal, one field); place cells **emerge first** in development.
26. **Concept cells** were recorded in **human epilepsy patients**; they respond to the **concept** (photo, printed name, spoken name).
27. **Engram cells:** **tagging** then **manipulate**; in Alzheimer's **mouse models** the deficit appeared to be **retrieval**, not encoding.
28. **Amygdala** strengthens emotional memories but shows **little evidence of storing** them; Bechara et al. (1995): no conditioned autonomic response, explicit memory intact.
29. **Weather-prediction task:** **Parkinson's** patients fail to learn but remember sessions; **amnesics** learn but do not remember.
30. **NMDA receptor:** needs **glutamate plus prior postsynaptic depolarisation**; **calcium** influx induces LTP.
31. **LTP** = high-frequency stimulation (Bliss & Lømo, **1973**); **LTD** = prolonged **low**-frequency stimulation; **metaplasticity** = modulation by prior activity.
32. **Crick (1984)** proposed **DNA methylation** for memory storage.
