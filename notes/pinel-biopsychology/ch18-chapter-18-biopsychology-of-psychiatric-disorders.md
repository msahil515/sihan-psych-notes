# Chapter 18: Biopsychology of Psychiatric Disorders

**What this chapter is really about.** The chapter's subtitle is "The Brain Unhinged". It covers five disorders, **schizophrenia**, the **depressive disorders**, **bipolar disorder**, the **anxiety disorders** and **Tourette's disorder**, and then explains how new psychiatric drugs are tested. For each disorder it asks the same questions: what does it look like, which drugs help and how were they found, what theory did those drugs suggest, what is known about its genetics and epigenetics, and how do patients' brains differ?

Asking the same questions each time shows a pattern. Nearly every major psychiatric drug was found **by accident**. Chlorpromazine was an antihistamine, iproniazid a tuberculosis drug, imipramine a failed antipsychotic, and lithium came out of experiments on guinea-pig urine. The theories came afterwards. Researchers worked out what the lucky drug did at the synapse and assumed the disorder was the opposite. That is how the **dopamine theory of schizophrenia** and the **monoamine theory of depression** were built. Both then ran into the same problem: the drug acts on the synapse within **hours**, but patients improve only after **weeks**. The second pattern is that DSM-5 diagnoses group together patients whose symptoms, brain changes and drug responses differ widely. Each diagnosis is probably several disorders under one name, and Tourette's disorder is the only one the authors exempt from this.

**How to read this chapter.** The opening sets up the diagnostic problem. 18.1 (schizophrenia) is the most examined module; learn the dopamine theory as a **sequence of revisions**, each forced by an experiment. 18.2 (depression) gives five antidepressant classes, two brain-stimulation treatments and two theories. 18.3 (bipolar) covers Cade's discovery of lithium step by step. 18.4 (anxiety) gives three drug types and three animal models. 18.5 (Tourette's) is short but heavily examined. 18.6 (clinical trials) gives three phases and six controversies. The case of S.B. runs through 18.2, 18.3 and 18.6, and its ending is the end of the book.

## Opening: The Brain Unhinged

**Psychiatric disorders** are disorders of psychological function severe enough to need treatment. Before treatment can start, the clinician has to make two decisions, and both are harder than they look.

The **first** is whether anything is wrong at all. Is the patient's functioning **pathological**, or just an extreme of normal variation? Pinel's example: is a patient with a poor memory ill, or a healthy person who happens to have a poor memory?

The **second** is which disorder it is. We cannot yet identify the brain pathology behind specific disorders, so diagnosis **rests almost entirely on the symptom profile**, currently guided by the **DSM-5** (the American Psychiatric Association's *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual*). This creates two problems:

1. **Patients with the same disorder often show different symptoms.**
2. **Patients with different disorders often show many of the same symptoms.**

So experts often disagree on particular cases, and the DSM's guidelines change with each edition (Blashfield et al., 2014). One stated aim of the chapter is to show **why diagnosis needs periodic revision**, and every module ends by returning to it.

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel had **four** disorders: schizophrenia, "affective disorders: depression and mania", anxiety disorders and "Tourette syndrome". The 11th edition has **five**. Depression and bipolar disorder are separate modules, following the DSM-5 split into "depressive disorders" and "bipolar and related disorders". The name is now **Tourette's disorder**, and there is a new clinical-trials module. Older terms that still appear in Indian papers mean the same things as the new ones: "affective disorder", "unipolar depression", "neuroleptics" and "antischizophrenic drugs".

## 18.1 Schizophrenia

**Schizophrenia** means "the splitting of psychic functions". The term was coined early in the 20th century to describe what was then thought to be the main symptom: a **breakdown of integration among emotion, thought and action**. It is a **severe** disorder affecting **about 1 percent** of people **in all races and cultural groups**. It typically begins in **adolescence or early adulthood** (Sikela & Quick, 2018).

> **Trap.** The split is *between* emotion, thought and action. It is **not a split personality**, which is dissociative identity disorder and a favourite distractor. Pinel does not say who coined the term. Exam keys expect **Eugen Bleuler** (1911), who replaced Kraepelin's *dementia praecox*.

### Schizophrenia: The Case of Lena

Lena's **mother was hospitalised with schizophrenia when Lena was 2** (Meyer & Salmon, 1988). As a child Lena had periods of hyperactivity, and as an adolescent she was seen as odd. She got good grades but had few friends. Shortly after her marriage her husband noticed she was becoming withdrawn. She would sit for hours barely moving, often talking at length with people who were not there.

One day he found her on the floor in an odd posture, staring and completely unresponsive. When he tried to move her she showed **waxy flexibility**: like a mannequin, she did not resist being moved and held each new position until moved again. She was diagnosed with **schizophrenia with catatonia**, meaning schizophrenia with long periods of immobility and waxy flexibility. In hospital she showed **echolalia**, the vocal repetition of some or all of what has just been heard:

> Doctor: How are you feeling today?
>
> Lena: I am feeling today, feeling the feelings today.
>
> Doctor: Are you still hearing the voices?
>
> Lena: Am I still hearing the voices, voices?

The case contains most of what the module comes back to. There is a **family history**, which points to genes. There is **oddness long before diagnosis**, which suggests a developmental disorder. The onset is in **early adulthood**. And it shows both **positive** features (voices) and **negative** ones (withdrawal, catatonia).

### What Is Schizophrenia?

The central difficulty is **defining** schizophrenia (Bhati, 2013). Its symptoms are varied, overlap heavily with other disorders, and change as the disorder progresses. Some neurological conditions, such as complex seizures, can mimic it. Because of the overlap, the **DSM-5 prefers the label schizophrenia spectrum disorders**.

No symptom appears in every case. Clinicians separate two kinds (Smigielski et al., 2020). **Positive symptoms** seem to be an **excess** of typical function. **Negative symptoms** seem to be a **reduction or loss** of typical function.

| Positive symptoms | Book's examples |
|---|---|
| **Delusions** | Of being controlled ("Martians are making me steal"), of persecution ("My mother is poisoning me"), of grandeur ("Steph Curry admires my jump shot") |
| **Hallucinations** | Imaginary voices making critical comments or giving orders |
| **Inappropriate affect** | Inappropriate emotional response to positive or negative events |
| **Disorganised speech or thought** | Illogical thinking, peculiar associations, belief in supernatural forces |
| **Odd behaviour** | Talking in rhymes, difficulty with everyday tasks |

| Negative symptoms | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Affective flattening** | Diminished emotional expression |
| **Avolition** | Reduction or absence of motivation |
| **Catatonia** | Remaining motionless, often in awkward positions, for long periods |

**The book's diagnostic rule:** frequent recurrence of **any two** of these symptoms **for 1 month** is enough, **provided one of them is delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech**. Negative symptoms alone never qualify. They are too unspecific, because they also occur in depression, after brain injury and as drug side effects.

> **Trap.** **(1)** Pinel lists **catatonia as a negative symptom**. The DSM-5 treats "grossly disorganised or catatonic behaviour" as a separate core symptom. **(2)** The 1-month figure is the **active-phase** rule only. The full DSM-5 criteria also need continuous signs for **6 months**. **(3)** DSM-IV keys allowed a **single** symptom if it was a bizarre delusion or voices commenting; the DSM-5 dropped that exception. The DSM-5 also **abolished the subtypes** (paranoid, disorganised, catatonic and so on), which is why Lena has schizophrenia "*with catatonia*" and not "catatonic schizophrenia".

### Discovery of the First Antipsychotic Drugs

The first breakthrough in the biochemistry of schizophrenia was the **accidental** discovery in the **early 1950s** of the first **antipsychotic drug** (a drug meant to treat certain symptoms of **schizophrenia and bipolar disorder**), **chlorpromazine**.

**Chlorpromazine** was developed by a **French drug company as an antihistamine**. In **1950** a **French surgeon** (Henri Laborit, though the book does not name him) noticed that chlorpromazine, given before surgery to reduce swelling, calmed some patients. He suggested it might calm difficult patients with **psychosis** (a loss of touch with reality). The research that followed showed that it relieves schizophrenic symptoms. It works **in both directions**: **agitated** patients were **calmed** and **emotionally blunted** patients were **activated**. A sedative would only quieten people. A drug that moves both groups towards normal is acting on the disorder. It does **not cure** schizophrenia, but it often reduces symptoms enough for **institutionalised patients to be discharged**.

**Reserpine.** Soon afterwards an **American psychiatrist** (Nathan Kline, also unnamed in the book) followed up reports that the **snakeroot plant had long been used in India** to treat mental illness. He gave **reserpine**, its active ingredient, to patients with schizophrenia and confirmed its antipsychotic action. Reserpine is **no longer used** because at therapeutic doses it causes a **dangerous fall in blood pressure**.

> **Trap.** The plant is Indian snakeroot, *Rauwolfia serpentina* (*sarpagandha*). Reserpine was dropped for **hypotension**, not because it did not work. Chlorpromazine came from **France** (an antihistamine noticed by a surgeon). Reserpine came from a **US** psychiatrist drawing on Indian medicine.

**Two shared features.** The two drugs have **dissimilar structures**, yet:

1. both take **2 or 3 weeks** to produce their antipsychotic effect;
2. the onset of that effect usually comes with **Parkinson-like motor effects** (muscular rigidity, a general decrease in voluntary movement).

Two unrelated molecules with the same delay and the same unusual side effect suggested **one shared mechanism, related to Parkinson's disease**.

### The Dopamine Theory of Schizophrenia

In **1960** it was reported that the **striatums** (caudate plus putamen) of people with Parkinson's disease were **depleted of dopamine** (Goetz, 2011). Older editions credit **Ehringer and Hornykiewicz (1960)**. Three facts now fitted together. Parkinson's involves too little striatal dopamine. Antipsychotics produce Parkinson-like symptoms. Antipsychotics relieve schizophrenia. So disrupted dopamine transmission might explain both the side effects and the therapeutic effect. This produced the **dopamine theory of schizophrenia**: schizophrenia is caused by **too much dopamine**, and antipsychotics work by **lowering dopamine levels** (McCutcheon, Abi-Dargham, & Howes, 2019).

Two established facts gave the theory **instant support**:

1. **Reserpine** was known to **deplete brain dopamine and other monoamines** by **breaking down the synaptic vesicles** that store them.
2. **Amphetamine and cocaine**, which can trigger **schizophrenia-like episodes in healthy users**, were known to **raise extracellular dopamine** and other monoamines.

The rest of the theory's history is a series of revisions, each forced by an experiment.

#### Carlsson and Lindqvist (1963): chlorpromazine as a receptor blocker

In **1963 Carlsson and Lindqvist** measured how chlorpromazine affects extracellular levels of dopamine and of its **metabolites** (substances created by the breakdown of another substance in cells). They expected chlorpromazine to deplete dopamine, as reserpine does. It did not.

> **Key study, Carlsson & Lindqvist (1963).** After chlorpromazine, extracellular **dopamine was unchanged** and extracellular **dopamine metabolites were increased**.

Compare what each possible mechanism predicts:

| If chlorpromazine... | Extracellular dopamine | Metabolites |
|---|---|---|
| depleted dopamine (like reserpine) | falls | falls |
| had no effect on dopamine | unchanged | unchanged |
| **blocked receptors, with feedback** | **about the same** | **rises** |

Only the last row matches. They concluded that both drugs **antagonise** dopamine transmission but **in different ways**. Reserpine **depletes** dopamine. Chlorpromazine **binds to dopamine receptors**. Chlorpromazine is a **receptor blocker**: it binds to the receptors **without activating them**, and so stops dopamine from activating them. Many psychoactive drugs are now known to be receptor blockers, but **chlorpromazine was the first identified as one**.

They also explained the numbers. Inactivity at **postsynaptic** receptors sends a **feedback signal to the presynaptic neurons**, which **release more dopamine**. That dopamine is **broken down in the synapse**. The overall result is less dopaminergic activity, roughly unchanged dopamine levels, and raised metabolites (Figure 18.1). **First revision:** the key factor is not high dopamine **levels** but high **activity at dopamine receptors**.

> **Trap.** **Reserpine depletes** (presynaptic, vesicles). **Chlorpromazine blocks** (postsynaptic receptors). "Chlorpromazine lowers brain dopamine" is wrong. It is the very expectation Carlsson and Lindqvist disproved. With blockade, **metabolites rise** and dopamine stays about the same. (Carlsson later shared the 2000 Nobel Prize for his dopamine work.)

#### Snyder's binding studies: the D2 revision

In the **mid-1970s Snyder and his colleagues** (Creese, Burt, & Snyder, 1976) measured how strongly the available antipsychotics bind to dopamine receptors:

1. They took dopamine-receptor-rich membrane from **calf striatums** and added **radioactively labelled dopamine**.
2. They rinsed away unbound dopamine and measured the remaining radioactivity, which gave a measure of the **number of dopamine receptors**.
3. In other samples they measured how well each drug **blocked** binding of the labelled dopamine. A high-affinity drug should occupy the receptors and leave **fewer sites** for dopamine.

In general, **chlorpromazine and other effective antipsychotics had high affinity** and ineffective drugs low affinity. The major exception was **haloperidol**: one of the **most potent** antipsychotics of its day, but with **relatively low affinity** in this assay.

**The solution.** Dopamine binds to **more than one receptor subtype**, and **five** are now known (Beaulieu, Espinoza, & Gainetdinov, 2015). The **phenothiazines** (chlorpromazine and its chemical relatives) bind well to **both D1 and D2** receptors. The **butyrophenones** (haloperidol and its relatives) bind well to **D2 but not D1**. The labelled dopamine in the calf membrane was binding to both D1 and D2 sites. Haloperidol could displace it only from the D2 sites, so it *looked* weak, even though it acts powerfully at the receptor that matters.

**Second revision:** schizophrenia is caused by **hyperactivity specifically at D2 receptors**. **Snyder (1978)** then confirmed that the degree to which **typical antipsychotics** (the **first generation**) bind to **D2** receptors is **highly correlated with their effectiveness** (Figure 18.2). The butyrophenone **spiroperidol** had the **greatest D2 affinity and the most potent antipsychotic effect**.

> **Trap.** In Figure 18.2 **chlorpromazine sits near the bottom left**. That does not make it a poor drug. **Potency** means how *small a dose* is needed, not how much the drug can achieve. The finding is the **correlation across drugs**. Classes: chlorpromazine = **phenothiazine** = D1 + D2. Haloperidol and spiroperidol = **butyrophenones** = D2 only.

#### The limits of the D2 theory and the current version

The D2 version could **not explain two findings**:

1. **The time lag.** Typical antipsychotics block D2 receptors **within hours**, but their therapeutic effects take **several weeks**. If D2 overactivity were the whole cause, relief should be immediate. The delay suggests that blocking D2 **starts** a slower downstream change, and that change is what helps.
2. **Symptom selectivity.** Almost all antipsychotics work against **positive** symptoms **but not negative** ones (Aleman et al., 2018; Osoegawa et al., 2018).

Hence the **current version**: excessive D2 activity is **one factor** in schizophrenia **among many others** (Poels et al., 2014; Sibley & Shi, 2018).

| Version | Claim | Forced by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Too much **dopamine** | Parkinson's depletion (1960); reserpine; amphetamine and cocaine |
| 2 | Too much **activity at dopamine receptors** | Carlsson & Lindqvist (1963) |
| 3 | Too much activity at **D2** receptors | Haloperidol puzzle; Snyder (1978) |
| 4 | D2 is **one factor among many** | Hours-versus-weeks lag; failure on negative symptoms |

**Which pathway?** Of the two dopamine pathways in Chapter 15, the dopamine theory blames **excessive activity in the mesocorticolimbic pathway**, not the nigrostriatal pathway. Remember this as the classic claim, because the neural-bases section below challenges it.

### Schizophrenia: Beyond the Dopamine Theory

The dopamine theory is still influential, but two current lines of research are opening new perspectives: **atypical antipsychotics** and the effects of **psychedelic drugs**.

#### Atypical antipsychotics

**Atypical antipsychotics** (**second-generation antipsychotics**) are now often the **drugs of choice** for schizophrenia. They are defined by a property the D2 theory would not predict: they are **effective but do not bind strongly to D2 receptors**. **Clozapine**, the **first atypical approved for clinical use**, has affinity for **D1 and D4 receptors and several serotonin and histamine receptors**, but only **slight affinity for D2** (Humbert-Claude et al., 2012). Others include **aripiprazole, risperidone and quetiapine**. A drug that works with weak D2 binding shows that D2 blockade cannot be the only route to an antipsychotic effect.

> **Trap.** **Typical = first-generation** (chlorpromazine, haloperidol; strong D2 blockers; older term "neuroleptics"). **Atypical = second-generation** (clozapine first; weak D2, strong serotonin action). Standard pharmacology adds that clozapine's serious risk is **agranulocytosis**. "First atypical" = **clozapine**, not risperidone.

#### Renewed interest in hallucinogenic drugs

**Psychedelic drugs** are drugs whose **primary action is to alter perception, emotion and cognition**. Their study began in **1943** with the discovery of **lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD)** (Garcia-Romeu & Richards, 2018; Nichols, 2016). They include **classical hallucinogens** (**LSD, psilocybin, mescaline**) and **dissociative hallucinogens** (**ketamine, phencyclidine**).

Researchers followed two lines. One used drugs whose effects **resemble psychiatric symptoms** (illusions, hallucinations, paranoia, panic) to **model** disorders. The other used the feelings of **boundlessness, unity and bliss** that some users report to try to **treat** disorders. Both **stopped in the 1970s**, when governments troubled by LSD's link with social subcultures made human research extremely difficult (Belouin & Henningfield, 2018; Rucker, Iliff, & Nutt, 2018; but see Oram, 2016). Interest **revived in the 1990s**, helped by **brain imaging of drug effects** and better understanding of psychedelic mechanisms (Tylš, Páleníček, & Horáček, 2014). This research reached **three conclusions**:

1. Classical hallucinogens such as LSD **mimic the positive symptoms** (hallucinations, disorganised thought) by acting as **agonists of the serotonin type-2a (5-HT2A) receptor**.
2. **5-HT2A antagonists are effective antipsychotics**, for example the atypical **risperidone** (Girgis et al., 2018).
3. Dissociative hallucinogens such as ketamine **mimic the negative symptoms** by acting as **antagonists of glutamate receptors** (Laruelle, 2014).

These fill the two gaps in the D2 theory. They explain why weak-D2 atypicals work (5-HT2A blockade), and why negative symptoms resist D2 blockers (they may depend on reduced glutamate signalling). The reasoning is the same drug-mimicry logic that once linked amphetamine psychosis to dopamine.

> **Trap.** **LSD = 5-HT2A agonist → positive symptoms.** **Risperidone = 5-HT2A antagonist → antipsychotic.** **Ketamine/PCP = glutamate antagonists → negative symptoms.** Ketamine reappears in 18.2 as a fast **antidepressant**.

### Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms of Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia involves **multiple genetic and epigenetic mechanisms**. Many genes have been linked to it (Flint & Munafò, 2014; Reardon, 2014; Ripke et al., 2014), but **no single gene can cause it by itself**, although some are more strongly implicated (Dhindsa & Goldstein, 2016; Sekar et al., 2016; Sikela & Quick, 2018). The study of these genes' expression is at an early stage (Kotlar et al., 2015). It already links them to **brain development** (Birnbaum & Weinberger, 2017; Jaffe et al., 2018), **myelination** (Voineskos et al., 2012), **glutamatergic and GABAergic transmission** (Sacchetti et al., 2013) and **dopaminergic neuron physiology** (Dong et al., 2018; but see Gürel et al., 2020). Some susceptibility genes are **also linked to other psychiatric and neurological disorders** (Rizzardi et al., 2019).

**Early experience.** Implicated early factors include **birth complications, maternal stress, prenatal infections, socioeconomic factors, urban birth or residence, and childhood adversity** (Owen, Sawa, & Mortensen, 2016). These are thought to **alter the course of neurodevelopment** and so produce schizophrenia **in genetically susceptible people**, presumably through **epigenetic mechanisms** (Birnbaum & Weinberger, 2017; Sharp & Akbarian, 2016). This is the **neurodevelopmental theory of schizophrenia**. Two lines of evidence support it:

1. Schizophrenia and **autism spectrum disorder share many causal factors**, genetic and environmental (Millan et al., 2016).
2. **Two famines.** Fetuses whose mothers lived through the **Nazi-induced Dutch famine of 1944 to 1945** or the **Chinese famine of 1959 to 1961** were **more likely to develop schizophrenia as adults** (Li et al., 2015; Schmitt et al., 2014).

> **Key study, the famine cohorts.** A famine is a natural experiment. It strikes a whole population at a known time, and nobody chooses who is exposed. Higher adult schizophrenia rates among people who were **fetuses** during the famine point to a **prenatal insult** whose effect appears about two decades later, as the neurodevelopmental theory predicts. The finding holds in two unrelated populations 15 years apart. It is still correlational, because famine brings stress and infection as well as hunger.

**Epigenetics.** Many epigenetic mechanisms contribute to the **emergence and persistence** of schizophrenia (Rizzardi et al., 2019). **DNA methylation** and **histone modification** have both been implicated in the expression of **genes for synapse-specific proteins in prefrontal cortex neurons** (Caldeira, Peça, & Carvalho, 2019). This research shows how genes interact with the environment (Breen et al., 2019; Gandal et al., 2018). **Transgenerational** epigenetic mechanisms are also of great interest (Yeshurun & Hannan, 2019).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition gives **no twin concordance** for schizophrenia. Older editions gave about **45 percent (MZ) and 10 percent (DZ)**, plus adoption studies and Gottesman and Bertelsen (1989). For such a question answer roughly **45 to 50 percent MZ**. The point is that this is **far below 100 percent**, so experience matters, which the 11th edition makes through the famine and epigenetic evidence.

### Neural Bases of Schizophrenia

Meta-analyses of brain development in patients or at-risk people (Fusar-Poli et al., 2011; Steen et al., 2006; Vita et al., 2006) give **four findings**:

1. **At-risk** people who have not been diagnosed (for example close relatives of patients) show **volume reductions**, for instance in the **hippocampus** (Haukvik et al., 2018).
2. **Extensive brain changes already exist** at the patient's **first** scan.
3. The changes **continue after diagnosis**.
4. **Different areas change at different rates** (Gogtay & Thompson, 2010).

Finding 1 matters causally. These people have never been treated, so the reductions **cannot be drug side effects**. Findings 2 and 3 describe a progressive developmental disorder that is under way long before diagnosis.

**The pathway challenge.** Recent research has challenged the classic claim that the **mesocorticolimbic** pathway is involved. It shows that the neuropathological changes occur in the **nigrostriatal pathway instead** (McCutcheon, Abi-Dargham, & Howes, 2019).

> **Trap.** **Classic** dopamine theory = **mesocorticolimbic**. **Newer** neuropathology = **nigrostriatal**. Older keys say mesolimbic. The irony is that the nigrostriatal pathway is the one traditionally blamed for the **parkinsonian side effects** that first led researchers to dopamine.

**Functional connectivity.** Imaging studies of **functional connectivity** (how activity co-varies between regions) have taken two forms. During **hallucinations**, connectivity patterns **change** compared with non-hallucinating periods (Weber et al., 2020). And researchers are testing whether **intrinsic functional connectivity** (measured at rest) can **predict a patient's response to antipsychotics** (Chan et al., 2019).

### Conclusion: One Disorder or Several?

There has been real progress, but final answers are unlikely **until the diagnosis is "sharpened up"**. There is **general consensus** that patients diagnosed under DSM-5 **do not have a single unitary disorder with one neural pathology** (Kim et al., 2017; Krynicki et al., 2018). The diagnosis seems to **group related disorders under one label**. The evidence is the **variability of symptoms**, of **neural pathology**, and of **response to particular antipsychotics**. When patients with one diagnosis respond to a drug in very different ways, the simplest explanation is that they have different conditions, and averaging across them can hide a drug that works well for a subgroup (the argument returns in 18.6).

## 18.2 Depressive Disorders

"Depression" in everyday speech means a reaction to grievous loss: of a loved one, of self-esteem, of health. The word also names a psychiatric disorder, and that disorder is the subject of this module.

### What Are Depressive Disorders?

Some people experience deep depression and/or **anhedonia** (the **loss of the capacity to experience pleasure**; Husain & Roiser, 2018), often **for no apparent reason** (Treadway & Zald, 2011). The depression can **impair cognition** (Cambridge et al., 2018) and make it almost impossible to keep a job, eat, or maintain social contact and hygiene. **Sleep disturbance** and **suicidal thoughts** are common. When the condition lasts **2 weeks or longer**, it is called **clinical depression**, or **major depressive disorder**.

> **Trap.** Durations: **schizophrenia** (book's rule) = two symptoms for **1 month**. **Major depressive disorder** = **2 weeks or longer**. **Rapid cycling** bipolar disorder (18.3) = **4 or more** episodes **per year**.

### The Case of S.B., the Depressed Biopsychology Student

S.B. earned top marks in his first year of a biopsychology programme. From his **second year** he became depressed. He **slept excessively**, could not concentrate, and thought often about **death and suicide**. He also had **delusions**: he believed he was stupid and disliked, and felt **persecuted** by instructors and peers. Having seen these symptoms in **family members**, he went to a psychiatrist. He **refused medication**, remembering the side effects his relatives had suffered, and attended **psychotherapy** for the rest of his degree. Talking helped, but he **improved little**. He still graduated with good grades and entered **graduate studies in biopsychology**.

A few months into graduate school he **could no longer function**. Memory and attention problems stopped him reading, and delusional and suicidal thoughts were constant. His psychiatrist **hospitalised him at once** and started him on an **antidepressant** and an **antipsychotic** for the delusions. He took a **leave of absence**, returned months later, and with symptoms persisting **despite all the medications**, was barely holding things together.

So depression can include **psychotic features**, and the antipsychotic was treating a symptom, not schizophrenia. There is a **family history**. The disorder is **partly resistant** to both psychotherapy and drugs. S.B. returns in 18.3 and 18.6.

### Reactive and Endogenous Depression; Who Gets Depressed

**Reactive depression** is triggered by an **obvious negative experience** (a death, a lost job). **Endogenous depression** has **no apparent cause**, as in S.B.'s case (Malki et al., 2014).

- Depression affects **2 to 5 percent of the global population** (Han, Russo, & Nestler, 2019).
- **Females are about twice as likely** to be diagnosed in their lifetime (Kuehner, 2017). The gap is **most pronounced in adolescence** (Salk, Hyde, & Abramson, 2017). The reasons are unclear, but **gonadal-hormone explanations** are currently popular (Altemus, Sarvaiya, & Epperson, 2014).
- **Non-Caucasian** individuals are more likely to have **chronic, more debilitating** depression, for reasons still unclear (Bailey, Mokonogho, & Kumar, 2019).
- **Lifetime risk of completed suicide** in clinical depression: **4 to 15 percent** across studies (Wang et al., 2015).
- Depression affects children, adolescents and adults. In adults it is often **comorbid** (two conditions tending to occur together in the same person) with **anxiety disorders, coronary heart disease and diabetes** (Scott, 2014).

> **Trap.** Older editions gave about 10 percent lifetime prevalence and a suicide risk of about 5 percent (Bostwick & Pankratz, 2000). The 11th edition says **2 to 5 percent** (global) and **4 to 15 percent**. The **2:1 female ratio** is unchanged, and it now peaks **in adolescence**.

### Two Subtypes with an Apparent Cause: SAD and Peripartum Depression

Two subtypes of major depressive disorder have an obvious cause because of **when** the episodes happen.

**Seasonal affective disorder (SAD)**: episodes of depression and lethargy recur in particular seasons, **usually winter** (Tyrer et al., 2016). Two lines of evidence implicate **reduced sunlight**. First, SAD is more common in **Alaska (9 percent)** than in **Florida (1 percent)**, where winter days are longer and brighter (Melrose, 2015). Second, **light therapy**, for example **15 to 30 minutes of very bright light each morning**, often reduces symptoms (Oren, Koziorowski, & Desan, 2013). The geographical comparison is only correlational. The light-therapy result shows that changing the suspected cause changes the outcome.

**Peripartum depression** is intense, sustained depression **during pregnancy, after birth, or both** (Serati et al., 2016). It seems to occur in about **13 percent of pregnancies** (Silver et al., 2018).

> **Trap.** **Peripartum**, not "postpartum". The DSM-5 specifier includes onset **during pregnancy**. Older keys say postpartum (about 10 percent of deliveries). The 11th edition gives **13 percent of pregnancies**. SAD figures: **Alaska 9, Florida 1** (older editions gave 1.5 for Florida).

### Antidepressant Drugs

The learning objective asks for **five classes**, with an example of each (Willner, Scheel-Krüger, & Belzung, 2013):

| Class | Example | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **MAO inhibitors** | **Iproniazid** | Inhibit monoamine oxidase |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | **Imipramine** | Block serotonin **and** norepinephrine reuptake |
| **Selective monoamine-reuptake inhibitors** | **Fluoxetine** (SSRI), **reboxetine** (SNRI) | Block reuptake of a specific monoamine |
| **Atypical antidepressants** | **Bupropion**, agomelatine | Catch-all; many mechanisms |
| **NMDA-receptor antagonists** | **Ketamine** | Block the glutamate NMDA receptor |

#### Monoamine oxidase inhibitors

**Iproniazid**, the **first antidepressant**, was developed for **tuberculosis** and failed at that. It was noticed that it made tuberculosis patients **less concerned about their disorder**. Tested on a mixed group of psychiatric patients, it seemed to help depression, and it was first marketed as an antidepressant in **1957**.

Iproniazid is a **monoamine agonist**. It raises norepinephrine and serotonin levels by **inhibiting monoamine oxidase (MAO)**, the enzyme that **breaks down monoamines in the neuron's cytoplasm**. Its most dangerous side effect is the **cheese effect** (Finberg & Gillman, 2011). **Cheese, wine and pickles** contain **tyramine**, a **potent elevator of blood pressure**. Normally it does little harm, because **MAO in the liver** breaks it down quickly. In someone on an MAO inhibitor that protection is gone, and tyramine-rich food can cause **blood-pressure surges and stroke**. The side effect follows directly from the drug's action: the enzyme blocked to treat depression is the one the liver needs to clear tyramine.

> **Trap.** **Tyramine** (not tyrosine or tryptophan). The danger is **hypertension** leading to stroke. Reserpine (18.1) is the drug that causes dangerous *hypotension*. Iproniazid: **tuberculosis**, **1957**.

#### Tricyclic antidepressants

**Tricyclics** are named for a structure with **three rings of atoms**. **Imipramine**, the first, was thought to be an **antipsychotic**. In a mixed patient sample it **did nothing for schizophrenia but helped some depressed patients**, another lucky accident. Tricyclics **block reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine**, and they are a **safer alternative to MAO inhibitors**.

#### Selective monoamine-reuptake inhibitors

The **selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** arrived in the **late 1980s**. They are **serotonin agonists** that **block serotonin reuptake** from synapses, so serotonin stays longer in the synapse and activates more receptors (Figure 18.3). **Fluoxetine** (**Prozac**) was the **first SSRI**, followed by paroxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine and others. Its structure is a slight variation on imipramine's, and **it is no more effective than imipramine**. It was still embraced at once and has been prescribed in many millions of cases, for **two reasons**: SSRIs have **fewer side effects** than tricyclics and MAOIs, and they work against a **wide range of disorders** besides depression.

The success of SSRIs led to the **selective norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)**, such as **reboxetine**, which are **just as effective**. Drugs that block reuptake of **more than one** monoamine, such as **venlafaxine**, also work (Harmer, Duman, & Cowen, 2017).

> **Trap.** SSRIs did **not** beat tricyclics on effectiveness; they won on **side effects and breadth**. In Pinel, **SNRI** = selective **norepinephrine**-reuptake inhibitor (**reboxetine**). Clinical usage often means *serotonin-norepinephrine* reuptake inhibitor, with venlafaxine as the example, but Pinel puts venlafaxine among drugs blocking **more than one** monoamine.

#### Atypical antidepressants

From the **1980s**, antidepressants appeared that fitted none of those three classes, so the **atypical antidepressants** became a **catch-all class** with many modes of action. **Bupropion** blocks **dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake** and also **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** (Carroll et al., 2014). **Agomelatine** is a **melatonin receptor agonist** (Taylor et al., 2014). The class has no single mechanism to learn.

#### NMDA-receptor antagonists

From the **early 1990s**, studies reported that **antagonising the glutamate NMDA receptor** helped depression. In the **early 2000s** the dissociative hallucinogen **ketamine** proved remarkably effective: **a single low dose rapidly reduces depression**, even during a severe episode (McGirr et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2018). Every monoamine antidepressant takes weeks. A drug that works within hours through **glutamate** is strong evidence against the idea that depression is basically a monoamine deficit. Because ketamine has **undesirable side effects**, researchers are looking for **more selective NMDA antagonists**, and antagonists of other glutamate receptors (Duman, Sanacora, & Krystal, 2019).

#### How effective are antidepressants?

The United States spends about **$15 billion** a year on antidepressants. Many studies find that about **50 percent** of clinically depressed patients **improve** on them (Cipriani et al., 2018b). But **control groups improve at about 35 percent**, so only **about 15 percent** are **actually helped by the drug** (Cipriani et al., 2018a). This subtraction is the key reasoning step. Depression often improves anyway (placebo, natural recovery, regression to the mean), so only the **difference from control** can be credited to the drug.

The book adds two qualifications. A **15 percent** gain is still **meaningful** for a disorder this dangerous (Maslej et al., 2020). And meta-analyses show that **some antidepressants work better than others**, with relative efficacy depending on the **sex and age** of the patient (Cipriani et al., 2018a).

> **Trap.** Older editions gave 50 versus about 25 percent and cited Kirsch et al. (2008). The 11th edition gives **50 versus 35 percent**, so about **15 percent** truly helped. "What proportion are actually helped?" = **about 15 percent**, not 50.

### Brain Stimulation to Treat Depression

Two brain-stimulation treatments have been developed for depression: **repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation** and **deep brain stimulation**.

#### Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

**Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)** is a form of TMS (Chapter 5). It delivers **repeated magnetic pulses non-invasively** to specific cortical areas, **usually the prefrontal cortex** (Gaynes et al., 2014). **High-frequency rTMS** (for example **five pulses per second**) is believed to **stimulate** the target region. **Low-frequency rTMS** (**less than one pulse per second**) is believed to **inhibit** it (Berlim et al., 2013, 2014). **Meta-analyses** show reliable improvement after **either** low-frequency (Berlim et al., 2013) **or** high-frequency (Berlim et al., 2014) rTMS **compared with sham rTMS**. The comparison with sham is what makes the evidence convincing, because patients cannot tell real stimulation from sham, so expectation cannot explain the difference. That both exciting and inhibiting the prefrontal cortex help is a puzzle the book leaves open.

> **Trap.** **High** frequency **stimulates**, **low** frequency **inhibits**. rTMS is **non-invasive**. Deep brain stimulation is **invasive**.

#### Deep brain stimulation

Chronic stimulation through an **implanted electrode** (Figure 18.4) has helped some depressed patients who had **failed to respond to other treatments**.

> **Key study, Lozano and colleagues (2008).** They implanted the tip of a stimulating electrode in the **white matter of the anterior cingulate gyrus** in the **medial prefrontal cortex** (Figure 18.5). A **stimulator implanted under the skin** delivered **continual pulses that the patients could not detect**. The **20 patients** had been chosen because they had **repeatedly failed to respond to conventional treatments**. The results were strikingly positive: **60 percent showed substantial improvement**, **35 percent were largely symptom-free**, and most stayed improved for **at least 1 year** (the length of the study).

The patients were **treatment-resistant by selection**, so their expected rate of spontaneous improvement was low. The benefit lasted a year, which is long for a placebo response, and the stimulation could not be felt. The book describes **no sham group**, though, so the strongest support is **replication at other centres** (Holtzheimer et al., 2011; Lozano et al., 2012; Lozano & Mayberg, 2015).

> **Trap.** The site is the **white matter** under the **anterior cingulate gyrus** (medial prefrontal cortex), just beneath the front bend (genu) of the **corpus callosum** in Figure 18.5. This is the subcallosal or subgenual cingulate. The electrodes are **bilateral**. Numbers: **20; 60 percent; 35 percent; 1 year**.

### Theories of Depression

The book points out that **most theories of depression are based almost entirely on the therapies that work**. They are reasoned backwards from the treatment, as the dopamine theory was.

#### The monoamine theory of depression

The **monoamine theory of depression** holds that depression is associated with **underactivity at serotonergic and noradrenergic synapses**. It rests mainly on the fact that **MAO inhibitors, tricyclics and selective monoamine-reuptake inhibitors are all agonists of serotonin, norepinephrine, or both**.

**Autopsy support.** Norepinephrine and serotonin **receptors are more numerous** in the brains of depressed people **who had not received drug treatment**. This implies **too little monoamine release**, through **up-regulation**: when too little of a transmitter is released, the number of its receptors usually **increases** to compensate. The restriction to **untreated** brains is essential, because antidepressants themselves change receptor numbers.

> **Trap.** **Up-regulation = more receptors** in response to **too little** transmitter. Increased receptor density in untreated depressed brains points to **reduced** release.

**Three lines of evidence against the theory:**

1. Monoamine agonists **do not work for most depressed patients** (Malhi, Lingford-Hughes, & Young, 2016), and when they do work they are **only slightly better than placebo** (Linde et al., 2015).
2. **The time course.** Monoamine activity rises **almost immediately**, but the antidepressant effect takes **days to weeks** (Harmer, Duman, & Cowen, 2017). This is the same lag that undermined the D2 theory.
3. **Other transmitters**, namely **GABA, glutamate and acetylcholine**, are involved in depression (Murrough, Abdallah, & Mathew, 2017; Northoff, 2013; Pytka et al., 2016). Ketamine is the clearest example.

#### The neuroplasticity theory of depression

This theory starts from the lag. Nearly all antidepressants **raise monoaminergic transmission rapidly**, but help only **weeks** later. So the synaptic agonism **cannot be the critical mechanism**. There must be a **downstream** change, and one proposal is an **increase in neuroplasticity**. The **neuroplasticity theory of depression** says depression results from **decreased neuroplastic processes** in structures such as the **hippocampus**, which leads to **neuron loss** and other pathology (Castrén & Hen, 2013; Miller & Hen, 2015).

Two kinds of evidence mirror each other (Brandon & McKay, 2015; Mahar et al., 2014; Samuels et al., 2015):

| Stress and depression go with... | Antidepressant treatments go with... |
|---|---|
| **Reduced** neurotrophin synthesis | **Increased** neurotrophin synthesis |
| **Decreased** adult hippocampal neurogenesis | **Increased** adult hippocampal neurogenesis |
| | **Increased** synaptogenesis |

Building new neurons and synapses takes weeks, which explains the lag.

**BDNF.** Treatments that improve depression, **drug and non-drug alike**, raise **brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)** **only in patients who improve** (Homberg et al., 2014). A rise that tracks improvement, rather than drug exposure, suggests BDNF is part of the mechanism. So **decreased blood BDNF** has been proposed as a **biomarker** (a biological state that predicts a particular disorder) **for depression**, and **increased blood BDNF** as a biomarker **for successful treatment** (Polyakova et al., 2015). The antidepressant-induced BDNF rise may **boost neuroplastic processes** such as hippocampal neurogenesis and so relieve depression (Björkholm & Monteggia, 2016).

> **Trap.** The book prints "neuro*tropic*". The correct term is **neuro*trophic*** (nourishing). Older editions gave the **diathesis-stress model** as the second theory. The 11th edition's two theories are **monoamine** and **neuroplasticity**.

### Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms of Depression

Until recently, **most genetic studies of depression could not be replicated**. **Since 2015**, studies have **reliably identified many contributing genes** (Ormel, Hartman, & Snieder, 2019). With so many genes involved, depression is a **complex trait** with many **gene-environment interactions through epigenetic mechanisms** (Heller et al., 2014; Nestler, 2014). **Several epigenetic mechanisms contribute** (Penner-Goeke & Binder, 2019), and a person's **epigenome might one day predict** a future depression (Barbu et al., 2020).

### Neural Bases of Depression

**Structural imaging** shows consistent **grey-matter reductions** in the **prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala and cingulate cortex** (Lener & Iosifescu, 2015), and **white-matter reductions**, **most reliably frontal** (Russo & Nestler, 2013). **Functional imaging** shows atypical activity in **frontal, cingulate and insular cortices**, the **amygdala, thalamus and striatum**. **Functional-connectivity** studies find many differences, so the **networks of depressed patients are markedly different** from healthy ones (Goldstein-Piekarski & Williams, 2019; Korgaonkar et al., 2019). Most of these differences have **not yet been linked to behaviour or cognition**, though such links are emerging (Albert et al., 2019). Keep the four structures in mind, because 18.4 compares anxiety disorders with them.

### Conclusion: Little Better Than 50 Years Ago

Despite promising research, **treatments for depression are not much better than they were 50 years ago**. Many researchers argue that diagnostic tools such as the **DSM-5**, which create **large heterogeneous patient groups**, are **misguiding research**, and that **more precise diagnosis** is needed (Insel & Cuthbert, 2015). This is the thinking behind the NIMH's **Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)**, which classifies by measurable dimensions of brain and behaviour rather than by DSM category.

## 18.3 Bipolar Disorder

People who have periods of clinical depression and also periods of **hypomania** or **mania** have **bipolar disorder**. It affects **about 3 percent of the global population** (Ashok et al., 2017; Vieta et al., 2018), with much cross-cultural variability in presentation and course (Subramanian, Sarkar, & Kattimani, 2017). It has **one of the highest rates of attempted and completed suicide** of any psychiatric disorder (Plans et al., 2019).

### What Is Bipolar Disorder?

Hypomania and mania are in some ways the **opposite of depression**. **Hypomania** involves a **reduced need for sleep, high energy and positive affect**. People are talkative, energetic, impulsive and very confident, can be very effective at some jobs, and are great fun to be with. **Mania** is the same **taken to an extreme**, plus **delusions of grandeur, overconfidence, impulsivity and distractibility**, and it **usually involves psychosis**. In full mania, incessant chatter hurtles from topic to topic, no goal seems unattainable, and leap-before-you-look impulsiveness leaves behind **unfinished projects, unpaid bills and broken relationships**.

| Type | Episodes |
|---|---|
| **Bipolar disorder type II** | **Depression and hypomania** only |
| **Bipolar disorder type I** | **Mania** (with or without depression), **or mania only** (Makin, 2019) |

> **Trap.** **Type I is the more severe** and requires **mania**. **Type II** = **hypomania plus depression**, never full mania. Someone who has had **only** manic episodes still has **bipolar I**. Older terms: "manic-depressive illness" = bipolar disorder; "unipolar depression" = depression without (hypo)mania.

### The Case of S.B. Revisited: The Biopsychology Student with Bipolar Disorder

Still carrying a **residual depression**, S.B. finished a **master's degree** and started a **Ph.D.** In the **first summer** he felt exceptionally good. His mood was elevated, he slept **less than 3 hours a night**, he was highly sociable and charismatic, and he read faster and understood difficult material. Many of the ideas behind his **Ph.D. thesis** came to him then. This was **hypomania**, and with depression already on record he now met criteria for **bipolar disorder type II**. It lasted **several months**.

Then he slept **less than 2 hours a night**, sometimes not at all for days. His living room became a maze of books and papers. He believed he had developed a **comprehensive theory explaining every aspect of how society works**, saw links between everything, and filled notebooks with diagrams nobody else understood. These were **delusions of grandeur**. People told him to **slow down** or gave him funny looks. He was in **mania** and met criteria for **bipolar disorder type I**.

His enthusiasm then faded. Feeling misunderstood and rejected, he developed **suicidal thoughts**. After **several weeks** of intense suicidal ideation, barely sleeping and forgetting to eat, he phoned his psychiatrist about his "brilliant" theory. Suspicious, she had him come to the hospital and recognised a **mixed state**: symptoms of **severe depression** (suicidal ideation) **and mania** (grandiosity) **at the same time**. She committed him, and he spent **6 weeks** on a psychiatric ward.

> **Trap.** A **mixed state** = depressive and manic symptoms **simultaneously**, not rapid alternation. S.B.'s sequence: **depression → bipolar II → bipolar I → mixed state**.

### Two Lessons from S.B.

**Bipolar disorder does not mean rapid mood swings.** Contrary to popular belief, mood does not alternate within hours or days. Episodes **usually last weeks to months**. Even **rapid cycling bipolar disorder** is defined as **4 or more mood episodes per year** (Cao et al., 2017; Carvalho et al., 2014), so it is counted per year, not per day.

**Patients often move between diagnoses** (Phillips & Kupfer, 2013). S.B. went from **clinical depression** to **bipolar II** to **bipolar I**. The book asks whether this reflects real change in the illness or a problem with diagnosis. Probably both. The illness did develop, but a system that relabels the same person three times according to which symptoms have appeared so far is classifying by surface features, not causes. In practice, any diagnosis of depression is **provisional**, because a first hypomanic or manic episode may still come.

### Mood Stabilisers

Ideally, **mood stabilisers** **treat depression or mania without increasing the risk of mania or depression**, respectively (Karanti et al., 2016). Preventing recurrence matters because untreated episodes typically become **more severe and more frequent** (Post, 2016; Post, Fleming, & Kapczinski, 2012). How mood stabilisers work is **still debated** (Oruch et al., 2014), but **many also treat epilepsy** (Yildiz et al., 2011) **and schizophrenia** (Post, 2016).

#### John Cade and the discovery of lithium

**Lithium**, a simple metallic ion, was the **first mood stabiliser**. Its discovery was largely accidental and built on a chain of wrong hypotheses:

1. **John Cade**, an **Australian psychiatrist**, injected **guinea pigs** with the **urine of psychiatric inpatients**. Urine from **manic** patients was the **most toxic** (it killed the most guinea pigs).
2. He injected **urea**, a urine constituent. It was toxic, but **much less** than manic urine, so something else was adding to the toxicity.
3. Suspecting **uric acid**, he injected urea with **lithium urate** (a soluble lithium salt of uric acid). **Contrary to his hypothesis**, the lithium urate **protected** the animals from the urea.
4. To separate lithium from uric acid, he injected urea with **lithium carbonate**. It **also protected** them, so the active agent was **lithium**.
5. He hypothesised that **manic patients have less lithium** than non-manic patients.
6. Lithium carbonate seemed to **calm** guinea pigs. At his doses it actually causes **extreme nausea**: they were **sick, not calm**.
7. In **1954** (the book's date) he gave lithium to **10 manic patients, 6 with schizophrenia and 3 depressed**. The effect was **dramatic but only in the manic patients**, and some were **discharged** (Mitchell & Hadzi-Pavlovic, 2000), which was unheard of before psychiatric drugs.

Every hypothesis in the chain was wrong, yet the trial was informative because it had a built-in comparison. The same drug, given by the same enthusiastic psychiatrist, helped the **manic** group and **not** the other two. A general sedative effect or the doctor's expectations would have affected all three groups.

**Slow uptake.** Few scientists were impressed by Cade's conference presentations, and few companies would pay to test a **metallic ion that could not be patented**. Lithium was **not conclusively shown to be effective until the late 1960s**. More than **50 years** later, many still consider it **the best mood stabiliser** (Machado-Vieira, 2018). It also has **neuroprotective** effects (Kerr, Bjedov, & Sofola-Adesakin, 2018) and **anti-suicidal** effects (Baldessarini, Tondo, & Vázquez, 2019).

> **Trap.** Pinel dates Cade's 10/6/3 patient trial to **1954**. Standard histories and most exam keys date Cade's report to **1949** (*Medical Journal of Australia*). 1954 is the year of Mogens Schou's controlled trial in Denmark. If both appear, **1949** is historically correct for Cade. If only 1954 appears, the question follows Pinel. The other details are undisputed: **Australian**, **guinea pigs**, **urine**, lithium **urate then carbonate**, **nausea** not calm, effect **only in mania**, **unpatentable**, accepted in the **late 1960s**.

**Shared limits.** Mood stabilisers are **lithium, certain anticonvulsants and certain atypical antipsychotics**. (Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine are the standard anticonvulsant examples, though the book names none.) **All act against mania**. **Some** act against depression and **some** against both (Prabhavalkar, Poovanpallil, & Bhatt, 2015). None eliminates all symptoms. Side effects such as **weight gain, tremor, blurred vision and dizziness** (Murru et al., 2015) often lead patients to **stop taking them** (Jann, 2014).

### Theories of Bipolar Disorder

Progress has been slowed by **two gaps**: we do **not understand how mood stabilisers work** (Can, Schulze, & Gould, 2014), so the backwards-from-the-drug strategy fails, and there is **no adequate animal model** (Logan & McClung, 2016). Several disturbances have still been identified (Ashok et al., 2017; Maletic & Raison, 2014):

- **HPA-axis dysregulation**;
- **marked circadian-rhythm disruption**, in patients **and in their non-bipolar relatives**, which suggests an inherited vulnerability rather than a consequence of illness;
- altered **GABA, glutamate and dopamine** transmission;
- **lower BDNF** in patients who are **either depressed or manic**, linking to the neuroplasticity theory.

#### The reward hypersensitivity theory

The **reward hypersensitivity theory of bipolar disorder** (Alloy, Nusslock, & Boland, 2015) proposes a **dysfunctional reward system that over-reacts to rewards or to their absence**. Evidence: people with bipolar disorder who are **euthymic** (no current symptoms of depression, hypomania or mania) make **much riskier choices** than other people (Johnson et al., 2019). Because they are between episodes, this points to a **trait**, not a mood state. The theory explains both poles with one mechanism:

1. **Repeated reward** for their activities → **excessive goal-seeking** → **hypomania or mania**.
2. **Failure to achieve goals** → **excessive decrease in reward-seeking** → **depression**.

Consistent with this, patients show **increased activity in prefrontal and striatal reward circuits in both depressive and manic states**. Other emerging theories propose roles for **inflammation** (So et al., 2017) and **altered synaptic function** (Harrison, Geddes, & Tunbridge, 2017).

### Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms of Bipolar Disorder

Genetic research began from the observation that **relatives of patients are very likely to develop the disorder**. The main conclusion is that **hundreds to thousands of genes** are associated with bipolar disorder (Gandal et al., 2018). Because each contributes very little, they have revealed **little about mechanisms**. Research now focuses on **epigenetic mechanisms that might affect the transcription** of those genes (Duffy et al., 2019).

### Neural Bases of Bipolar Disorder

**Cognitive deficits are common** and go with various changes in brain function (Lima, Peckham, & Johnson, 2019). MRI studies report **consistent overall grey-matter reductions** (Maletic & Raison, 2014). The **extent of frontal and limbic grey-matter loss correlates with clinical outcome** (Dusi et al., 2018). Several structures have been reported **smaller**: the **medial prefrontal cortex, left anterior cingulate, left superior temporal gyrus, certain prefrontal regions and the hippocampus** (Hanford et al., 2016; Savitz, Price, & Drevets, 2014). **fMRI meta-analyses** find atypical activation in the **frontal cortex, medial temporal lobe and basal ganglia**, with **atypical functional connectivity** among them (Favre et al., 2014), **centred on emotional-processing networks** (Hafeman et al., 2019; Perry et al., 2019).

## 18.4 Anxiety Disorders

**Anxiety** is **chronic fear that persists in the absence of any direct threat**, a common psychological correlate of stress (Mahan & Ressler, 2012). Fear of a snake in front of you is fear. Persistent dread when there is no snake is anxiety. Anxiety is **adaptive** if it motivates effective coping. When it is so severe that it **disrupts functioning**, it is an **anxiety disorder**. All anxiety disorders involve feelings of anxiety (fear, worry) plus **physiological stress reactions**: **tachycardia** (rapid heartbeat), **hypertension** (high blood pressure), nausea, breathing difficulties, sleep disturbance and **high glucocorticoid levels**.

Anxiety disorders are the **most prevalent of all psychiatric disorders**. Between **14 and 34 percent** of people have one at some point in life, and incidence is **almost twice as high in females** (Bandelow & Michaelis, 2015; Gallo et al., 2018).

> **Trap.** "Most prevalent psychiatric disorders" = **anxiety disorders**, not depression. Older editions gave about **17 percent** (Somers et al., 2006). The 11th edition gives **14 to 34 percent**.

### The Case of M.R., the Woman Who Was Afraid to Go Out

M.R., **35**, developed a pathological fear of leaving her house. The onset was **sudden**. After an **argument with her husband** she went out to post a letter and calm down, but was overwhelmed by **dizziness and fear**. She struggled home and **rarely left it again for about 2 years**, then gradually improved. Her recovery was cut short by her **sister's death** and **another argument**. She tried to go shopping, **panicked**, and had to be **escorted home by a stranger**. After that, leaving home alone brought on **dizziness, sweating and a pounding heart**, and she would **flee home to avoid a full-blown panic attack**. She could go out if **escorted by her husband or one of her children**, but felt anxious the whole time.

Onset and relapse both followed **stressful interpersonal events**. Her **avoidance maintains itself**, because every escape home relieves the anxiety and so rewards the escape. Needing a **trusted companion** to go out is typical of agoraphobia. The book states her diagnosis: **agoraphobia**, with **panic attacks**.

### Four Anxiety Disorders

- **Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD):** extreme anxiety and worry about **a large number of different activities or events** (Mochcovitch et al., 2017).
- **Specific phobia:** strong fear of **particular objects** (birds, spiders) or **situations** (enclosed spaces, darkness), usually with **avoidance**.
- **Agoraphobia:** pathological fear of **public places and open spaces**. It could be counted as a specific phobia, but it is **more incapacitating** than most, so the **DSM-5 treats it as a separate category**. M.R. had agoraphobia.
- **Panic disorder:** **recurrent rapid-onset attacks of extreme fear** with severe stress symptoms (choking, palpitations, shortness of breath). **Panic attacks** also occur in some cases of GAD, specific phobia and agoraphobia (Cosci & Mansueto, 2019a), so a panic attack is a **symptom**, while panic disorder is a **diagnosis**.

> **Trap.** Older, DSM-IV-based editions listed **OCD** and **PTSD** as anxiety disorders. The **DSM-5 moved them** to their own chapters, and the 11th edition's four include **neither**. The DSM-5 also made **agoraphobia** independent of panic disorder.

### Pharmacological Treatment of Anxiety Disorders

Three sorts of drugs are commonly used: **benzodiazepines**, **certain antidepressants** and **pregabalin** (Quagliato, Freire, & Nardi, 2018).

#### Benzodiazepines

**Benzodiazepines** such as **chlordiazepoxide (Librium)** and **diazepam (Valium)** are also prescribed as **hypnotics** (sleep-inducing drugs), **anticonvulsants** and **muscle relaxants**. They are the **most widely prescribed psychoactive drugs**: about **5 percent of adult North Americans** take them (Balon & Starcevic, 2020). Their effects are thought to come from **agonistic action at GABA-A receptors**. (Strictly, they bind to their own site on the receptor complex and **enhance GABA's effect**.) Boosting the brain's main inhibitory transmitter explains all four uses. **Side effects** are **sedation, ataxia** (disruption of motor activity), **tremor, nausea**, and a **withdrawal reaction including rebound anxiety**. They are also **addictive**, so they are usually prescribed **only short-term**.

> **Trap.** Librium = **chlordiazepoxide**; Valium = **diazepam**; receptor = **GABA-A**. Benzodiazepine withdrawal gives real **rebound anxiety**. Contrast Tourette's (18.5), where the supposed rebound after tic suppression **does not occur**.

#### Antidepressant drugs

Anxiety disorders show **high comorbidity**. About **47 percent of people with bipolar disorder** and **53 percent with major depressive disorder** have a comorbid anxiety disorder (Moscati, Flint, & Kendler, 2015). The drug effects match in both directions. **Antidepressants** (monoamine agonists, tricyclics) often work against **anxiety disorders** (Bandelow, 2020), and **anxiolytic drugs** (antianxiety drugs) often work against **depression**. Conditions that keep co-occurring and respond to each other's drugs probably share mechanisms.

#### Pregabalin

**Pregabalin** is one of the newest anxiolytics and is **particularly effective for GAD** (Generoso et al., 2017). It is thought to work by **modulating voltage-gated calcium channels**, which changes calcium levels inside nerve cells. This mechanism has **nothing to do with GABA or serotonin**, which matters for the animal-model problem below.

> **Trap.** 11th edition: **benzodiazepines, antidepressants, pregabalin**. Older editions had **buspirone** (a 5-HT1A agonist) instead of pregabalin. Pregabalin acts on **calcium channels**, best for **GAD**.

**Conclusion on treatment.** Many drugs produce **slight but statistically significant** group improvements, but treatment **leaves a lot to be desired**. **Many patients are not helped at all**, and many **stop because of side effects**. With a large enough sample, a tiny average benefit can be statistically significant and still matter little to the typical patient.

### Animal Models of Anxiety Disorders

Animal models are used to study anxiety and to screen **new anxiolytics**. Their **weakness** is that they rely on **defensive behaviours**, assuming that such behaviours are **motivated by fear** and that **fear and anxiety are similar** (Dias et al., 2013; LeDoux, 2014). Anxiety is defined as fear **without a direct threat**, yet each model below confronts the animal **with** a threat.

#### The elevated-plus-maze test

Rats are placed on a **four-armed, plus-shaped maze** about **50 centimetres above the floor**. **Two arms have sides and two do not**. Anxiety is measured as the **proportion of time spent in the enclosed arms** rather than on the exposed ones.

#### The defensive-burying test

Rats are **shocked by a wire-wrapped wooden dowel** on the wall of a **familiar chamber**. Anxiety is measured as the **time spent spraying bedding at the shock source** with forward thrusts of the head and forepaws.

#### The risk-assessment test

After **one brief exposure to a cat** on the surface of a **laboratory burrow system**, rats **flee to their burrows and freeze**. They then show **risk-assessment behaviours** (scanning the surface from the burrow mouth, exploring in a **cautious stretched posture**) before behaving normally again. Anxiety is measured as **time spent freezing and in risk assessment**.

#### How the models were validated, and the catch

All three were **validated** by showing that **benzodiazepines reduce their anxiety measures** while **non-anxiolytic drugs usually do not**. The catch is that **many cases of anxiety respond poorly to benzodiazepines**. So the tests may model **benzodiazepine-sensitive anxiety** rather than anxiety in general, and may **miss anxiolytics that act by non-GABAergic mechanisms**, such as pregabalin. A test accepted as an anxiety measure because the standard drug changes it will tend to find only drugs like the standard one. The book lists this among the chapter's "thinking creatively" points.

> **Trap.** Measures: **enclosed-arm time**, **burying time**, **freezing plus risk-assessment time**. Validation drug: **benzodiazepines**. Two criticisms: the models may capture only **benzodiazepine-sensitive** anxiety, and defensive behaviour reflects **fear**, not threat-free anxiety.

### Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms of Anxiety Disorders

Researchers have been **unable to definitively locate genes** for anxiety disorders (Meier & Deckert, 2019), so research has shifted to **environmental factors and epigenetics**. **Many epigenetic mechanisms** are implicated, and identifying the critical ones is a long way off (Cosci & Mansueto, 2020b; Lin & Tsai, 2020). Part of the problem is **heterogeneity**: there are so many types and presentations that it will be more fruitful to **study specific symptoms rather than diagnostic categories**.

### Neural Bases of Anxiety Disorders

As with the other disorders, theories **rest heavily on drug effects**. Because anxiolytics act on **GABAergic** (benzodiazepines) or **serotonergic** (fluoxetine) transmission, attention has focused on **those two transmitters**.

The **prefrontal cortex, hippocampus and amygdala**, implicated in depression, are **also implicated in anxiety disorders** (Calhoon & Tye, 2015), which fits their comorbidity and shared drug responses. But the **evidence differs**. In depression these structures show **atrophy** (shrinkage). In anxiety disorders there is **no significant atrophy**, and the evidence comes from **functional imaging**, which shows atypical activity during **emotional tasks** (Fonzo & Etkin, 2017; Maron & Nutt, 2017). Attention now centres on **intrinsic functional connectivity**, and **each anxiety disorder has a distinct pattern** (Northoff, 2020), which is another argument against treating "anxiety disorders" as one category.

> **Trap.** Same structures, different evidence. **Depression = atrophy. Anxiety = no significant atrophy**, only functional differences. "Hippocampal atrophy in anxiety disorders" is the wrong option.

## 18.5 Tourette's Disorder

**Tourette's disorder** differs from the chapter's other disorders in the **specificity of its symptoms**: they are distinctive and unmistakable. That is why it is the one diagnosis the authors finally exempt from their criticism of psychiatric categories.

### The Case of R.G.: Barking Like a Dog

At **15**, R.G. developed **tics**, which are **involuntary, repetitive, stereotyped movements or vocalisations** (Spitzer et al., 1983). For the **first week** they were **involuntary blinking**. Then they spread to other parts of the body, especially his **arms and legs**. His family was **religious**, so it was especially distressing when the tics became **verbal**: he began **cursing repeatedly and involuntarily** (common in Tourette's and several other disorders), then **barking like a dog**, and finally **echolalia**. When his mother said "Dinner is ready", he answered "Is ready, is ready."

He had been a **top student**, happy and outgoing. Once the tics began he was **jeered at, imitated and ridiculed**, became **anxious, depressed and withdrawn**, and his grades fell sharply. A psychiatrist **diagnosed him easily**, and **medication eliminated 99 percent of his symptoms**. His **anxiety and depression then lifted** and he returned to his old self. The sequence is the point: the anxiety and depression came **after** the ridicule and **lifted after** the tics were controlled, so they came from other people's reactions, not from the neurology.

An extreme case, with obscene gestures and barking, can make social life and employment almost impossible however kind and intelligent the person is (Smith, Fox, & Trayner, 2015). With understanding friends, family and colleagues, people with Tourette's can live happy, productive lives. The book's example is **Tim Howard**, a goalkeeper with Tourette's who plays for **Memphis 901 FC** (USL Championship).

### What Is Tourette's Disorder?

Tourette's disorder is a **disorder of tics**. It usually begins in **childhood or early adolescence** with **simple motor tics** (**eye blinking, head movements**). The symptoms become **more complex and severe** with age, typically **peak after a few years**, and then **gradually subside** as the patient matures (Cox, Seri, & Cavanna, 2018).

- **Complex motor tics:** hitting, touching objects, squatting, hopping, twirling, sometimes lewd gestures.
- **Verbal tics:** **inarticulate sounds** (barking, coughing, grunting), **coprolalia** (uttering obscenities), **echolalia** (repeating **another's** words), **palilalia** (repeating **one's own** words).

**Epidemiology.** Tourette's occurs in **0.3 to 1 percent of the global population** (Serajee & Huq, 2015). It is **four times more frequent in male children** (Hallett, 2015), but the sex difference is **smaller in adults** (Jackson et al., 2015). Some patients also show **ADHD, OCD or both**. R.G. was **obsessed with odd numbers** and refused to sit in **even-numbered seats**.

> **Trap.** Older editions said "Tourette **syndrome**", about **0.7 percent**, **3:1** males, twin concordance about **55 versus 8 percent**. The 11th edition says **Tourette's disorder**, **0.3 to 1 percent**, **4:1 in male children** (less in adults), twins **80 versus 20 percent**. It is named after **Gilles de la Tourette** (1885). The DSM-5 requires **multiple motor tics plus at least one vocal tic**, for **more than a year**, with **onset before 18**. **Coprolalia is not required.** Keep **echolalia** (seen in both Lena and R.G.) apart from **palilalia**.

#### Tic suppression and the rebound myth

Tics are involuntary but can be **temporarily suppressed** with concentration and effort. **Many medical professionals believe suppression is inevitably followed by a rebound**, with tics worse than before (e.g., Novotny, Valis, & Klimova, 2018). **This is not the case** (Figure 18.6).

> **Key study, Himle & Woods (2005), Figure 18.6.** Children with Tourette's were observed through five consecutive **5-minute periods**: **baseline, first suppression, first post-suppression, second suppression, second post-suppression**. Tics were scored as the **percentage of 10-second intervals with at least one tic**. Reading from the figure: baseline about **50 percent**; each suppression period about **20 percent**; each post-suppression period in the **mid-to-high 30s**, still **below baseline**. **No rebound above baseline** followed either suppression.

The baseline period is the yardstick for the rebound prediction ("above baseline after suppression"), and the second suppression cycle checks whether any rebound builds up with repetition. Neither showed a rebound. Clinically, this means children need not be discouraged from suppressing tics.

### Pharmacological Treatment of Tourette's Disorder

Treatment usually starts with things other than the tics, in a fixed order: **(1) education** of the patient, family, friends and teachers; **(2) the ancillary emotional problems** (anxiety, depression); **(3) only then the tics**. R.G.'s case shows why: much of the suffering comes from other people's reactions.

Tics are usually treated with **antipsychotics** (Kim et al., 2018; Quezada & Coffman, 2018), though **behavioural interventions can also be effective** (Pringsheim et al., 2019). Antipsychotics **reduce tics by about 70 percent**, but patients or caregivers often **refuse them because of side effects** (**weight gain, fatigue, dry mouth**). Their success fits the hypothesis that Tourette's involves the **cortical-striatal-thalamic-cortical circuit**, which **relies heavily on dopaminergic signalling** (Jackson et al., 2015). This is the same backwards-from-the-drug reasoning used for schizophrenia.

> **Trap.** **About 70 percent** = average tic reduction with antipsychotics. **99 percent** = R.G.'s individual relief. The drug class is **antipsychotics** (dopamine blockers).

### Genetic and Epigenetic Mechanisms of Tourette's Disorder

**Monozygotic twins** are both affected **about 80 percent** of the time, **dizygotic twins 20 percent** (Burton et al., 2020), so Tourette's appears **highly heritable**. Yet **no genes have been definitively linked** to it. High heritability shows *that* genes matter, not *which*, especially when many genes each have small effects. **Epigenetic studies have been more productive.** Specific genes, such as **potassium-channel genes**, are **methylated** in people with Tourette's, and **one gene's degree of methylation correlates with tic severity** (Burton et al., 2020). A graded relationship like this is stronger evidence than a simple difference between groups, though still correlational.

### Neural Bases of Tourette's Disorder

A well-defined disorder with observable symptoms should be **easier to study**, but there are obstacles. There is **no strong link to any gene**. The **greatest difficulty** is that symptoms **subside with age**, so patients are rarely under care when they die and there are **few post-mortem studies**. Research therefore depends almost entirely on **brain imaging**, which requires patients to **stay motionless**, the one thing tics make hard.

**Striatum and caudate.** Most research has focused on the **striatum (caudate plus putamen)**. Patients tend to have **smaller striatal volumes** (Jackson et al., 2015). When they **suppress tics**, fMRI shows activity in **both the prefrontal cortex and the caudate** (Thomas & Cavanna, 2013). Presumably the **decision to suppress comes from the prefrontal cortex**, which **acts on the caudate**. So Tourette's is sometimes viewed as a **dysfunctional caudate unable to suppress unwanted movements** (Gagné, 2019).

**Circuit and cortex.** There is also evidence of **dysfunctional dopaminergic and GABAergic signalling in cortical-striatal-thalamic-cortical circuits**. These circuits matter for **motor learning, including habit formation**, and tics resemble habits that have escaped control (Jackson et al., 2015). The differences are not limited to the striatum. An MRI study of children with Tourette's found **thinning of sensorimotor cortex grey matter**, most marked in areas controlling the **face, mouth and larynx** (Jackson et al., 2015). These are the body parts behind the commonest tics.

### The Case of P.H., the Neuroscientist with Tourette's Disorder

**P.H.**, a scientist who **counsels Tourette's patients and their families**, has Tourette's himself (Hollenbeck, 2001). He has lived with it for **more than three decades**, and as a **medical school faculty member** he gives regular lectures on it, attended by students, patients and families. Meeting patients of his own generation surprised him. Most **lacked his thick skin**, and **about half were still in treatment for psychological wounds from childhood**. Their pain came mostly **not from the tics** but from **ridicule and torment** and from the self-righteous advice of well-meaning "clods". In his words, **the malfunction may be in a patient's striatum, but this is more a disorder of the onlooker than of the patient**.

The authors later received an e-mail from a **professor of biological sciences at Purdue University** whose department used the textbook. He thanked them for their coverage of Tourette's but found the case study "a bit eerie". The message began "From one case study to another" and ended "All the best, P.H."

> **Trap.** **R.G.** = 15-year-old who barked (Spitzer et al., 1983), 99 percent relief. **P.H.** = neuroscientist (Hollenbeck, 2001), "**disorder of the onlooker**", about half his generation still in treatment. **Tim Howard** = goalkeeper.

## 18.6 Clinical Trials: Development of New Psychotherapeutic Drugs

News reports of exciting discoveries appear almost daily, but most of them come to nothing. **Almost 50 years** after the molecular-biology revolution began, **no form of gene therapy is in widespread use for psychiatric disorders**. Getting from promising basic research to a useful treatment is **complex, slow and expensive**. Research that turns basic discoveries into effective treatments is called **translational research**.

The early discoveries in this chapter came from a **hit-or-miss** era. Drugs were tested on patients with little justification and quickly marketed to an unsuspecting public, often before anyone learned they were dangerous or ineffective. Testing on human volunteers and release for sale are now **strictly regulated by government agencies**. Before any human trial there must be **synthesis** of the drug, **economically efficient methods** of synthesising it, and **evidence from non-human subjects** that it is probably safe and potentially beneficial. This takes **at least 5 years**. Only then may **clinical trials** begin: studies **on volunteers with the disorder** that assess the **therapeutic efficacy** of an untested treatment. Table 18.1 summarises the process.

### Clinical Trials: The Three Phases

Clinical trials run in **three phases** (Zivin, 2000): **(1) screening for safety, (2) establishing the testing protocol, (3) final testing**. If any phase fails, study of the drug usually stops.

#### Phase 1: Screening for safety

Phase 1 asks **whether the drug is safe** for humans and **how much can be tolerated**. Giving a drug to humans for the first time is always risky. The subjects are usually **healthy paid volunteers**. Testing **always starts with tiny injections**, which are **gradually increased**, and reactions are **meticulously monitored**. Testing stops if strong adverse reactions appear. Efficacy is not being tested yet, which is why patients are not needed.

#### Phase 2: Establishing the testing protocol

Phase 2 establishes the **protocol** (conditions) under which the final tests are likely to give a **clear result**: which **doses** work, how **often** and for how **long** to give them, what **benefits** to expect, and **which patients** are likely to be helped. It is run on **volunteer patients with the target disorder**, usually with **placebo-control groups** (patients who receive a control substance instead of the drug), and usually **double-blind**: neither patients nor the physicians dealing with them know who received the drug. Blinding patients removes the effect of their expectations on improvement. Blinding physicians removes the effect of theirs on treatment and ratings.

#### Phase 3: Final testing

Phase 3 is usually a **double-blind, placebo-control study** on **large numbers of patients, often many thousands**, designed from the phase 2 results so that real benefits will show if they exist. The first phase 3 test is often **inconclusive**. If it is promising, a **redesigned** second test may follow. Usually **two independent successful tests** are needed to convince regulators. A test succeeds if **benefits outweigh adverse side effects**.

> **Trap.** **Phase 1 = safety** (healthy volunteers, maximum safe dose). **Phase 2 = protocol** (doses, schedules, who benefits; patients; placebo; double-blind). **Phase 3 = final efficacy testing** (thousands; usually two successes). Preclinical work takes **at least 5 years**. After approval comes "selling to the public" (recovering costs, **continued safety monitoring**), which regulators often call post-marketing surveillance or "phase 4". Pinel's scheme still has only **three** trial phases.

### Controversial Aspects of Clinical Trials

The book lists **six** controversies (Goldacre, 2013; London, Kimmelman, & Carlisle, 2012).

#### 1. The requirement for double-blind design and placebo controls

Patients are assigned **randomly** and do not know their group, so some patients whose **only hope may be the experimental drug** will unknowingly get **placebo**. Companies and regulators accept this, but argue that **no evidence of effectiveness is convincing until a double-blind, placebo-control trial is complete**. Because **psychiatric disorders often improve on placebo**, this design is **essential** for psychotherapeutic drugs. The ethical cost is real and is accepted for the sake of valid evidence.

#### 2. The need for active placebos

The double-blind placebo design is **not the perfect control** it was thought to be (Benedetti, 2014). At therapeutic doses **many drugs have obvious side effects**, so participants on the drug can often **guess correctly that they are not on placebo** (Geuter, Koban, & Wager, 2017). That knowledge **may greatly add to the drug's apparent benefit**, independent of any real therapeutic effect. **Active placebos** are therefore preferred: control drugs with **no therapeutic effect** that produce **side effects similar to the test drug's** (Kirsch, 2019; Shader, 2017). This bears on the antidepressant figures in 18.2. If some of the drug group's improvement came from broken blinding, the true drug effect is **smaller** than the 15 percent gap over inert placebo.

> **Trap.** An **active placebo** is not a low dose of the drug. It has **no therapeutic effect** and **mimics side effects** so the blind holds.

#### 3. Length of time required

Desperate patients are frustrated by how long trials take. Researchers, companies and regulators are trying to **speed evaluation without weakening the safeguards** against ineffective or dangerous treatments. The right compromise is essential.

#### 4. Financial issues

**Drug companies pay** everyone involved in their trials. They spend **billions per candidate drug** (Fleming, 2018), and **only about 22 percent of drugs entering phase 1 ever gain approval** (Figure 18.7). Reading from the figure, roughly three-quarters reach phase 2 and under half reach phase 3. So companies are anxious to recover costs, and **fewer are investing in novel drugs** (Grabb & Gobburu, 2017). Many have **questioned the impartiality** of those running and reporting trials (Normile, 2014; Roest et al., 2015). Scientists complain that sponsors make them sign agreements **forbidding publication or discussion of negative findings** without company consent. **Any new drug will look promising if all negative evidence is suppressed** (Goldacre, 2013), and a meta-analysis of the published trials will then overestimate its effect.

Two more financial problems. Companies rarely develop drugs for **rare disorders** because they are unprofitable. Drugs whose market is **too small to be profitable** are **orphan drugs**, and some governments have passed laws to encourage them. And the cost of trials has created a **translational bottleneck**: **only a small proportion of potentially valuable ideas receive funding** for translational research.

> **Trap.** **Orphan drug** = market **too small to be profitable**, not "unknown mechanism" or "unpatented" (though Cade's unpatentable lithium was held back by the same commercial logic). Approval rate = **22 percent** of drugs entering phase 1.

#### 5. Targets of psychopharmacology

**Hyman and Fenton (2003)** argued that a major obstacle is that drug development aims at **disease entities as currently conceived**, such as DSM-5 categories. Most psychiatric disorders are probably **clusters of disorders with different brain dysfunctions**. An effective drug may therefore **help only a proportion** of patients with a diagnosis, and its **effectiveness may go unrecognised** because averaging dilutes it. The book's "thinking creatively" list includes the corresponding proposal: target **specific measurable symptoms**, not diseases as currently conceived.

#### 6. Lack of diversity

Two related issues have emerged **over the past decade**. Trial volunteers **lack diversity** (Clark et al., 2019; Polo et al., 2019), so we cannot be sure a drug works across **sexes and ethnicities**. Recall that antidepressant efficacy **varies with sex and age**. And **even diverse samples** are usually **pooled in analysis**, which **hides group differences** and invites risky generalisations (Eid, Gobinath, & Galea, 2019).

### Effectiveness of Clinical Trials

Despite the controversies, **the process works**. Zivin wrote:

> "A long, dismal history tells of charlatans who make unfounded promises and take advantage of people at the time when they are least able to care for themselves. The clinical trial process is the most objective method ever devised to assess the efficacy of a treatment. It is expensive and slow, and in need of constant refinements, and oversight, but the process is trustworthy." (Zivin, 2000, p. 75)

Ethical concerns about randomised double-blind placebo studies are **warranted**, but most professionals accept them as **the essential critical test** of a new therapy. This is especially so for psychotherapeutic drugs, because psychiatric disorders **often respond to placebo** (Rutherford & Roose, 2013; Wager & Atlas, 2015), and because ratings of severity can be **greatly influenced by the therapist's expectations**.

Everyone agrees trials cost too much and take too long. **Zivin (2000)**: clinical trials can be **trustworthy, fast, or cheap; but in any one trial, only two of the three are possible**. A fast, cheap trial is too small or short to be trustworthy. A trustworthy, fast trial needs huge enrolment and so is expensive. A trustworthy, cheap trial has to be slow. Trials are **monitored while they run**, and the protocol is changed when results justify it, to cut cost, improve safety or save time. The authors propose a **legal requirement to publish all partial or completed trials**, so that patients, doctors and scientists can see all the evidence.

### Conclusion: The Diagnostic Problem

Ideally, patients in one diagnostic category would share **symptoms, pathology and causes**, and, most important, **respond to the same treatments**. For brain disorders this is rarely achieved, and for psychiatric diagnosis "we have **not even been close**". The authors hope the chapter has shown that psychiatric diagnosis **needs improvement**, with the possible exception of **Tourette's disorder** (Demkow & Wolańczyk, 2017; Nusslock & Alloy, 2017). For schizophrenia, depressive disorders, bipolar disorder and anxiety disorders, **two people with the same diagnosis may share few symptoms**. **Many genetic and environmental factors** are implicated in each. **Neural changes vary widely**. And, most problematic, **many patients do not substantially improve** despite many drugs with several mechanisms. **Each diagnosis seems to contain several disorders**, and research and treatment would benefit from distinguishing them.

### Conclusion of the Case of S.B.: The Biopsychology Student Who Took Control

The chapter, and the book, ends with S.B., last seen committed in a mixed state with **bipolar I**. Heavily sedated with **benzodiazepines**, he slept through most of his **first week** on the ward. Then the resident psychiatrist told him he would be put on a **particular mood stabiliser**, probably **for life**. Two things bothered him. **Many patients on the ward took this drug and looked like zombies.** And **the psychiatrist seemed to know less about the drug than he did.** He asked to use the **hospital library**. He found that the favoured drug had been shown **several months earlier** to be **no more effective than placebo** in the **long-term** treatment of bipolar disorder. He also found that a **new drug that had recently cleared clinical trials** was proving **effective with fewer side effects**. When he showed the resident this evidence, the psychiatrist was surprised and **prescribed the new drug**.

S.B. recovered, finished graduate school, and at the time of writing holds a **faculty position at a leading Canadian university**. **S.B. is Steven Barnes**, the book's co-author. He still has mood episodes, but they are **far less severe** than before he started a mood stabiliser, and he manages residual symptoms with **pharmacological and psychosocial methods** (Geddes & Miklowitz, 2013; Murray et al., 2011). The ending draws on the module just finished. His recovery depended on placebo-controlled trial evidence that existed, was published, and was read. Note too that he did not reject medication, as he had as an undergraduate. He chose a better-supported drug and added psychosocial methods.

> **Trap.** **S.B. = Steven Barnes**. Endogenous depression with delusions (refused drugs, then hospitalised on an antidepressant plus antipsychotic) → **bipolar II** (hypomania, under 3 hours' sleep) → **bipolar I** (mania, under 2 hours, a "theory of everything") → **mixed state** (6 weeks on a ward) → library → a **newer mood stabiliser** that had recently cleared trials.

### Themes Revisited

**Clinical implications** dominated the chapter. **Thinking creatively** appeared in four ideas: anxiety models may be **models of benzodiazepine effects**; **active placebos** are needed; treatments should target **specific measurable symptoms**; and drug companies should be **forced to publish negative findings**. The **evolutionary perspective** appeared **twice**: in animal models of anxiety, and in the role of **non-human research** in clearing drugs for human trials. **Neuroplasticity** appeared substantively **once**, in the **neuroplasticity theory of depression**. Of the emerging themes, **epigenetics** was **explicit** throughout, and **consciousness** was **implicit**, since many psychiatric disorders alter consciousness.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Accident first, theory second.** Chlorpromazine (an antihistamine), reserpine (Indian folk medicine), iproniazid (a tuberculosis drug), imipramine (a failed antipsychotic) and lithium (guinea-pig urine) were all found by luck. Theories were then reasoned **backwards** from the drug's synaptic action: dopamine excess for schizophrenia, monoamine deficit for depression, GABA and serotonin for anxiety, dopamine circuitry for Tourette's. When the drug's mechanism is unknown, as with lithium, this strategy stalls.
2. **Hours versus weeks.** Antipsychotics block D2 within hours, and antidepressants raise monoamines almost immediately, yet both take weeks to help. The delay shows the synaptic action only **starts** something slower. For depression, the neuroplasticity theory proposes what that is (neurotrophins, BDNF, neurogenesis). Ketamine's rapid, non-monoamine action is the exception that confirms the point.
3. **Revision under evidence.** The dopamine theory had four versions, each forced by an experiment. The monoamine theory was challenged on three fronts, and the mesocorticolimbic locus has given way to nigrostriatal findings.
4. **Compared with what?** 50 percent improve on antidepressants, but 35 percent improve on control. rTMS convinces because it beats **sham**. Lozano's uncontrolled DBS result relies on replication. Trials need double-blind placebo control, and **active** placebos, because psychiatric disorders and clinicians' ratings both respond to expectation.
5. **Many genes, then epigenetics.** Every disorder has the same pattern: many genes, no single causal gene, a move to epigenetics. Tourette's sharpens it: **80 versus 20 percent** twin concordance, yet no definitive gene, while methylation tracks tic severity.
6. **Categories too broad.** Every module ends the same way: schizophrenia "lumps together" disorders; the DSM-5 is "misguiding research" in depression; S.B. moves through three diagnoses; anxiety research should study symptoms; Hyman and Fenton's drugs go unrecognised. Only **Tourette's**, with its specific symptoms, escapes.

## Worked example

*A 24-year-old postgraduate student is brought to a clinic in India. For three weeks she has slept two to three hours a night, talks rapidly and jumps between topics, says she has discovered "the hidden mathematics behind all religions", and has spent her semester's fees on books. Eighteen months ago she had two months of low mood, oversleeping and thoughts of death, diagnosed as depression and treated with an SSRI. Her maternal uncle was once hospitalised for a mental illness. A junior doctor proposes restarting the SSRI "since she has a history of depression", and wonders about schizophrenia because of the grandiose belief.*

**Current state.** Reduced need for sleep, topic-jumping speech, grandiosity and reckless spending are **mania** as 18.3 describes it ("incessant chatter that hurtles from topic to topic", "unpaid bills"). Her belief is a **delusion of grandeur**, like S.B.'s theory of society. Mania "usually involves psychosis", so a grandiose delusion does not by itself point to schizophrenia.

**Diagnosis.** Depression followed by mania = **bipolar disorder type I**. The earlier depression diagnosis was **provisional**, like S.B.'s, because no manic episode had happened yet. Her episodes lasted weeks to months, the typical pattern. Rapid cycling would need **4 or more episodes a year**.

**Schizophrenia?** She arguably meets the book's two-symptom rule (a delusion plus disorganised speech). But the delusion is mood-congruent, it occurs within a clear manic syndrome, and there is a previous depressive episode. This is the symptom overlap the opening section warns about, and the **mood history** is what separates the two.

**The SSRI.** A mood stabiliser treats one pole **without raising the risk of the other**. An antidepressant alone may do the reverse. The chapter's answer is a **mood stabiliser** (lithium, certain anticonvulsants, certain atypical antipsychotics), **all of which act against mania**. **Lithium** is still considered by many the best and is **anti-suicidal**, which matters given bipolar disorder's very high suicide rate.

**Adherence.** Side effects (**weight gain, tremor, blurred vision, dizziness**) are the main reason patients stop, and **untreated episodes grow more severe and frequent**. So adherence has to be planned from the start.

**Family history.** It raises the probability of a heritable mood disorder, but with **hundreds to thousands of genes** involved no single test helps.

**Future risk.** The **reward hypersensitivity theory** predicts that goal-related events (success, deadlines, rejection) may trigger episodes, through escalating goal-seeking or a collapse of reward-seeking. Her sleep loss reflects the **circadian disruption** seen in bipolar disorder. Both point to psychosocial management of sleep and goals alongside the drug, as in S.B.'s own approach.

**Evidence.** Like S.B., she is entitled to ask whether the drug offered has beaten placebo in the long term. Approval means **two successful phase 3 trials** with benefits outweighing harms, and the evidence is only as complete as what gets published.

## MCQ traps

1. **"Splitting"** in schizophrenia = splitting of psychic functions (emotion, thought, action), **not split personality**; keys expect **Bleuler** as the coiner.
2. **Prevalence:** schizophrenia **1 percent** (all cultures); depression **2 to 5 percent**; bipolar **3 percent**; anxiety disorders **14 to 34 percent** (most prevalent); Tourette's **0.3 to 1 percent**.
3. **Book's schizophrenia rule:** any **two** symptoms for **1 month**, one being **delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech**.
4. **Catatonia** is a **negative** symptom in Pinel. Lena: **waxy flexibility**, **echolalia**, "schizophrenia **with catatonia**" (DSM-5 abolished subtypes).
5. **Chlorpromazine:** French **antihistamine**; calming effect noticed by a **surgeon in 1950**; calms the agitated **and** activates the blunted; does **not cure**.
6. **Reserpine:** from **snakeroot** (used in **India**); depletes monoamines by **breaking down vesicles**; abandoned for **dangerous hypotension**.
7. Both drugs: effect after **2 to 3 weeks**, plus **parkinsonian** side effects.
8. **1960:** Parkinson's striatum **dopamine-depleted** → original theory = **too much dopamine**.
9. **Carlsson & Lindqvist (1963):** dopamine **unchanged**, metabolites **raised** → chlorpromazine is the **first receptor blocker** identified → too much **receptor activity**.
10. **Creese, Burt & Snyder (1976):** radioactive dopamine on **calf striatum**; **haloperidol** potent but apparently low-affinity.
11. **Phenothiazines** (chlorpromazine) bind **D1 + D2**; **butyrophenones** (haloperidol, spiroperidol) bind **D2 only**; **five** dopamine receptor subtypes.
12. **Snyder (1978):** D2 binding correlates with potency; **spiroperidol** highest. **Potency is not efficacy.**
13. The D2 theory failed on the **hours-versus-weeks lag** and on **negative symptoms**. Current version: D2 is **one factor among many**.
14. **Clozapine** = **first atypical**: **D1, D4, serotonin, histamine**, only **slight D2**.
15. **LSD (1943)** = 5-HT2A **agonist** → positive symptoms; **risperidone** = 5-HT2A **antagonist**; **ketamine/PCP** = glutamate antagonists → **negative** symptoms.
16. **Famines:** **Dutch 1944 to 1945**, **Chinese 1959 to 1961**; **prenatal** exposure → schizophrenia → **neurodevelopmental** theory.
17. **Classic** theory = **mesocorticolimbic**; **newer** neuropathology = **nigrostriatal**.
18. **MDD** = **2 weeks or more**. **Reactive** = obvious trigger; **endogenous** = no cause (S.B.). Females **2:1**, most marked in **adolescence**; suicide **4 to 15 percent**.
19. **SAD:** **Alaska 9 vs Florida 1 percent**; **15 to 30 minutes** of bright morning light. **Peripartum** depression: **13 percent of pregnancies**.
20. **Iproniazid:** first antidepressant, failed **tuberculosis** drug, marketed **1957**, MAOI. **Cheese effect** = **tyramine** → hypertension → stroke.
21. **Imipramine:** first tricyclic, first thought an **antipsychotic**; blocks **5-HT and NE** reuptake; safer than MAOIs.
22. **Fluoxetine (Prozac):** first SSRI, **late 1980s**, **no more effective than imipramine**; popular for fewer side effects and broad use.
23. **SNRI** in Pinel = **norepinephrine**-selective (**reboxetine**); **venlafaxine** blocks more than one monoamine; **bupropion** blocks DA/NE reuptake and **nicotinic** receptors; **agomelatine** = melatonin agonist.
24. **Ketamine:** **single low dose**, **rapid** antidepressant effect (NMDA antagonist).
25. **Antidepressants:** **50 vs 35 percent** → about **15 percent** truly helped; **$15 billion** a year (USA).
26. **rTMS:** **high frequency (5/s) stimulates**, **low (under 1/s) inhibits**; both beat **sham**.
27. **Lozano et al. (2008):** **20** patients; **anterior cingulate** white matter; **60 percent** improved, **35 percent** symptom-free, **1 year**.
28. **Monoamine theory:** **up-regulated** receptors in **untreated** depressed brains → **reduced release**; challenged by poor efficacy, the lag, and **GABA/glutamate/ACh**.
29. **Neuroplasticity theory:** BDNF rises **only in patients who improve**; low blood BDNF a possible **biomarker**.
30. **Depression:** grey-matter **atrophy** in PFC, hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate. **Anxiety:** same structures, **no significant atrophy**.
31. **Bipolar I** needs **mania** (even mania alone); **II** = hypomania + depression. **Mixed state** = both at once. **Rapid cycling** = **4 or more a year**.
32. **Cade:** Australian; guinea pigs; urine; lithium **urate then carbonate**; "calm" = **nauseated**; **10 manic, 6 schizophrenic, 3 depressed**, helped **only mania**; book **1954**, histories **1949**; unpatentable, accepted **late 1960s**.
33. **Mood stabilisers:** lithium, certain **anticonvulsants**, certain **atypical antipsychotics**; **all** act against **mania**.
34. **Reward hypersensitivity** (Alloy, Nusslock & Boland, 2015): **euthymic** patients take more risks; reward circuits overactive in **both** poles.
35. **Anxiety disorders (11th ed.):** **GAD, specific phobia, agoraphobia, panic disorder**; **not OCD or PTSD**. M.R. = **agoraphobia with panic attacks**.
36. **Benzodiazepines:** **Librium = chlordiazepoxide**, **Valium = diazepam**; **GABA-A**; **5 percent** of adult North Americans; addictive; **rebound anxiety**.
37. **Comorbid anxiety:** **47 percent** of bipolar, **53 percent** of MDD. **Pregabalin:** **calcium channels**, best for **GAD** (older editions: buspirone).
38. **Animal models:** plus maze (**50 cm**, **enclosed-arm time**), defensive burying (**dowel shock**, bedding-spraying time), risk assessment (**cat**, freezing). Validated with **benzodiazepines** → may model only benzodiazepine-sensitive anxiety.
39. **Tourette's:** **4:1 male children**; **coprolalia/echolalia/palilalia**; **no rebound** after suppression (Himle & Woods, 2005); antipsychotics cut tics about **70 percent**; R.G. **99 percent**.
40. **Tourette's twins:** **80 vs 20 percent**, **no gene** found; potassium-channel gene **methylation**; smaller **striatum**; **PFC + caudate** in suppression; sensorimotor thinning for **face, mouth, larynx**.
41. **Trial phases:** **1 safety** (healthy volunteers), **2 protocol**, **3 final testing** (thousands; **two** successes); preclinical **at least 5 years**; **22 percent** approved.
42. **Active placebo** = no therapeutic effect, **mimics side effects**. **Orphan drug** = unprofitable market. **Zivin:** trustworthy, fast, cheap, **pick two**. **S.B. = Steven Barnes.**

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Psychiatric disorders** | Disorders of psychological function severe enough to need treatment |
| **DSM-5** | Current *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual* of the American Psychiatric Association |
| **Schizophrenia spectrum disorders** | DSM-5 label for schizophrenia and related, overlapping disorders |
| **Positive symptoms** | Excess of typical function: delusions, hallucinations, inappropriate affect, disorganised speech/thought, odd behaviour |
| **Negative symptoms** | Loss of typical function: affective flattening, avolition, catatonia |
| **Catatonia / waxy flexibility** | Prolonged motionlessness in awkward positions / mannequin-like holding of imposed postures |
| **Echolalia** | Vocal repetition of what has just been heard |
| **Antipsychotic drug** | Drug meant to treat certain symptoms of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder |
| **Psychosis** | A loss of touch with reality |
| **Chlorpromazine** | First antipsychotic; former antihistamine; phenothiazine; first receptor blocker identified |
| **Reserpine** | Snakeroot alkaloid that depletes monoamines; abandoned for dangerous hypotension |
| **Dopamine theory of schizophrenia** | Schizophrenia caused by too much dopamine (later D2 activity; now one factor among many) |
| **Metabolites** | Substances created by the breakdown of another substance in cells |
| **Receptor blocker** | Binds a receptor without activating it, preventing the transmitter from doing so |
| **Haloperidol** | Potent butyrophenone binding D2 but not D1 |
| **Phenothiazines / butyrophenones** | Antipsychotic classes binding D1 and D2 / D2 only |
| **Typical antipsychotics** | First-generation antipsychotics; D2 binding correlates with potency |
| **Atypical antipsychotics** | Second-generation antipsychotics effective without strong D2 binding |
| **Clozapine** | First atypical; binds D1, D4, serotonin, histamine receptors, slight D2 |
| **Psychedelic drugs** | Drugs whose primary action is to alter perception, emotion and cognition |
| **Lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD)** | Classical hallucinogen (1943); 5-HT2A agonist mimicking positive symptoms |
| **Neurodevelopmental theory** | Early adversity disturbs brain development in genetically susceptible people, producing schizophrenia |
| **Functional connectivity** | Co-variation of activity between brain regions (intrinsic = at rest) |
| **Anhedonia** | Loss of the capacity to experience pleasure |
| **Clinical depression (major depressive disorder)** | Depression or anhedonia lasting 2 weeks or more and disrupting life |
| **Reactive / endogenous depression** | Triggered by an obvious negative experience / no apparent cause |
| **Comorbid** | Tendency of two health conditions to occur together in one individual |
| **Seasonal affective disorder (SAD)** | Depression recurring in particular seasons, usually winter |
| **Peripartum depression** | Sustained depression during pregnancy, after birth, or both |
| **Iproniazid** | First antidepressant; former tuberculosis drug; MAO inhibitor (1957) |
| **MAO inhibitors** | Block monoamine oxidase, the enzyme degrading monoamines in the cytoplasm |
| **Cheese effect** | Blood-pressure surge from tyramine-rich foods in people taking MAO inhibitors |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | Three-ring drugs blocking serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake |
| **Imipramine** | First tricyclic; initially thought to be an antipsychotic |
| **Selective serotonin-reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** | Serotonin agonists that block serotonin reuptake |
| **Fluoxetine** | Prozac; first SSRI; no more effective than imipramine |
| **Selective norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)** | Block norepinephrine reuptake (reboxetine) |
| **Atypical antidepressants** | Catch-all class with varied mechanisms (bupropion, agomelatine) |
| **Ketamine** | Dissociative hallucinogen, NMDA antagonist, rapid antidepressant |
| **Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)** | Non-invasive repeated magnetic pulses; high frequency stimulates, low inhibits |
| **Deep brain stimulation** | Chronic stimulation through implanted electrodes |
| **Monoamine theory of depression** | Depression = underactivity at serotonergic and noradrenergic synapses |
| **Up-regulation** | Compensatory increase in receptors when too little transmitter is released |
| **Neuroplasticity theory of depression** | Depression results from decreased neuroplastic processes and neuron loss |
| **BDNF** | Brain-derived neurotrophic factor; rises only in patients who improve |
| **Biomarker** | A biological state predictive of a particular disorder |
| **Bipolar disorder** | Episodes of depression plus hypomania or mania |
| **Hypomania** | Reduced need for sleep, high energy, positive affect |
| **Mania** | Extreme hypomania plus grandiosity, impulsivity, distractibility; usually psychotic |
| **Bipolar disorder type II / type I** | Depression and hypomania only / mania (with or without depression) |
| **Rapid cycling** | Four or more mood episodes per year |
| **Mixed state** | Simultaneous severe depression and mania |
| **Mood stabilisers** | Treat one pole without raising the risk of the other; all act against mania |
| **Lithium** | Metallic ion; first mood stabiliser (Cade); neuroprotective, anti-suicidal |
| **Euthymic** | No current symptoms of depression, hypomania or mania |
| **Reward hypersensitivity theory** | Bipolar disorder reflects a reward system over-reacting to reward and its absence |
| **Anxiety** | Chronic fear persisting in the absence of any direct threat |
| **Anxiety disorder** | Anxiety severe enough to disrupt functioning |
| **Generalised anxiety disorder** | Extreme worry about many different activities or events |
| **Specific phobia** | Strong fear of particular objects or situations |
| **Agoraphobia** | Pathological fear of public places and open spaces |
| **Panic disorder / panic attacks** | Recurrent rapid-onset attacks of extreme fear / the attacks themselves |
| **Benzodiazepines** | GABA-A-acting anxiolytics (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam); addictive |
| **Hypnotics / ataxia** | Sleep-inducing drugs / disruption of motor activity |
| **Anxiolytic drugs** | Antianxiety drugs |
| **Pregabalin** | Anxiolytic acting on voltage-gated calcium channels; best for GAD |
| **Elevated-plus-maze test** | Anxiety = proportion of time in enclosed arms of a raised plus maze |
| **Defensive-burying test** | Anxiety = time spraying bedding at a shock-delivering dowel |
| **Risk-assessment test** | Anxiety = freezing and risk-assessment time after brief cat exposure |
| **Tourette's disorder** | Disorder of tics beginning in childhood or early adolescence |
| **Tics** | Involuntary, repetitive, stereotyped movements or vocalisations |
| **Coprolalia / palilalia** | Uttering obscenities / repeating one's own words |
| **Cortical-striatal-thalamic-cortical circuit** | Dopamine-dependent habit circuit implicated in Tourette's |
| **Translational research** | Turning basic discoveries into effective clinical treatments |
| **Clinical trials** | Studies on volunteers with the disorder testing an untested treatment |
| **Double-blind / placebo control** | Neither patients nor physicians know the treatment / patients given a control substance |
| **Active placebos** | Control drugs with no therapeutic effect but similar side effects |
| **Orphan drugs** | Drugs whose market is too small to be profitable |
| **Translational bottleneck** | Only a small proportion of promising ideas get translational funding |

## Likely exam points

1. **Two diagnostic difficulties** (same disorder, different symptoms; different disorders, same symptoms); diagnosis by symptom profile under DSM-5.
2. **Schizophrenia**: meaning, 1 percent in all cultures, onset, schizophrenia spectrum disorders; positive versus negative symptoms; the book's two-symptom, 1-month rule.
3. **Lena**: maternal schizophrenia, catatonia, waxy flexibility, echolalia.
4. **Chlorpromazine** (antihistamine, French surgeon, 1950) and **reserpine** (Indian snakeroot, hypotension); their two shared features.
5. **Four versions of the dopamine theory** with the evidence for each, including the logic of **Carlsson & Lindqvist (1963)** and the **haloperidol puzzle**.
6. **Phenothiazines versus butyrophenones**; Snyder's D2 correlation; spiroperidol.
7. **Atypical antipsychotics**, clozapine, and the three psychedelic conclusions (LSD/5-HT2A, risperidone, ketamine/glutamate).
8. **Neurodevelopmental theory**: early factors, ASD overlap, the Dutch and Chinese famine cohorts, epigenetics in prefrontal neurons.
9. **Neural bases of schizophrenia**: four meta-analytic findings; nigrostriatal versus mesocorticolimbic; connectivity; diagnosis lumps several disorders.
10. **Depression**: anhedonia, 2-week rule, reactive versus endogenous, 2 to 5 percent, 2:1 female, 4 to 15 percent suicide, comorbidity; **SAD** and **peripartum** depression.
11. **Five antidepressant classes** with examples, mechanisms and the accidental discoveries (iproniazid, imipramine); the **cheese effect**.
12. **SSRIs** versus tricyclics (no more effective; fewer side effects, broader use); SNRIs; atypicals; ketamine's rapid action.
13. **Antidepressant efficacy**: 50 versus 35 percent, about 15 percent truly helped.
14. **rTMS** (high stimulates, low inhibits, beats sham) and **Lozano et al. (2008)** DBS (20; 60; 35; 1 year).
15. **Monoamine theory**: basis, up-regulation, three challenges.
16. **Neuroplasticity theory**: explains the lag; neurotrophins, neurogenesis, synaptogenesis; **BDNF** as a biomarker.
17. **Neural bases of depression**: grey-matter reductions in PFC, hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate; "not much better than 50 years ago".
18. **Bipolar disorder**: hypomania versus mania; type I versus II; mixed state; rapid cycling; episodes last weeks to months; 3 percent; high suicide.
19. **S.B.**: depression → bipolar II → bipolar I → mixed state → library → newer drug; S.B. = Steven Barnes.
20. **Cade and lithium**: the experimental chain, the 10/6/3 trial, the nausea artefact, the patent problem, late-1960s acceptance, the 1954/1949 date.
21. **Mood stabilisers**: definition, three families, all anti-manic, side effects and non-adherence, link to epilepsy.
22. **Theories of bipolar disorder**: HPA, circadian (also in relatives), neurotransmitters, BDNF; **reward hypersensitivity theory** and euthymic risk-taking.
23. **Anxiety**: definition, most prevalent (14 to 34 percent), four disorders (not OCD or PTSD), M.R.
24. **Three anxiety drug types**: benzodiazepines (GABA-A, 5 percent, addiction), antidepressants (47/53 percent comorbidity), pregabalin (calcium channels, GAD).
25. **Three animal models**, their measures, benzodiazepine validation and its circularity.
26. **Anxiety versus depression in the brain**: same structures; atrophy only in depression; distinct connectivity per anxiety disorder.
27. **Tourette's**: tic types, prevalence, 4:1 boys, ADHD/OCD; R.G.; Tim Howard.
28. **No tic rebound** after suppression (Himle & Woods, 2005).
29. **Tourette's treatment and biology**: education first, antipsychotics about 70 percent; twins 80/20 with no gene; methylation; striatum, caudate, CSTC circuit, sensorimotor thinning; P.H. and the "disorder of the onlooker".
30. **Clinical trials**: translational research, three phases, six controversies, 22 percent approval, active placebos, orphan drugs, Hyman & Fenton, Zivin's "two of three"; the closing argument that each diagnosis contains several disorders, Tourette's excepted.
