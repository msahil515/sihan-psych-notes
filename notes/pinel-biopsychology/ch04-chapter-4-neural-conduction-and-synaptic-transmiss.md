# Chapter 4: Neural Conduction and Synaptic Transmission

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 3 gave you the parts list of the nervous system. This chapter tells you how the parts talk. Every thought, perception, memory and movement you will meet in the rest of the book is, at bottom, a pattern of signals passing within neurons and between them, and there are only two kinds of signal to understand. *Within* a neuron the signal is **electrical**: small graded voltage changes called **postsynaptic potentials** spread across the dendrites and cell body, and if enough of them add up at the start of the axon they trigger a large, stereotyped, all-or-none **action potential** that runs the length of the axon. *Between* neurons the signal is usually **chemical**: the action potential arriving at the terminal button releases **neurotransmitter** molecules that cross the synapse and bind to **receptors** on the next cell, where they produce new postsynaptic potentials, and the cycle begins again. The chapter is the story of that conversion, electrical to chemical and back to electrical, told step by step.

The chapter opens with a patient for a reason. Almost every drug that changes mood, thought or behaviour, from L-dopa and antipsychotics to nicotine, morphine and Botox, acts on one specific step of synaptic transmission. Know the steps and you can predict what a drug will do, why it has its side effects, and why some obvious treatments (giving dopamine to a patient who lacks dopamine, for example) fail.

**How to read this chapter.** The 11th edition organises the chapter into six modules, numbered here 4.1 to 4.6. Section 4.1 explains the **resting membrane potential**, the baseline that every signal disturbs. Section 4.2 covers **postsynaptic potentials** and how a neuron *integrates* thousands of them to decide whether to fire. Section 4.3 is the **action potential**: its ionic basis, refractory periods, and how it is conducted along unmyelinated and myelinated axons. Section 4.4 crosses the synapse: structure, synthesis, release, receptors, and how transmission is switched off. Section 4.5 is a field guide to the **neurotransmitter classes**. Section 4.6 shows how **drugs** act on synaptic transmission, through three historically important lines of research. Sections 4.1 to 4.3 are a single chain of reasoning, each step depending on the one before, so read them in order and do not skip the ionic detail; most of the numerical MCQs in this chapter come from those three sections.

## The Lizard: A Case of Parkinson's Disease

The chapter's opening case is taken from *Newton's Madness* by the neurologist **Harold Klawans** (1990).

"I have become a lizard," the patient began. "A great lizard frozen in a dark, cold, strange world."

His name was **Roberto Garcia d'Orta**. He was a tall, thin man in his sixties, but like most patients with **Parkinson's disease** he looked much older than his actual age. Not many years before, he had been an active, vigorous businessman. Then the disease arrived, not all at once and not suddenly, but slowly, subtly, insidiously. By the time Klawans saw him he turned like a piece of granite, walked in slow shuffling steps, and spoke in a monotonous whisper.

His first symptom had been a **tremor**. It had not been disabling. "My hands shake worse when they are doing nothing at all," he said, which describes the symptom called **tremor-at-rest**: a tremor that is most pronounced when the limb is idle and tends to diminish during purposeful movement.

The other symptoms of Parkinson's disease are less benign, and they are what turned a vigorous man into a "lizard":

- **rigid muscles**;
- a marked **poverty of spontaneous movements**;
- **difficulty in starting to move**; and
- **slowness in executing voluntary movements** once they have been initiated.

The term **reptilian stare** describes the characteristic lack of blinking and the widely opened eyes gazing out of a motionless face, a set of features that seems more reptilian than human.

> **Trap.** Pinel describes the movement symptoms in plain language. Clinical papers and many Indian question setters use the technical terms instead: rigidity (rigid muscles), **akinesia** or hypokinesia (poverty of spontaneous movement and difficulty initiating it), and **bradykinesia** (slowness of movement once begun). The tremor of Parkinson's disease is a **resting** tremor, worse at rest. An option describing an "intention tremor" (worse during purposeful movement) is describing a different, cerebellar, kind of tremor.

### What was happening in Mr. d'Orta's brain

A group of neurons called the **substantia nigra** ("black substance") were dying, for no known reason. These neurons make a particular chemical, **dopamine**, which they deliver to another part of the brain, the **striatum**. As the cells of the substantia nigra die, the amount of dopamine they can deliver to the striatum falls. The striatum helps control movement, and to do that normally it needs dopamine. Parkinson's disease is therefore, at the level this chapter cares about, a failure of one particular kind of synaptic transmission: too little of one neurotransmitter reaching one target structure.

### Why L-dopa, and not dopamine

The obvious remedy, giving the patient dopamine, does not work. Dopamine is not an effective treatment because it **does not readily penetrate the blood-brain barrier** (the mechanism, met in Chapter 3, that keeps many bloodborne chemicals out of brain tissue). Knowledge of how dopamine is made, however, led to a treatment that does work: **L-dopa**, the **chemical precursor of dopamine**. L-dopa readily penetrates the blood-brain barrier and, once inside the brain, is **converted to dopamine**.

Mr. d'Orta's neurologist prescribed L-dopa, and it worked. He still had a slight tremor, but his voice became stronger, his feet no longer shuffled, his reptilian stare faded, and he was again able to perform many of the activities of daily life with ease: eating, bathing, writing, speaking, even having sex with his wife. He had seemed destined to spend the rest of his life trapped in a body that was increasingly difficult to control, but his life sentence was repealed, as the book puts it, "at least temporarily".

Notice the qualification. L-dopa replaces the missing transmitter; it does not stop the substantia nigra neurons from dying. It is standard clinical knowledge that its benefits tend to wane and side effects tend to accumulate with years of use, which is why Pinel says the reprieve was temporary.

> **Trap.** "Dopamine cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so L-dopa is given because it **is** dopamine in a different form" is wrong in a way examiners like. L-dopa is the **precursor** from which dopamine is synthesised (you will see the exact synthetic pathway in 4.5: tyrosine to L-dopa to dopamine). It crosses the barrier and is converted to dopamine **inside the brain**. In the language of 4.6, L-dopa is a dopamine **agonist** that works by **increasing synthesis**.

Mr. d'Orta's story does not end here; the chapter returns to Parkinson's disease in its final module, where it turns out to have been the key to discovering the first antipsychotic drugs. For now, the case makes Pinel's point (citing Südhof, 2017): you cannot understand his illness, or his treatment, without knowing the fundamentals of neural conduction and synaptic transmission.

### Neural communication as a social network

Pinel frames the whole chapter with a modern metaphor, a social network such as **Twitter**. When one person tweets a message, it is carried to the people who follow them. If the message is not compelling enough, it is lost in the void. If it is compelling, some of those people retweet it, propagating it to more people in their own networks, and so on outward. A neuron behaves in the same way within its network: when it sends a message to the cells it contacts, some of them will pass the message on, **if the message is strong enough**; if it is not, the message dies out.

Keep the phrase "if the message is strong enough" in mind. The whole of 4.2 is about exactly what "strong enough" means for a neuron, and the answer is the **threshold of excitation**.

## 4.1 Resting Membrane Potential

To understand how a message is conducted *within* a neuron, or passed *from* one neuron to the next, you first need the concept of the **membrane potential**: the **difference in electrical charge between the inside and the outside of a cell**. Every signal in this chapter is a change in the membrane potential, so the starting point is the value it holds when nothing is happening.

### Recording the membrane potential

To record a neuron's membrane potential you need two electrodes: the tip of one is positioned **inside** the neuron, and the tip of the other sits **outside** it, in the extracellular fluid. The size of the extracellular electrode is not critical. The intracellular electrode is the hard part, because its tip must be fine enough to pierce the neural membrane without damaging it. Intracellular electrodes are therefore **microelectrodes**, with tips **less than one-thousandth of a millimetre** in diameter, far too small to be seen with the naked eye.

The logic of the measurement is simple and worth stating because it defines what the numbers mean:

1. When **both** electrode tips are in the extracellular fluid, the voltage difference between them is **zero**. There is no charge difference between two points in the same fluid.
2. When the intracellular tip is inserted into a neuron **at rest** (not receiving signals from other cells), a steady potential of about **-70 millivolts (mV)** is recorded.

The minus sign means that the inside of the resting neuron is about **70 mV less** (more negative) than the outside. This steady membrane potential of about -70 mV is the neuron's **resting potential**. In this resting state, with a -70 mV charge built up across its membrane, a neuron is said to be **polarised**, which simply means that its membrane potential is not zero.

### Ionic basis of the resting potential: the two ions that matter

Why should the inside of a neuron be negative? The answer lies in **ions**. Like all salts in solution, the salts in neural tissue separate into positively and negatively charged particles called **ions**. Many kinds of ion are present in and around neurons, but the 11th edition focuses on just two:

- **sodium ions (Na+)**, and
- **potassium ions (K+)**.

The abbreviations come from the Latin names, *natrium* (Na) and *kalium* (K). The plus signs indicate that each carries a **single positive charge**.

The essential fact is that these two ions are **unequally distributed** across the membrane of a resting neuron:

| Ion | Where it is more concentrated at rest |
|---|---|
| **Na+** | **Outside** the neuron (more outside than inside) |
| **K+** | **Inside** the neuron (more inside than outside) |

This unequal distribution is maintained *even though* the neural membrane contains specialised pores, called **ion channels**, through which ions can pass. Each type of ion channel is specialised for the passage of particular ions; some are specialised for Na+, some for K+, some for other ions. The puzzle the rest of this section solves is: if there are pores in the membrane, why do the ions not simply even themselves out?

> **Trap.** This distribution is easily reversed under exam pressure: **sodium** (as in table salt, sodium chloride) is the ion of the salty **outside** fluid; **potassium** is the ion of the **inside**.

### Two pressures on sodium: electrostatic pressure and the concentration gradient

Consider Na+ first. There is substantial pressure on Na+ ions to *enter* the resting neuron, and it comes from two independent sources that happen to push in the same direction.

**First, electrostatic pressure.** Opposite charges attract. The inside of the resting neuron is at -70 mV, and Na+ ions are positively charged, so they are attracted inward.

**Second, the concentration gradient, produced by random motion.** Like all ions in solution, the ions in neural tissue are in **constant random motion**, and particles in random motion tend to become **evenly distributed**. The reason is purely statistical: a randomly moving particle is more likely to move **down its concentration gradient** (from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration) than up it, simply because there are more particles on the crowded side available to wander across. Pinel's illustration is a drop of red ink placed in a bathtub of water: the ink spreads outward into the areas where there is no ink, and gradually the whole bath turns pink. Since Na+ is more concentrated outside, random motion pushes it inward too.

So for Na+, both forces agree: electrostatic pressure says "in", the concentration gradient says "in".

For K+, the two forces **disagree**, and this is the subtle part. K+ is more concentrated *inside*, so its concentration gradient pushes it **out**. But K+ is also positively charged, so the negative interior attracts it **in**. Figure 4.1 summarises the three factors acting on the two ions in a resting neuron:

1. **Ions move down their concentration gradients**, so Na+ tends to enter and K+ tends to exit.
2. **The negative internal charge** creates pressure for **both** Na+ and K+ to enter.
3. **Sodium-potassium pumps** transport **3 Na+ out** for every **2 K+ in** (explained below).

### Why sodium does not rush in, and why potassium does not pour out

If Na+ is under double pressure to enter, why does it not come rushing in and abolish the resting potential? The answer is simple: **the sodium ion channels in resting neurons are closed**. With its channels shut, the membrane is largely impermeable to Na+, and the flow of Na+ ions into the neuron is greatly reduced. Na+ is like water held behind a dam: enormous pressure, very little flow. Hold on to that image, because in 4.3 the action potential is exactly what happens when the dam's gates are thrown open.

K+ is in a different position. **The potassium channels are open in resting neurons**, so K+ *can* move freely. But only a **few** K+ ions actually leave, because as soon as they start to, the **electrostatic pressure from the negative resting potential largely holds them inside**. For K+, the outward push of its concentration gradient and the inward pull of the negative interior come close to balancing.

This gives you an intuitive picture of where the -70 mV comes from, in the terms the 11th edition uses: the resting neuron is a cell whose K+ channels are open and whose Na+ channels are shut. The ion that can move (K+) is held in near-balance by the negativity across the membrane; the ion that would destroy that negativity (Na+) is kept out because its gates are closed.

### Hodgkin, Huxley and the sodium-potassium pump

> **Key study, Hodgkin & Huxley (1950s).** In the 1950s **Alan Hodgkin** and **Andrew Huxley** became interested in the *stability* of the resting membrane potential. The puzzle was this: some Na+ ions do manage to enter resting neurons despite the closed sodium channels, and some K+ ions do exit. Left uncorrected, these leaks would slowly run the gradients down. So why does the resting potential stay fixed? In a series of clever experiments, for which they were awarded **Nobel Prizes**, they found the answer: **at the same rate that Na+ ions leaked in, other Na+ ions were actively transported out; and at the same rate that K+ ions leaked out, other K+ ions were actively transported in.**

The ion transport is performed by mechanisms in the cell membrane that continually **exchange three Na+ ions inside the neuron for two K+ ions outside**. These mechanisms are commonly called **sodium-potassium pumps**. The word "actively" matters: these pumps move ions *against* their gradients, which requires energy, whereas ion movement through open channels is passive and goes *down* the gradients.

Read the logic of the finding carefully, because it shows that the resting potential is not a static state but a **dynamic equilibrium**. Ions are continually leaking in both directions, and the pumps continually undo the leaks at exactly the same rate. The resting potential looks constant only because inflow and outflow are matched.

Since the discovery of sodium-potassium pumps, several other classes of **transporters** have been discovered (see Kaila et al., 2014). A transporter is defined broadly as a **mechanism in the membrane of a cell that actively transports ions or molecules across the membrane**. The sodium-potassium pump is one kind; you will meet another kind in 4.4, where transporters pull released neurotransmitter molecules back into the terminal button (reuptake).

> **Trap.** The direction of the pump is the single most frequently reversed fact in this chapter: **3 Na+ OUT, 2 K+ IN**. Each ion is pumped back to the side where it is already concentrated, which is the point of pumping. Be warned that the 11th edition's own "Scan Your Brain" exercise words this item backwards ("three Na+ ions move inside the cell and two K+ ions move outside"); the body text and Figure 4.1 give the correct direction, and that is the one to use.

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel (and the older Indian keys built on them) taught the resting potential with **four factors**: two "homogenising" forces (random motion and electrostatic pressure) opposed by two membrane properties (**differential permeability** and the **sodium-potassium pump**). They also included **chloride ions (Cl−)**, which pass readily and were described as being at equilibrium at -70 mV, and **negatively charged protein ions** trapped inside, and they reported Hodgkin and Huxley's calculations: K+ would need about **-90 mV** to be held in balance, so it is driven out with about **20 mV** of net pressure, while Na+ faces about **120 mV** of inward pressure (50 mV from its concentration gradient plus 70 mV electrostatic). The 11th edition drops all of this and presents only **Na+ and K+**, **three factors** (concentration gradients, the negative internal charge, the pump) and the closed-Na+/open-K+ channel account. If a paper asks about Cl− equilibrium or the 120 mV figure, it is using the older edition; answer from that framework, but do not mix the two.

> **High-yield.** 11th-edition resting potential in four lines: more **Na+ outside**, more **K+ inside**; Na+ pushed **in** by both the concentration gradient and the negative interior but kept out because **Na+ channels are closed**; K+ channels are **open** but K+ is largely **held in** by the negative interior; leaks are matched by **sodium-potassium pumps** (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in), discovered by **Hodgkin and Huxley** in the **1950s**.

With the resting neuron understood, you are ready for what happens when its membrane potential is disturbed by input from other cells.

## 4.2 Generation, Conduction, and Integration of Postsynaptic Potentials

What happens when the resting membrane potential is disturbed? Typically the disturbance comes from other cells: neurons that synapse on the neuron in question release chemicals onto it. Because these disturbances arise on the *receiving* side of a synapse, they are called **postsynaptic potentials (PSPs)**. This module covers three things in order: how PSPs are **generated** by input to a neuron, how they are **conducted** to other parts of the neuron, and how they are **integrated** so that they can cause the neuron to fire (produce an action potential).

### Depolarisation and hyperpolarisation: EPSPs and IPSPs

When neurons fire, they release chemicals called **neurotransmitters** from their **terminal buttons**. The neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the **synaptic clefts** and interact with specialised **receptor** molecules on the receptive membranes of the next neuron in the circuit. When neurotransmitter molecules bind to postsynaptic receptors they typically have one of **two** effects, depending on the neurotransmitter, the receptor and the postsynaptic neuron in question:

- They may **depolarise** the receptive membrane, that is, **decrease** the resting membrane potential, for example from **-70 to -67 mV**.
- They may **hyperpolarise** it, that is, **increase** the resting membrane potential, for example from **-70 to -72 mV**.

Postsynaptic depolarisations are called **excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs)**, because they **increase** the likelihood that the neuron will fire. Postsynaptic hyperpolarisations are called **inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs)**, because they **decrease** the likelihood that the neuron will fire. The reason for the names will be obvious once you meet the threshold of excitation below: an EPSP moves the membrane potential *towards* the firing threshold, an IPSP moves it *away*.

> **Trap.** The words "increase" and "decrease" refer to the **size** (magnitude) of the potential, not to the arithmetic value. Going from -70 to -67 mV makes the number arithmetically larger but **decreases** the membrane potential (the charge difference across the membrane shrinks from 70 to 67 mV); this is **depolarisation**. Going from -70 to -72 mV **increases** the membrane potential; this is **hyperpolarisation**. The book's review item "the neurotransmitters may ___ the postsynaptic membrane, which implies that the resting membrane potential will increase" has the answer **hyperpolarise**. If you think "less polarised = depolarised, more polarised = hyperpolarised" you cannot be caught.

### PSPs are graded potentials

All PSPs, EPSPs and IPSPs alike, are **graded potentials**. This means that their **amplitude is proportional to the intensity of the signal that elicits them**: weak signals elicit small PSPs, and strong signals elicit large ones. A PSP is therefore an analogue signal. Its size carries information about how strongly the input synapse was activated. Contrast this with the action potential below, which has no such graded property.

### How PSPs travel: rapidly and decrementally

EPSPs and IPSPs travel **passively** from their sites of generation at synapses, which are usually on the **dendrites or cell body**, much as electrical signals travel through a cable. Passive, cable-like transmission has two important characteristics.

**First, it is rapid**, so rapid that for most purposes it can be assumed to be **instantaneous**. Pinel adds a warning here that is easy to skim past: do not confuse the **duration** of a PSP with its **rate of transmission**. The duration of PSPs varies considerably (some are brief, some long-lasting), but all PSPs, brief or enduring, are **transmitted almost instantaneously**. How long a PSP lasts at a given point and how fast it spreads from that point are different properties.

**Second, it is decremental**: PSPs **decrease in amplitude as they travel** through the neuron, just as a ripple on a pond gradually disappears as it travels outward. Most PSPs do not travel more than **a couple of millimetres** from their site of generation before they fade out completely.

These two properties set up the central problem of neural integration. A PSP generated on a distant dendrite reaches the rest of the neuron almost instantly, but shrunken; whether it has any effect depends on what else is arriving at the same time.

> **High-yield.** PSPs: **graded**, conducted **passively**, **rapidly** (effectively instantaneously) and **decrementally** (fade within a couple of millimetres). EPSP = depolarisation = more likely to fire. IPSP = hyperpolarisation = less likely to fire.

### Where the firing decision is made: the axon initial segment

The PSPs created at a **single synapse** typically have **little effect** on the firing of the postsynaptic neuron. The receptive areas of most neurons are covered with **thousands of synapses**, and whether a neuron fires is determined by the **net effect of their activity**. More specifically, whether a neuron fires depends on the **balance between the excitatory and inhibitory signals reaching its axon**.

Where exactly on the axon? It was **once believed** that action potentials were generated at the **axon hillock**, the conical structure at the junction between the cell body and the axon. They are **actually generated in the adjacent section of the axon**, called the **axon initial segment** (see Kuba, Adachi, & Ohmori, 2014; Tian et al., 2014). So the graded EPSPs and IPSPs created at particular receptive sites on the membrane are conducted **instantly and decrementally to the axon initial segment**, and it is there that the neuron's decision is taken.

> **Trap.** This is a direct old-versus-new terminology conflict. Older textbooks, older editions of Pinel and many Indian answer keys say that action potentials are generated at the **axon hillock**. The 11th edition says explicitly that this was the **old belief** and that APs are generated at the **axon initial segment**, the section of axon just beyond the hillock. If both options appear, choose **axon initial segment**. If only "axon hillock" appears (an older paper), it is the intended answer for "trigger zone".

### Threshold of excitation and the action potential

If the sum of the depolarisations and hyperpolarisations reaching the axon initial segment at any moment is sufficient to depolarise the membrane to a level called its **threshold of excitation**, usually about **-65 mV**, an action potential is generated.

Do the arithmetic, because it shows how small the margin is. From a resting potential of -70 mV, the threshold of -65 mV is only **5 mV** of net depolarisation away. A single EPSP of a few millivolts (the book's example, -70 to -67, is 3 mV) would not be enough on its own, which is exactly why a single synapse typically has little effect and why summation matters.

The **action potential (AP)** is a **massive but momentary reversal of the membrane potential**, lasting about **1 millisecond**, from about **-70 mV to about +50 mV**. In other words, during an AP the inside of the axon briefly becomes *positive* relative to the outside, a swing of about 120 mV.

Unlike PSPs, APs are **not graded**. Their magnitude is not related in any way to the intensity of the stimuli that elicit them. They are **all-or-none responses**: they either occur to their full extent or they do not occur at all. Figure 4.2 shows an EPSP, an IPSP, and an EPSP that crosses threshold and is followed by an AP.

One qualification, which the 11th edition adds: although many neurons display APs of the type in Figure 4.2, others do not. Some neurons display APs that have a **longer duration**, a **lower amplitude**, or **multiple spikes**. The textbook AP is a typical case, not a universal one.

> **High-yield.** Threshold of excitation = about **-65 mV**. AP = reversal from about **-70 to about +50 mV**, lasting about **1 ms**, **all-or-none**. PSPs = graded; APs = all-or-none. Some neurons show longer, lower-amplitude or multi-spike APs.

### Integration: adding up the PSPs

In effect, each neuron **adds together all the graded excitatory and inhibitory PSPs reaching its axon initial segment** and decides to fire or not on the basis of their sum. Combining individual signals into one overall signal in this way is called **integration** (the term the book's review exercise asks for). The adding-up happens in two ways, **over space** and **over time**, and the two are examined separately below. In a real neuron both happen at once, continuously.

### Spatial summation

**Spatial summation** is the summing of PSPs that are produced **simultaneously on different parts of the receptive membrane**. Figure 4.3 shows the **three possible combinations**, using a neuron with two excitatory synapses (A and B) and two inhibitory synapses (C and D):

| Synapses stimulated together | Result |
|---|---|
| Two excitatory (A + B) | The two simultaneous EPSPs **sum to produce a greater EPSP** |
| Two inhibitory (C + D) | The two simultaneous IPSPs **sum to produce a greater IPSP** |
| One excitatory, one inhibitory (A + C) | The simultaneous EPSP and IPSP **cancel each other out** |

The third row is the conceptually important one. Inhibition in the nervous system is not merely the absence of excitation; an IPSP actively subtracts from whatever excitation arrives at the same time. A neuron receiving strong excitation can be kept silent by simultaneous inhibition.

### Temporal summation

**Temporal summation** is the summing of PSPs **produced in rapid succession at the same synapse**. Figure 4.4 shows its **two possible combinations**: two EPSPs elicited in rapid succession at an excitatory synapse sum to produce a larger EPSP, and two IPSPs elicited in rapid succession at an inhibitory synapse sum to produce a larger IPSP.

Why can successive stimulations add together over time? Because **the PSPs they produce often outlast them**. If a synapse is activated, and then activated again **before the original PSP has completely dissipated**, the effect of the second stimulation is **superimposed on the lingering PSP** produced by the first. Two consequences follow directly:

- A **brief subthreshold excitatory stimulus can fire a neuron** if it is administered **twice in rapid succession**. Neither stimulus alone reaches -65 mV; the second, riding on the remains of the first, does.
- In the same way, an **inhibitory synapse activated twice in rapid succession** can produce a greater IPSP than a single stimulation.

> **Trap.** Spatial = **different synapses, same time**. Temporal = **same synapse, different (successive) times**. Examiners swap the definitions. Also note the counts: Figure 4.3 shows **three** combinations of spatial summation (EPSP + EPSP, IPSP + IPSP, EPSP + IPSP), Figure 4.4 only **two** of temporal summation (EPSP + EPSP, IPSP + IPSP), because successive activation of the *same* synapse can only repeat the same sign.

### Ripples on a pond: summation is continuous

PSPs **continuously summate over both time and space** as a neuron is bombarded with stimuli from **thousands of synapses**. Schematic diagrams of neural circuits rarely show neurons with more than a few representative synaptic contacts, but most neurons have **thousands** of synaptic contacts covering their dendrites and cell body. Pinel's image is the surface of a pond with many ripples on it: the ripples are continuously interacting with one another to generate larger or smaller ripples. The membrane potential at the axon initial segment at any instant is the moment-to-moment outcome of that interaction.

### Does the location of a synapse matter?

The location of a synapse on a neuron's membrane had long been assumed to be an important factor in determining its influence on the neuron's firing. The reasoning followed directly from decremental conduction: because PSPs fade as they travel, synapses **near the axon** should have the most influence, and synapses on distant dendrites the least.

That reasoning is sound as far as passive conduction goes, but it turns out to be incomplete. It has been demonstrated that **some neurons have a mechanism for amplifying dendritic signals that originate far from their axon** (see Adrian et al., 2014; Araya, 2014). In such neurons a distant synapse need not be a weak one. This is an early example of a recurring lesson of the chapter: the simple passive-cable picture is a useful first approximation, but real neurons often have extra machinery that changes the rules.

### The firing-gun metaphor

Pinel compares the firing of a neuron to the firing of a gun, and the comparison captures both halves of the process.

1. **Both reactions are triggered by graded responses.** As a trigger is squeezed, it moves back gradually until it causes the gun to fire. As a neuron is stimulated, it becomes gradually less polarised until the threshold of excitation is reached and firing occurs. The graded part (trigger movement, PSPs) comes first and can take any value.
2. **Both are all-or-none events.** Squeezing a trigger harder does not make the bullet travel faster or farther. Stimulating a neuron more intensely does not increase the **speed** or **amplitude** of the resulting action potential.

The metaphor is worth remembering in full because it encodes the distinction examiners test most often in this module: graded inputs, all-or-none output. (You will see in 4.3 that stronger stimulation *does* change something about the neuron's output, namely its **rate** of firing; the gun metaphor is silent on that, which is one of its limits.)

## 4.3 Conduction of Action Potentials

Two questions organise this module. How are action potentials produced? And how are they conducted along the axon? The answer to both is the same: **through the action of voltage-gated ion channels** (see Moran et al., 2015). **Voltage-gated ion channels** are ion channels that **open or close in response to changes in the membrane potential**. Contrast them with the channels in 4.4 that open when a neurotransmitter binds (ligand-activated channels); the ones that matter here respond to voltage itself.

### The ionic basis of the action potential

Recall the resting state from 4.1. The membrane potential of a resting neuron is relatively constant despite the high pressure driving Na+ into the cell, for two reasons: the resting membrane is **relatively impermeable to Na+** (its sodium channels are closed), and the few Na+ ions that do get in are **pumped out**. Everything changes when the membrane of the **axon initial segment** is depolarised to the **threshold of excitation** by a sufficiently large EPSP. Follow the sequence step by step, because each step causes the next.

**Step 1: the rising phase.** At threshold, the **voltage-gated sodium channels** in the axon membrane **open wide**. Na+ ions, which have been held back against both their concentration gradient and the electrostatic pull of the negative interior, **rush in**. The inrush of positive charge suddenly **reverses the membrane potential**, driving it from about **-70 to about +50 mV**. This is the dam of 4.1 being opened: the pressure was there all along, and only the gate was missing.

**Step 2: potassium channels open.** The rapid change in membrane potential caused by the Na+ influx in turn triggers the opening of **voltage-gated potassium channels**. K+ ions near the membrane are now driven **out** of the cell through these channels. Notice *what* drives them, because it changes during the AP:

- at first, their **relatively high internal concentration** (the concentration gradient, which always pointed outward); and then,
- when the AP is near its peak, the **positive internal charge**. At rest, the negative interior *held K+ in*; once the interior has become positive, electrostatic pressure *pushes K+ out* as well. For a moment, both forces on K+ point the same way.

**Step 3: sodium channels close; repolarisation.** After about **1 millisecond**, the sodium channels **close**. This closure marks the **end of the rising phase** and the **beginning of the repolarisation phase**. Repolarisation is the result of the **continued efflux of K+ ions**: positive charge leaving the cell brings the membrane potential back down towards its resting level.

**Step 4: potassium channels close gradually; hyperpolarisation.** Once repolarisation has been achieved, the potassium channels **gradually close**, and this marks the **beginning of the hyperpolarisation phase**. Because they close *gradually* rather than all at once, **too many K+ ions flow out**, and the neuron is left **hyperpolarised** (more negative than -70 mV) for a brief period.

The AP thus has **three phases**, each tied to a channel event:

| Phase | Channel event that begins it | Ion movement driving it |
|---|---|---|
| **Rising phase** | Voltage-gated **Na+ channels open** at threshold | **Na+ influx** |
| **Repolarisation** | **Na+ channels close** (after about 1 ms); K+ channels already open | Continued **K+ efflux** |
| **Hyperpolarisation** | **K+ channels begin to close**, gradually | Excess **K+ efflux** before closure is complete |

Figure 4.5 shows the timing of the opening and closing of the sodium and potassium channels during an AP and the three associated phases.

> **Trap.** Two direction errors are common. (1) The **rising phase is Na+ coming in**, not K+ going out. (2) **Repolarisation is K+ going out**, not Na+ being pumped out. The pump is not involved in either phase. And note the sequence of channel events: Na+ channels open, K+ channels open, Na+ channels close, K+ channels close. The K+ channels open *before* the Na+ channels close, but their effect only dominates once the Na+ channels have shut.

> **Trap.** The hyperpolarisation phase (the "undershoot" in many textbooks) happens because K+ channels close **slowly**, not because the sodium-potassium pump overshoots.

### Why one action potential barely changes the ion concentrations

It is natural to assume that, after an AP has let Na+ in and K+ out, the sodium-potassium pumps must work hard to restore the resting state. Pinel corrects this. The **number of ions** that flow through the membrane during an AP is **extremely small** in relation to the total number inside and around the neuron, because the AP involves only those ions **right next to the membrane**. Therefore:

- a single AP has **little effect on the relative concentrations** of ions inside and outside the neuron;
- the resting ion concentrations next to the membrane are **rapidly re-established by the random movement of ions** (the same diffusion that spreads the ink in the bathtub); and
- the **sodium-potassium pumps play only a minor role** in the re-establishment of the resting potential.

> **Trap.** "After each action potential, the sodium-potassium pump restores the resting potential" is **wrong** for Pinel: restoration is mainly by **random movement of ions**, and the pumps play only a **minor** role.

### Refractory periods

For a brief period of about **1 to 2 milliseconds** after the initiation of an AP, it is **impossible to elicit a second AP**. This is the **absolute refractory period**. It is followed by the **relative refractory period**, during which it is **possible** to fire the neuron again but **only by applying higher-than-normal levels of stimulation**. The end of the relative refractory period is the point at which the amount of stimulation needed to fire the neuron has **returned to baseline**.

### The two consequences of refractory periods

Refractory periods are responsible for **two important characteristics of neural activity**, and the book's learning objective asks for both.

**First, APs normally travel along axons in only one direction.** The portions of an axon over which an AP has just travelled are left momentarily refractory. The AP can regenerate only ahead of itself, into membrane that is still excitable; behind it the membrane cannot fire again yet. So **an AP cannot reverse direction**.

**Second, the rate of neural firing is related to the intensity of stimulation.** This is how an all-or-none signal can still carry information about stimulus strength:

- Under **continual high-intensity stimulation**, a neuron fires and then fires again **as soon as its absolute refractory period is over**, up to a **maximum of about 1,000 times per second**.
- Under continuous stimulation of an intensity **just sufficient to fire the neuron at rest**, the neuron does not fire again until **both the absolute and the relative refractory periods** have run their course, so it fires slowly.
- **Intermediate intensities** produce **intermediate rates**.

Put the two halves of 4.2 and 4.3 together and you have the neuron's full coding scheme. The **size** of each AP never changes with stimulus intensity (all-or-none); the **frequency** of APs does. Stimulus intensity is coded in the **rate of firing** (often called rate coding).

> **Trap.** "A stronger stimulus produces a larger action potential" is false; "a stronger stimulus produces a higher rate of firing" is true. The maximum rate, about **1,000 per second**, is the rate at which a neuron fires if it fires again the moment each absolute refractory period ends. If an option offers 100 per second or 10,000 per second, it is wrong for Pinel.

### Axonal conduction: nondecremental, but slower than PSPs

The conduction of APs along an axon differs from the conduction of PSPs in **two important ways**:

1. Conduction of APs along an axon is typically **nondecremental**: APs do not grow weaker as they travel along the axonal membrane.
2. APs are conducted **more slowly** than PSPs.

Both differences have the same cause, the **voltage-gated sodium channels**. Once an AP has been generated, it travels along the axon *as a graded potential*; that is, the electrical disturbance spreads **rapidly and decrementally** along the membrane, exactly like a PSP. But when that spreading graded potential reaches the **next voltage-gated sodium channel** along the axon, and if it is still large enough to exceed the threshold of excitation, those channels **open**, Na+ rushes in, and **another full-blown AP** is generated. In essence the AP is **continually regenerated at each sodium channel** along the length of the axon, again and again, until a full-blown AP is triggered at the terminal buttons.

Now both properties follow. The AP is nondecremental because it is **re-created at full size** at each sodium channel rather than travelling as a single fading disturbance. It is slower than a PSP because each regeneration takes time; a PSP simply spreads, whereas an AP must repeatedly stop and rebuild itself.

> **Trap.** The long-distance signal is the *slower* one: **PSPs are transmitted faster than APs**, but **APs are nondecremental** and PSPs decremental. "APs are faster than PSPs" is wrong.

### The mouse traps on a wobbly shelf

Pinel's analogy for axonal conduction is a row of **mouse traps on a wobbly shelf**, all set and ready to be triggered.

- Each trap **stores energy** by holding back its striker against the pressure of its spring, in the same way that each voltage-gated sodium channel stores energy by holding back Na+ ions, which are under pressure to move down their concentration and electrostatic gradients into the neuron.
- When the first trap is triggered, the **vibration is transmitted through the shelf rapidly and decrementally** (the graded potential).
- When the vibration reaches the next trap and is **sufficiently large**, that trap is sprung, and so on down the line (regeneration at each channel).

In the neuron: when a sodium channel at the axon initial segment is opened by an EPSP, an AP is generated; that electrical signal travels instantly and decrementally, as a graded potential, to the next sodium channel along the axon; that channel opens to generate an AP; and so on down the length of the axon.

The analogy makes two further points visible. The **nondecremental** nature of AP conduction is obvious from it: **the last trap on the shelf strikes with no less intensity than the first**. And it shows that the row of traps **can transmit in either direction**, just like an axon.

### Antidromic and orthodromic conduction

If electrical stimulation of sufficient intensity is applied to the **midpoint of an axon**, **two APs** are generated, one travelling each way:

- one AP travels along the axon **back towards the cell body**: this is **antidromic conduction**;
- the other travels along the axon **towards the terminal buttons**: this is **orthodromic conduction**.

How does this square with the claim above that APs travel in only one direction? The two statements are about different situations. The axon membrane itself is symmetrical and can conduct either way, which is what midpoint stimulation reveals. Under **normal** conditions, however, APs begin at the **axon initial segment** at one end of the axon, and the refractory membrane left behind them stops them turning back, so their natural direction is orthodromic. Antidromic conduction of a full AP along the axon is therefore seen mainly when an axon is stimulated partway along, as in experiments.

Figure 4.6 shows the usual sequence in a multipolar neuron, which is worth reciting in order because it is the whole of 4.2 and 4.3 in five steps:

1. **PSPs are elicited** on the cell body and dendrites.
2. PSPs are **conducted decrementally** to the axon initial segment.
3. When the summated PSPs **exceed the threshold of excitation** at the axon initial segment, an **AP is triggered**.
4. The AP is **conducted nondecrementally** down the axon to the terminal button (**orthodromic conduction**).
5. Arrival of the AP at the terminal button **triggers exocytosis** (neurotransmitter release, 4.4).

There is one important exception to "only forward". The generation of an AP at the axon initial segment **also spreads back through the cell body and dendrites** of the neuron, as a **large graded potential**. These **antidromic (backpropagating) potentials** are believed to play a role in **certain forms of synaptic plasticity** (see Stuart & Spruston, 2015). The logic is that a backpropagating signal tells the dendrites, and therefore the synapses on them, that the neuron has just fired, which is the kind of information a synapse would need in order to strengthen itself when its activity contributes to firing.

> **Trap.** **Orthodromic** = the normal direction, cell body to terminal buttons. **Antidromic** = backwards, towards the cell body. Note that the backpropagation into the dendrites is described as a **graded potential**, not a full AP travelling backwards along the axon.

### Conduction in myelinated axons: saltatory conduction

Recall from Chapter 3 that the axons of many neurons are insulated from the extracellular fluid by segments of fatty tissue called **myelin**. In myelinated axons, ions can pass through the axonal membrane **only at the nodes of Ranvier**, the **gaps between adjacent myelin segments**. Indeed, in myelinated axons, the axonal **voltage-gated sodium channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier**. How, then, are APs transmitted along a myelinated axon?

Return to the mouse traps. In a myelinated axon it is **just as if the traps were placed further apart** along the wobbly shelf. Because the sodium channels are concentrated at some distance from one another, at the nodes, the electrical signal generated at the sodium channels of one node **travels instantly and decrementally** (as a graded potential) to the sodium channels at the next node, where the AP is regenerated, and so on down the length of the axon.

Now the speed advantage is easy to explain. Conduction along the myelinated segments is **instantaneous**, because it is a graded potential; the regeneration, which is the slow part, happens only at the nodes. The signal therefore appears to **"jump" along the axon from one node of Ranvier to the next**. There is, of course, a slight delay at each node while the AP is regenerated, but conduction is still **much faster** in myelinated than in unmyelinated axons. The transmission of APs in myelinated axons is called **saltatory conduction** (*saltare* means "to skip or jump").

Given the importance of myelin in neural conduction, it is hardly surprising that diseases that attack myelin, such as **multiple sclerosis**, have devastating effects on neural activity and behaviour.

> **Trap.** In saltatory conduction the AP is **regenerated at each node**; it is not regenerated along the myelinated segments, and it does not literally leap through the air. Between nodes it travels as a fast, decremental graded potential. "Myelin speeds conduction by increasing the number of sodium channels along the whole axon" is wrong: the channels are **concentrated at the nodes**, and the ions cross only there.

### The velocity of axonal conduction

At what speed are APs conducted? The answer depends on **two properties of the axon**:

1. **Diameter**: conduction is faster in **large-diameter** axons.
2. **Myelination**: conduction is faster in **myelinated** axons.

The extremes the book gives:

| Axon type | Example | Conduction speed |
|---|---|---|
| Large, myelinated | Human **motor neurons** (neurons that synapse on skeletal muscles) | Some conduct at up to **60 metres per second** (about **134 miles per hour**) |
| Small, unmyelinated | Small unmyelinated axons | About **1 metre per second** |

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel gave two figures for fast motor neurons, about **100 m/s in cats** and about **60 m/s in humans**, and question setters sometimes offer 100 m/s as a distractor. The 11th edition gives only the human figure: **up to 60 m/s** for large myelinated human motor neurons, and about **1 m/s** for small unmyelinated axons. The two determinants are **diameter** and **myelination**; length of the axon is not one of them.

### Conduction in neurons without axons

APs are the means by which axons conduct all-or-none signals **nondecrementally over relatively long distances**. To keep the account of APs in perspective, Pinel stresses that **most neurons in mammalian brains either do not have axons or have very short ones**, and **many of these neurons do not normally display APs**. Conduction in these **interneurons** is typically **only through graded potentials**.

This is a significant correction to the impression the module may have given. The action potential is the solution to a specific engineering problem, getting a signal over a long distance without it fading. A neuron whose output target is close by has no need for it, and graded signalling alone suffices.

### The Hodgkin-Huxley model in perspective

The account of neural conduction given so far is based largely on the **Hodgkin-Huxley model**, the theory first proposed by Hodgkin and Huxley in the **early 1950s** (see Catterall et al., 2012). Pinel's point in this subsection is about the **status** of what you have just learned: it is a model, and you should know both why it was so successful and where it breaks down.

**Why it deserves credit.** In introductory biology and psychology courses it is often presented as a factual account of neural conduction rather than as a theory. To be fair, it was a **major advance**. Fully deserving of the **1963 Nobel Prize**, it provided a simple, effective introduction to what we now understand about the general ways in which neurons conduct signals.

**Why it must be applied with caution.** The problem is that the simple neurons and mechanisms of the model are **not representative of the variety, complexity and plasticity** of many of the neurons in the mammalian brain. Three lines of argument support this:

1. **The model's original preparation.** The Hodgkin-Huxley model was based on the study of **squid motor neurons**. Motor neurons are **simple, large, and readily accessible in the PNS**, and squid motor neurons are particularly large. These very properties contributed to the initial success of the research, but they also make it difficult to apply the model directly to the mammalian brain. A model built on the easiest neuron to study is a model of an unusually simple neuron.
2. **Diversity of brain neurons.** **Hundreds of different kinds of neurons** are found in the mammalian brain, and many of them have **actions not found in motor neurons**.
3. **Conduction may not be purely electrical.** There is mounting evidence that neural conduction is **not merely due to electrical impulses** (see Holland, De Regt, & Drukarch, 2019). For example, there is evidence that electrical impulses such as APs and PSPs are accompanied by **mechanical impulses**: **travelling waves of expansion and contraction of the neural membrane**, exactly like ripples on a pond (see Fox, 2018).

In summary, the Hodgkin-Huxley model **should be applied to cerebral neurons with great caution**.

> **Trap.** The resting-potential work and the AP model are both **Hodgkin and Huxley**, **1950s**; the **Nobel Prize** came in **1963** (standard history: shared with John Eccles). Pinel calls the preparation "squid motor neurons"; most textbooks say the **squid giant axon**. The criticism is not that the model is *wrong* but that it is **unrepresentative** of brain neurons. The 8th edition made that point with a list of specific differences (many brain neurons fire continually without input, some dendrites conduct APs actively, APs vary between neurons); the 11th edition replaces the list with the "hundreds of kinds of neurons" point plus the new **mechanical impulse** evidence, so expect the list in older papers and the mechanical waves in newer ones.

## 4.4 Synaptic Transmission: From Electrical Signals to Chemical Signals

Sections 4.2 and 4.3 dealt with communication *within* a single neuron, through PSPs and APs. This module crosses the gap to the next cell. It explains how APs arriving at terminal buttons trigger the release of neurotransmitters into synapses, and how neurotransmitters then carry signals to other cells. Pinel organises the module around **five aspects of synaptic transmission**, which make a useful skeleton for any long answer:

1. the **structure** of synapses;
2. the **synthesis, packaging and transport** of neurotransmitter molecules;
3. the **release** of neurotransmitter molecules;
4. the **activation of receptors** by neurotransmitter molecules; and
5. the **reuptake, enzymatic degradation and recycling** of neurotransmitter molecules.

A sixth topic, the role of **glia and gap junctions**, closes the module and qualifies everything before it.

> **Trap.** The chapter itself does not describe how chemical transmission was discovered. If a paper asks, the standard answer is **Otto Loewi's (1921)** experiment on frog hearts, in which fluid from a vagus-stimulated heart slowed a second heart; Loewi's "vagusstoff" was later identified as **acetylcholine**. This is general history, not 11th-edition content.

### Structure of synapses: axodendritic and axosomatic synapses, dendritic spines

Some communication among neurons occurs across synapses like the one in Figure 4.7. At such synapses, **neurotransmitter molecules are released from specialised sites on buttons into synaptic clefts**, where they induce EPSPs or IPSPs in other neurons by binding to **receptors on their postsynaptic membranes**. The labelled parts of the figure are worth knowing because they reappear throughout the module: the **button** (terminal button), containing **synaptic vesicles**, **mitochondria**, **microtubules** and a **Golgi complex**; the **presynaptic membrane** of the button; the **synaptic cleft**; the **postsynaptic membrane** on a **dendritic spine**; and **astrocytic processes** surrounding the synapse.

The two most common arrangements are named by what connects to what:

- An **axodendritic synapse** is a synapse of an axon terminal button onto a **dendrite**. The synapse in Figure 4.7 is of this kind.
- An **axosomatic synapse** is a synapse of an axon terminal button onto a **soma** (cell body).

Many axodendritic synapses terminate on **dendritic spines**, nodules of various shapes located on the surfaces of many dendrites (Pinel refers you back to Figure 3.30).

### The tripartite synapse

Notice that Figure 4.7 also shows an **astrocyte** situated at the synapse. This is not decoration. **Most synapses in the brain form a tripartite synapse**: a synapse that involves **two neurons and an astroglial cell** (see Allen & Eroglu, 2017; Grosche & Reichenbach, 2013; Navarrete & Araque, 2014; Sun et al., 2013). **All three cells communicate with one another through synaptic transmission.** The classic two-cell picture of a synapse, presynaptic neuron to postsynaptic neuron, is therefore incomplete for most brain synapses; the astrocyte is a participant, a point the module develops at the end.

> **Trap.** "Tripartite" means **three parts**: presynaptic neuron, postsynaptic neuron and **astrocyte** (an astroglial cell). It is not three neurons, and it is not the button, cleft and spine. The 11th edition says **most** synapses in the brain are tripartite.

### Other kinds of synapse: dendrodendritic, axoaxonic and axomyelenic

Although axodendritic and axosomatic synapses are the most common arrangements, there are many others (see Matthews & Fuchs, 2010). Three are singled out:

**Dendrodendritic synapses** (dendrite to dendrite) are interesting because they are **often capable of transmission in either direction** (see Urban & Castro, 2010). This contrasts with the usual one-way traffic of an axon terminal onto a receiving membrane.

**Axoaxonic synapses** (axon onto axon) are particularly important because they can mediate **presynaptic facilitation and inhibition**. As Figure 4.8 illustrates, an axoaxonic synapse on or near a terminal button can **selectively facilitate or inhibit the effects of that button** on the postsynaptic neuron. In the figure, neuron A synapses on the terminal button of neuron B. Some such axoaxonic synapses **increase** the effects of B on a third neuron C (**presynaptic facilitation**); others **decrease** the effects of B on C (**presynaptic inhibition**).

Why does this arrangement exist, when the neuron could simply be excited or inhibited by ordinary PSPs? The answer is **selectivity**. A PSP on B's dendrites or cell body would change B's firing and therefore change B's output at *every one* of its terminal buttons at once. An axoaxonic synapse acting on *one* of B's buttons changes B's influence on that one target, C, while leaving B's other connections untouched. The advantage of presynaptic facilitation and inhibition, compared with PSPs, is that **they can selectively influence one particular synapse rather than the entire presynaptic neuron**.

**Axomyelenic synapses** occur in the central nervous system; here an **axon synapses on the myelin sheath of an oligodendrocyte**. This **newly discovered** type of synapse represents yet another form of **neuron-glia communication** (see Dimou & Simons, 2017; Micu et al., 2018).

> **Trap.** Presynaptic facilitation and inhibition are mediated by **axoaxonic** synapses, not dendrodendritic ones; the synapse acts on the **presynaptic terminal** of another neuron. The **bidirectional** synapse is the **dendrodendritic** one. And the **axomyelenic** synapse (axon onto **oligodendrocyte myelin**) is a recent discovery (the book's references date from 2017 and 2018), so it will not appear in older keys.

### Directed and nondirected synapses

The synapses in Figures 4.7 and 4.8 are **directed synapses**: synapses at which the **site of neurotransmitter release and the site of neurotransmitter reception are in close proximity**. This is a common arrangement, but there are also many **nondirected synapses** in the mammalian nervous system: synapses at which the **site of release is at some distance from the site of reception**.

One type of nondirected synapse is shown in Figure 4.9. Here, neurotransmitter molecules are released from a series of **varicosities** (bulges or swellings) **along the axon and its branches**, and are thus **widely dispersed to surrounding targets**. Because of their appearance, these are often called **string-of-beads synapses**. A directed synapse is a private message to one recipient; a string-of-beads synapse is closer to a broadcast to the neighbourhood. You will see in 4.5 that the **monoamines** are characteristically released in this diffuse way.

### Synthesis, packaging and transport of neurotransmitter molecules

There are **two basic categories** of neurotransmitter molecule: **large** and **small**.

**All large neurotransmitters are neuropeptides.** **Neuropeptides** are **short amino acid chains composed of between 3 and 36 amino acids**; in effect, they are short proteins.

The two categories are made and delivered in completely different ways, and the difference follows from where the cell's protein-making machinery is located.

**Small-molecule neurotransmitters** are typically **synthesised in the cytoplasm of the terminal button** and packaged in **synaptic vesicles** by the button's **Golgi complex**. (Pinel suggests reviewing the internal structures of neurons in Figure 3.6.) Once filled with neurotransmitter, the vesicles are stored in **clusters next to the presynaptic membrane**. Everything happens locally, at the place of release.

**Neuropeptides**, like other proteins, are **assembled in the cytoplasm of the cell body on ribosomes**; they are then packaged in vesicles by the **cell body's Golgi complex** and **transported by microtubules to the terminal buttons** at a rate of about **40 centimetres (about 16 inches) per day**. Proteins are built on ribosomes, and ribosomes are in the cell body, so a peptide transmitter has to be shipped down the axon after it is made.

Two physical differences follow and are visible in Figure 4.7: the vesicles that contain neuropeptides are **usually larger** than those containing small-molecule neurotransmitters, and they **do not usually congregate as closely to the presynaptic membrane** as the other vesicles do.

| | Small-molecule neurotransmitters | Neuropeptides |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Small | Large: **3 to 36 amino acids** |
| Where synthesised | **Cytoplasm of the terminal button** | **Ribosomes in the cell body** cytoplasm |
| Packaged by | Golgi complex **of the button** | Golgi complex **of the cell body** |
| Transport | None needed | **Microtubules**, about **40 cm (16 in) per day** |
| Vesicles | Smaller; clustered next to the presynaptic membrane | **Larger**; not clustered as closely to the membrane |

> **Trap.** Two numbers are easy to mix up: neuropeptides are **3 to 36** amino acids long, and they are transported at about **40 cm per day**. And it is the **neuropeptides** that need axonal transport; small-molecule transmitters are made in the button itself.

### Coexistence

It was **once believed that each neuron synthesises and releases only one neurotransmitter**, but it has been clear for some time that **many neurons contain two neurotransmitters**, a situation called **coexistence**.

Pinel points out that you may already have seen the evidence without noticing it: the button in Figure 4.7 contains **synaptic vesicles of two sizes**. This suggests that it contains two neurotransmitters, a **neuropeptide in the larger vesicles** and a **small-molecule neurotransmitter in the smaller vesicles**. That peptide-plus-small-molecule pairing was the **first type of coexistence to be discovered**. We now know that there is also **coexistence of multiple small-molecule neurotransmitters** in the same neuron (see Granger, Wallace, & Sabatini, 2017). Adding further complexity, **neurons can change the types of neurotransmitters they release over their lifespan** (see Spitzer, 2017).

> **Trap.** The "one neuron, one transmitter" view that coexistence overturned is often called **Dale's principle** (a later simplification of Henry Dale's claim that a neuron releases the same transmitter at all its terminals); Pinel does not use the name. The finding that contradicts it is **coexistence**.

### Release of neurotransmitter molecules: exocytosis

**Exocytosis** is the process of neurotransmitter release (Figure 4.10; see Shin, 2014). Work through it as a causal chain:

1. When a neuron is **at rest**, synaptic vesicles containing small-molecule neurotransmitters tend to **congregate near sections of the presynaptic membrane that are particularly rich in voltage-gated calcium channels** (see Simms & Zamponi, 2014).
2. When **APs** arrive and depolarise the button, these **calcium channels open**, and **Ca2+ (calcium) ions enter the button**.
3. The **entry of Ca2+ triggers a chain reaction** that ultimately causes **synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane** and **empty their contents into the synaptic cleft** (see Zhou et al., 2017).

Notice that this is where the electrical signal becomes a chemical one. The AP is an electrical event; the calcium channel is a voltage-gated channel of the kind met in 4.3; and the calcium entering through it is the trigger for a chemical release. The module's title, "from electrical signals to chemical signals", happens at step 2.

> **Trap.** The ion that triggers exocytosis is **calcium (Ca2+)**, entering through **voltage-gated calcium channels**. Not Na+, not K+. And vesicles of small-molecule transmitters wait **near** the calcium channels, which is why release can follow an AP so quickly.

### Small-molecule versus neuropeptide release

The release of small-molecule neurotransmitters differs from the release of neuropeptides:

- **Small-molecule neurotransmitters** are typically released **in a pulse each time an AP triggers a momentary influx of Ca2+** into the button.
- **Neuropeptides** are typically released **gradually**, in response to **general increases in the level of intracellular Ca2+**, such as might occur during a **general increase in the rate of neuron firing**.

So a single AP produces a burst of small-molecule release, whereas peptide release tracks the neuron's sustained level of activity. This is the first of several differences that point to different functions for the two classes, summarised below.

### Extracellular vesicles

The 11th edition adds a qualification to the standard account: **not all vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane**. Some vesicles are released **as intact packages into the extracellular space**. These **extracellular vesicles** often carry **larger molecules (for example, proteins and RNA molecules)** between different neurons and glia in the central nervous system (see Holm, Kaiser, & Schwab, 2018; Paolicelli, Bergamini, & Rajendran, 2018). Some of the molecules they transmit can induce **persistent changes in the expression of genes through epigenetic mechanisms** (see Bakhshandeh, Kamaleddin, & Aalishah, 2017). Pinel flags this as one of the two places in the chapter where the book's emerging theme of **epigenetics** appears.

### Activation of receptors by neurotransmitter molecules

Once released, neurotransmitter molecules produce signals in postsynaptic neurons by **binding to receptors in the postsynaptic membrane**. Each **receptor** is a **protein** that contains **binding sites for only particular neurotransmitters**. It follows that a neurotransmitter can influence **only those cells that have receptors for it**; the effect of a transmitter depends as much on where its receptors are as on where it is released.

Any molecule that binds to another is referred to as its **ligand**, so a neurotransmitter is said to be a **ligand of its receptor**.

It was initially assumed that there is **only one type of receptor for each neurotransmitter**, but this has not proved to be the case. As more receptors have been identified, it has become clear that **most neurotransmitters bind to several different types of receptor**. The different types of receptor to which a particular neurotransmitter can bind are called the **receptor subtypes** for that neurotransmitter. The various subtypes for a transmitter are typically **located in different brain areas**, and they typically **respond to the neurotransmitter in different ways**. One advantage of receptor subtypes, therefore, is that they **enable one neurotransmitter to transmit different kinds of messages to different parts of the brain**. (Module 4.6 tells the story of how subtypes were discovered, through acetylcholine.)

The binding of a neurotransmitter to one of its receptor subtypes can influence a postsynaptic neuron in one of **two fundamentally different ways**, depending on whether the receptor is **ionotropic** or **metabotropic** (Figure 4.11).

### Ionotropic receptors

**Ionotropic receptors** are associated with **ligand-activated ion channels**: the receptor and the channel are, in effect, one unit. When a neurotransmitter molecule binds to an ionotropic receptor, the associated ion channel usually **opens or closes immediately**, thereby inducing an **immediate postsynaptic potential**.

The ionic mechanisms of PSPs, promised in 4.2, can now be filled in:

- In some neurons, **EPSPs** (depolarisations) occur because the neurotransmitter **opens sodium channels**, increasing the flow of **Na+ into** the neuron. Positive charge coming in makes the inside less negative.
- **IPSPs** (hyperpolarisations) often occur because the neurotransmitter **opens potassium channels or chloride (Cl−) channels**, increasing the flow of **K+ out of** the neuron or the flow of **Cl− into** it. Either positive charge leaving or negative charge entering makes the inside more negative.

### Metabotropic receptors, G proteins and second messengers

**Metabotropic receptors** are typically associated with **signal proteins** and **G proteins** (**guanosine-triphosphate-sensitive proteins**). Compared with ionotropic receptors, metabotropic receptors are:

- **more prevalent**; and their effects are
- **slower to develop**,
- **longer-lasting**,
- **more diffuse**, and
- **more varied**.

There are many different kinds of metabotropic receptor, but each is attached to a **serpentine signal protein** that **winds its way back and forth through the cell membrane seven times**. The metabotropic receptor is attached to a portion of the signal protein **outside** the neuron; the **G protein** is attached to a portion of the signal protein **inside** the neuron.

When a neurotransmitter binds to a metabotropic receptor, **a subunit of the associated G protein breaks away**. Then one of **two** things happens, depending on the particular G protein:

1. The subunit may **move along the inside surface of the membrane and bind to a nearby ion channel**, thereby inducing an **EPSP or IPSP**. (So metabotropic receptors can produce PSPs too, only indirectly and more slowly.)
2. Or it may **trigger the synthesis of a chemical called a second messenger**. Neurotransmitters are considered the **first messengers**. Once created, a second messenger **diffuses through the cytoplasm** and may influence the activities of the neuron in a variety of ways (Lyon, Taylor, & Tesmer, 2014); for example, it may **enter the nucleus and bind to the DNA**, thereby **influencing genetic expression**.

This is why a neurotransmitter's binding to a metabotropic receptor can have **radical, long-lasting effects**: through second messengers it can reach the genome and change what the neuron is made of. The 11th edition adds that there is now evidence that **ionotropic receptors can also produce second messengers** that can have enduring effects (see Valbuena & Lerma, 2016; Reiner & Levitz, 2018), so the division between the two receptor types is not absolute.

| Feature | **Ionotropic receptor** | **Metabotropic receptor** |
|---|---|---|
| Associated with | **Ligand-activated ion channel** | **Signal protein** (crosses membrane **7 times**) + **G protein** |
| Mechanism | Channel opens or closes **immediately** | **G-protein subunit breaks away**; binds an ion channel **or** triggers a **second messenger** |
| Speed of effect | **Immediate** | **Slower** to develop |
| Duration | Brief | **Longer-lasting** (can alter gene expression) |
| Spread and variety | Localised | **More diffuse, more varied** |
| Prevalence | Less prevalent | **More prevalent** |

> **Trap.** Metabotropic receptors are **more prevalent** than ionotropic receptors, even though ionotropic receptors dominate textbook diagrams. And the G-protein subunit has **two** possible actions, binding an ion channel (producing a PSP) or triggering a second messenger; an option saying metabotropic receptors act "only through second messengers" is too narrow. Note also the spelling-out of G protein as **guanosine-triphosphate-sensitive** protein; examiners occasionally ask for it.

### Epigenetics and receptor structure

Epigenetic mechanisms (Chapter 2) that act on **both ionotropic and metabotropic receptors** are of increasing interest. There is strong evidence that the **structures of both types of receptor, and thus their functionality, can be altered through epigenetic mechanisms** (see Fomsgaard et al., 2018), and **certain disorders may result from epigenetic modifications to receptor structure** (see Matosin et al., 2017). This is the second appearance of the epigenetics theme in the chapter.

### Autoreceptors

One type of metabotropic receptor warrants special mention. **Autoreceptors** are **metabotropic receptors** with **two unconventional characteristics**:

1. they **bind to their own neuron's neurotransmitter** molecules; and
2. they are located on the **presynaptic**, rather than the postsynaptic, membrane.

Their usual function is to **monitor the number of neurotransmitter molecules in the synapse**, to **reduce subsequent release when levels are high**, and to **increase subsequent release when levels are low**. An autoreceptor is a negative feedback device built into the terminal: the neuron reads its own output and adjusts it.

> **Trap.** Autoreceptors are **presynaptic** and **metabotropic**, and they respond to the neuron's **own** transmitter. Because their normal effect is *inhibitory feedback* on release, a drug that **activates** autoreceptors **reduces** release (an antagonistic effect) and a drug that **blocks** them **increases** release (an agonistic effect). This reversal is tested in 4.6.

### Small-molecule versus peptide neurotransmitters: different jobs

The differences between small-molecule and peptide neurotransmitters in their patterns of release and receptor binding suggest that they **serve different functions**:

| | Small-molecule neurotransmitters | Neuropeptides |
|---|---|---|
| Where released | Into **directed synapses** | **Diffusely** |
| Receptors | **Ionotropic**, or metabotropic receptors that **act directly on ion channels** | Virtually all **metabotropic** receptors that act through **second messengers** |
| Release pattern | **Pulse** with each AP | **Gradual**, with general increases in firing |
| Apparent function | **Rapid, brief** excitatory or inhibitory signals to **adjacent** cells | **Slow, diffuse, long-lasting** signals |

Every row of the table points the same way. Small-molecule transmitters are built for speed and precision; neuropeptides for broad, slow, modulatory influence.

### Reuptake, enzymatic degradation and recycling

If nothing intervened, a neurotransmitter molecule would remain active in the synapse, in effect **clogging that channel of communication**: the postsynaptic cell could not distinguish a new message from a lingering old one. Two mechanisms terminate synaptic messages and prevent this (Figure 4.12): **reuptake by transporters** and **enzymatic degradation**.

**Reuptake** is the **more common** of the two. The **majority of neurotransmitters**, once released, are **almost immediately drawn back into the presynaptic buttons by transporter mechanisms** (the same broad class of membrane transporters introduced in 4.1).

**Enzymatic degradation** is the alternative: some neurotransmitters are **degraded (broken apart) in the synapse by the action of enzymes**. **Enzymes** are **proteins that stimulate or inhibit biochemical reactions without being affected by them**. The key example is **acetylcholine**, which is **one of the few neurotransmitters for which enzymatic degradation is the main mechanism of synaptic deactivation**; it is broken down by the enzyme **acetylcholinesterase**.

**Recycling.** Terminal buttons are models of efficiency. Once released, neurotransmitter molecules **or their breakdown products** are drawn back into the button and **recycled, regardless of the mechanism of their deactivation**. Even the **vesicles**, once they have done their job, are drawn back into the neuron from the presynaptic membrane and used to create new vesicles (see Soykan, Maritzen, & Haucke, 2016).

> **Trap.** Reuptake, not enzymatic degradation, is the **more common** deactivation mechanism. Acetylcholine is the **exception** that is mainly degraded (by **acetylcholinesterase**), and examiners like to offer "most neurotransmitters are broken down by enzymes in the cleft" as a distractor. Also note that breakdown products are recycled too, so enzymatic degradation does not mean the material is lost.

### Glia, gap junctions and synaptic transmission

**Glial cells**, once overlooked as playing merely supportive roles in the nervous system, have been thrust to centre stage by a wave of remarkable findings. For example, **astrocytes** have been shown to:

- **release chemical transmitters**;
- **contain receptors for neurotransmitters**;
- **conduct signals**; and
- **influence synaptic transmission between neurons** (see Bazargani & Attwell, 2016; Martín et al., 2015).

It is now inappropriate, Pinel argues, to think of brain function solely in terms of neuron-to-neuron connections; neurons are only part of the story. His joke makes the point memorable: will "neuroscience" prove to be a misnomer, and is anybody for "**gliascience**"?

### Gap junctions and electrical synapses

The explosion of interest in glia has gone hand in hand with increased interest in **gap junctions**. **Gap junctions** are **narrow spaces between adjacent cells that are bridged by fine, tubular, cytoplasm-filled protein channels called connexins**. Consequently, gap junctions **connect the cytoplasm of two adjacent cells**, allowing **electrical signals and small molecules (for example, second messengers)** to pass directly from one cell to the next (Figure 4.13). Gap junctions are responsible for the existence of **electrical synapses**, which can transmit signals **much more rapidly than chemical synapses** (see Coulon & Landisman, 2017). There is no release, no diffusion across a cleft and no receptor binding to wait for; the current simply flows through.

The history of the idea is a lesson in how the choice of experimental species shapes scientific belief, and the dates are examinable:

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1950s** | Gap junctions between adjacent neurons **first reported**, but only in **invertebrates and simple vertebrates**, so gap-junction communication was assumed to be **of little significance in the mammalian brain** |
| **Early 1970s** | Gap junctions established in **mammalian (rodent) brains**; still, a major role in human brain function was **not widely entertained** |
| **1990s** | Stimulated by **several important technical developments** and the **identification of the gap junction gene**, gap junctions became a **focus of neuroscientific research** (see McCracken & Roberts, 2006) |
| **Now** | **Firmly established** that glial cells and gap junctions play **major roles in brain function** (see Rusakov et al., 2014; Coulon & Landisman, 2017; Szczupak, 2016) |

> **Trap.** Gap junctions are bridged by **connexins**, and they produce **electrical** synapses that are **faster** than chemical synapses. Do not confuse gap junctions with the **synaptic cleft** of a chemical synapse; the defining feature of a gap junction is that the **cytoplasm of the two cells is continuous**. (It is standard textbook knowledge that electrical synapses can also pass current in both directions; Pinel's emphasis is on their speed.) Dates: first reported **1950s**, in mammals **early 1970s**, research focus **1990s**.

### How astrocytes and gap junctions are distributed

The principles by which astrocytes and gap junctions are distributed in the mammalian brain provide some of the best clues to their function, and the argument here is an inference from anatomy to function.

**Gap junctions.** Cerebral gap junctions occur **between all classes of cerebral cells**, but **the majority seem to occur between cells of the same kind**. For example, many gap junctions **link astrocytes together into networks of glial cells**. And gap junctions between neurons are **particularly prevalent between inhibitory interneurons of the same type** (e.g., Lee et al., 2014). If gap junctions mostly couple like with like, their function is likely to make like cells act together: **one function of gap junctions appears to be to synchronise the activities of like cells in a particular area**.

**Astrocytes.** One aspect of astrocytic organisation suggests that astrocytes, too, synchronise the activity of cells in an area. Unlike neurons, astrocytes are **distributed evenly throughout a particular area**, with **only one astrocyte per location** and **little overlap between the projections of adjacent astrocytes**. This tiling suggests that **each astrocyte coordinates the activity of neurons in its own domain**, and with **as many as 40,000 processes**, each astrocyte has great potential to coordinate activity (see Pannasch & Rouach, 2013). Gap junctions on astrocytes tend to occur **at the end of each process**, where it contacts processes from adjacent astrocytes, which is exactly where they would need to be to link neighbouring domains into a network.

Notice the status of these conclusions: they are **inferences from distribution** ("suggests", "appears to be"), not direct demonstrations of function. They are good clues, not proof.

## 4.5 Neurotransmitters

With the general machinery of synaptic transmission in place, the chapter turns to the transmitters themselves. **Well over 100 neurotransmitters** have been identified; Pinel examines a select few. The aim of this module is narrow and examinable: for each transmitter in bold, know its **class** and at least **one function or distinguishing fact**.

### Overview of the neurotransmitter classes

The classification has two levels (Figure 4.16). The **small-molecule neurotransmitters** comprise three classes of **conventional** small-molecule transmitters, the **amino acids**, the **monoamines** and **acetylcholine**, plus a fourth group of various small-molecule transmitters often called **unconventional neurotransmitters** because their mechanisms of action are unusual. The **large-molecule neurotransmitters** form only **one** class, the **neuropeptides**.

| Class | Members discussed in the chapter |
|---|---|
| **Amino acids** | Glutamate, aspartate, glycine, GABA |
| **Monoamines: catecholamines** | Dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine |
| **Monoamines: indolamines** | Serotonin |
| **Acetylcholine** | Acetylcholine (a class by itself) |
| **Unconventional: soluble gases** | Nitric oxide, carbon monoxide |
| **Unconventional: endocannabinoids** | Anandamide |
| **Neuropeptides** (large-molecule) | Pituitary, hypothalamic, brain-gut, opioid and miscellaneous peptides |

> **Trap.** Acetylcholine is a **small-molecule** transmitter in a **class of its own**; it is not a monoamine and not an amino acid. The soluble gases and endocannabinoids are **small-molecule** transmitters, grouped as **unconventional**. And the only large-molecule class is the **neuropeptides**. Older editions sometimes listed the small-molecule classes as amino acids, monoamines, soluble gases and acetylcholine; the 11th edition puts the soluble gases together with the endocannabinoids under **unconventional neurotransmitters**.

### Amino acid neurotransmitters

The neurotransmitters in the **vast majority of fast-acting, directed synapses in the central nervous system** are **amino acids**, the molecular building blocks of proteins. The four most widely studied amino acid neurotransmitters are:

- **glutamate**;
- **aspartate**;
- **glycine**; and
- **gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)**.

The first three are **common in the proteins we consume**, whereas **GABA is synthesised by a simple modification of the structure of glutamate**. There is an elegant irony in that last fact, because the two have opposite roles:

- **Glutamate** is the **most prevalent excitatory neurotransmitter** in the mammalian central nervous system.
- **GABA** is the **most prevalent inhibitory neurotransmitter**; however, it has **excitatory effects at some synapses** (see Watanabe, Fukuda, & Nabekura, 2014).

The 11th edition gives no specific functions for aspartate and glycine. It is standard textbook knowledge that glycine is generally inhibitory (notably in the spinal cord and brain stem) and aspartate generally excitatory, which is consistent with the book but not stated in it.

> **Trap.** "GABA is the main excitatory transmitter" and "glutamate is synthesised from GABA" are both reversals: **glutamate** is the main **excitatory** CNS transmitter, **GABA** the main **inhibitory** one, and **GABA is made from glutamate**. Also note Pinel's qualification that GABA is excitatory **at some synapses**; an option claiming that GABA is "always inhibitory" is too strong for the 11th edition.

### Monoamine neurotransmitters

**Monoamines** are another class of small-molecule neurotransmitter. Each is **synthesised from a single amino acid**, hence the name *monoamine* (one amine). Compared with the amino acid neurotransmitters, monoamines are **slightly larger** and their effects tend to be **more diffuse**.

Their anatomy explains the diffuseness. The monoamines are present in **small groups of neurons whose cell bodies are, for the most part, located in the brain stem**. These neurons often have **highly branched axons with many varicosities** (the **string-of-beads synapses** of 4.4), from which monoamine neurotransmitters are **diffusely released into the extracellular fluid** (Figures 4.9 and 4.14). A small number of cells, each broadcasting widely, can influence large regions of the brain. Figure 4.14 is a photograph of string-of-beads **noradrenergic** fibres, in which the bright beaded structures are the sites where **norepinephrine** is stored and released.

There are **four monoamine neurotransmitters**: **dopamine**, **epinephrine**, **norepinephrine** and **serotonin**. They are subdivided on the basis of structure into two groups, **catecholamines** and **indolamines**.

### Catecholamines and their synthesis

**Dopamine, norepinephrine and epinephrine are catecholamines.** Each is **synthesised from the amino acid tyrosine**, and their synthesis is a single chain in which each transmitter is made from the one before (Figure 4.15):

**Tyrosine → L-dopa → dopamine → norepinephrine → epinephrine**

The logic of the chain is that each type of neuron stops at a different point because it has a different set of enzymes:

- **Tyrosine is converted to L-dopa, which in turn is converted to dopamine.** A dopamine-releasing neuron stops here.
- **Neurons that release norepinephrine have an extra enzyme** (one not present in dopaminergic neurons) that **converts the dopamine in them to norepinephrine**.
- **Neurons that release epinephrine** have **all the enzymes present in norepinephrine neurons, plus an extra enzyme that converts norepinephrine to epinephrine**.

Now the opening case clicks into place. **L-dopa is the step just before dopamine** in this chain, which is why giving L-dopa to Mr. d'Orta allowed his surviving dopaminergic neurons to make more dopamine. Dopamine itself cannot get past the blood-brain barrier; its immediate precursor can.

> **Trap.** The order is **tyrosine → L-dopa → dopamine → norepinephrine → epinephrine**. Norepinephrine is made **from dopamine**, and epinephrine **from norepinephrine**, not the other way round. Students who remember "epinephrine is the famous one" sometimes put it first.

### Serotonin, an indolamine, and the naming of adrenergic neurons

In contrast to the other monoamines, **serotonin** (also called **5-hydroxytryptamine**, or **5-HT**) is **synthesised from the amino acid tryptophan** and is classified as an **indolamine**.

| Monoamine | Subgroup | Precursor amino acid |
|---|---|---|
| Dopamine | Catecholamine | Tyrosine |
| Norepinephrine | Catecholamine | Tyrosine (via dopamine) |
| Epinephrine | Catecholamine | Tyrosine (via norepinephrine) |
| **Serotonin (5-HT)** | **Indolamine** | **Tryptophan** |

**Naming.** Neurons that release norepinephrine are called **noradrenergic**; those that release epinephrine are called **adrenergic**. Pinel gives two reasons for these names. First, epinephrine and norepinephrine used to be called **adrenaline** and **noradrenaline** by many scientists, until a drug company registered **Adrenalin** as a brand name. Second, try saying "norepinephrinergic".

> **Trap.** Serotonin is the **only indolamine** among the four, and it comes from **tryptophan**; the catecholamines come from **tyrosine**. "Adrenergic" refers to **epinephrine** (adrenaline) and "noradrenergic" to **norepinephrine** (noradrenaline). Adrenaline and epinephrine are the **same** molecule under two names; so are noradrenaline and norepinephrine.

### Acetylcholine

**Acetylcholine** (abbreviated **ACh**) is a small-molecule neurotransmitter that is, in Pinel's joke, like a professor who is late for a lecture: it is **in a class by itself**. It is created by **adding an acetyl group to a choline molecule**.

Acetylcholine is the neurotransmitter:

- at **neuromuscular junctions** (the synapses between motor neurons and skeletal muscle);
- at **many of the synapses in the autonomic nervous system**; and
- at synapses in **several parts of the central nervous system**.

Recall from 4.4 that acetylcholine is **broken down in the synapse by the enzyme acetylcholinesterase**, which makes it one of the few transmitters whose main means of deactivation is enzymatic degradation. Neurons that release acetylcholine are said to be **cholinergic**.

Because acetylcholine is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, every voluntary movement depends on it. That single fact explains the dramatic effects of curare and Botox in 4.6.

### Unconventional neurotransmitters: the soluble gases

The **unconventional neurotransmitters** act in ways different from those neuroscientists have come to think of as typical. One class, the **soluble-gas neurotransmitters**, includes **nitric oxide** and **carbon monoxide**. Compare each property with the conventional pattern you learned in 4.4 (synthesis in advance, storage in vesicles, release by exocytosis, binding to membrane receptors, reuptake or degradation):

1. **No storage.** They are **produced in the neural cytoplasm** and **immediately diffuse through the cell membrane** into the extracellular fluid, and then **into nearby cells**.
2. **No membrane receptors needed.** They pass easily through cell membranes because they are **soluble in lipids**. Once inside another cell, they **stimulate the production of a second messenger**.
3. **Very short-lived.** In **a few seconds** they are **deactivated by being converted to other molecules**. For this reason they are **difficult to study**: they exist for only a few seconds.

Soluble-gas neurotransmitters have been shown to be involved in **retrograde transmission**. At some synapses they transmit **feedback signals from the postsynaptic neuron back to the presynaptic neuron**. The function of retrograde transmission seems to be to **regulate the activity of presynaptic neurons** (see Iremonger, Wamsteeker Cusulin, & Bains, 2013).

> **Trap.** **Retrograde** transmission means **postsynaptic → presynaptic**, against the usual direction of chemical transmission. Do not confuse it with **antidromic** conduction (4.3), which is an AP travelling backwards along an *axon*. Retrograde refers to signalling *across a synapse*; antidromic to conduction *within a neuron*.

### Unconventional neurotransmitters: the endocannabinoids

Another class of unconventional neurotransmitters is the **endocannabinoids**: neurotransmitters that are **similar to delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)**, the main **psychoactive** (producing psychological effects) constituent of **marijuana**.

- **So far, two endocannabinoids have been discovered** (see Di Marzo, Stella, & Zimmer, 2015). The most widely studied is **anandamide**, from the Sanskrit word **ananda**, meaning "**eternal bliss**". (The other, not named in the chapter, is standardly identified as 2-arachidonoylglycerol, 2-AG.)
- Like the soluble gases, the endocannabinoids are **produced immediately before they are released**; they are not stored in vesicles.
- They are **synthesised from fatty compounds in the cell membrane**.
- They tend to be **released from the dendrites and cell body**, not from terminal buttons.
- They tend to have most of their effects **on presynaptic neurons, inhibiting subsequent synaptic transmission** (see Ohno-Shosaku & Kano, 2014; Younts & Castillo, 2014).

Put those last two points together and you can see that endocannabinoids are also, in effect, **retrograde messengers**: released by the receiving neuron, acting back on the sending neuron, and turning its output down.

### Neuropeptides

**About 100 neuropeptides** have been identified. The actions of each depend on its **amino acid sequence** (recall from 4.4 that neuropeptides are chains of 3 to 36 amino acids). It is usual to group neuropeptide transmitters **loosely** into **five categories**. Three of these categories acknowledge that neuropeptides often function in **multiple capacities**, not just as neurotransmitters, by naming them after where they were *first* found:

| Category | Defined by |
|---|---|
| **Pituitary peptides** | First identified as **hormones released by the pituitary** |
| **Hypothalamic peptides** | First identified as **hormones released by the hypothalamus** |
| **Brain-gut peptides** | First **discovered in the gut** |
| **Opioid peptides** | **Similar in structure to the active ingredients of opium** |
| **Miscellaneous peptides** | A **catch-all** for neuropeptide transmitters that fit none of the other four |

The grouping is historical rather than functional, which is why Pinel calls it loose. Cholecystokinin (CCK), the satiety peptide of Chapter 12, is a familiar brain-gut peptide, and the enkephalins and endorphins of 4.6 are opioid peptides.

> **Trap.** Two counts are easy to confuse: **well over 100 neurotransmitters** in total, and **about 100 neuropeptides**. Five neuropeptide categories, of which **three** are named for where the peptide was first found (pituitary, hypothalamic, brain-gut). The opioid category is defined by **structural similarity to opium's active ingredients**, not by location.

## 4.6 Pharmacology of Synaptic Transmission and Behaviour

The reason for learning so much about neurotransmitters is that they play a key role in how the brain works, and therefore in behaviour. The chapter began on a behavioural note, with the pathological behaviour of Roberto Garcia d'Orta, which resulted from a Parkinson's-disease-related disruption of his **dopamine** function. This last module returns to behaviour.

Most of the methods biopsychologists use to study the behavioural effects of neurotransmitters are **pharmacological** (involving drugs). The research logic is straightforward: administer to human or nonhuman subjects drugs that have **particular effects on particular neurotransmitters**, and then assess the effects of those drugs on behaviour. If a drug that selectively changes transmission at one kind of synapse changes a behaviour, that kind of synapse is implicated in the behaviour.

### Agonists and antagonists

Drugs have **two fundamentally different kinds of effect** on synaptic transmission: they **facilitate** it or they **inhibit** it.

- Drugs that **facilitate** the effects of a particular neurotransmitter are said to be **agonists** of that neurotransmitter.
- Drugs that **inhibit** the effects of a particular neurotransmitter are said to be its **antagonists**.

Notice that the definitions are about the **effect on transmission**, not about the mechanism. A drug can be an agonist by increasing synthesis, by increasing release, by blocking reuptake, by mimicking the transmitter at its receptor, and so on; all of these facilitate transmission. That is why the next step is to list the places where a drug can act.

> **Trap.** "Agonist" does not mean "binds to and activates the receptor" in Pinel's usage; that is only one of **six** agonistic mechanisms in Figure 4.18. Any drug that **facilitates** the effects of a transmitter, by whatever route, is its agonist. Likewise, "antagonist" is broader than "receptor blocker"; a **receptor blocker** is one particular kind of antagonist.

### How drugs influence synaptic transmission: the seven steps

Although synthesis, release and action vary from one neurotransmitter to another, **seven general steps** are common to most neurotransmitters (Figure 4.17). They are the five aspects of 4.4 rearranged into a single sequence, with two extra regulatory steps inserted:

1. **Synthesis** of the neurotransmitter: molecules are synthesised from **precursors** under the influence of **enzymes**.
2. **Storage in vesicles.**
3. **Breakdown in the cytoplasm** of any neurotransmitter that **leaks from the vesicles**: leaked molecules are destroyed by **degrading enzymes**.
4. **Exocytosis**: APs cause vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release their contents into the synapse.
5. **Inhibitory feedback via autoreceptors**: released molecules bind with autoreceptors and inhibit subsequent release.
6. **Activation of postsynaptic receptors.**
7. **Deactivation**, by either **reuptake** or **enzymatic degradation**.

Each step is a potential point of drug action, and at each step a drug can push in either direction.

### Agonistic and antagonistic mechanisms

Figure 4.18 sets out some of the mechanisms. Read the two columns side by side, because almost every agonistic mechanism has an antagonistic mirror image:

| Step | **Agonistic drug effect** (facilitates transmission) | **Antagonistic drug effect** (inhibits transmission) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Synthesis | Drug **increases synthesis** of the neurotransmitter (e.g., by increasing the amount of **precursor**) | Drug **blocks synthesis** (e.g., by **destroying synthesising enzymes**) |
| 3. Breakdown of leaked transmitter | Drug **increases the number of transmitter molecules** by **destroying degrading enzymes** | Drug **causes transmitter to leak from the vesicles** and be **destroyed by degrading enzymes** |
| 4. Release | Drug **increases release** from terminal buttons | Drug **blocks release** from terminal buttons |
| 5. Autoreceptors | Drug **binds to autoreceptors and blocks** their inhibitory effect on release | Drug **activates autoreceptors** and **inhibits** release |
| 6. Postsynaptic receptors | Drug **binds to postsynaptic receptors** and either **activates** them or **increases the effect** of the transmitter on them | Drug is a **receptor blocker**: it **binds to postsynaptic receptors** and **blocks** the effect of the transmitter |
| 7. Deactivation | Drug **blocks deactivation** by **blocking degradation or reuptake** | (none shown) |

The two most frequently examined entries are at the receptor. Some **agonists** of a particular neurotransmitter bind to postsynaptic receptors and **activate** them. Some **antagonists**, called **receptor blockers**, bind to postsynaptic receptors **without activating them**, and in doing so **block the access of the usual neurotransmitter**. A receptor blocker is like a key that fits the lock but will not turn, and sits there so that the right key cannot get in.

Now apply the table to drugs already met. **L-dopa**, in the opening case, is an agonist at step 1: it increases synthesis by supplying the precursor. The three lines of research below add a receptor blocker (atropine), another receptor blocker (curare), a release blocker (Botox), and dopamine antagonists (the antipsychotics). As standard examples not given in this chapter: drugs that block reuptake (such as cocaine, which Pinel lists below as a dopamine agonist) act at step 7, and drugs that inhibit degrading enzymes act at steps 3 or 7.

> **Trap.** The autoreceptor row reverses intuition. **Activating** autoreceptors is **antagonistic** (it turns release *down*); **blocking** autoreceptors is **agonistic** (it removes the brake, so release goes *up*). Also note the step-3 row: destroying the enzymes that break down *leaked* transmitter is agonistic, whereas making transmitter *leak* so that those enzymes destroy it is antagonistic.

### Wrinkles and darts: discovery of receptor subtypes

It was originally assumed that there was **one kind of receptor for each neurotransmitter**. This notion was dispelled by research on **acetylcholine receptors** (see Changeux, 2013; Papke, 2014).

**The finding.** Some acetylcholine receptors bind to **nicotine** (a CNS stimulant and the major psychoactive ingredient of tobacco), whereas other acetylcholine receptors bind to **muscarine** (a poisonous substance found in some mushrooms). These two kinds of acetylcholine receptor therefore became known as **nicotinic receptors** and **muscarinic receptors**. The logic is worth noticing: if two different drugs each mimic acetylcholine but each acts only at *some* acetylcholine receptors, the receptors cannot all be the same.

**The elaboration.** It was then discovered that nicotinic and muscarinic receptors are **distributed differently** in the nervous system, have **different modes of action**, and consequently have **different behavioural effects**:

| | **Nicotinic receptors** | **Muscarinic receptors** |
|---|---|---|
| Named after | Nicotine (tobacco) | Muscarine (poisonous mushrooms) |
| Found in CNS? | Yes | Yes |
| Main PNS location | Many at the **junctions between motor neurons and muscle fibres** (neuromuscular junctions) | Many in the **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** |
| Receptor type | **Ionotropic** | **Metabotropic** |
| Classic blocker in this chapter | **Curare** | **Atropine** |

> **Trap.** **Nicotinic = ionotropic = neuromuscular junction (curare blocks)**. **Muscarinic = metabotropic = autonomic nervous system (atropine blocks)**. Both kinds occur in **both** the CNS and the PNS; the location contrast is about where *many* of each are found in the PNS. Swapping either pairing is the commonest error.

Many drugs used in research and medicine are extracts of plants long used for medicinal and recreational purposes, and the cholinergic agonists and antagonists illustrate this well.

**Belladonna and atropine.** The ancient Greeks consumed extracts of the **belladonna** plant to treat stomach ailments and to make themselves more attractive: Greek women believed that the **pupil-dilating** effects of the extracts enhanced their beauty (*belladonna* means "beautiful lady"). **Atropine**, the main active ingredient of belladonna, is a **receptor blocker** that exerts its antagonist effect by **binding to muscarinic receptors**, thereby blocking the effects of acetylcholine on them. Its effects divide neatly by location:

- the **pupil-dilating** effects of atropine are mediated by its antagonist actions on **muscarinic receptors in the ANS**;
- the **disruptive effects of large doses of atropine on memory** are mediated by its antagonistic effect on **muscarinic receptors in the CNS**.

That second effect has historical importance: the disruptive effect of high doses of atropine on memory was **one of the earliest clues that cholinergic mechanisms may play a role in memory** (taken up in Chapter 11).

**Curare.** South Americans have long used **curare**, an extract of a certain class of woody vines, on the tips of the darts they use to kill game. Like atropine, curare is a **receptor blocker at cholinergic synapses**, but it acts at **nicotinic receptors**. By binding to nicotinic receptors, curare **blocks transmission at neuromuscular junctions**, thus **paralysing** its recipients and **killing them by blocking their respiration** (the breathing muscles are skeletal muscles too). Surprisingly, the active ingredient of curare is sometimes administered to human patients **during surgery** to ensure that their muscles do not contract during an incision. When it is used this way, the patient's breathing must be **artificially maintained by a respirator**.

**Botox.** **Botox** (short for **botulinum toxin**; the book spells it "Botulinium"), a **neurotoxin released by a bacterium often found in spoiled food**, is another **nicotinic antagonist**, but its **mechanism of action is different**: it **blocks the release of acetylcholine at neuromuscular junctions**. It is therefore a **deadly poison**. Injected in **minute doses at specific sites**, however, it has applications in **medicine** (e.g., **reduction of tremors**) and **cosmetics** (e.g., **reduction of wrinkles**; Figure 4.19). This is the "wrinkles" of the section's title, as curare's darts are the "darts".

> **Trap.** **Atropine and curare are both receptor blockers**, at muscarinic and nicotinic receptors respectively. **Botox is not a receptor blocker**: it is an antagonist that **blocks acetylcholine release** (step 4 in Figure 4.18). If a question asks which of these acts presynaptically, the answer is Botox. Also, "atropine dilates the pupils by acting in the CNS" is wrong; the pupil effect is **ANS** muscarinic, the memory effect **CNS** muscarinic.

### Pleasure and pain: discovery of endogenous opioids

**Opium**, the sticky resin obtained from the seed pods of the **opium poppy**, has been used by humans **since prehistoric times** for its pleasurable effects. **Morphine**, its major psychoactive ingredient, is **addictive**. But morphine also has a good side: it is an effective **analgesic** (painkiller), as Chapters 7 and 15 discuss.

**The receptor discovery.** In the **1970s** it was discovered that opioid drugs such as morphine **bind effectively to receptors in the brain**. These receptors were generally found in the **hypothalamus and other limbic areas**, but they were **most concentrated** in the area of the brain stem around the **cerebral aqueduct** (the duct connecting the third and fourth ventricles). This part of the brain stem is called the **periaqueductal gray (PAG)**. **Microinjection of morphine into the PAG**, or even **electrical stimulation of the PAG**, produces **strong analgesia**.

**The inference.** The existence of selective opioid receptors in the brain raised an interesting question: why are they there? They are certainly not there so that, once humans discovered opium, opioids would have somewhere to bind. The existence of a receptor for a plant chemical suggested that **opioid chemicals occur naturally in the brain**, and that possibility triggered an **intensive search** for them. This is a clear example of reasoning from a receptor to its natural ligand: a lock implies a key.

**What was found.** **Several families of endogenous** (occurring naturally within the body) **opioids** have been discovered:

- first discovered were the **enkephalins** (meaning "**in the head**");
- another major family is the **endorphins** (a contraction of "**endogenous morphine**").

**All endogenous opioid neurotransmitters are neuropeptides, and their receptors are metabotropic.** They belong to the opioid-peptide category of 4.5.

> **Trap.** Pinel does not name the researchers. In standard histories, opiate receptors in the brain were demonstrated by **Candace Pert and Solomon Snyder (1973)**, and the enkephalins were isolated by **John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz (1975)**. Know that the **enkephalins came first** and the **endorphins** second in Pinel's account, that "enkephalin" means **"in the head"** and "endorphin" is from **"endogenous morphine"**, that the opioid receptors are most concentrated in the **periaqueductal gray** around the **cerebral aqueduct**, and that endogenous opioids are **neuropeptides** acting on **metabotropic** receptors.

### Tremors and mental illness: discovery of antipsychotic drugs

Arguably the most important event in the treatment of mental illness has been the **development of drugs for the treatment of schizophrenia**. Surprisingly, **Parkinson's disease**, the disease from which Roberto Garcia d'Orta suffered, played a major role in their discovery. Follow the reasoning as a sequence of clues.

**Clue 1: two accidental discoveries.** In the **1950s**, **largely by chance**, two drugs were found to have **antipsychotic** effects; that is, they **reduced the severity of psychosis**, the major symptom of schizophrenia. The two drugs were **not related structurally**. (Pinel names them in Chapter 18; they were **chlorpromazine** and **reserpine**.)

**Clue 2: a curious shared pattern.** Despite their structural differences, both drugs produced the same curious pattern of effects. **Neither appeared to have any antipsychotic activity until patients had been taking it for about 3 weeks**, at which point the drug **also started to produce mild Parkinsonian symptoms** (for example, **tremor-at-rest**). Two structurally unrelated drugs sharing both a delay and a side effect suggests a shared mechanism, and the side effect pointed to which one.

**Clue 3: what was already known.** Researchers put this together with two other findings:

1. **Parkinson's disease is associated with the degeneration of a main dopamine pathway** in the brain (the substantia nigra to striatum pathway of the opening case); and
2. **dopamine agonists** (for example, **cocaine and amphetamines**) produce a **transient condition that resembles schizophrenia**.

**The conclusion.** Together, these findings suggested that **schizophrenia might be caused by excessive activity at dopamine synapses**, and therefore that **potent dopamine antagonists would be effective in its treatment**.

Look at the structure of this argument, because it is an exam favourite and because its strength should not be overstated. It is a **convergence of indirect evidence**: if Parkinsonian symptoms mean too little dopamine activity, then drugs that produce Parkinsonian symptoms are probably reducing dopamine activity; if those same drugs relieve psychosis, and drugs that increase dopamine activity mimic psychosis, then psychosis may involve too much dopamine activity. Each link is an inference, not a direct measurement, which is why Pinel says the findings "suggested" the hypothesis. Chapter 18 takes the story further.

Pinel adds a caution: it would be a mistake to think that antipsychotic drugs **cure** schizophrenia or that they **help in every case**. They do, however, **improve the quality of life of many individuals with schizophrenia**.

> **Trap.** The antipsychotic effect appeared after **about 3 weeks**, together with **Parkinsonian** side effects such as **tremor-at-rest**. The resulting hypothesis is that schizophrenia involves **excessive** dopamine activity, so the drugs are dopamine **antagonists**. Cocaine and amphetamines are dopamine **agonists** that **mimic** schizophrenia-like symptoms. An option saying the antipsychotics were designed from dopamine theory is wrong: they were found **largely by chance**, and the theory came **from** them.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the six modules. If you can trace each one, the chapter becomes a single argument rather than a list of facts.

1. **Graded in, all-or-none out, rate as the code.** PSPs are graded and summate (4.2); the AP is all-or-none (4.2, 4.3); so stimulus intensity is carried by **firing rate**, which the refractory periods make possible (4.3).
2. **Stored energy released by gates.** The Na+ gradient is energy held back by **closed channels** (4.1); the AP is that energy released by **voltage-gated** Na+ channels and reset by voltage-gated K+ channels (4.3), which is what the mouse-trap metaphor depicts; at the button, **voltage-gated Ca2+ channels** do the same job for release (4.4).
3. **Electrical to chemical to electrical.** Electrical within the neuron, chemical across most synapses, electrical again at the receptor, either immediately (ionotropic) or via G proteins (metabotropic). Gap junctions carry the electrical signal straight across.
4. **Speed versus reach.** PSPs are fast but fade; APs are slower but do not fade; myelin buys back speed. The same trade-off reappears chemically: small-molecule transmitters on ionotropic receptors are fast, brief and local; neuropeptides on metabotropic receptors are slow, diffuse and long-lasting.
5. **Models are simplifications, and several have been revised.** APs arise at the **axon initial segment**, not the hillock; most brain neurons have **no or short axons**; the Hodgkin-Huxley model needs **caution** and conduction may be partly **mechanical**; "one receptor per transmitter" fell to receptor subtypes, "one transmitter per neuron" to coexistence, and the neuron-only view to **tripartite synapses**, gap junctions and active astrocytes. Many of the chapter's traps are exactly these revisions.
6. **Every step is a drug target, and Parkinson's disease frames the chapter.** The chapter opens with a dopamine deficit treated by a precursor (L-dopa, an agonist acting on synthesis) and closes with dopamine antagonists discovered because they produced Parkinsonian symptoms: one transmitter, one pathway, approached from opposite directions.

**Themes revisited.** Pinel's own summary links the chapter to the book's themes. **Clinical implications**: the Lizard case, then curare, Botox, endogenous opioids and antipsychotics. **Thinking creatively**: **four metaphors**, the **social network** (neural network communication), **ripples on a pond** (PSP summation), the **firing gun** (action potentials) and **mouse traps on a wobbly shelf** (axonal conduction). **Evolutionary perspective**: implicit throughout, because almost all neurophysiological research uses the neurons and synapses of **nonhuman** subjects. The emerging theme of **epigenetics** appears with **extracellular vesicles** and with **epigenetic modification of receptors**.

## Worked example

*A 26-year-old man with schizophrenia was started on an antipsychotic drug 25 days ago. His voices have only begun to quieten this week. Over the same week his hands have started to shake while resting in his lap, his face looks "frozen" and unblinking, and he moves slowly and stiffly. His mother, who nursed her own father through Parkinson's disease, asks: Why did the drug take so long? Has he now got Parkinson's disease? Should he be given L-dopa, as her father was? Would it not be simpler to give him dopamine?*

**What is the drug doing?** On the account in 4.6, antipsychotics are **dopamine antagonists**, most directly **receptor blockers** (Figure 4.18): they bind dopamine receptors without activating them. The rationale is the hypothesis that psychosis reflects **excessive activity at dopamine synapses**.

**Why did it take so long?** The delay is the documented pattern: neither of the original 1950s drugs showed any antipsychotic activity until patients had taken it for **about 3 weeks**. The chapter reports the delay as an observation without explaining it, and a good answer does not invent a mechanism.

**Has he got Parkinson's disease?** He has **Parkinsonian symptoms** (tremor-at-rest, a reptilian-stare-like face, rigidity, slowness), the same as Mr. d'Orta's, and they point to the same functional problem: **too little dopamine action in the striatum**. But the failing step differs. In Parkinson's disease the **substantia nigra neurons are dying**, so less dopamine is delivered; here the neurons are intact and the drug is **blocking the receptors** dopamine acts on. Nor is the side effect an accident unrelated to the drug's action: Parkinsonian symptoms appearing **at the same time as** the antipsychotic effect are what led researchers to the dopamine hypothesis in the first place.

**Should he be given L-dopa?** L-dopa is a dopamine **agonist** acting by **increasing synthesis**, which is why it helped Mr. d'Orta, whose problem was a shortage of dopamine. Here the therapeutic goal is to **reduce** dopamine activity, and the chapter supplies the warning: **dopamine agonists such as cocaine and amphetamines produce a transient schizophrenia-like condition**. L-dopa would pull against the treatment.

**Would dopamine tablets be simpler?** No: dopamine **does not readily penetrate the blood-brain barrier**, which is why L-dopa is used even in Parkinson's disease, and if it did get in it would raise the same objection as L-dopa.

**An extension.** It is standard clinical practice (not in this chapter) to treat antipsychotic-induced Parkinsonian symptoms with **anticholinergic** drugs that block **muscarinic** receptors. With **atropine** as the model muscarinic blocker, 4.6 lets you predict their side effects: **dilated pupils** (ANS muscarinic receptors) and, at high doses, **memory disruption** (CNS muscarinic receptors).

**An exam variant of the same logic.** A toxin is applied to a nerve-muscle preparation. Stimulating the motor nerve no longer produces contraction, though APs still reach the terminals; acetylcholine applied directly to the muscle **does** produce contraction. Conduction is intact and the **nicotinic receptors** work, so the failure is in **release**: the profile of **Botox**. Had directly applied acetylcholine also failed, the receptors would be blocked: the profile of **curare**. Testing each step in turn is the seven-step scheme of Figure 4.17 put to work.

## MCQ traps

1. **Resting potential** about **-70 mV**, inside **negative**; "polarised" = not zero; both electrodes outside = **0 mV**; microelectrode tip **< 1/1000 mm**.
2. More **Na+ outside**, more **K+ inside**; at rest **Na+ channels are closed** and **K+ channels open**, K+ being largely held in by the negative interior.
3. **Sodium-potassium pump** = **3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in**. The book's own "Scan Your Brain" item words it backwards; trust the text and Figure 4.1.
4. **Hodgkin & Huxley (1950s)**: leaks matched by active transport **at the same rate**, so the resting potential is a **dynamic** equilibrium; **Nobel 1963**.
5. **Edition clash:** the 11th edition has **three factors** and only Na+ and K+; Cl− equilibrium, protein anions, "differential permeability", **-90 mV** for K+ and **120 mV** pressure on Na+ are **older-edition** content.
6. **Depolarise** = **decrease** the membrane potential (-70 → -67 mV) = EPSP; **hyperpolarise** = **increase** it (-70 → -72 mV) = IPSP.
7. PSPs are **graded**, **rapid** (effectively instantaneous) and **decremental** (a couple of millimetres); **duration** is not **speed**.
8. APs arise at the **axon initial segment**; the **axon hillock** is the **old** belief (Kuba, Adachi & Ohmori, 2014; Tian et al., 2014).
9. **Threshold** about **-65 mV**; **AP** about **-70 to +50 mV**, about **1 ms**, **all-or-none**; some neurons show longer, lower or multi-spike APs.
10. **Spatial** = different synapses, same time (**3** combinations, EPSP + IPSP cancel); **temporal** = same synapse, rapid succession (**2**), possible because PSPs **outlast** their stimuli.
11. Distant dendritic synapses are not necessarily weak: **some neurons amplify them** (Adrian et al., 2014; Araya, 2014).
12. **Rising phase = Na+ in**; **repolarisation = K+ out** after Na+ channels close at about **1 ms**; **hyperpolarisation** because K+ channels close **gradually**. Not the pump.
13. After an AP, resting concentrations return mainly by **random ion movement**; the **pumps play only a minor role**.
14. **Absolute refractory** about **1 to 2 ms** (no AP possible); **relative** = AP only with **higher-than-normal** stimulation.
15. Refractory periods give **one-way conduction** and **rate coding**; maximum about **1,000 firings per second**. Stronger stimulus = higher **rate**, never a bigger AP.
16. APs are **nondecremental** but **slower** than PSPs, because they are **regenerated** at each voltage-gated Na+ channel.
17. **Orthodromic** = towards the terminals (normal); **antidromic** = towards the cell body; midpoint stimulation gives **two** APs. Backpropagating dendritic potentials are **graded** and linked to **plasticity** (Stuart & Spruston, 2015).
18. **Saltatory conduction**: Na+ channels **concentrated at nodes of Ranvier**, AP regenerated **only at nodes**; *saltare* = to skip or jump; myelin disease = **multiple sclerosis**.
19. **Speed** depends on **diameter** and **myelination**: up to **60 m/s** (about 134 mph) in large myelinated human motor neurons, about **1 m/s** in small unmyelinated axons; "100 m/s" was the older edition's **cat** figure.
20. **Most** mammalian brain neurons have **no or very short axons**, and many show **no APs** (graded potentials only).
21. **Hodgkin-Huxley model**: early **1950s**, **squid motor neurons**, **Nobel 1963**; apply to brain neurons **with great caution**; **mechanical impulses** (Holland, De Regt & Drukarch, 2019; Fox, 2018).
22. **Tripartite synapse** = **two neurons + an astrocyte**; **most** brain synapses.
23. **Axoaxonic** = presynaptic facilitation/inhibition, selective for **one synapse**; **dendrodendritic** = often **bidirectional**; **axomyelenic** = axon onto **oligodendrocyte myelin**.
24. **Neuropeptides**: **3 to 36** amino acids; made on **cell-body ribosomes**, carried by **microtubules** at about **40 cm/day**; **larger** vesicles. Small molecules are made **in the button**.
25. **Coexistence** overturned "one neuron, one transmitter"; the first type found was **peptide + small-molecule**.
26. **Exocytosis** is triggered by **Ca2+** through **voltage-gated calcium channels**; small molecules released in a **pulse** per AP, peptides **gradually**.
27. **Metabotropic** receptors are **more prevalent**; slower, longer-lasting, more diffuse, more varied; **seven-pass** signal protein; the G-protein subunit binds an **ion channel** or triggers a **second messenger**.
28. **Ionotropic EPSP** = Na+ in; **IPSP** = K+ out or **Cl− in**. Ionotropic receptors can **also** make second messengers.
29. **Autoreceptors**: **presynaptic**, **metabotropic**, own transmitter; **activating** them is **antagonistic**, **blocking** them **agonistic**.
30. **Reuptake** is the **commoner** deactivation; **acetylcholine** is one of the **few** mainly **degraded** (**acetylcholinesterase**).
31. **Gap junctions**: **connexins**, continuous **cytoplasm**, **electrical synapses** faster than chemical ones; **1950s** invertebrates, **early 1970s** mammals, **1990s** research focus.
32. **Astrocytes**: up to **40,000 processes**, **one per location**; gap junctions mostly couple **like cells**, suggesting **synchronisation**.
33. **Glutamate** = main **excitatory**; **GABA** = main **inhibitory**, **made from glutamate**, **excitatory at some synapses**.
34. **Tyrosine → L-dopa → dopamine → norepinephrine → epinephrine**; **serotonin (5-HT)** = the **indolamine**, from **tryptophan**.
35. **Adrenergic** = epinephrine, **noradrenergic** = norepinephrine (from adrenaline/noradrenaline, dropped after **Adrenalin** became a brand name).
36. **Acetylcholine**: a **class by itself** (acetyl + choline); **neuromuscular junctions**, many **ANS** synapses, parts of the **CNS**.
37. **Soluble gases** (nitric oxide, carbon monoxide): **lipid-soluble**, not stored, second messengers, last **seconds**, **retrograde** transmission (not the same as **antidromic**).
38. **Endocannabinoids**: **two** discovered; **anandamide**, Sanskrit *ananda*, "**eternal bliss**"; from **dendrites and cell body**; **inhibit** presynaptic neurons.
39. **Well over 100** neurotransmitters but **about 100** neuropeptides, in **five** loose categories.
40. **Agonist** = anything that **facilitates** a transmitter (six mechanisms in Figure 4.18); a **receptor blocker** is one kind of **antagonist**.
41. **Nicotinic** = **ionotropic**, neuromuscular junction, blocked by **curare**; **muscarinic** = **metabotropic**, ANS, blocked by **atropine**.
42. **Atropine** (**belladonna**, "beautiful lady"): pupils via **ANS**, memory via **CNS** muscarinic receptors.
43. **Botox** **blocks acetylcholine release**; it is **not** a receptor blocker.
44. Opioid receptors densest in the **PAG**, around the **cerebral aqueduct**; **enkephalins** ("in the head") found **before endorphins** ("endogenous morphine"); all **neuropeptides** on **metabotropic** receptors.
45. **Antipsychotics**: **1950s**, **by chance**, two **unrelated** drugs, effect after about **3 weeks** with **Parkinsonian** symptoms; dopamine agonists (**cocaine, amphetamines**) mimic schizophrenia; hence **excess dopamine**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Tremor-at-rest** | Tremor worst when the limb is idle; Mr. d'Orta's first symptom |
| **Reptilian stare** | Unblinking, wide-open eyes in a motionless face, typical of Parkinson's disease |
| **Substantia nigra / striatum** | Dopamine neurons that die in Parkinson's disease / the movement structure they supply |
| **L-dopa** | Dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the brain |
| **Membrane potential** | Difference in electrical charge between the inside and outside of a cell |
| **Microelectrodes** | Intracellular electrodes with tips under 1/1000 mm |
| **Resting potential** | Steady membrane potential of a resting neuron, about -70 mV |
| **Polarised** | Having a non-zero membrane potential |
| **Ions** | Charged particles into which salts separate in solution |
| **Ion channels** | Membrane pores specialised for particular ions |
| **Sodium-potassium pumps** | Exchange 3 Na+ inside for 2 K+ outside |
| **Transporters** | Membrane mechanisms that actively move ions or molecules across the membrane |
| **Postsynaptic potentials (PSPs)** | Disturbances of the resting potential produced by synaptic input |
| **Depolarise / hyperpolarise** | Decrease / increase the membrane potential |
| **EPSPs / IPSPs** | Postsynaptic depolarisations (firing more likely) / hyperpolarisations (firing less likely) |
| **Graded potentials** | Amplitude proportional to the intensity of the eliciting signal |
| **Axon hillock** | Cone where axon meets cell body; formerly thought to generate APs |
| **Axon initial segment** | Adjacent section of axon where APs are actually generated |
| **Threshold of excitation** | Depolarisation level (about -65 mV) that triggers an AP |
| **Action potential (AP)** | About 1 ms reversal of membrane potential from about -70 to +50 mV |
| **All-or-none responses** | Occur fully or not at all |
| **Integration** | Summing of PSPs into one signal that determines firing |
| **Spatial summation** | Summing of simultaneous PSPs from different sites |
| **Temporal summation** | Summing of rapidly successive PSPs at one synapse |
| **Voltage-gated ion channels** | Channels that open or close in response to membrane potential changes |
| **Absolute refractory period** | About 1 to 2 ms after AP initiation when no second AP is possible |
| **Relative refractory period** | When a second AP needs higher-than-normal stimulation |
| **Orthodromic / antidromic conduction** | AP conduction towards the terminals / back towards the cell body |
| **Myelin** | Fatty insulating segments around many axons |
| **Nodes of Ranvier** | Gaps between myelin segments, where Na+ channels are concentrated |
| **Saltatory conduction** | Node-to-node conduction in myelinated axons |
| **Hodgkin-Huxley model** | Early-1950s conduction theory based on squid motor neurons; Nobel 1963 |
| **Axodendritic / axosomatic synapses** | Axon terminal onto a dendrite / onto a soma |
| **Dendritic spines** | Nodules on dendrites where many axodendritic synapses end |
| **Tripartite synapse** | Synapse of two neurons plus an astroglial cell |
| **Dendrodendritic / axoaxonic / axomyelenic synapses** | Dendrite to dendrite (often bidirectional) / axon onto a terminal (presynaptic facilitation and inhibition) / axon onto oligodendrocyte myelin |
| **Directed synapses** | Release and reception sites in close proximity |
| **Nondirected synapses** | Release at some distance from reception, e.g., string-of-beads synapses at varicosities |
| **Neuropeptides** | Large-molecule transmitters of 3 to 36 amino acids |
| **Synaptic vesicles** | Membrane packages storing transmitter in the button |
| **Golgi complex** | Organelle that packages transmitter into vesicles |
| **Coexistence** | More than one neurotransmitter in the same neuron |
| **Exocytosis** | Transmitter release by vesicle fusion, triggered by Ca2+ entry |
| **Extracellular vesicles** | Intact vesicles carrying proteins or RNA between cells |
| **Receptors** | Proteins with binding sites for particular neurotransmitters |
| **Ligand** | Any molecule that binds to another |
| **Receptor subtypes** | The different receptors to which one transmitter can bind |
| **Ionotropic receptors** | Linked to ligand-activated ion channels; immediate PSPs |
| **Metabotropic receptors** | Linked to signal proteins and G proteins; slower, longer-lasting, more prevalent |
| **G proteins** | Guanosine-triphosphate-sensitive proteins coupled to metabotropic receptors |
| **Second messenger** | Chemical made in response to a G protein; can alter gene expression |
| **Autoreceptors** | Presynaptic metabotropic receptors for the neuron's own transmitter |
| **Reuptake** | Transporter-driven return of transmitter to the button; the commoner deactivation |
| **Enzymatic degradation** | Breakdown of transmitter in the synapse by enzymes |
| **Enzymes** | Proteins that stimulate or inhibit biochemical reactions without being changed |
| **Acetylcholinesterase** | Enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine |
| **Gap junctions** | Connexin-bridged gaps joining the cytoplasm of adjacent cells; electrical synapses |
| **Amino acid neurotransmitters** | Glutamate, aspartate, glycine, GABA; most fast directed CNS synapses |
| **Glutamate** | Most prevalent excitatory CNS transmitter |
| **Aspartate / glycine** | Amino acid transmitters common in dietary proteins |
| **GABA** | Gamma-aminobutyric acid; most prevalent inhibitory transmitter; made from glutamate |
| **Monoamine neurotransmitters** | Each made from one amino acid; diffuse; brain-stem cell bodies |
| **Catecholamines** | Dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine; from tyrosine |
| **Indolamines** | Serotonin; from tryptophan |
| **Dopamine / norepinephrine / epinephrine** | Successive products of the tyrosine pathway |
| **Serotonin** | 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) |
| **Acetylcholine** | Acetyl group plus choline; a class by itself |
| **Soluble-gas neurotransmitters** | Nitric oxide and carbon monoxide; lipid-soluble, seconds-long, retrograde |
| **Nitric oxide / carbon monoxide** | The two soluble-gas neurotransmitters; made in the cytoplasm, deactivated within seconds |
| **Endocannabinoids** | THC-like transmitters acting mainly on presynaptic neurons |
| **Anandamide** | Most studied endocannabinoid ("eternal bliss") |
| **Neuropeptide transmitters** | About 100 peptides in five loose categories |
| **Pituitary / hypothalamic peptides** | First identified as pituitary / hypothalamic hormones |
| **Brain-gut peptides** | First discovered in the gut |
| **Opioid peptides** | Structurally similar to opium's active ingredients |
| **Miscellaneous peptides** | Catch-all category |
| **Agonists / antagonists** | Drugs that facilitate / inhibit a transmitter's effects |
| **Receptor blockers** | Antagonists that bind receptors without activating them |
| **Nicotinic / muscarinic receptors** | Ionotropic ACh receptors (neuromuscular junction) / metabotropic ACh receptors (ANS) |
| **Atropine** | Belladonna's active ingredient; muscarinic receptor blocker |
| **Curare** | Dart-poison extract; nicotinic receptor blocker |
| **Botox** | Botulinum toxin; blocks acetylcholine release |
| **Periaqueductal gray (PAG)** | Grey matter around the cerebral aqueduct; densest opioid receptors |
| **Endogenous** | Occurring naturally within the body |
| **Enkephalins / endorphins** | First-discovered endogenous opioids ("in the head") / "endogenous morphine" |
