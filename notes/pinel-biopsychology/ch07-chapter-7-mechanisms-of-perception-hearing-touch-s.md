# Chapter 7: Sensory Systems, Perception, and Attention

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 6 used vision to teach you how a sensory system is built. This chapter takes the other four **exteroceptive** senses, hearing, touch (including pain), smell and taste, and shows that each of them is built on the same plan. Once you can state that plan in one sentence (sensory systems are **hierarchical, functionally segregated and parallel**), most of the anatomy in the chapter stops being a list of names and becomes a set of variations on a theme: receptors that transduce one kind of energy, a relay in the thalamus (with one famous exception), a primary cortex with a map, secondary areas that do more specific jobs, and association cortex where the senses meet. The second half of the chapter then moves up the hierarchy from *sensation* to *perception* and *attention*: how the brain decides what it is looking at, how it stitches separately analysed features into one object, and why you notice only a sliver of what reaches your receptors.

The chapter is held together by patients. A man who could see only one object at a time, a musician who tried to put on his wife's head as a hat, a woman who no longer recognised her own left arm, a stroke patient who could see better when he put his hand in his blind field, and a young woman who felt no pain and died of it at 29. Each one is a natural experiment that tells you something about how the healthy system is organised, which is exactly how the authors use them.

**How to read this chapter.** 7.1 gives you the vocabulary (primary, secondary and association cortex) and the three principles of organisation; everything after leans on it. 7.2 (hearing) and 7.3 (touch and pain) are the anatomy-heavy modules and the main source of MCQs: learn the pathways in order and know where each one crosses. 7.4 covers smell and taste, where the exam traps are about the thalamus and the cranial nerves. 7.5 (perception) and 7.6 (attention) are shorter and more conceptual, but they hold the answer to the opening case. Older editions of Pinel (and older Indian exam keys) used a different chapter title and included material on sound localisation, dermatomes and gate-control theory that the 11th edition has dropped; those topics are covered here in clearly marked boxes so you can answer either kind of paper.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition calls this chapter **"Sensory Systems, Perception, and Attention: How You Know the World."** Earlier editions called it **"Mechanisms of Perception: Hearing, Touch, Smell, Taste, and Attention,"** and opened with Dr. P. (the man who mistook his wife for a hat). In the 11th edition Dr. P. has moved into module 7.1 and the chapter now opens with a **simultanagnosia** case. The content overlaps heavily, but if a question cites "the chapter-opening case," check which edition the paper is following.

## The Case of the Man Who Could See Only One Thing at a Time

The chapter opens with a 68-year-old man described by **Williams (1970)**. He was referred because he kept getting lost, even inside his own house, and he put this down to not "seeing properly." Testing showed something much stranger than poor eyesight. If two objects, say two pencils, were held up in front of him together, he saw only one of them. It did not matter whether they were side by side, one above the other, or one partly behind the other. A picture of a single object or a single face, however detailed, he could identify. A picture containing two objects he could report only one at a time: first one object would occupy his perception, then it would be replaced by the other, then the first would come back, and so on. When two drawings were superimposed on each other, he reported seeing one and denied that the other was there at all.

Notice what is *not* wrong with him. He is not blind in any part of his visual field, because a single object is recognised wherever it appears. His acuity and his object recognition are good enough to identify complex pictures and faces. What fails is his capacity to hold more than one object in view at the same moment. The book asks you to carry three questions through the chapter: what is the nature of his deficit, what kind of process has broken, and where is his brain damage likely to be. The answers arrive in 7.6, and by then you will have the concepts (the dorsal stream, posterior parietal cortex, selective attention) to reach them yourself.

## 7.1 Principles of Sensory System Organisation

Vision is the most thoroughly studied and therefore best understood sensory system. The important discovery of the last few decades is that the other sensory systems, as they have been studied more closely, turn out to be organised like vision in their fundamental features. That is why Pinel and Barnes start with general principles: they are the template you lay over every system that follows.

Two definitions come first. **Sensation** is the process of detecting the presence of stimuli. **Perception** is the higher-order process of integrating, recognising and interpreting patterns of sensations. The **exteroceptive sensory systems** are the ones that detect stimuli arising outside the body; humans have five (visual, auditory, somatosensory, olfactory, gustatory). The book reminds you that these are not the only possible external senses: many species detect the earth's magnetic field and navigate by it (Nordmann, Hochstoeger and Keays, 2017), and sharks, electric fish and many amphibians pick up minute electrical signals and use them for navigation, hunting and communication (Bellono, Leitch and Julius, 2017). We simply lack those senses.

### Types of sensory areas of cortex

By convention, sensory cortex comes in three fundamentally different types, and each is defined by **where its input comes from**, not by what it does. That definitional point is what examiners test.

| Type | Defining input | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary sensory cortex** | Most of its input comes *directly from the thalamic relay nuclei* of that system | Primary visual cortex, fed mainly by the lateral geniculate nucleus |
| **Secondary sensory cortex** | Most of its input comes from the primary sensory cortex of that system, or from other secondary areas of the same system | The visual areas beyond V1 that receive from it |
| **Association cortex** | Any cortex that receives input from **more than one** sensory system | Posterior parietal cortex, prefrontal cortex |

Most of the input to association cortex arrives via secondary sensory areas. So the default flow is thalamus to primary, primary to secondary, secondary to association. The interactions among these three tiers, and among the subcortical structures that feed them, are described by three principles: **hierarchical organisation**, **functional segregation**, and **parallel processing**.

> **Trap.** "Association cortex" is defined by *multimodal input*, not by a vague notion of "higher thinking." A cortical area that gets all of its input from one sense, however complex its analysis, is secondary sensory cortex, not association cortex. And primary cortex is defined by its *thalamic* input, which helps explain why the book calls the label "primary olfactory cortex" somewhat arbitrary: the olfactory system's main route to cortex skips the thalamus altogether (7.4).

### Hierarchical organisation

A **hierarchy** is any system whose members can be ranked relative to one another; an army is the book's example, where every soldier has a rank. Sensory structures form a hierarchy ranked by the **specificity and complexity** of what their neurons respond to. Moving upward from receptors to thalamic nuclei, then primary sensory cortex, then secondary sensory cortex, then association cortex, you meet neurons whose optimal stimuli are ever more specific and complex. Each level takes much of its input from the level below, adds a further layer of analysis, and passes the result upward (Rees, Kreiman and Koch, 2002).

The best evidence for the hierarchy is clinical, and it comes from comparing the effects of damage at different levels. The rule is simple: **the higher the level of damage, the more specific and complex the deficit**. Destroy a system's receptors and the person loses that sense entirely (total blindness, total deafness). Destroy an area of secondary or association cortex and the result is typically a narrow, complicated deficit with the basic sensory abilities left intact.

### The Case of the Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat

The book's illustration of that rule is **Dr. P.**, described by the neurologist **Oliver Sacks (1985)** in *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat*. Dr. P. was a respected, charming and intelligent musician and music teacher who came to Sacks because other people thought he had a problem with his vision; he conceded only that he sometimes made odd mistakes.

The striking dissociation is between his *sensation* and his *perception*. His **visual acuity was excellent**: he could pick out a pin lying on the floor. Yet after a routine reflex test he looked down at his own foot and was unsure whether it was his shoe. Handed a glove, he studied it and could do no better than describe it as some kind of container with five pouches, and even a prompt about whether it might fit on part of the body produced no recognition. As he got up to leave, apparently pleased with how the examination had gone, he reached for his wife's head and tried to lift it onto his own, evidently taking it for his hat. His wife was not surprised; it happened often.

The lesson for 7.1: Dr. P. can *detect* fine visual detail (sensation is intact at the lower levels of the hierarchy) but cannot *integrate and interpret* what he sees into recognised objects (perception, a higher-level function, has failed). That is precisely the pattern the hierarchical principle predicts for damage high in the system. His deficit is a **visual agnosia**; in the book's closing "Themes Revisited" he is called "the visual agnosic."

> **Trap.** Dr. P. is a **visual agnosia** case (failure of perception with intact sensation), not a case of blindness, prosopagnosia alone, or simultanagnosia. Do not merge him with the chapter-opening patient: Dr. P. *misidentifies* single objects; the simultanagnosia patient *identifies single objects well* but cannot see two at once.

### Functional segregation

The older view held that each level of a sensory hierarchy was **functionally homogeneous**: all the cortex at a given level (all of primary, all of secondary, all of association) worked together to perform one common function. Research has replaced that with **functional segregation**. At each of the three cortical levels, in every sensory system, there are **functionally distinct areas that specialise in different kinds of analysis**. In vision you have already met this as separate areas for colour, motion and form; in this chapter you will see it again in the auditory "what" and "where" streams, the four functional strips of primary somatosensory cortex, and the separate cortical patches for each taste.

### Parallel processing

The older view also held that the levels were connected **serially**: information flowed from component to component along a single route, like a string threaded through beads. We now know sensory systems are **parallel systems**, in which information moves through the components over **multiple pathways at once** (Lleras et al., 2017). **Parallel processing** is the simultaneous analysis of one signal in different ways by the multiple parallel pathways of a neural network. One practical consequence turns up repeatedly in the chapter: because so many pathways carry overlapping information, damage to any one of them often produces surprisingly mild effects (the classic example is the mildness of primary somatosensory cortex lesions in 7.3).

### Summary model of sensory system organisation

Put the three principles together and you have Figure 7.1's two models. In the **1960s**, sensory systems were thought to be **hierarchical, functionally homogeneous and serial**. Subsequent research has established that they are **hierarchical, functionally segregated and parallel** (Rauschecker, 2015). Notice that the hierarchy survives; it is the other two assumptions that were overturned.

What the figure does not show, and what the book makes a point of adding, is the large number of neurons that run **downward** through each hierarchy. Sensory systems carry information from lower to higher levels, but they also carry it from higher to lower levels. These descending influences are **top-down** signals (Bressler and Richter, 2015; Marques et al., 2018; Ruff, 2013), and they are one way in which higher levels can shape the input they receive. Keep the term; it returns in 7.6, where endogenous attention is described as a top-down process.

> **Trap.** In "former versus current model" questions, **hierarchical** appears in *both* models. The two changes are **homogeneous to segregated** and **serial to parallel**. A distractor offering "non-hierarchical" as the modern view is wrong.

> **High-yield.** Primary cortex = input mostly from thalamic relay nuclei; secondary = input mostly from primary (or other secondary) of the same system; association = input from more than one system. Current model: hierarchical, functionally segregated, parallel (1960s model: hierarchical, functionally homogeneous, serial). Higher damage gives more specific, complex deficits.

## 7.2 The Auditory System

The auditory system exists to perceive **sound**, and sounds are vibrations of air molecules that stimulate it. Humans hear only molecular vibrations between roughly **20 and 20,000 hertz** (Hz, cycles per second). Everything in this module follows a signal from those vibrations to the cortex, then looks at what goes wrong when parts of the route are damaged.

### Physical and perceptual dimensions of sound

Sounds are usually recorded and drawn as waves, and each physical property of the wave maps most closely onto one perceptual quality:

| Physical dimension | Perceptual dimension | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amplitude** (size of the pressure change) | **Loudness** | Soft | Loud |
| **Frequency** (vibrations per second) | **Pitch** | Low | High |
| **Complexity** (mixture of component waves) | **Timbre** | Pure | Rich |

The words "most closely linked" matter: the mapping is not one-to-one, and pitch in particular is more complicated than frequency, as the next subsection shows.

> **Trap.** Amplitude, frequency, complexity map to **loudness, pitch, timbre**, in that order. The classic swap is pairing frequency with loudness or complexity with pitch. Timbre is what lets you tell a clarinet from a violin playing the same note at the same loudness.

### Fourier analysis and the missing fundamental

A **pure tone** is a single sine wave, and pure tones exist only in laboratories and recording studios. Real-world sound is always a complex pattern of vibration; Figure 7.3 shows the complex wave produced by a single clarinet note. Mathematically, any complex wave can be decomposed into a set of sine waves of different frequencies and amplitudes which, added back together, reproduce the original. The procedure that does this decomposition is **Fourier analysis**, and one theory of hearing holds that the auditory system performs something like a Fourier analysis on incoming sound, breaking it into its component sine waves.

For a pure tone, frequency and perceived pitch are closely related. For natural sounds, which always mix many frequencies, the relationship is complex (Bidelman and Grall, 2014). The pitch of a complex sound is related to its **fundamental frequency**, defined as the highest common divisor of its component frequencies. A mixture of 100, 200 and 300 Hz usually sounds as if it has a pitch related to 100 Hz, because 100 is the largest number that divides all three.

Now the surprising part. The pitch of a complex sound need not correspond to the frequency of *any* of its components (Lau and Werner, 2014). Play a mixture of **200, 300 and 400 Hz** and listeners hear the same pitch as a pure **100 Hz** tone, even though no 100 Hz energy is present at all, because 100 Hz is still the highest common divisor. This is the **missing fundamental** (Oxenham, 2018). It is important because it proves that pitch is *computed* by the brain from the pattern of components, not simply read off the loudest frequency, and it gives experimenters a way to pull pitch and frequency apart (which Bendor and Wang exploited, below).

> **Trap.** The fundamental frequency is the **highest common divisor**, not the lowest component and not the average. For 200 + 300 + 400 Hz, the lowest component is 200 Hz, but the perceived pitch corresponds to **100 Hz**.

### The ear: from eardrum to oval window

Follow the sound inward (Figure 7.4):

1. Sound waves pass from the **outer ear** down the **auditory canal** and set the **tympanic membrane** (the eardrum) vibrating.
2. The vibrations pass to the **ossicles**, the three small bones of the middle ear: the **malleus** (hammer), the **incus** (anvil) and the **stapes** (stirrup). These are the three smallest bones in the body.
3. The stapes vibrates against a membrane called the **oval window**, which passes the vibrations into the fluid of the **cochlea**, the snail-shaped inner-ear organ (the Greek *kokhlos* means "land snail").
4. The cochlea is a long coiled tube with an internal structure running almost to its tip. That structure is the auditory receptor organ, the **organ of Corti**.
5. After travelling through the cochlear fluid, the vibrations are finally dissipated by the **round window**, an elastic membrane in the cochlear wall.

> **Trap.** **Oval** window = where vibrations *enter* the cochlea (pushed by the stapes). **Round** window = where they are *dissipated*. The order of the ossicles from eardrum inward is malleus, incus, stapes (hammer, anvil, stirrup); the stapes is the one touching the oval window.

### The organ of Corti and cochlear coding

Each pressure change at the oval window runs along the organ of Corti as a travelling wave. The organ of Corti is built from several membranes, and you need two of them:

- The **basilar membrane**, in which the auditory receptors, the **hair cells**, are mounted.
- The **tectorial membrane**, which rests on top of the hair cells.

Because the hair cells are sandwiched between the two, any deflection of the organ of Corti at a given point along its length produces a **shearing force** on the hair cells at that same point. The shearing stimulates the hair cells, and they in turn increase the firing of axons in the **auditory nerve** (Wu et al., 2017), which is a branch of the **auditory-vestibular nerve**, one of the 12 cranial nerves (the eighth).

The cochlea is extraordinarily sensitive (Hudspeth, 2014): humans can discriminate pure tones whose frequencies differ by only **0.2 percent**. The key to how it codes frequency is place. **Different frequencies produce maximal stimulation of hair cells at different points along the basilar membrane.** Higher frequencies produce their greatest activation **close to the windows** (the base of the cochlea); lower frequencies produce their greatest activation toward the **tip** of the basilar membrane. A complex sound, made of many component frequencies, therefore excites hair cells at many points along the membrane at once, and the many resulting signals leave the ear on many different auditory neurons. In effect, the cochlea is the first stage of the Fourier-like analysis.

Most other auditory structures are also laid out by frequency. Just as the visual system is largely **retinotopic** (organised according to a map of the retina), the auditory system is largely **tonotopic**, organised according to frequency (Schreiner and Polley, 2014).

> **Trap.** **High** frequencies peak near the **base** (near the oval and round windows); **low** frequencies peak at the **apex** (tip). Students often reverse this because they imagine the "big" end handling "big" sounds. Age-related hearing loss, which strikes the high frequencies first, is a loss of hair cells at the base.

### The auditory scene problem

The book calls the following the major unsolved mystery of auditory processing. Picture yourself at a party: music, dancing, eating, several conversations at once. Each individual sound is made of many component frequencies, each activating its own site on the basilar membrane, so at any instant the number of simultaneously active sites is enormous. Somehow the auditory system sorts all those frequency messages into separate groups and recombines each group so that you hear each sound *source* as a distinct thing (Bremen and Middlebrooks, 2013; Christison-Lagay and Cohen, 2014; Christison-Lagay, Gifford and Cohen, 2015). You hear the speech of the person beside you as one coherent stream even though it shares many component frequencies with the sounds coming from everywhere else. The mechanism is not known. One theory is that the frequency components belonging to one source fluctuate **synchronously** over time, and the system groups components that rise and fall together (Oxenham, 2018).

### The vestibular system

Figure 7.4 also shows the **semicircular canals**, which are not part of hearing at all. They are the receptive organs of the **vestibular system**, which carries information about the direction and intensity of head movements and so helps maintain balance (Brandt and Dieterich, 2017; Gu, 2018). They share the inner ear and the auditory-vestibular nerve with the cochlea, which is why they appear in the same figure.

### From the ear to the primary auditory cortex

Vision has one dominant route to cortex (retina, lateral geniculate, striate cortex). Hearing has no equivalent single pathway; it has a **network** of pathways, some of which are shown in Figure 7.5. Learn the main sequence:

1. Each **auditory nerve** synapses in the **ipsilateral cochlear nuclei**.
2. From the cochlear nuclei, many projections go to the **superior olives** on **both sides** of the brain stem at the same level.
3. Axons of the olivary neurons ascend via the **lateral lemniscus** to the **inferior colliculi**.
4. Inferior collicular neurons project to the **medial geniculate nuclei** of the thalamus.
5. The medial geniculate nuclei project to the **primary auditory cortex**.

Two consequences follow. First, signals from the two ears are **combined at a very low level**, in the superior olives, only one synapse beyond the cochlear nuclei. Second, each ear's signals reach **both ipsilateral and contralateral** auditory cortex. So unilateral damage to auditory cortex does not deafen one ear, a point that contrasts sharply with the clean crossing of the somatosensory and visual systems.

The subcortical auditory pathways are inherently complex and involve **many more synapses than the other senses** (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019; Wang, 2018). Some researchers think this subcortical complexity reflects the complexity of the computations the auditory system has to carry out (Wang, 2018).

> **Trap.** Thalamic relays: **medial** geniculate = **hearing**; **lateral** geniculate = **vision** ("M for music, L for light"). And the first point where the two ears converge is the **superior olive**, not the inferior colliculus or the cortex.

> **Older-edition material (not in the 11th edition): sound localisation in the superior olives.** Earlier editions of Pinel, and many exam keys based on them, gave the superior olives a specific job: **localising sounds in space**, which is possible precisely because the olives are the first place the two ears' signals meet. A sound off to one side reaches the nearer ear slightly **earlier** and slightly **louder** (the head casts a sound shadow). The **medial superior olives** respond to **interaural time differences** (differences in arrival time between the ears) and the **lateral superior olives** respond to **interaural intensity differences** (differences in loudness between the ears). In standard accounts, time differences are most useful for low-frequency sounds and intensity differences for high-frequency sounds. The older editions also noted that the olives project to the colliculi and that the deep layers of the **superior colliculus** contain a map of auditory space lying in register with the visual map in its superficial layers. The 11th edition mentions only that the two ears' signals combine in the superior olives; if a question asks which structure computes interaural time differences, the answer from the older material is the **medial superior olive**.

### Auditory cortex: core, belt and parabelt

Progress on human auditory cortex has come from combining functional brain imaging in humans with invasive neural recording in monkeys (Saenz and Langers, 2014). Even so, primate auditory cortex remains poorly understood, and our knowledge of it lags far behind our knowledge of visual cortex.

In primates, **primary auditory cortex** receives most of its input from the **medial geniculate nucleus** and lies in the **temporal lobe**, hidden from view inside the **lateral fissure** (Figure 7.6). Its layout:

- **Core region**: primary auditory cortex consists of **three adjacent areas**, together called the core (Moerel, De Martino and Formisano, 2014).
- **Belt**: a band of secondary auditory cortex areas surrounding the core.
- **Parabelt**: areas of secondary auditory cortex lying outside the belt (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019).

In total, primates appear to have about **13 separate areas** of auditory cortex (Brewer and Barton, 2016).

### Three organising principles of primary auditory cortex

1. **Functional columns.** As in primary visual cortex, the neurons met along a vertical microelectrode penetration (one at right angles to the cortical layers) tend to respond best to sounds in the **same frequency range** (Mizrahi, Shalev and Nelken, 2014).
2. **Tonotopic organisation.** As in the cochlea, each area of auditory cortex seems to have a gradient of preferred frequencies running from low to high along its length (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019; Schreiner and Polley, 2014).
3. **Periodotopy.** Auditory cortex is also organised by the **temporal** properties of sound, that is, by how the amplitude of particular frequencies varies over time. Natural sounds almost never hold a constant intensity, and auditory cortex appears to be tuned to those fluctuations. This third principle is called **periodotopy** (Brewer and Barton, 2016).

> **Trap.** **Tonotopy** = organisation by *frequency*. **Periodotopy** = organisation by *temporal fluctuation of amplitude*. **Retinotopy** = organisation by retinal location (vision). **Somatotopy** = organisation by body surface (touch, 7.3). **Chemotopy** = organisation by chemical (smell and taste, 7.4). Matching each "-topy" to its system is a standard one-mark question.

### What sounds should be used to study auditory cortex?

Why has auditory research lagged behind visual research? A major reason is that nobody has a clear idea of the **dimensions** along which auditory cortex evaluates sound (Sharpee, Atencio and Schreiner, 2011). The book draws the parallel with vision: research on visual cortex took off only once it was found that most visual neurons respond to **contrast**. No equivalent key has been found for hearing.

What is clear is that auditory cortex is **hierarchical**: responses in secondary auditory cortex are more complex and varied than those in primary auditory cortex (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019). And the stimulus choice matters. Electrophysiologists have traditionally used **pure tones**, yet many auditory cortex neurons respond only weakly to them. The practice is changing, partly because **natural sounds** are generally better at driving neurons in mammalian auditory cortex (Gervain and Geffen, 2019; Kopp-Scheinpflug, Sinclair and Linden, 2019).

### Auditory-visual interactions

Sensory systems were traditionally assumed to interact only in association cortex; indeed, that is how association cortex is defined. Much of the work on cross-modal interaction has focused on hearing and vision, especially in **posterior parietal cortex** (Brang et al., 2013; Cohen, 2009). In a monkey study by **Mullette-Gillman, Cohen and Groh (2005)**, posterior parietal neurons came in three kinds: some had visual receptive fields, some auditory receptive fields, and some both.

Functional brain imaging has an advantage here, because it records from the whole brain rather than from one chosen spot. Imaging studies have confirmed that sensory interactions happen in association cortex but, more importantly, have repeatedly found interactions at the **lowest cortical level, in primary sensory cortex** (Man et al., 2013; Smith and Goodale, 2015). That finding changes the picture. Interaction between the senses is not a final step bolted on after each **unimodal** (single-system) analysis is finished; it seems to be an **early and integral** part of sensory processing. Hold on to this; it is the basis for one of the two proposed solutions to the binding problem in 7.5.

### Where does the perception of pitch occur?

This seemed an easy question, since most auditory cortex is clearly tonotopic. But tonotopy is about *frequency*, and pitch is not the same thing (recall the missing fundamental). When experimenters used stimuli in which frequency and pitch were deliberately made to differ, using the missing-fundamental technique, most auditory neurons tracked the change in **frequency**, not the change in pitch.

That set up the decisive study. **Bendor and Wang (2005)** recorded from individual neurons in primary and secondary areas of monkey auditory cortex while presenting missing-fundamental stimuli. They found one small area just **anterior to primary auditory cortex** containing many neurons that responded to **pitch rather than frequency**, whatever the quality of the sound. The same small area also contained frequency-responsive neurons, so Bendor and Wang proposed that it is probably where frequency information is **converted into the perception of pitch**. fMRI studies have since identified a comparable pitch area in a similar location in the human brain.

### What analyses does auditory cortex perform?

We now know that auditory cortex does not produce a faithful, recording-like representation of incoming sound (Tsunada et al., 2016; Wang, 2018). Instead, it combines the sound with information about what the animal is currently perceiving and doing, and outputs auditory signals tailored to the animal's present situation (Kuchibhotla and Bathellier, 2018; Lima, Krishnan and Scott, 2016; Schneider and Mooney, 2018).

One such situation-relevant output is the representation of **auditory objects**. A piano produces a complex mixture of frequencies, but what you experience is "a piano." Auditory cortex is believed to be what converts the mixture into that object-level representation (Angeloni and Geffen, 2018; Kuchibhotla and Bathellier, 2018; Tsunada et al., 2016).

### Two streams of auditory cortex

Ideas about the large-scale organisation of auditory cortex have been borrowed from vision. Just as visual cortex has a dorsal and a ventral stream, researchers have proposed two main streams of auditory analysis. Auditory signals ultimately reach two large regions of association cortex, **prefrontal cortex** and **posterior parietal cortex**, and there is good evidence (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019; van der Heijden et al., 2019) for a division of labour (Figure 7.7):

| Stream | Main role |
|---|---|
| **Anterior auditory pathway** | Identifying sounds (**what**) |
| **Posterior auditory pathway** | Locating sounds (**where**) |

> **Trap.** In hearing, **anterior = what**, **posterior = where**. In vision the equivalent is **ventral = what**, **dorsal = where** (or "how"). The logic is the same (identification versus location), but the anatomical labels differ, and a question asking about the "ventral auditory pathway" is really asking about the anterior one.

### Effects of damage to the auditory system

Studying auditory damage serves two purposes: it reveals how the auditory system works, and it informs the causes and treatment of clinical deafness.

**Auditory cortex damage.** After **bilateral** lesions of primary auditory cortex there is often a **complete loss of hearing** at first. This is presumed to be due to the shock of the lesion, because hearing returns over the following weeks. The lasting deficit is a loss of the ability to process the **structural aspects of sounds**, which is exactly what speech perception requires. Patients with bilateral auditory cortex lesions are therefore often described as **"word deaf"** (Jasmin, Lima and Scott, 2019). Damage to the two streams produces the predicted dissociation: damage to the **anterior** ("what") pathway impairs **identifying** sounds, and damage to the **posterior** ("where") pathway impairs **localising** them.

**Deafness in humans.** Deafness is among the most common human disabilities: an estimated **360 million** people currently have disabling hearing impairments (Lesica, 2018). The cost goes beyond not hearing: hearing impairment can produce feelings of **social isolation** and has been associated with an **increased risk of dementia** (Lesica, 2018; Peelle and Wingfield, 2016). **Total** deafness is rare, occurring in only **1 percent** of hearing-impaired people.

Severe hearing problems usually come from damage to the **middle or inner ear** or the nerves leaving them, not from central damage. There are two common classes:

| Type | Site of damage | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Conductive deafness** | The **ossicles** | Sound is not mechanically transmitted to the cochlea |
| **Nerve deafness** | The **cochlea** or **auditory nerve** | Main cause is **loss of hair cell receptors** (Wallis, 2018; Wong and Ryan, 2015) |

If only part of the cochlea is damaged, nerve deafness can be limited to some frequencies. **Age-related hearing loss** is the standard example: it specifically impairs **high frequencies**. That is why older people often struggle to distinguish "s," "f" and "t" sounds; they can hear that someone is talking but have trouble making out what is said, and family and friends often fail to realise that much of their apparent confusion comes from difficulty discriminating sounds (Wingfield, Tun and McCoy, 2005). Hearing aids, unfortunately, often do not help with the processing of speech (Lesica, 2018; Peelle and Wingfield, 2016).

> **Trap.** **Conductive** = ossicles (middle ear). **Nerve** (sensorineural) = cochlea or auditory nerve, usually hair-cell loss. Age-related loss is **nerve** deafness for **high** frequencies. Bilateral auditory *cortex* lesions do not cause lasting deafness; they cause **word deafness** after an initial transient loss.

### Tinnitus

Hearing loss is sometimes accompanied by **tinnitus**, a ringing in the ears. When only one ear is damaged, the ringing is heard as coming from that ear. The revealing experiment is what happens when the nerve from the ringing ear is cut: **the ringing does not stop**. If the ringing were generated in the damaged ear, cutting its nerve should abolish it. Because it persists, the cause must lie further in, and the favoured explanation is **neuroplastic changes in the auditory system produced by the deafness itself** (Eggermont and Tass, 2015; Elgoyhen et al., 2015; Shore, Roberts and Langguth, 2016; Sedley et al., 2016). The book's hint is that sensory neurons do not simply fall silent when their input is removed; deprived auditory circuits reorganise and can generate activity that is heard as sound. Tinnitus is one of the chapter's three examples of sensory-system plasticity.

### Cochlear implants

Some people with **nerve deafness** benefit from **cochlear implants** (Figure 7.8). An implant bypasses the damaged hair cells: a microphone on the ear picks up sound, the sound is converted into electrical signals, and a bundle of electrodes carries those signals into the cochlea, where they **excite the auditory nerve directly**. Implants can give major benefits, but they **do not restore normal hearing**. Timing matters: the sooner a person receives an implant after becoming deaf, the more likely it is to help, because **disuse alters the auditory neural pathways** (Kral and Sharma, 2012).

> **Trap.** A cochlear implant helps **nerve** deafness caused by hair-cell loss, because it stimulates the surviving auditory nerve. It cannot help if the auditory nerve itself is destroyed, and it is not the treatment logic for conductive (ossicle) deafness. And the earlier it is fitted, the better, because of plastic changes from disuse.

## 7.3 The Somatosensory System: Touch and Pain

Sensations from your body are **somatosensations**, and the system that mediates them, the **somatosensory system**, is really three separate but interacting systems.

### Three somatosensory systems

| System | What it monitors | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Exteroceptive** | External stimuli applied to the skin | Cutaneous receptors |
| **Proprioceptive** | The position of the body | Receptors in muscles, joints and the organs of balance |
| **Interoceptive** | General conditions inside the body, such as temperature and blood pressure | Internal receptors |

This module is almost entirely about the **exteroceptive** system, which itself has three somewhat distinct divisions: one for **mechanical** stimuli (touch), one for **thermal** stimuli (temperature), and one for **nociceptive** stimuli (pain).

> **Trap.** **Proprioceptive** = body *position* (muscles, joints, balance organs). **Interoceptive** = *internal state* (temperature, blood pressure). **Exteroceptive** = stimuli on the *skin* from outside. The organs of balance belong to proprioception in this classification, even though the vestibular receptors sit in the ear.

### Cutaneous receptors

The skin contains many kinds of receptor (Owens and Lumpkin, 2014; Zimmerman, Bai and Ginty, 2014). Figure 7.9 shows four of them, and you should know each by its structure, its depth and how fast it adapts:

| Receptor | Structure and position | Adaptation | Responds to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Free nerve endings** | The simplest receptors: neuron endings with no specialised structure | (not specified) | Especially sensitive to **temperature change and pain** |
| **Pacinian corpuscles** | The **largest and deepest**; onion-like layered capsule | **Rapid** | **Sudden displacements** of the skin, not constant pressure |
| **Merkel's disks** | Disk-shaped endings | **Slow** | **Gradual skin indentation** |
| **Ruffini endings** | Elongated endings | **Slow** | **Skin stretch** |

Despite their structural differences, the receptors tend to work in the same general way. A stimulus on the skin **deforms** the receptor or **changes its chemistry**, this changes the permeability of the receptor's membrane to various ions, and the result is a neural signal (Delmas, Hao and Rodat-Despoix, 2011).

### Fast versus slow adaptation

Why should some receptors adapt quickly and others slowly? Consider a constant pressure applied to the skin. At first, **all** receptors fire a burst, and that burst corresponds to the sensation of being touched. After **a few hundred milliseconds**, the rapidly adapting receptors fall silent and only the slowly adapting ones remain active, so the quality of the sensation changes. Often you stop noticing constant pressure altogether; you are usually unaware of your clothes pressing on your skin until you turn your attention to them.

That is why, when you engage in **stereognosis** (identifying objects by touch), you keep moving the object around in your hands: manipulation keeps changing the pattern of stimulation, so the rapidly adapting receptors keep reporting. Having both kinds of receptor gives you information about both the **dynamic** (changing) and the **static** (steady) qualities of a touch.

> **Trap.** **Pacinian** = largest, deepest, **rapidly** adapting, sudden displacement and vibration. **Merkel** = **slowly** adapting, gradual indentation. **Ruffini** = **slowly** adapting, stretch. **Free nerve endings** = temperature and pain. The "Scan Your Brain" items in the book test exactly this: the receptor that is largest and deepest and ignores constant pressure is the Pacinian corpuscle; the slowly adapting pair is Merkel's disks and Ruffini endings.

> **Trap.** **Meissner's corpuscles** are *not* among the four receptors in the 11th edition's Figure 7.9, but they appear in many other textbooks and older keys. In standard accounts they are small, **superficial**, **rapidly adapting** receptors in hairless skin (fingertips, lips) that signal light touch and low-frequency vibration (flutter). Do not confuse them with the Pacinian corpuscle, which is also rapidly adapting but large and **deep**. A useful pairing: superficial receptors (Meissner, Merkel) give fine spatial detail; deep receptors (Pacinian, Ruffini) have large receptive fields; within each pair, one adapts fast (Meissner, Pacinian) and one slowly (Merkel, Ruffini).

### One receptor, one sensation? An assumption that failed

It was originally assumed that each type of skin receptor mediated a different tactile sensation, one for touch, one for pain, one for heat and so on. That has not turned out to be true. Each tactile sensation appears to arise from the **interaction of several receptor mechanisms**, and each receptor mechanism appears to **contribute to several sensations** (Hollins, 2010; Lumpkin and Caterina, 2007; McGlone and Reilly, 2010). The skin cells surrounding a receptor also seem to help shape the quality of the sensation it produces (Zimmerman, Bai and Ginty, 2014), and new forms of tactile sensation are still being discovered (McGlone, Wessberg and Olausson, 2014; Ran, Hoon and Chen, 2016). This is the somatosensory version of the chapter's general message: one-to-one, single-line mappings rarely survive close study.

### Two major somatosensory pathways

Somatosensory information ascends from each side of the body to the cortex over several pathways, but two are major (Ran et al., 2016):

| Pathway | Tends to carry |
|---|---|
| **Dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system** | **Touch** and **proprioception** |
| **Anterolateral system** | **Pain** and **temperature** |

The book stresses the words **"tends to."** The functional division between the two pathways is far from complete, so lesions of the dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system do **not** abolish touch or proprioception, and lesions of the anterolateral system do **not** abolish pain or temperature perception. That incomplete separation is a direct example of parallel processing.

### The dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system

Follow it step by step (Figure 7.10):

1. Sensory neurons from the skin enter the spinal cord through a **dorsal root**.
2. They ascend **ipsilaterally** (on the same side) in the **dorsal columns**.
3. They synapse in the **dorsal column nuclei** of the **medulla**.
4. The axons of the dorsal column nuclei neurons **decussate** (cross to the other side of the brain).
5. They then ascend in the **medial lemniscus** to the **contralateral ventral posterior nucleus** of the thalamus.
6. Most ventral posterior neurons project to **primary somatosensory cortex (SI)**; others go to **secondary somatosensory cortex (SII)** or **posterior parietal cortex**.

The face is handled separately but ends up in the same place: the ventral posterior nuclei also receive input from the **three branches of the trigeminal nerve**, which carry somatosensory information from the contralateral side of the face.

A piece of trivia the authors invite you to collect: the dorsal column neurons that begin in the **toes** are the **longest neurons in the human body**. It follows from step 2: the first-order neuron does not synapse until the medulla, so a neuron from the big toe runs from the foot all the way up the spinal cord to the brain stem.

### The anterolateral system

The anterolateral system (Figure 7.11) is organised differently:

1. Most dorsal root neurons of the anterolateral system **synapse as soon as they enter the spinal cord**.
2. Most of the second-order axons **decussate in the spinal cord** and ascend in the **contralateral anterolateral** portion of the cord; some, however, do not cross and ascend ipsilaterally.
3. The system comprises **three tracts**: the **spinothalamic tract**, the **spinoreticular tract** and the **spinotectal tract** (to the thalamus, the reticular formation and the tectum respectively).
4. Pain and temperature from the face are carried by the three branches of the **trigeminal nerve** to the same thalamic sites.
5. The pain and temperature information reaching the thalamus (ventral posterior, intralaminar, parafascicular and other nuclei) is then distributed to somatosensory cortex and to other parts of the brain.

> **Trap.** **Where they cross.** Dorsal-column medial-lemniscus: first synapse and crossing in the **medulla** (dorsal column nuclei); the ascent in the spinal cord is **ipsilateral**. Anterolateral: first synapse **immediately in the spinal cord**, and most axons cross **in the spinal cord**, ascending **contralaterally** (some ipsilaterally). This difference is why a half-section of the spinal cord produces loss of touch and proprioception on the *same* side below the cut but loss of pain and temperature on the *opposite* side (the standard clinical picture known as Brown-Séquard syndrome, not named in the 11th edition).

> **Trap.** Both systems relay through the **ventral posterior nucleus** of the thalamus (the anterolateral system also uses intralaminar and parafascicular nuclei). The **medial lemniscus** is a *somatosensory* tract; the **lateral lemniscus** is an *auditory* tract (superior olives to inferior colliculi, 7.2). Same word, different senses.

### Spinal cord injury and dermatomes

If **both** ascending pathways are completely cut by a spinal injury, the patient feels nothing from the body **below the level of the cut**. The book's dry conclusion: with spinal injuries, **lower is better**, because the lower the cut, the less of the body is cut off.

> **Older-edition material (not in the 11th edition): dermatomes.** Earlier editions introduced the somatosensory pathways with **dermatomes**. A **dermatome** is the area of the body innervated by the left and right dorsal roots of a given segment of the spinal cord. The body surface is divided into bands corresponding to the **cervical (C), thoracic (T), lumbar (L) and sacral (S)** segments, with the face supplied not by spinal roots but by the three branches of the **trigeminal nerve**. Two facts used to be tested: the dermatomal map of the body is a set of stripes running around the trunk and down the limbs, and **adjacent dermatomes overlap considerably**, so destroying a single dorsal root rarely produces a complete loss of sensation anywhere. The dermatome map is also what lets a clinician infer the level of a spinal injury from the upper border of sensory loss.

### Penfield's mapping and the somatosensory homunculus

In **1937**, **Penfield** and his colleagues mapped primary somatosensory cortex in patients undergoing neurosurgery. The patients were **fully conscious under local anaesthetic**, so as Penfield electrically stimulated sites on the cortical surface, they could report what they felt. Stimulating the **postcentral gyrus** produced sensations in various parts of the body. When Penfield plotted each stimulation site against the body part where the sensation was felt, he found that human **SI is somatotopic**: organised according to a map of the body surface (Chen et al., 2015). This map is the **somatosensory homunculus** ("homunculus" means "little man").

The homunculus is **distorted**. The largest share of SI receives input from the body parts we use for tactile discrimination, especially the **hands, lips and tongue**; large body regions not normally used for discrimination, such as the **back**, get only small areas of SI. The book's "Check It Out: Touching a Back" demonstration makes the consequence concrete: touch a friend's back with one, two or three fingers at the same moment and ask how many they feel. Unless the fingers are well apart, the back cannot tell separate touches from one, whereas fingertips can count touches even when they are very close together. (The touches must be simultaneous, because timing differences would give the game away.) Cortical territory, not skin area, sets tactile resolution.

The figure carries a correction worth knowing. The conventional drawing shows the body **upside down** along the postcentral gyrus (feet at the top, near the midline) but the face **right side up**. An fMRI study by **Servos and colleagues (1999)** suggests that this is wrong and that the **face representation is also inverted**.

> **Trap.** SI is in the **postcentral** gyrus (parietal lobe), immediately **behind** the central fissure; primary motor cortex is in the **precentral** gyrus in front of it. The homunculus's distortion reflects **discrimination use** (hands, lips, tongue), not body size. And the 11th edition flags the face as probably **inverted** too (Servos et al., 1999), contrary to the classic drawing.

### Secondary somatosensory cortex (SII)

A second somatotopically organised area, **SII**, lies just **ventral** to SI on the postcentral gyrus, and much of it extends into the **lateral fissure**. Because SII gets most of its input from SI, it counts as secondary somatosensory cortex. The important contrast:

| | SI | SII |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Postcentral gyrus | Just ventral to SI, largely within the lateral fissure |
| Main input | Ventral posterior thalamus | SI |
| Side of body | Largely **contralateral** | Substantial input from **both sides** |

Much of the output of both SI and SII goes to the association cortex of the **posterior parietal lobe** (McGlone and Reilly, 2010).

### Columnar organisation and four functional strips

Single-neuron recordings in SI found **columnar organisation**, as in visual and auditory cortex. Every neuron in a given column had its receptive field on the **same part of the body** and responded best to the **same type of tactile stimulus** (light touch, say, or heat). The recordings also suggested that SI is made up of **four functional strips**, each with its own similar but separate somatotopic map, and each most sensitive to a different kind of somatosensory input (for example, light touch or pressure). Recording across the four strips at the level of one body part, you would meet neurons preferring four different kinds of tactile stimulation, all for that same body part. This is functional segregation inside a single primary area.

### Dorsal and ventral somatosensory streams

Mirroring vision and hearing again, two streams of analysis have been proposed to flow out of SI (Yau, Connor and Hsiao, 2013):

| Stream | Destination | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal** | Posterior parietal cortex | Multisensory integration and the direction of attention |
| **Ventral** | SII | Perception of the **shapes of objects** |

### Effects of damage to primary somatosensory cortex

Damage to SI is often **remarkably mild**, presumably because the somatosensory system has so many parallel pathways. **Corkin, Milner and Rasmussen (1970)** tested the somatosensory abilities of patients with epilepsy **before and after** a unilateral excision that included SI. Afterwards the patients showed just **two minor contralateral deficits**: **light touch** became harder to detect, and **objects** became harder to identify by touch (a deficit in stereognosis). The deficits were **bilateral only** in the cases where the unilateral lesion had **encroached on SII**, which fits SII's bilateral input.

The before-and-after design matters: because each patient served as their own control, the deficits can be attributed to the excision rather than to pre-existing differences, and the mildness of the deficits is itself the finding.

### Somatosensory system and association cortex

Somatosensory signals eventually reach the top of the hierarchy, the association cortex of the **prefrontal** and **posterior parietal** lobes.

Posterior parietal cortex contains **bimodal neurons**, neurons that respond to activation of two different sensory systems, and some respond to both somatosensory and visual stimuli. Their two receptive fields are spatially linked: a neuron whose somatosensory receptive field is centred on the left hand has a visual receptive field next to the left hand (Crochet, Lee and Peterson, 2019). Remarkably, **when the hand moves, the visual receptive field moves with it**. The neuron is coding "the space around my hand," wherever the hand happens to be. That property led directly to the next case.

### The Case of W.M., Who Reduced His Scotoma with His Hand

**Schendel and Robertson (2004)** studied W.M., who had suffered a stroke in his **right posterior cerebral artery**. The stroke damaged a large region of his right occipital and parietal lobes and left him with a severe **left hemianopsia**, a condition in which a scotoma covers half of the visual field.

The test was simple: detect visual stimuli in each half of the visual field. With his left hand resting in his lap, W.M. detected **97.8 percent** of stimuli in his right visual field and only **13.6 percent** in his left. When he was tested with his **left hand extended into his left visual field**, his detection in the left field improved significantly. Closer analysis showed that the improvement came from a much better ability to see objects in the left field that were **near his left hand**. And when he held a **tennis racket** in that extended hand, the zone of improved vision **expanded further**, out along the racket.

Why does this make sense? If bimodal posterior parietal neurons have visual receptive fields anchored to the hand, then putting the hand in the blind field recruits neurons that can support vision around it. The racket result suggests that the brain treats a held tool as an extension of the hand's space. W.M. is one of the chapter's three examples of sensory-system plasticity.

### Somatosensory agnosias

There are two major types of **somatosensory agnosia**:

1. **Astereognosia**: the inability to recognise objects by touch. **Pure** astereognosia, without simple sensory deficits, is **rare** (Corkin, Milner and Rasmussen, 1970).
2. **Asomatognosia**: the failure to recognise **parts of one's own body**. It is usually **unilateral**, affecting only the **left side** of the body, and is usually associated with extensive damage to the **right temporal and posterior parietal lobes** (Feinberg et al., 2010).

> **Trap.** **Astereognosia** = cannot recognise **objects** by touch (the failure of stereognosis). **Asomatognosia** = cannot recognise **one's own body parts** ("soma" = body). **Anosognosia** = failure to recognise one's own **symptoms**. Three look-alike words, three different deficits; asomatognosia often comes packaged with anosognosia and contralateral neglect.

### The Case of Aunt Betty, Who Lost Half of Her Body

The neurologist **Harold Klawans (1990)** described this case; the patient was his mother's best friend, whom he called Aunt Betty. She had a **left hemiplegia** (paralysis of the left side) after a **right-hemisphere stroke**.

When he arrived she was lying on her back with her head and eyes turned to the right. He approached from her left side and called her name, but she did not turn toward him and instead called out asking where he was. Once he gently turned her head toward him, it was clear that her speech, memory and alertness were intact. Yet her gaze kept drifting back to the right, as though the left half of the world was not there. When he held up her right hand she identified it, with some impatience, as her own. When he held up her paralysed left hand in her view, she called it a hand but attributed it to him. She also could not say why she was in hospital, offering instead that she had come to see him.

The case packs three phenomena together. Her failure to recognise her own left hand is **asomatognosia**. Her apparent unawareness of her paralysis and of her reason for being in hospital is **anosognosia**, the failure of neuropsychological patients to recognise their own symptoms. The book notes that anosognosia is a common but curious feature of many neurological disorders: patients with severe behavioural problems often believe they are doing quite well. And her failure to orient to the left is part of **contralateral neglect**, the tendency not to respond to stimuli on the side opposite a **right-hemisphere** injury; asomatognosia is commonly a component of contralateral neglect, which is covered in Chapter 8.

### The rubber-hand illusion

We experience our body parts as our own so automatically that the experience is taken for granted, which is why failures such as asomatognosia are so striking. Over the past decade research has focused on an exception that runs in the **opposite** direction: the **rubber-hand illusion**, the feeling that an extraneous object, here a rubber hand, is actually **part of one's own body**.

The usual induction (Kilteni et al., 2015; Moseley, Gallace and Spence, 2012; Figure 7.13):

1. A healthy volunteer's real hand is **hidden behind a screen**.
2. A **rubber hand** is placed beside it, in full view.
3. The experimenter strokes the hidden real hand and the visible rubber hand **synchronously**, again and again.

In **less than a minute**, many volunteers start to feel that the rubber hand belongs to them (Blanke, Slater and Serino, 2015). There is a physiological signature too: when the illusion takes hold, the **temperature of the hidden real hand drops** (Moseley et al., 2008), as though the body were partly disowning it. The synchrony is the active ingredient: the illusion is built from the match between what is seen (the rubber hand being stroked) and what is felt (one's own hand being stroked at the same moment).

The neural mechanisms are **not known**. Functional imaging implicates association cortex in the **posterior parietal and frontal lobes** in inducing the illusion (Limanowski and Blankenburg, 2015; Tsakiris et al., 2007), and it has been suggested that frontal and parietal **bimodal neurons with both visual and somatosensory receptive fields** play a critical role (Kilteni et al., 2015), the same kind of neuron that explained W.M. The illusion is **not limited to humans**: rubber tails have been demonstrated in mice and rubber arms in monkeys (Brecht et al., 2017).

> **Trap.** The rubber-hand illusion is the **opposite** of asomatognosia: an external object is *accepted* as one's own, rather than one's own limb being *disowned*. Its key ingredients are a **hidden** real hand, a **visible** rubber hand and **synchronous** stroking; its marker is a **drop in temperature** of the real hand (Moseley et al., 2008); its proposed substrate is **frontal and posterior parietal bimodal (visual plus somatosensory) neurons**.

### Perception of pain: three paradoxes

A **paradox** is a logical contradiction, and the book organises everything it says about pain around **three** of them. Learn them as a set, because "which of the following is not one of the paradoxes of pain" is an easy question to write:

1. **Pain is adaptive**: an experience that feels wholly bad is essential to survival.
2. **Pain has no clear cortical representation**: the most compelling sensation has no obvious cortical home.
3. **Pain is modulated by cognition and emotion**: the most compelling sensation can be switched off by psychological factors.

### Paradox 1: pain is adaptive

There is **no special stimulus for pain**. Pain is a response to potentially harmful stimulation of **any** type, and its job is to make us stop doing something that could damage us, or to seek treatment (Navratilova and Porreca, 2014). The value of pain is shown most clearly by people who cannot feel it.

### The Case of Miss C., the Woman Who Felt No Pain

Miss C., described by **Melzack and Wall (1982)**, was a very intelligent university student who was normal in every respect except one: she had never felt pain. Her condition is now called **congenital insensitivity to pain**.

The details are what examiners use. Strong electric shock, very hot water and an ice bath produced **no pain**, and also **no autonomic response**: her blood pressure, heart rate and breathing did not change. She did not **sneeze**, **cough** or show **corneal reflexes** (the protective blink). As a child she had bitten off the tip of her tongue while eating and had been badly burned after kneeling on a radiator. As an adult she developed pathological changes in her **knees, hip and spine**, because without pain she never made the small protective adjustments the rest of us make unconsciously: shifting weight while standing, turning over during sleep, avoiding damaging postures. She **died at the age of 29** from massive infections and extensive skin and bone trauma.

The point of the case is the first paradox. Pain is not a nuisance layered on top of a healthy life; it is the warning system that keeps the body intact, and a life without it is short. In the book's "thinking creatively" theme, Miss C. is the evidence that pain is a **positive** sensation we cannot live without.

### The genetics of congenital insensitivity to pain

**Cox and colleagues (2006)** studied **six** cases of congenital insensitivity to pain in members of one family from **Pakistan**. They traced the disorder in all six to an abnormality in a gene that influences the synthesis of **sodium ion channels**. Converging evidence came from animals: **knockout mice** lacking this sodium channel gene show a comparable indifference to pain (Gingras et al., 2014). (Outside the book, the gene is usually identified as *SCN9A*, coding the Nav1.7 sodium channel found in pain-signalling neurons; that detail is not in the 11th edition.)

Why sodium channels? Recall from Chapter 4 that action potentials depend on voltage-gated sodium channels. If the channel used by pain-transmitting neurons is missing, the pain signal is never generated or conducted, even though the person is otherwise neurologically normal.

> **Trap.** Miss C. was reported by **Melzack and Wall (1982)**; the **genetic** study of **six** Pakistani family members was **Cox et al. (2006)**, and the gene affects **sodium** channels (not potassium, not opioid receptors). Miss C. died at **29**. Do not mix her up with neuropathic pain, which is the opposite problem (pain without a pain stimulus).

### Paradox 2: pain has no clear cortical representation

The second paradox is that pain has no obvious cortical representation (Rainville, 2002). Painful stimuli activate many brain areas, including the **thalamus, SI, SII, the insula and the anterior cingulate cortex** (Navratilova and Porreca, 2014). But **none of them seems necessary** for perceiving pain. Two observations make the case:

- Painful stimuli usually produce responses in SI and SII (Zhuo, 2008), yet **removing SI and SII in humans does not change the pain threshold**.
- **Hemispherectomised** patients (those with one cerebral hemisphere removed) can still perceive pain from **both sides** of the body.

The logic is the standard one for localisation: an area that is active during a function but whose removal leaves the function intact cannot be *the* site of that function. Pain must be distributed across many areas, with parallel routes able to compensate for any one loss.

### The anterior cingulate cortex and the thermal grid illusion

The cortical area most often linked to pain is the **anterior cingulate cortex** (ACC), the front part of the cingulate gyrus on the medial surface of the hemisphere (Figure 7.14).

The key experiment used an illusion. In the **thermal grid illusion**, a person places a hand on a grid of metal rods that **alternate between cool and warm** (Figure 7.15; Bokiniec et al., 2018). None of the rods is hot or cold enough to hurt, yet the combination is perceived as painful. **Craig and colleagues (1996)** had participants experience this illusory pain during fMRI. **Only the anterior cingulate cortex** showed a marked increase in activity.

The design is clever because the illusion separates the *perception* of pain from the *stimulus* for pain. Areas that respond to intense thermal stimulation as such should not respond much to a grid of mild temperatures; an area that tracks the experience of pain should. The ACC behaved like the latter.

> **Trap.** The ACC is the area **most frequently linked** to pain, and the thermal grid fMRI study (**Craig et al., 1996**) found activity increases **only** in the ACC. But the second paradox still stands: **no** cortical area has been shown to be *necessary* for pain, and removing SI and SII does not alter the pain threshold. An option stating that "SI is the cortical pain centre" is wrong.

### Paradox 3: pain is modulated by cognition and emotion

The third paradox is that this most compelling of sensations can be powerfully suppressed by cognitive and emotional factors (Bushnell, Čeko and Low, 2013; Senkowski, Höfle and Engel, 2014). The book's examples:

- Men taking part in a particular religious ceremony hang objects from **hooks embedded in their backs** with little sign of pain; walking on hot coals in a ritual (Figure 7.16) is another example.
- **Soldiers** with severe battle wounds often report little pain.
- People injured in **life-threatening situations** often feel no pain until the danger has passed.

In each case, the meaning of the situation, not the tissue damage, determines how much pain is felt.

### The discovery of a descending pain-control circuit

How could psychological factors block pain? **Three discoveries**, taken together, pointed to a specific circuit:

1. **Electrical stimulation of the periaqueductal grey (PAG) is analgesic** (pain-blocking). **Reynolds (1969)** performed surgery on rats using **no analgesia other than PAG stimulation**.
2. The PAG and other brain areas contain **specialised receptors for opioid analgesic drugs** such as morphine.
3. Several **endogenous** (internally produced) **opioid analgesics**, the **endorphins** (Chapter 4), were isolated.

Put the three together and the inference is direct. If the brain has its own opioids, and has opioid receptors in a region whose stimulation blocks pain, then both analgesic drugs and psychological factors might act through an **endorphin-sensitive circuit descending from the PAG**.

### Basbaum and Fields's model of descending analgesia

**Basbaum and Fields (1978)** proposed the first model of that circuit (Figure 7.17). Learn it as three numbered steps, because the first one is counter-intuitive:

1. **In the PAG**, opioids **inhibit inhibitory interneurons**. Removing that inhibition **increases** the activity of PAG neurons whose axons descend to the raphé nuclei. (Opioids are inhibitory, so they work here by *disinhibition*.)
2. The descending PAG axons **excite neurons of the raphé nuclei**, a cluster of **serotonergic** nuclei in the core of the **medulla**. The raphé axons descend in the **dorsal columns** of the spinal cord.
3. The **serotonergic** descending axons **excite inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn** of the spinal cord, and those interneurons **block incoming pain signals**.

So the chain is **PAG to raphé nuclei (serotonin) to spinal inhibitory interneurons**, with endorphins switching it on at the top.

Descending analgesia has been studied intensively ever since, and to accommodate the mass of data the models have become far more complex (Lau and Vaughan, 2014). In particular, the **anterior cingulate cortex** and **prefrontal cortex** are now thought to be important parts of descending analgesia circuits (Davis et al., 2017). That fits paradox 3 neatly: cortical areas concerned with meaning and emotion can feed into the circuit that gates pain at the spinal cord.

> **Trap.** In the Basbaum and Fields (1978) model the **raphé nuclei** are in the **medulla**, the transmitter of the descending raphé axons is **serotonin**, and the descending axons run in the **dorsal columns** (a reminder that the dorsal columns carry descending as well as ascending fibres). Opioids act in the PAG by **inhibiting inhibitory interneurons**, which *increases* PAG output. An option saying "opioids directly excite PAG output neurons" or "the raphé uses dopamine" is wrong.

> **Older-edition material (not in the 11th edition): gate-control theory.** Older editions of Pinel, and most Indian exam keys, present the **gate-control theory** of **Melzack and Wall (1965)** as the forerunner of the descending analgesia research. Its core claim was that pain is not a simple read-out of damage: signals **descending from the brain** can activate **neural gating circuits in the spinal cord** (the dorsal horn) that block incoming pain signals before they ascend. In the standard version, activity in large-diameter touch fibres tends to close the gate, and activity in small-diameter pain fibres tends to open it, which is why rubbing an injured spot can ease the pain. The theory's lasting importance is that it explained how cognition and emotion could reduce pain, and it set the stage for the discoveries about the PAG, opioid receptors and endorphins. Do not confuse the two Melzack and Wall dates: **1965** is gate-control theory; **1982** is the book that reported Miss C.

### Neuropathic pain

Neural plasticity usually helps the nervous system work better. **Neuropathic pain** is a case where it does the opposite (Luo, Kuner and Kuner, 2014). Neuropathic pain is **severe chronic pain in the absence of a recognisable pain stimulus**. A typical case begins with an injury. The injury heals, so there is no longer any obvious reason for pain, yet the patient goes on experiencing chronic, excruciating pain. Attacks are often **triggered by an innocuous stimulus such as a gentle touch**.

Although the pain may be felt in a limb, even in an **amputated** limb (Chapter 10), it is caused by **abnormal activity in the CNS**. Two clinical consequences follow. **Cutting the nerves** from the place where the pain is felt usually gives little or no relief, because the generator is central. And **medications developed for injury-related pain are usually ineffective** against neuropathic pain.

### Three lines of research on neuropathic pain

1. **Microglia.** Aberrant **microglial** activity has been implicated, including the induction of **neuroplastic changes** that keep pain going long after the injury has healed (Inoue and Tsuda, 2018; Kuner and Flor, 2017).
2. **Epigenetics.** There is considerable evidence that **epigenetic mechanisms** (Chapter 2) are involved (Birklein et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2016), and **drugs are being developed** to modify those epigenetic changes as a treatment (Neiderberger et al., 2017). This is the chapter's "thinking about epigenetics" theme.
3. **Where the changes occur.** Both kinds of change (neuroplastic and epigenetic) show up most strongly in the **anterior cingulate cortex** (Bliss et al., 2016; Zhuo, 2016) and **prefrontal cortex** (Peng et al., 2017), the same two structures now believed to be part of the descending analgesia pathways (Davis et al., 2017).

The third point ties the section together: the cortical areas that can *suppress* pain through descending circuits are the same areas whose plastic changes may *sustain* pathological pain.

> **Trap.** **Neuropathic** pain = chronic pain **without** a recognisable pain stimulus, generated **centrally**, often set off by a gentle touch, and **not** relieved by cutting peripheral nerves or by standard analgesics. **Congenital insensitivity to pain** (Miss C.) = **no** pain **despite** harmful stimuli. They are opposite failures of the same system.

## 7.4 Chemical Senses: Smell and Taste

**Olfaction** (smell) and **gustation** (taste) are called the **chemical senses** because their job is to monitor the chemical content of the environment. The two differ in the medium the chemicals arrive in:

- **Smell** is the olfactory system's response to **airborne** chemicals drawn over receptors in the **nasal passages** by inhalation.
- **Taste** is the gustatory system's response to chemicals **in solution** in the **oral cavity**.

### Adaptive roles of the chemical senses

When we eat, smell and taste work together. Molecules from the food excite both olfactory and gustatory receptors, and the result is a single integrated impression called **flavour**. People routinely underestimate how much of flavour is smell; the book's reminder is that people with no sense of smell have trouble telling the flavour of an **apple from an onion**. Flavour is also shaped by the food's temperature, texture and appearance, and by how satiated the eater is (Chaudhauri and Roper, 2010; Rolls et al., 2010).

In humans, the main adaptive role of the chemical senses is **evaluating potential foods**: encouraging consumption of sources of energy and nutrients while avoiding toxins, in natural environments where foods do not come labelled (Yarmolinsky, Zuker and Ryba, 2009). In many other species the chemical senses have a second major role, the **regulation of social interactions**.

### Pheromones: Murphy and Schneider's hamsters

Many species release **pheromones**, chemicals that influence the physiology and behaviour of **conspecifics** (members of the same species) (Stowers and Kuo, 2015; Leighton and Sternberg, 2016).

**Murphy and Schneider (1970)** showed that the sexual and aggressive behaviour of **hamsters** is under pheromonal control. The baseline: a normal male hamster attacks and kills an unfamiliar male placed in its colony, but mounts and impregnates an unfamiliar sexually receptive female. The first manipulation: male hamsters **unable to smell** the intruders showed **neither** aggression nor sexual behaviour, so both responses depend on smell. The second, cleverer manipulation confirmed it. They **swabbed a male intruder with the vaginal secretions of a sexually receptive female** and then put it into an unfamiliar colony. The resident males, instead of attacking it, tried to mate with it. The smell alone was enough to switch the residents' response from lethal aggression to sexual behaviour.

What about humans? The idea of human sexual pheromones attracts a great deal of interest because of its commercial and recreational appeal, but there is **little evidence** that humans release or respond to sexual pheromones (Wyatt, 2017).

> **Trap.** Murphy and Schneider (1970) used **hamsters**, and the key manipulation was **swabbing a male intruder with a receptive female's vaginal secretions**, which converted attack into mounting. Anosmic males showed *neither* aggression *nor* sex. The book's verdict on human sexual pheromones is "**little evidence**," so an option asserting that human pheromones are well established is wrong.

### The olfactory receptors and the olfactory bulbs

The anatomy, from the outside in (Figure 7.18):

1. **Olfactory receptor cells** sit in the upper part of the nose, embedded in a mucus-covered layer of tissue, the **olfactory mucosa**.
2. Their **dendrites** lie in the nasal passages, where airborne chemicals reach them.
3. Their **axons** pass through a porous part of the skull, the **cribriform plate**, and enter the **olfactory bulbs**.
4. In the bulbs they synapse on neurons that project to the brain through the **olfactory tracts**.

### How many kinds of olfactory receptor?

For decades it was assumed that there were only a **few** types of olfactory receptor, and that different smells were coded by different activity profiles across that small set, just as colours were once thought to be coded entirely by profiles across the three types of cone. Then, at the **turn of the 21st century**, it was found that **rats and mice have about 1,000** different olfactory receptor proteins and **humans about 300** (Grabska-Barwińska et al., 2017; Uchida, Poo and Haddad, 2014).

In mammals, **each olfactory receptor cell contains only one type of receptor protein** (Giesel and Datta, 2014; Uchida, Poo and Haddad, 2014). The receptor proteins sit in the membranes of the receptor cells' dendrites, where circulating airborne chemicals can stimulate them. Researchers have looked for an organising principle in how the different receptor types are distributed across the mucosa and found none: every type seems to be **scattered throughout the mucosa**.

How, then, is a particular odour coded? Each receptor type responds, to differing degrees, to a wide variety of odours, so no single receptor type stands for one smell. Instead each odour appears to be encoded by **component processing**, that is, by the **pattern of activity across different receptor types** (Giesel and Datta, 2014).

> **Trap.** About **1,000** receptor protein types in **rats and mice**, about **300** in **humans**, and **one** receptor protein type per receptor cell. Smells are coded by the **pattern across** receptor types (component processing), not by one dedicated receptor per odour, and not by just a few receptor types as the old view held.

### Olfactory glomeruli and the chemotopic map

The axons of the olfactory receptor cells end in discrete clusters of neurons near the surface of the olfactory bulbs: the **olfactory glomeruli**. Here the scattered layout of the mucosa is sorted out. Each glomerulus receives input from **several thousand** receptor cells, **all expressing the same receptor protein** (Gupta, Albeanu and Bhalla, 2015; Wanner et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2016). In **mice**, each olfactory bulb has **one or two glomeruli for each receptor protein type** (Schoppa, 2009).

Since other sensory systems have systematic maps (retinotopic, tonotopic), researchers asked whether glomeruli sensitive to particular odours are laid out systematically on the bulbs. **Three lines of evidence** say yes (Cheetham and Belluscio, 2014; Tsai and Barnea, 2014):

1. **Mirror symmetry** between the left and right bulbs: glomeruli sensitive to a given odour tend to sit at the same sites on both.
2. **Consistency within a species**: odour-specific glomeruli are arranged the same way in different members of the same species.
3. **Similarity across related species**: the layout is similar in rats and mice.

So the bulbs are organised topographically and the arrangement is not random (Wanner et al., 2016), but the principle that governs it has **not yet been discovered** (Murthy, 2011). This poorly understood topographic organisation is called a **chemotopic** map (Falasconi et al., 2012).

### Replacement of olfactory receptor cells

Olfactory receptor cells are **replaced throughout life**. Each new receptor cell survives only **a few weeks** before it in turn is replaced. A new cell grows an axon that must travel all the way to the correct target glomerulus in the bulb. Given that receptors of the same type are scattered all over the mucosa, how each new axon finds the right glomerulus **remains a mystery** (Mori and Sakano, 2011).

### Olfactory pathways to the cortex

Each olfactory bulb projects to several **medial temporal lobe** structures, among them the **amygdala** and the **piriform cortex**, a strip of medial temporal cortex lying beside the amygdala (Bekkers and Suzuki, 2013). The piriform cortex is regarded as **primary olfactory cortex**, although the book notes that this designation is somewhat arbitrary (Gottfried, 2010).

Here is the most-tested fact in the module: **of all the sensory systems, only olfaction sends its major pathway to the cerebral cortex without a relay in the thalamus first.**

From the amygdala-piriform area, **two major olfactory pathways** leave:

| Pathway | Route | Thought to mediate |
|---|---|---|
| **Limbic pathway** | Diffuse projections to the **limbic system** | The **emotional** response to odours |
| **Thalamic-orbitofrontal pathway** | Via the **medial dorsal nuclei** of the thalamus to the **orbitofrontal cortex** (the cortex on the underside of the frontal lobes, next to the orbits) | The **conscious perception** of odours |

> **Trap.** "Olfaction does not use the thalamus" is an over-statement. The correct claim is that olfaction's **major** route reaches **cortex (piriform)** without a thalamic relay. A **secondary** route does pass through the thalamus, via the **medial dorsal nucleus** to the **orbitofrontal cortex**, and it is this route that is linked to **conscious** odour perception. The limbic route handles the **emotional** response.

### Taste receptors, taste buds and papillae

**Taste receptor cells** are found on the tongue and also **throughout the gastrointestinal tract** (Nolden and Feeney, 2020). On the tongue they occur in clusters of **50 to 100** called **taste buds** (Barretto et al., 2015), which are often located around small protuberances called **papillae** (singular papilla). Figure 7.19 shows the relation between receptors, buds and papillae; its caption adds that of the two sizes of papillae visible in the photograph, **only the larger ones contain taste buds**.

Each taste bud's receptor cells are of **three types** (Roper and Chaudhari, 2017):

1. Cells that detect **bitter, sweet and umami** (savoury).
2. Cells that detect **sour**.
3. Cells that detect **salty**.

Within each bud, **only one** receptor cell type, the **presynaptic cell**, synapses onto the neuron that carries signals out of the bud; the other cells appear to communicate with each other through **gap junctions** (Dando and Roper, 2009). Like olfactory receptor cells, gustatory receptor cells live only **a few weeks** before being replaced.

The most studied tastes are **sweet, sour, bitter, salty and umami**, but a case can be made for others (Liman, Zhang and Montell, 2014; Vincis and Fontanini, 2016).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition lists **five** studied tastes (sweet, sour, bitter, salty, **umami**), while older keys sometimes give only four. Also, the familiar "tongue map" (sweet at the tip, bitter at the back, and so on) is not in this edition and is not supported in modern accounts; tastes are detected wherever there are taste buds. Each taste bud contains **50 to 100** receptor cells, and only the **larger** papillae contain taste buds.

### Taste transduction: metabotropic versus ionotropic receptors

The five tastes are transduced by two different families of receptor (Nolden and Feeney, 2020):

| Taste | Receptor family | Number of receptors |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweet** | Metabotropic | **2** |
| **Umami** | Metabotropic | **1** |
| **Bitter** | Metabotropic | **about 25** |
| **Sour** | Ionotropic | **3** |
| **Salty** | Ionotropic | **2** |

As in the olfactory system, there appears to be only **one type of receptor protein per receptor cell**.

The pattern is worth reasoning about. Bitter has by far the most receptors, which fits the chemical senses' main adaptive job: bitterness signals potential toxins, and a wide net of bitter receptors catches many different poisonous compounds. Sour and salty are detected through ion channels, which suits stimuli that are themselves ions (hydrogen ions for acid, sodium for salt).

> **Trap.** **Metabotropic** (G-protein-coupled, Chapter 4): **sweet, umami, bitter**. **Ionotropic** (ion channels): **sour and salty**. Bitter has **about 25** receptors; sweet **two**; umami **one**; sour **three**; salty **two**. The cell type that detects sweet, umami *and* bitter is the same one, which is easy to forget because sweet and bitter feel like opposites.

### Gustatory pathways to the cortex

Follow the signal (Figure 7.20):

1. Gustatory afferents leave the mouth in **three cranial nerves**:
   - **Facial (VII)**: the **front of the tongue**.
   - **Glossopharyngeal (IX)**: the **back of the tongue**.
   - **Vagus (X)**: the **back of the oral cavity**.
2. All of these fibres end in the **solitary nucleus** of the **medulla**.
3. Solitary nucleus neurons project to the **ventral posterior nucleus** of the thalamus.
4. Gustatory axons from the ventral posterior nucleus project to **primary gustatory cortex**, in the **insula**, an area of cortex hidden in the **lateral fissure** (Linster and Fontanini, 2014). A **different area of primary gustatory cortex represents each taste** (Peng et al., 2015).
5. **Secondary gustatory cortex** is in the **orbitofrontal cortex**.

One feature sets gustation apart: unlike the projections of other sensory systems, gustatory projections are **primarily ipsilateral**.

> **Trap.** **Thalamic relays of the chemical senses.** Gustation: **solitary nucleus** (medulla), then the **ventral posterior nucleus** of the thalamus (the same nucleus that relays touch), then the **insula**. Olfaction: no thalamic relay on the major route to the **piriform** cortex; the secondary route uses the **medial dorsal** nucleus to reach the **orbitofrontal cortex**. The orbitofrontal cortex is therefore a target of *both* chemical senses (secondary gustatory cortex and the thalamic olfactory route). And gustatory projections are **ipsilateral**, the opposite of touch.

### Is primary gustatory cortex chemotopic?

Some evidence suggests that primary gustatory cortex, like the olfactory bulbs, is **chemotopically** organised. **Schoenfeld and colleagues (2004)** measured fMRI responses to the five primary tastes and found that **each taste activated a different area of primary gustatory cortex**. Three qualifications are part of the finding: the map was **different in each volunteer**, the five areas **overlapped considerably**, but each volunteer's map was **stable over time**. A similar result has been reported in **mice** (Chen et al., 2011).

### Brain damage and the chemical senses

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Anosmia** | The inability to smell |
| **Ageusia** | The inability to taste |

**Anosmia.** The most common neurological cause is a **blow to the head** that displaces the brain inside the skull and **shears the olfactory nerves where they pass through the cribriform plate**. Less complete olfactory deficits have been linked to many neurological disorders, including **Alzheimer's disease, Down syndrome, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, Korsakoff's syndrome and Parkinson's disease** (Godoy et al., 2015).

**Ageusia** is **rare**, presumably because taste signals leave the mouth by **three separate pathways** (VII, IX and X), so damage to one leaves the others. **Partial** ageusia, limited to the **anterior two-thirds of the tongue on one side**, is sometimes seen after **damage to the ear on the same side**. The reason is anatomical: the branch of the **facial nerve (VII)** that carries taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue **passes through the middle ear** (in standard anatomy this branch is the chorda tympani).

> **Trap.** **Anosmia** (smell) versus **ageusia** (taste): the commonest neurological cause of anosmia is **head trauma shearing the olfactory nerves at the cribriform plate**. Ageusia is rare because of **three** nerve routes; unilateral partial ageusia of the **anterior two-thirds** of the tongue after **ear** damage implicates the **facial (VII)** nerve. If a question pairs "posterior third of the tongue" with VII, it is wrong; that is IX.

## 7.5 Perception

The previous three modules covered four of the five sensory systems. Knowing how each system works, however, does not explain how we build a rich, **multisensory percept** of the world from moment to moment. A **percept** is the outcome of perception, the thing you end up experiencing. The theme of this module is that the nervous system does not simply receive its input; it **decides** how to interpret incoming sensory information, and it bases those decisions on **prior experience**.

### Role of prior experience in perception

Prior knowledge strongly shapes what we perceive. In our visual world shadows usually fall **below** objects rather than above them, because our main light source, the sun, shines from above. The visual system has absorbed that regularity and uses it. The book's "Check It Out: How Knowledge Affects Perception" demonstrates it with a set of shaded circles: each is seen as either a bump or a dent, and always in whichever way makes the shading consistent with **light coming from above**. Nothing in the image specifies depth; the depth comes from an assumption the viewer brings.

The second half of the same demonstration shows that knowing *what* an object is changes how it looks. A blotchy black-and-white image looks like meaningless patches to someone seeing it for the first time. After they have been shown a clearer version, they can see a **Dalmatian** in the original image, and the percept stays richer from then on (de Lange, Heilbron and Kok, 2018). The retinal image has not changed; the knowledge has.

Prior knowledge about the **temporal order** of events matters too (Nobre and van Ede, 2018). Through learning we come to expect the sequence in which the lights of a traffic signal change (green, then yellow, then red, then green again), and that expectation shapes what we perceive at the junction.

### Perceptual decision making

Many researchers now think we build **mental models** of the world from predictable, recurring sensory events. On this view we are **"prediction machines"** that actively construct models of ourselves and our surroundings through our senses (de Lange, Heilbron and Kok, 2018; Freedman and Assad, 2016; Whitney and Leib, 2018; Murray et al., 2016). From moment to moment the brain is deciding what we should perceive, combining **prior experience** with **current sensory input**. This ongoing **perceptual decision making** seems to use up a large share of the brain's energy (Richmond and Zacks, 2017).

### Bistable figures

**Bistable figures** show perceptual decision making in action. The book's example is the classic image that can be seen either as **two faces** in profile or as a **chalice**. The stimulus does not change, but the percept flips back and forth between the two interpretations, because the brain can settle on only one at a time. Interestingly, the switching happens at a **regular frequency**, and that frequency **differs from person to person** (Brascamp et al., 2018).

### Phantom percepts

If perception is a construction, it should be able to run even with no input at all, and it does. **Phantom percepts** are the products of perception in the **absence of sensory input** (Mohan and Vanneste, 2017).

- **Phantom limbs**: amputees continue to perceive their missing limb long after it was lost to injury or amputation.
- **Charles Bonnet syndrome**: some people who lose vision later in life, for example through **glaucoma** (a condition involving irreversible damage to the optic nerve), experience rich and complex visual **hallucinations**, such as people's faces or elaborate landscapes (Mohan and Vanneste, 2017; Sacks, 2012).

The content of the phantom percepts in Charles Bonnet syndrome seems to depend on the person's **experience**: some musicians with the syndrome see phantom **musical notation** (Sacks, 2013). That is exactly what the prediction-machine account expects. When input disappears, the brain's models keep generating perceptions, and the models are built from what the person has spent their life perceiving.

> **Trap.** **Charles Bonnet syndrome** = complex visual hallucinations in people who have **lost vision** (e.g., glaucoma), typically with insight and without psychiatric illness in standard clinical accounts. It is an example of a **phantom percept**, not of psychosis. Its content reflects experience (musicians seeing notation). Do not confuse it with **tinnitus**, which is the auditory analogue the book attributes to plastic change after deafness (7.2).

### Brain mechanisms of perceptual decisions

The mechanisms are not fully understood, but mounting evidence implicates several areas, especially the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** and the **posterior parietal cortex** (Brascamp et al., 2018; Fleming, van der Putten and Daw, 2018). Both structures are also involved in deciding to **initiate a physical movement** (Chapter 8). So the brain areas implicated in deciding what to *do* are the same ones implicated in deciding what to *perceive*.

### The binding problem

Sensory systems work by **division of labour**: many specialised areas, at many levels, linked by many parallel pathways. In vision, for example, separate areas specialise in shape, colour and movement. Yet we do not experience a red ball rolling past as "redness" plus "roundness" plus "motion"; we experience **one integrated object**. How does the brain put the separately analysed attributes back together? This is the **binding problem** (Bizley, Maddox and Lee, 2016; Feldman, 2013; but see Di Lollo, 2012, who questions the framing).

Notice that the binding problem is the price of two of the principles from 7.1. Functional segregation and parallel processing are efficient, but they scatter the analysis of one object across the brain, and something has to reunite it.

### Two proposed solutions to the binding problem

**Solution 1: a single binding site at the top of the hierarchy.** Perhaps one cortical area sits at the top of the sensory hierarchy, receives signals from all the other areas of all the sensory systems, and assembles them into a percept. A structure that has recently attracted attention as a possible binding site is the **claustrum** (Figure 7.21), a thin sheet of neurons lying just beneath the cortex toward the middle of the brain (Goll, Atlan and Citri, 2015; Tan et al., 2017).

**Solution 2: no single binding site.** Alternatively, perceptions might emerge from **many interactions at every cortical level** of the hierarchy, with no area responsible for assembling them. This view draws on what is now known about communication between sensory areas: integration of information from multiple senses begins **as early as the primary sensory cortices** (Murray et al., 2016), as you saw with auditory-visual interactions in 7.2. Binding might therefore arise from the **constant exchange of information** among the many sensory cortices, within and across modalities (Bizley, Maddox and Lee, 2016). Subcortical structures may also take part in that exchange (Romo and Rossi-Pool, 2020).

> **Trap.** The **claustrum** is the structure proposed as a **single binding site**; it is a thin sheet of neurons beneath the cortex, not a thalamic nucleus and not association cortex. The rival, **distributed** solution says binding emerges from interaction at **all** levels, beginning in **primary** sensory cortex. If a question asks which finding supports the distributed view, the answer is multisensory integration in primary sensory cortex.

## 7.6 Selective Attention

At any moment, your sense organs are being stimulated by thousands of things, and you consciously perceive only a small subset of them while largely ignoring the rest (Peelen and Kastner, 2014; Squire et al., 2013). The process that does this selecting is **selective attention**.

### Characteristics of selective attention

Selective attention has **two characteristics** (Sprague, Saproo and Serences, 2015):

1. It **improves** the perception of the stimuli it is focused on.
2. It **interferes** with the perception of the stimuli it is not focused on.

Picture yourself straining to catch an important announcement in a noisy airport. Focusing on the announcement makes you more likely to understand it, and correspondingly less likely to understand what your travelling companion says to you at the same moment. Attention is a trade, not a free gain.

### Endogenous versus exogenous attention

Attention can be steered in two ways (Chica, Bartolomeo and Lupiáñez, 2013; but see Macaluso and Doricchi, 2013):

| Type | What directs it | Example | Neural direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Endogenous attention** | **Internal** cognitive processes | Scanning the table because you are looking for your keys | **Top-down** (higher to lower levels) |
| **Exogenous attention** | **External** events | Your eyes jump to the table because the cat has knocked over a lamp | **Bottom-up** (lower to higher levels) |

(Miller and Buschman, 2013, for the top-down and bottom-up mapping.)

> **Trap.** **Endo**genous = from **within** = goal-driven = **top-down**. **Exo**genous = from **outside** = stimulus-driven = **bottom-up**. The common error is to link "top-down" with the external event because the event "comes down" on you. Anchor it with the keys (endogenous) and the cat (exogenous).

### Covert versus overt attention

Eye movements are often important for visual attention, but visual attention can be shifted **without** moving the eyes (Krauzlis, Lovejoy and Zénon, 2013). The book's "Check It Out" asks you to fix your gaze on a central cross and then shift your attention from letter to letter around it without moving your eyes. What you experience is **covert attention**: a shift of visual attention **without any corresponding eye movement**. A shift of attention that **does** involve a shift of gaze is **overt attention**.

### The cocktail-party phenomenon

One more characteristic of selective attention is the **cocktail-party phenomenon** (Du et al., 2011). You can be so absorbed in one conversation that you have no idea what the surrounding conversations are about, yet if someone in one of those other conversations mentions **your name**, it immediately reaches your awareness. The implication is that the brain can keep everything but one kind of stimulus out of conscious awareness while still **unconsciously monitoring** the blocked-out stimuli, in case something important turns up. Unattended input is filtered from consciousness, not from processing.

### Change blindness

The book calls **change blindness** the best illustration of the importance of attention (Land, 2014). The procedure:

1. A volunteer looks at what seems to be one photograph on a computer screen and is told to report any change in it the moment they notice one.
2. In fact two images are alternating, with a gap of **less than 0.1 second** between them.
3. The two images are identical except for **one gross feature**.

You might expect anyone to spot a large feature vanishing and reappearing at once. In fact most volunteers stare at the image for **many seconds**, actively searching as instructed, before they notice the change; once they finally see it, they are astonished that it took so long. In the 11th edition's Figure 7.22, two women are working at a laptop and the changing feature is a **building in the background** behind the woman on the left. (The main text, carried over from an earlier version of the figure, describes the change as a picture disappearing from a wall; both describe the same principle.)

**Why does it happen?** Because, contrary to our strong impression that we take in a whole scene, we have **no memory for the parts of a scene that are not the focus of attention**. Viewers attend to the people, so when an unattended background feature changes, nothing has been stored to compare it with, and the change goes unnoticed.

**The control condition** is what nails this explanation. Change blindness does **not** occur if the brief blank interval is removed. Without the gap, the change produces a visible flicker of motion at its location, no memory is needed, and the change is seen **immediately**. The gap forces the viewer to compare the new image with a remembered one, and only attended parts are remembered.

> **Trap.** Change blindness depends on the **brief interval (under 0.1 second)** between the two images; remove it and changes are seen at once. The explanation is the **absence of memory for unattended parts** of a scene, not poor eyesight or a failure of the visual cortex to register the stimulus.

### Neural mechanisms of attention: where top-down control comes from

Where do the top-down influences on sensory systems originate? There is general agreement that **prefrontal cortex** and **posterior parietal cortex** both play major roles in directing top-down attention (Baluch and Itti, 2011; Noudoost et al., 2010).

### Moran and Desimone (1985): attention changes single neurons

**Moran and Desimone (1985)** were the **first** to show that attention changes the activity of neurons. They trained monkeys to stare at a fixation point while they recorded from neurons in a **prestriate** area of the **ventral stream** that was especially sensitive to **colour**. One experiment targeted single cells that would fire to a **red bar or a green bar** of light falling in their receptive fields.

If the task forced the monkey to attend to the **red** cue, the neurons fired **more** to the red cue and **less** to the green cue. When the task required attention to **green**, the opposite happened. The stimuli in the receptive field were physically the same in both conditions; only the monkey's attention changed, and the neurons' responses followed it. That is direct evidence that attention operates **within sensory cortex**, not just after it.

### Attention in the human brain: Corbetta and Ungerleider and Haxby

Human functional imaging studies paralleled the monkey work.

- **Corbetta and colleagues (1990)** showed volunteers a display of moving, coloured stimuli of various shapes and asked them to discriminate among the stimuli by **movement**, **colour** or **shape**. Attending to **shape or colour** increased activity in areas of the **ventral stream**; attending to **movement** increased activity in an area of the **dorsal stream**.
- **Ungerleider and Haxby (1994)** showed volunteers a series of faces and asked either whether two faces belonged to the **same person** or whether they were in the **same position** relative to the frame. Attending to **identity** activated **ventral stream** regions; attending to **position** activated **dorsal stream** regions.

Both studies hold the stimulus constant and vary only the task, so the activity differences must reflect what is being attended. And both map neatly onto the what (ventral) versus where (dorsal) division of visual cortex from Chapter 6.

### The push-pull principle

The studies above reveal the general principle by which selective attention works: it **strengthens** neural responses to attended stimuli and **weakens** responses to the others (Buschman, 2015; Luo and Maunsell, 2015). This dual mechanism is called a **push-pull mechanism** (Stevens and Bavelier, 2012), and it is the neural counterpart of the two behavioural characteristics of attention (improving the attended, interfering with the unattended).

### Attention reshapes receptive fields

Some attentional mechanisms involve a surprising degree of **neural plasticity**. A neuron's receptive field location had been assumed to be a fixed property, but it can be shifted by spatial attention (Anton-Erxleben and Carrasco, 2013):

- **Wommelsdorf and colleagues (2006)**, recording in an area of monkey **secondary visual cortex in the dorsal stream**, found that the receptive fields of many neurons **shifted toward** the points in the visual field the animals were attending to.
- **Rolls (2008)** found that the visual receptive fields of **inferotemporal cortex** neurons **shrink** until they are little bigger than the object being attended to.

The shifting receptive fields are the chapter's third example of sensory-system plasticity (after tinnitus and W.M.'s hand).

### Covert attention and the frontal eye field

**Covert attention** (attending to a stimulus without fixating it) is especially useful to biopsychologists, because it lets them study shifts in **attentional gaze**, the movement of attention from one perceptual object to another, without the confounding effects of eye movements. Those studies have identified the **frontal eye field** as important.

The frontal eye field is active during **eye movements**, and it is also active during **shifts of attentional gaze** without eye movement. More tellingly, stimulating frontal eye field neurons at intensities **below the threshold needed to produce eye movements** **improves the detection of stimuli** through covert attention (Moore and Zirnsak, 2017). So the frontal eye field appears to mediate shifts in visual attention, although other brain areas have been implicated as well (Schmitz and Duncan, 2018).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition describes the frontal eye field as lying on the **ventral** surface of the frontal cortex; most anatomy sources place it in the posterior part of the middle frontal gyrus, just in front of motor cortex. If asked for the lobe, the answer is unambiguously **frontal**. The key experimental finding is **sub-threshold** stimulation (too weak to move the eyes) **improving covert detection**, which shows that attention shifts and eye movements share machinery without being identical.

### Attention in the other senses

If you have concluded that most research on the neural mechanisms of attention concerns vision, you are right. There are, however, studies of attention to **auditory** (Shamma, Elhilali and Micheyl, 2011), **somatosensory** (Fujiwara et al., 2002), **gustatory** (Stevenson, 2012; Veldhuizen, Gitelman and Small, 2012) and **olfactory** (Veldhuizen and Small, 2011) stimuli.

### Simultanagnosia: the opening case solved

Return to the man who could see only one thing at a time. He could identify objects anywhere in his visual field when they were shown **one at a time**, so he was **not blind** and had no visual field defect. His disorder was a disorder of **attention**, called **simultanagnosia**; specifically, **visual simultanagnosia**, a difficulty in **attending visually to more than one object at a time**.

Where was the damage? The **dorsal stream**, which includes the **posterior parietal association cortex**, is responsible for locating objects in space, and attending to several objects at once requires keeping several locations active together. If you predicted posterior parietal damage, you were right: **simultanagnosia is usually associated with bilateral damage to the posterior parietal cortex**.

> **Trap.** Simultanagnosia is a disorder of **attention**, not of acuity or object recognition: single objects are recognised normally. It follows **bilateral posterior parietal** (dorsal stream) damage. Contrast **Dr. P.** (visual agnosia: cannot recognise single objects despite good acuity) and **contralateral neglect** (usually **unilateral right**-hemisphere damage, ignoring the left side). In clinical neurology, simultanagnosia is one component of **Bálint's syndrome** (with optic ataxia and oculomotor apraxia) after bilateral parieto-occipital damage; that label is not used in the 11th edition but appears in many exam keys.

## How the chapter fits together

Seven threads run through all six modules. Trace each one and the anatomy stops being a list.

1. **One plan, five senses.** Every system has receptors that transduce one kind of energy, a thalamic relay (medial geniculate for hearing, ventral posterior for touch and taste, lateral geniculate for vision), a primary cortex with a map, secondary areas and association cortex. The maps change name (retinotopic, tonotopic, periodotopic, somatotopic, chemotopic) but the idea is the same. Olfaction is the instructive exception, reaching its primary cortex without a thalamic relay.
2. **Hierarchical, segregated, parallel.** The 1960s model (hierarchical, homogeneous, serial) lost two of its three terms. Segregation shows up as core/belt/parabelt and what/where streams in hearing, four strips and dorsal/ventral streams in touch, a separate gustatory patch for each taste. Parallelism shows up as the mildness of SI lesions (Corkin, Milner and Rasmussen, 1970), the "tends to" in the two somatosensory pathways, the survival of pain after SI/SII removal or hemispherectomy, and the rarity of ageusia (three nerves).
3. **Higher damage, more specific deficit.** Receptor loss abolishes a sense; cortical damage leaves sensation and removes something specific: word deafness, astereognosia, asomatognosia, visual agnosia (Dr. P.), simultanagnosia. Every patient in the chapter can be placed on this ladder.
4. **The senses meet early, not just late.** Bimodal posterior parietal neurons (Mullette-Gillman, Cohen and Groh, 2005; W.M.'s hand; the rubber-hand illusion), and multisensory effects in **primary** cortex, show that cross-modal integration is part of sensory processing from the start. This is also the evidence behind the distributed solution to the binding problem.
5. **What versus where, again and again.** Vision's ventral/dorsal split reappears as anterior/posterior auditory streams and ventral/dorsal somatosensory streams, and in the attention studies of Corbetta (1990) and Ungerleider and Haxby (1994). Damage follows the split: anterior auditory damage impairs identification, posterior impairs localisation; posterior parietal (dorsal) damage produces simultanagnosia.
6. **Top-down signals shape what arrives.** Descending neurons in every hierarchy, endogenous attention, descending analgesia from the PAG (and now the ACC and prefrontal cortex), prior experience in perception, and perceptual decision making in dorsolateral prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex all describe the same thing: higher levels changing lower-level processing.
7. **Plasticity cuts both ways.** The book's three plasticity examples are tinnitus after deafness, W.M.'s improved vision near his hand, and receptive fields shifting toward attended locations. Add neuropathic pain (microglia, epigenetics, ACC and prefrontal changes) and the delay-sensitive benefit of cochlear implants, and you have plasticity as both rescue and pathology.

The book's own "Themes Revisited" adds the case list you should be able to recite: the **visual simultanagnosia** patient (opening case, Williams, 1970), **Dr. P.** the visual agnosic (Sacks, 1985), **Aunt Betty** the asomatognosic (Klawans, 1990), **Miss C.** who felt no pain (Melzack and Wall, 1982), and **W.M.** who reduced his scotoma with his hand (Schendel and Robertson, 2004). Its "thinking about epigenetics" theme is neuropathic pain, and its "consciousness" theme runs through perception and attention.

## Worked example

*A 30-year-old man is seen after a motorcycle accident in which he struck his head and was also stabbed in the back during a fight at the scene. Four complaints: (a) "Food has no taste any more." He cannot tell apple juice from onion juice with his eyes closed, yet he correctly names sweet, salty, sour and bitter solutions dropped on his tongue. (b) Below a mid-thoracic level, he cannot tell which way his right toes are being moved and cannot feel a vibrating tuning fork on his right leg; on his left leg, a pinprick and hot water feel like nothing. (c) Months later, although the back wound has healed, he has burning pain in a band around his trunk at the level of the injury, set off by the light touch of his shirt, and pain medication does not help. A surgeon suggests cutting the nerves that supply that band of skin. (d) Since the accident he hears ringing in his right ear, which was injured, and asks whether cutting the right auditory nerve would stop it.*

Reason from the chapter's machinery rather than from memory.

**(a) Has he lost taste?** No. His gustatory system works: he identifies the basic tastes, which are carried by cranial nerves VII, IX and X to the solitary nucleus, ventral posterior thalamus and insula. What he has lost is **smell**, and with it most of **flavour**. The book's own test case is exactly his complaint: people without smell cannot tell apples from onions. The likely mechanism is the commonest neurological cause of **anosmia**: a blow to the head displacing the brain and **shearing the olfactory nerves at the cribriform plate**. His complaint that "food has no taste" is a misattribution, because flavour feels like taste even though much of it is smell.

**(b) Why are the losses on opposite sides?** This is a **right-sided hemisection** of the cord. Touch, vibration and proprioception travel in the **dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system**, which ascends **ipsilaterally** and does not cross until the **medulla**; cutting the right half of the cord therefore removes them from the **right** leg. Pain and temperature travel in the **anterolateral system**, whose fibres synapse on entry and **cross within the spinal cord**; fibres from the **left** leg have already crossed into the right half of the cord by the level of the cut, so the left leg loses pain and temperature. The level matters too: sensation is lost only below the cut, which is why with spinal injuries lower is better.

**(c) Should the nerves be cut?** No, and the chapter tells you why. This is **neuropathic pain**: severe chronic pain with no recognisable pain stimulus, appearing after an injury has healed and triggered by an innocuous touch. It is generated by **abnormal CNS activity**, so cutting peripheral nerves usually brings little or no relief, and ordinary injury analgesics are usually ineffective, as he has already found. The mechanisms under study (aberrant **microglia**, **epigenetic** changes, plastic change in the **anterior cingulate** and **prefrontal cortex**) are all central. The fact that the ACC and prefrontal cortex are also parts of the descending analgesia circuit suggests where future treatments may act.

**(d) Will cutting the auditory nerve cure the ringing?** Almost certainly not. The book's key observation is that when only one ear is damaged, the ringing is heard in that ear, yet **cutting the nerve from that ear does not stop it**. Tinnitus is attributed to **neuroplastic changes** in the central auditory system produced by the hearing loss itself. Removing the remaining input would, if anything, add deprivation.

**What would you check next?** Whether his hearing loss is **conductive** (ossicles) or **nerve** (hair cells or auditory nerve). If it is nerve deafness from hair-cell loss and becomes severe, a **cochlear implant** is an option, and the earlier it is fitted, the more likely it is to help, because disuse alters the auditory pathways. Note that a lesion of one auditory cortex would not explain hearing loss in one ear, because each ear projects to **both** hemispheres from the superior olives onward.

## MCQ traps

1. **Current model of sensory organisation** = hierarchical, **functionally segregated**, **parallel**. The 1960s model = hierarchical, functionally **homogeneous**, **serial**. "Hierarchical" is in both.
2. **Primary vs secondary vs association cortex** is defined by **input**: thalamic relay nuclei / primary cortex of the same system / more than one sensory system.
3. **Sensation vs perception:** detecting stimuli vs integrating, recognising and interpreting patterns. Dr. P. (Sacks, 1985) had excellent acuity (spotted a pin) but failed perception.
4. **Opening case (11th ed.)** = the man who could see only one thing at a time (**Williams, 1970**), a **simultanagnosia** case, **not** Dr. P., who opened older editions.
5. **Hearing range** = about **20 to 20,000 Hz**; frequency discrimination as fine as **0.2 percent**.
6. **Amplitude, frequency, complexity** map to **loudness, pitch, timbre** respectively.
7. **Missing fundamental:** 200 + 300 + 400 Hz is heard with the pitch of **100 Hz** (highest common divisor), though no 100 Hz component is present.
8. **Ossicles in order:** malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), stapes (stirrup); stapes drives the **oval** window; vibrations are dissipated at the **round** window.
9. **Hair cells sit in the basilar membrane**; the **tectorial** membrane rests on them; transduction is by **shearing**.
10. **Place coding:** **high** frequencies peak **near the windows** (base); **low** frequencies at the **tip** (apex).
11. **Auditory pathway order:** cochlear nuclei (ipsilateral), **superior olives** (bilateral), lateral lemniscus, **inferior colliculi**, **medial** geniculate, primary auditory cortex. The ears first converge in the **superior olives**.
12. **Primary auditory cortex** = **core** of **three** areas in the temporal lobe within the lateral fissure; belt and parabelt are secondary; about **13** auditory areas in primates.
13. **Three principles of primary auditory cortex:** functional columns, **tonotopy** (frequency), **periodotopy** (temporal amplitude fluctuations).
14. **Bendor and Wang (2005):** pitch-selective neurons in a small area **just anterior to primary auditory cortex** in monkeys, found with missing-fundamental stimuli.
15. **Auditory streams:** **anterior = what** (identify), **posterior = where** (locate). Damage follows the same split.
16. **Bilateral auditory cortex lesions** give **transient** total deafness, then lasting **word deafness**, not permanent deafness.
17. **Conductive deafness** = ossicles; **nerve deafness** = cochlea or auditory nerve, mainly hair-cell loss. Total deafness = only **1 percent** of hearing-impaired; about **360 million** disabled by hearing loss (Lesica, 2018).
18. **Tinnitus** persists after cutting the nerve from the ringing ear, so it is attributed to **central neuroplastic change**.
19. **Cochlear implants** stimulate the **auditory nerve**, do not restore normal hearing, and work better the **sooner** they are fitted.
20. **Pacinian corpuscles** are the **largest, deepest, rapidly adapting** receptors (sudden displacement); **Merkel's disks** (indentation) and **Ruffini endings** (stretch) adapt **slowly**; **free nerve endings** handle temperature and pain. Meissner's corpuscles are not in the 11th edition's figure.
21. **Dorsal-column medial-lemniscus:** ipsilateral ascent, synapse and **decussation in the medulla**, medial lemniscus to the **contralateral ventral posterior nucleus**. Longest neurons in the body start in the **toes**.
22. **Anterolateral system:** synapse **on entry**, most axons cross **in the spinal cord**; three tracts: **spinothalamic, spinoreticular, spinotectal**.
23. **The two somatosensory pathways only "tend to"** divide function; lesions of either do not abolish its modality.
24. **Penfield (1937)** mapped SI in conscious patients; SI is **somatotopic**; the homunculus is distorted toward hands, lips, tongue; **Servos et al. (1999)**: the **face** is probably **inverted** too.
25. **SII** lies ventral to SI, largely in the lateral fissure, gets input mainly from SI and from **both sides** of the body.
26. **Corkin, Milner and Rasmussen (1970):** SI excision gave only **two minor contralateral** deficits (light touch, stereognosis), **bilateral** only if **SII** was encroached.
27. **W.M. (Schendel and Robertson, 2004):** right PCA stroke, left hemianopsia; **97.8 percent** right-field vs **13.6 percent** left-field detection; better near his extended left hand, and further with a **tennis racket**.
28. **Astereognosia** (objects by touch) vs **asomatognosia** (own body parts) vs **anosognosia** (own symptoms). Asomatognosia is usually **left-sided**, after **right temporal and posterior parietal** damage (Feinberg et al., 2010).
29. **Aunt Betty (Klawans, 1990):** left hemiplegia after right-hemisphere stroke; called her own left hand the doctor's.
30. **Rubber-hand illusion:** synchronous stroking, under a minute, **hidden hand temperature drops** (Moseley et al., 2008); in mice (tails) and monkeys (arms) too (Brecht et al., 2017).
31. **Three paradoxes of pain:** adaptive; no clear cortical representation; modulated by cognition and emotion.
32. **Miss C. (Melzack and Wall, 1982)** died at **29**; **Cox et al. (2006)**: **six** members of a **Pakistani** family, **sodium-channel** gene.
33. **Removing SI and SII** does not change the pain threshold; hemispherectomy patients feel pain on **both** sides.
34. **Thermal grid illusion (Craig et al., 1996):** illusory pain activated **only the anterior cingulate cortex**.
35. **Reynolds (1969)** operated on rats under **PAG stimulation** alone.
36. **Basbaum and Fields (1978):** opioids **inhibit inhibitory interneurons** in the PAG; PAG excites **serotonergic raphé** neurons (**medulla**); raphé axons descend in the **dorsal columns** and excite **inhibitory dorsal-horn interneurons**.
37. **Gate-control theory = Melzack and Wall (1965)**, an older-edition topic; do not confuse with their **1982** Miss C. report.
38. **Neuropathic pain** is central; cutting nerves and standard analgesics usually fail; implicated: microglia, epigenetics, **ACC and prefrontal cortex**.
39. **Murphy and Schneider (1970):** hamster intruder swabbed with a receptive female's vaginal secretions was **mounted, not attacked**. Little evidence for human sexual pheromones.
40. **Olfactory receptor types:** about **1,000** in rats and mice, about **300** in humans; **one receptor type per cell**; coding by **component processing**.
41. **Each glomerulus** gets input from several thousand receptor cells **with the same receptor protein**; in mice, **one or two** glomeruli per receptor type per bulb.
42. **Olfaction's major pathway reaches cortex (piriform) without the thalamus**; the secondary route goes via the **medial dorsal** nucleus to the **orbitofrontal** cortex (conscious smell); the limbic route mediates emotional response.
43. **Taste nerves:** **VII** front of tongue, **IX** back of tongue, **X** back of oral cavity, all to the **solitary nucleus**, then **ventral posterior** thalamus, then **insula** (primary), **orbitofrontal** (secondary). Gustatory projections are **ipsilateral**.
44. **Taste receptors:** metabotropic for **sweet (2), umami (1), bitter (about 25)**; ionotropic for **sour (3), salty (2)**. Taste buds hold **50 to 100** cells; only the **presynaptic cell** synapses on the outgoing neuron.
45. **Anosmia's** commonest neurological cause is head trauma shearing the olfactory nerves at the **cribriform plate**; **ageusia** is rare (three nerves); one-sided anterior-two-thirds ageusia after ear damage implicates **VII**.
46. **Binding problem solutions:** a single site (**claustrum**) vs distributed interaction at all levels (supported by multisensory integration in **primary** cortex).
47. **Endogenous = top-down** (keys); **exogenous = bottom-up** (cat knocking over a lamp).
48. **Change blindness** needs the **under 0.1 second** gap; it reflects **no memory for unattended parts** of a scene.
49. **Moran and Desimone (1985)** were **first** to show attention changes neuron firing (prestriate, ventral stream, colour; red vs green).
50. **Push-pull mechanism:** attention strengthens responses to attended stimuli and weakens responses to the rest.
51. **Receptive fields move with attention** (Wommelsdorf et al., 2006, dorsal stream) and **shrink** around attended objects in inferotemporal cortex (Rolls, 2008).
52. **Frontal eye field:** sub-threshold stimulation (no eye movement) **improves covert detection**.
53. **Simultanagnosia** = **bilateral posterior parietal** damage; single objects are recognised normally.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Exteroceptive sensory systems** | Sensory systems that detect stimuli arising outside the body (visual, auditory, somatosensory, olfactory, gustatory) |
| **Sensation** | The process of detecting the presence of stimuli |
| **Perception** | The higher-order process of integrating, recognising and interpreting patterns of sensations |
| **Primary sensory cortex** | Sensory cortex receiving most of its input directly from the thalamic relay nuclei of its system |
| **Secondary sensory cortex** | Sensory cortex receiving most of its input from the primary (or other secondary) cortex of the same system |
| **Association cortex** | Any cortex receiving input from more than one sensory system, mostly via secondary areas |
| **Hierarchical organisation** | Ranking of sensory structures by the specificity and complexity of their function, from receptors to association cortex |
| **Functional segregation** | Each cortical level of a sensory system contains distinct areas specialised for different analyses |
| **Parallel processing** | Simultaneous analysis of a signal in different ways by multiple parallel pathways |
| **Top-down / bottom-up** | Signals or processes running from higher to lower levels / from lower to higher levels |
| **Fourier analysis** | Mathematical decomposition of a complex wave into its component sine waves |
| **Fundamental frequency** | The highest common divisor of a complex sound's component frequencies, to which its pitch is related |
| **Missing fundamental** | Perception of the pitch of a fundamental frequency that is absent from the sound (200, 300, 400 Hz heard as 100 Hz) |
| **Tympanic membrane** | The eardrum, set vibrating by sound waves in the auditory canal |
| **Ossicles** | The three small middle-ear bones: malleus, incus, stapes |
| **Oval window** | Membrane through which stapes vibrations enter the cochlear fluid |
| **Round window** | Elastic cochlear membrane that dissipates the vibrations |
| **Cochlea** | Snail-shaped, coiled, fluid-filled inner-ear tube containing the organ of Corti |
| **Organ of Corti** | The auditory receptor organ running along the inside of the cochlea |
| **Hair cells** | The auditory receptors, mounted in the basilar membrane |
| **Basilar membrane** | Organ of Corti membrane holding the hair cells; site of place coding of frequency |
| **Tectorial membrane** | Organ of Corti membrane resting on the hair cells; deflection produces shearing |
| **Auditory nerve** | Branch of the auditory-vestibular (eighth cranial) nerve carrying signals from the hair cells |
| **Retinotopic** | Organised according to a map of the retina |
| **Tonotopic** | Organised according to sound frequency |
| **Semicircular canals** | Inner-ear receptive organs of the vestibular system |
| **Vestibular system** | System signalling the direction and intensity of head movements; supports balance |
| **Cochlear nuclei** | First brain stem relay, receiving the ipsilateral auditory nerve |
| **Superior olives** | Brain stem nuclei where signals from the two ears first combine |
| **Lateral lemniscus** | Auditory tract from the superior olives to the inferior colliculi |
| **Inferior colliculi** | Midbrain (tectal) auditory nuclei projecting to the medial geniculate |
| **Medial geniculate nuclei** | Thalamic auditory relay to primary auditory cortex |
| **Core, belt, parabelt** | Primary auditory cortex (three core areas) and the surrounding rings of secondary auditory cortex |
| **Periodotopy** | Organisation of auditory cortex by temporal fluctuations in sound amplitude |
| **Unimodal** | Involving only one sensory system |
| **Word deafness** | Lasting inability to process the structure of speech sounds after bilateral auditory cortex lesions |
| **Conductive deafness** | Hearing loss from damage to the ossicles |
| **Nerve deafness** | Hearing loss from damage to the cochlea or auditory nerve, mainly hair-cell loss |
| **Tinnitus** | Ringing in the ears, attributed to central plastic change after hearing loss |
| **Cochlear implant** | Device converting sound into electrical signals delivered by electrodes in the cochlea to excite the auditory nerve |
| **Somatosensory system** | The exteroceptive, proprioceptive and interoceptive systems mediating bodily sensation |
| **Proprioceptive system** | Monitors body position via receptors in muscles, joints and organs of balance |
| **Interoceptive system** | Monitors internal conditions such as temperature and blood pressure |
| **Free nerve endings** | Unspecialised receptors sensitive especially to temperature change and pain |
| **Pacinian corpuscles** | Largest, deepest, onion-like, rapidly adapting receptors for sudden skin displacement |
| **Merkel's disks** | Slowly adapting receptors for gradual skin indentation |
| **Ruffini endings** | Slowly adapting receptors for skin stretch |
| **Stereognosis** | Identifying objects by touch |
| **Dorsal-column medial-lemniscus system** | Pathway mainly for touch and proprioception: ipsilateral dorsal columns, crossing in the medulla, medial lemniscus to the ventral posterior thalamus |
| **Anterolateral system** | Pathway mainly for pain and temperature: synapse on entry, crossing in the cord; spinothalamic, spinoreticular and spinotectal tracts |
| **Dorsal columns** | Spinal cord tracts in which dorsal-column system axons ascend ipsilaterally |
| **Dorsal column nuclei** | Medullary nuclei where dorsal column axons synapse before decussating |
| **Medial lemniscus** | Somatosensory tract from the dorsal column nuclei to the contralateral ventral posterior nucleus |
| **Ventral posterior nucleus** | Thalamic relay for somatosensation (and for taste) |
| **Trigeminal nerve** | Cranial nerve whose three branches carry somatosensory information from the face |
| **Somatotopic** | Organised according to a map of the body surface |
| **Somatosensory homunculus** | The distorted body map of SI ("little man") |
| **SI / SII** | Primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus, largely contralateral input) / secondary somatosensory cortex (ventral to SI, bilateral input) |
| **Bimodal neurons** | Neurons responding to two sensory systems, e.g., touch and vision near the hand |
| **Hemianopsia** | A scotoma covering half the visual field |
| **Astereognosia** | Inability to recognise objects by touch |
| **Asomatognosia** | Failure to recognise parts of one's own body, usually the left side |
| **Anosognosia** | Failure of neuropsychological patients to recognise their own symptoms |
| **Contralateral neglect** | Tendency not to respond to stimuli contralateral to a right-hemisphere injury |
| **Rubber-hand illusion** | Feeling that a rubber hand is part of one's own body after synchronous stroking |
| **Congenital insensitivity to pain** | Inborn absence of pain perception (Miss C.; sodium-channel gene in Cox et al., 2006) |
| **Thermal grid illusion** | Pain perceived from a grid of alternating cool and warm rods |
| **Anterior cingulate cortex** | Front part of the cingulate gyrus; the cortical area most often linked to pain |
| **Periaqueductal grey (PAG)** | Midbrain grey matter whose stimulation is analgesic; top of the descending analgesia circuit |
| **Endorphins** | Endogenous opioid analgesics |
| **Raphé nuclei** | Serotonergic nuclei in the core of the medulla relaying descending analgesia |
| **Neuropathic pain** | Severe chronic pain without a recognisable pain stimulus, caused by abnormal CNS activity |
| **Chemical senses** | Olfaction and gustation, which monitor the chemical content of the environment |
| **Flavour** | Integrated impression of food produced by smell and taste together |
| **Pheromones** | Chemicals that influence the physiology and behaviour of conspecifics |
| **Conspecifics** | Members of the same species |
| **Olfactory mucosa** | Mucus-covered tissue in the upper nose containing olfactory receptor cells |
| **Cribriform plate** | Porous skull plate through which olfactory receptor axons pass |
| **Olfactory bulbs** | Structures where olfactory receptor axons terminate, projecting via the olfactory tracts |
| **Component processing** | Coding of an odour by the pattern of activity across receptor types |
| **Olfactory glomeruli** | Clusters near the bulb surface, each receiving thousands of receptor axons of one receptor type |
| **Chemotopic** | Organised according to chemical (odour or taste) |
| **Piriform cortex** | Medial temporal cortex next to the amygdala; primary olfactory cortex |
| **Medial dorsal nuclei** | Thalamic nuclei relaying olfactory input to the orbitofrontal cortex |
| **Orbitofrontal cortex** | Cortex on the underside of the frontal lobes by the orbits; conscious smell and secondary gustatory cortex |
| **Taste buds** | Clusters of 50 to 100 taste receptor cells |
| **Papillae** | Small tongue protuberances around which taste buds are often found |
| **Presynaptic cell** | The only taste bud receptor cell that synapses on the outgoing neuron |
| **Solitary nucleus** | Medullary nucleus receiving gustatory afferents from VII, IX and X |
| **Insula** | Cortex hidden in the lateral fissure; primary gustatory cortex |
| **Anosmia** | Inability to smell |
| **Ageusia** | Inability to taste |
| **Percept** | The outcome of perception |
| **Perceptual decision making** | Ongoing brain decisions about what to perceive, based on prior experience and current input |
| **Bistable figures** | Images whose percept alternates between two interpretations (faces or chalice) |
| **Phantom percepts** | Products of perception in the absence of sensory input |
| **Phantom limbs** | Perception of a missing limb after amputation |
| **Charles Bonnet syndrome** | Complex visual hallucinations after loss of vision later in life (e.g., glaucoma) |
| **Binding problem** | The question of how separately analysed sensory attributes are combined into integrated percepts |
| **Claustrum** | Thin sheet of neurons beneath the cortex proposed as a binding site |
| **Selective attention** | The process by which a small subset of stimuli is consciously perceived and the rest largely ignored |
| **Endogenous attention** | Attention directed by internal cognitive processes (top-down) |
| **Exogenous attention** | Attention captured by external events (bottom-up) |
| **Covert attention** | Shift of attention without an eye movement |
| **Overt attention** | Shift of attention accompanied by a shift of gaze |
| **Cocktail-party phenomenon** | Detection of one's own name in an unattended conversation |
| **Change blindness** | Failure to notice a gross change between two alternating images separated by a brief gap |
| **Push-pull mechanism** | Attention strengthening responses to attended stimuli while weakening responses to others |
| **Attentional gaze** | The shift of attention from one perceptual object to another |
| **Frontal eye field** | Frontal cortex area active in eye movements and in covert shifts of attentional gaze |
| **Simultanagnosia** | Difficulty attending to more than one object at a time; bilateral posterior parietal damage |
| **Interaural time / intensity differences** | (Older editions) Between-ear differences in arrival time / loudness used for localisation by the medial / lateral superior olives |
| **Dermatome** | (Older editions) Body area innervated by the left and right dorsal roots of one spinal segment |
| **Gate-control theory** | (Older editions) Melzack and Wall (1965): descending signals activate spinal gating circuits that block pain |

## Likely exam points

1. **Three types of sensory cortex** and their defining inputs; **three principles** (hierarchical, functionally segregated, parallel) versus the **1960s** model (hierarchical, functionally homogeneous, serial); top-down descending signals.
2. **Dr. P. (Sacks, 1985):** visual agnosia with excellent acuity; higher-level damage gives more specific deficits.
3. **Physical vs perceptual dimensions of sound:** amplitude/loudness, frequency/pitch, complexity/timbre; hearing range 20 to 20,000 Hz.
4. **Fourier analysis, fundamental frequency and the missing fundamental** (200, 300, 400 Hz heard as 100 Hz).
5. **Ear anatomy in sequence:** tympanic membrane, ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes), oval window, cochlea, organ of Corti, round window.
6. **Organ of Corti:** hair cells in the basilar membrane, tectorial membrane above, shearing force; high frequencies at the base, low at the apex; tonotopy; 0.2 percent discrimination.
7. **Auditory pathway:** cochlear nuclei, superior olives (binaural convergence), lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculi, medial geniculate, auditory cortex; bilateral projection; more synapses than other senses.
8. **Auditory cortex:** core (three areas), belt, parabelt, about 13 areas; columns, tonotopy, periodotopy; natural sounds better than pure tones.
9. **Bendor and Wang (2005)** pitch area anterior to primary auditory cortex; human fMRI pitch area in a similar location.
10. **Two auditory streams:** anterior what, posterior where; corresponding deficits after damage; word deafness after bilateral lesions.
11. **Deafness:** conductive vs nerve; hair-cell loss; age-related high-frequency loss; 360 million; 1 percent total; isolation and dementia risk.
12. **Tinnitus** (central plasticity; nerve section fails) and **cochlear implants** (bypass hair cells, earlier is better).
13. **Three somatosensory systems** (exteroceptive, proprioceptive, interoceptive) and the three exteroceptive divisions (touch, temperature, pain).
14. **Cutaneous receptors and adaptation:** free nerve endings, Pacinian (rapid), Merkel and Ruffini (slow); why we manipulate objects during stereognosis.
15. **DCML vs anterolateral:** functions ("tends to"), where each synapses and crosses, the three anterolateral tracts, trigeminal input, longest neurons from the toes.
16. **SI and the homunculus:** Penfield (1937), somatotopy, distortion, back vs fingertips, Servos et al. (1999) inverted face.
17. **SII:** ventral to SI, in the lateral fissure, bilateral input; columns and four functional strips in SI; dorsal (posterior parietal) and ventral (SII, shape) streams (Yau, Connor and Hsiao, 2013).
18. **Corkin, Milner and Rasmussen (1970):** mild contralateral deficits after SI excision; bilateral only with SII involvement.
19. **Bimodal posterior parietal neurons** and **W.M.** (Schendel and Robertson, 2004): 97.8 vs 13.6 percent; hand and tennis racket.
20. **Somatosensory agnosias:** astereognosia vs asomatognosia; **Aunt Betty** (Klawans, 1990) with anosognosia and contralateral neglect.
21. **Rubber-hand illusion:** method, timing, temperature drop (Moseley et al., 2008), frontal and parietal bimodal neurons, animal versions.
22. **Three paradoxes of pain** with their evidence.
23. **Miss C.** (Melzack and Wall, 1982) and **Cox et al. (2006)** sodium-channel gene; knockout mice (Gingras et al., 2014).
24. **No necessary pain cortex:** SI/SII removal and hemispherectomy; **ACC** and the **thermal grid illusion** (Craig et al., 1996).
25. **Descending analgesia:** Reynolds (1969) PAG stimulation, opioid receptors, endorphins; **Basbaum and Fields (1978)** three-step PAG-raphé-dorsal horn model; ACC and prefrontal cortex in modern models; gate-control theory (Melzack and Wall, 1965) from older editions.
26. **Neuropathic pain:** definition, central origin, failure of nerve section and standard analgesics; microglia, epigenetics, ACC and prefrontal cortex.
27. **Chemical senses:** flavour (apple vs onion), food evaluation, pheromones and **Murphy and Schneider (1970)**; little evidence for human sexual pheromones.
28. **Olfactory system:** mucosa, cribriform plate, bulbs, about 1,000 vs 300 receptor types, one per cell, component processing, glomeruli and the chemotopic map, receptor turnover, piriform cortex, no thalamic relay on the major route, limbic vs medial dorsal to orbitofrontal pathways.
29. **Gustatory system:** taste buds (50 to 100 cells), papillae, three cell types, presynaptic cell, five tastes, metabotropic vs ionotropic receptor counts, VII/IX/X to solitary nucleus to ventral posterior thalamus to insula, orbitofrontal secondary cortex, ipsilateral projections, Schoenfeld et al. (2004) chemotopy.
30. **Anosmia and ageusia:** head trauma at the cribriform plate; neurological disorders with partial olfactory loss; why ageusia is rare; ear damage and VII.
31. **Perception:** light-from-above and Dalmatian demonstrations, prediction machines, bistable figures, phantom limbs, Charles Bonnet syndrome; dorsolateral prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex in perceptual decisions.
32. **Binding problem:** claustrum vs distributed interaction from primary cortex upward.
33. **Selective attention:** two characteristics; endogenous/top-down vs exogenous/bottom-up; covert vs overt; cocktail-party phenomenon; change blindness and its interval control.
34. **Neural mechanisms of attention:** Moran and Desimone (1985), Corbetta et al. (1990), Ungerleider and Haxby (1994), push-pull, receptive-field shifts (Wommelsdorf et al., 2006; Rolls, 2008), frontal eye field and sub-threshold stimulation.
35. **Simultanagnosia:** the opening case; attention not acuity; bilateral posterior parietal damage.
