# Chapter 17: Biopsychology of Emotion, Stress, and Health

**What this chapter is really about.** Everyday language hands us a ready-made list of emotions, fear, anger, happiness, disgust, and invites us to assume that each one is a *thing* with a home somewhere in the brain. The central lesson of this chapter is that this assumption has repeatedly misled research, and that progress came only when investigators stopped hunting for the brain's "anger centre" and started from careful descriptions of what animals and people actually *do*, in what situations, and to what end. The lesson plays out three times: in emotion theory (the big early theories were each half right), in aggression research ("aggression" had to be split into distinct behaviours before any circuit could be found), and in human imaging (emotion "centres" gave way to distributed, overlapping networks).

Fear gets most of the attention, and Pinel and Barnes say why: it is the **easiest emotion to infer from behaviour** across species; it has an obvious **adaptive function**, motivating the avoidance of threatening situations; and **chronic fear is a common source of stress**. That third point is the bridge to the chapter's final module, which leaves emotion proper and asks how stress gets under the skin to damage health, through glucocorticoids, cytokines, the immune system and the hippocampus.

**How to read this chapter.** 17.1 is history plus two classic empirical questions (does each emotion have its own autonomic signature, and are facial expressions universal?). 17.2 is the conceptual hinge: how aggression and defence were broken into real behavioural categories, and why the testosterone story is weaker in humans than in mice. 17.3 is the best-mapped circuit in the chapter, LeDoux's fear-conditioning pathway; be able to draw it. 17.4 moves to human emotion and keeps undercutting "one structure, one emotion" claims. 17.5 is a self-contained module on stress, psychosomatic illness and psychoneuroimmunology, dense with numbers. Two cases frame the chapter, Phineas Gage at the start and Charles Whitman at the end, both about what happens to emotion when a specific part of the brain is damaged.

## The Mind-Blowing Case of Phineas Gage

In **1848**, Phineas Gage, a **25-year-old construction foreman for the Rutland and Burlington Railroad**, was in charge of blasting rock to level the ground for new track. The routine was: drill a hole, pour in gunpowder, cover it with sand, tamp the charge down with a large tamping iron, and detonate it with a fuse. On the fateful day the gunpowder exploded while Gage was tamping it, and the iron, **3 cm thick and 90 cm long**, was launched through his face, skull and brain and out the other side.

Gage survived, but he survived a changed man. Before the accident he had been responsible, intelligent and socially well adapted, liked by friends and fellow workers. After recovery he seemed as able-bodied and intellectually capable as before, but his personality and emotional life had been transformed. The religious, respectful, reliable man became **irreverent and impulsive**; his abundant profanity offended many; he became so unreliable that he soon lost his job and was **never again able to hold a responsible position**. He drifted around the country for **a dozen years** until he died in **San Francisco**. The accident and his apparent recovery made headlines around the world; his death went largely unnoticed.

Gage was buried next to the tamping iron. **Five years later**, the neurologist **John Harlow** obtained permission from Gage's family to exhume the body and the iron for study. Gage's skull and the tamping iron have since been on display in the **Warren Anatomical Medical Museum at Harvard University**.

The case did not become modern science until **1994**, when **Damasio and her colleagues** applied computerised reconstruction to it. They X-rayed the skull, measured it precisely, paying particular attention to the positions of the entry and exit holes, and from those measurements reconstructed the trajectory of the iron and the likely region of brain damage. The damage affected **both medial prefrontal lobes**, regions now known to be involved in **planning, decision making and emotion**.

What makes Gage important is the dissociation: intellect and movement survived, the regulation of emotion and social behaviour did not. Emotion depends on particular brain regions, not just on general cognition. The medial prefrontal lobes return in 17.4, doing exactly what Gage seems to have lost: cognitively controlling emotional reactions.

> **Trap.** Gage's damage is **bilateral medial prefrontal**, not "left" or "lateral" prefrontal; the reconstruction is **Damasio et al. (1994)**; Harlow only exhumed the skull. Gage is also the first of the six "early landmarks" (1848).

## 17.1 Biopsychology of Emotion: Introduction

This module does three jobs: it reviews six classic early discoveries, asks how the autonomic nervous system relates to emotion, and examines the facial expression of emotion.

### Six early landmarks

Pinel and Barnes present the history as six landmarks in chronological order (Table 17.1). Gage (1848) is the first and has already been covered. The other five follow.

#### Darwin's theory of the evolution of emotion (1872)

The first major event in the biopsychology of emotion was the publication in **1872** of Darwin's ***The Expression of Emotions in Man and Animals***. Darwin argued that particular emotional responses, such as human facial expressions, tend to **accompany the same emotional states in all members of a species**. Because he regarded expressions of emotion, like other behaviours, as **products of evolution**, he tried to understand them by **comparing them across species**.

From those interspecies comparisons he built a theory with three main ideas, and you should be able to state all three in order:

1. **Expressions of emotion evolve from behaviours that indicate what an animal is likely to do next.**
2. **If the signals provided by such behaviours benefit the animal that displays them, they will evolve in ways that enhance their communicative function, and their original function may be lost.**
3. **Opposite messages are often signalled by opposite movements and postures**, the **principle of antithesis**.

Darwin's account of threat displays shows how the three ideas work together. Originally, facing one's enemies, rising up and exposing one's weapons were simply the opening moves of a fight. Once enemies learned to recognise these behaviours as signals of impending attack, an advantage went to attackers who could communicate aggression *most effectively* and intimidate opponents **without actually fighting**. So the displays became elaborate (idea 2: the signal is shaped for communication) and actual combat declined.

Antithesis follows from the need for clarity: signals of aggression and submission are most useful when they cannot be confused, so they evolved in **opposite directions**:

| Species | Aggression | Submission |
|---|---|---|
| Gulls | Point beaks **at** one another | Point beaks **away** from one another |
| Primates | **Staring** | **Averting** the gaze |
| Dogs (Darwin's woodcuts) | Ears forward, back up, hair up, tail up | Ears back, back down, hair down, tail down |

> **Trap.** Antithesis means *opposite messages, opposite postures*. It is neither the universality claim nor the idea that signals are elaborated for communication; the dog woodcuts illustrate antithesis.

#### James-Lange and Cannon-Bard theories

The **first physiological theory of emotion** was proposed **independently by William James and Carl Lange in 1884** (the date Pinel gives). The **James-Lange theory** runs in this order:

1. Emotion-inducing sensory stimuli are received and interpreted by the **cortex**.
2. The cortex triggers changes in the **visceral organs** via the **autonomic nervous system** and in the **skeletal muscles** via the **somatic nervous system**.
3. Those autonomic and somatic responses then **trigger the experience of emotion** in the brain.

What James and Lange did, in effect, was to **reverse** the commonsense causal order. Common sense says you see a bear, feel afraid, and therefore your heart races and you run. James-Lange says you see the bear, your heart races and you run, and the *feeling* of fear is your brain's registration of those bodily changes. The popular summary is that we do not tremble because we are afraid; we are afraid because we tremble.

**Around 1915, Walter Cannon** proposed an alternative, which was subsequently **extended and promoted by Philip Bard**. According to the **Cannon-Bard theory**, emotional stimuli have **two independent excitatory effects**: they excite both the **feeling** of emotion in the brain and the **expression** of emotion in the autonomic and somatic nervous systems. Experience and expression are **parallel processes with no direct causal relation** between them.

The two theories make opposite predictions about the role of **feedback** from the body:

| | **James-Lange** | **Cannon-Bard** |
|---|---|---|
| Causal order | Stimulus → bodily response → feeling | Stimulus → feeling **and** bodily response, in parallel |
| Role of autonomic/somatic feedback | Emotional experience depends **entirely** on it | Emotional experience is **totally independent** of it |
| ANS pattern across emotions | **Different** pattern for each emotion | The **same** general sympathetic pattern for all |

**Both extreme positions have proved incorrect**, and the evidence against each is worth knowing.

*Against James-Lange: feedback is not necessary.* Patients whose autonomic and somatic feedback has been largely eliminated by a **broken neck** are capable of a **full range of emotional experiences**, though there does seem to be **some dampening of fear and anger** (Pistoia et al., 2015). If feeling were nothing but the perception of bodily change, removing most of the bodily signal should remove most of the feeling. It does not. (Note the word "largely": a broken neck spares the face and the cranial nerves, so the test is strong but not perfectly clean. The slight dampening is itself a hint that feedback contributes something.)

*Against Cannon-Bard: feedback can influence experience.* There are numerous reports, several of which appear later in this module (the facial feedback studies are the clearest), that **autonomic and somatic responses to emotional stimuli can influence emotional experience**. If the two processes were truly independent, changing the body should not change the feeling.

The failure of both extremes led to the **modern biopsychological view**: each of the three principal factors in an emotional response, **the perception of the emotion-inducing stimulus, the autonomic and somatic responses to it, and the experience of the emotion, can influence the other two** (e.g., Scherer & Moors, 2019). Figure 17.3 lays out all four views (commonsense, James-Lange, Cannon-Bard, modern) side by side with the same three boxes, and the exam question is usually "which arrows does each view draw?"

> **Trap.** Indian papers often use labels Pinel does not: Cannon-Bard is called the **"thalamic" theory** (Cannon located emotion's generation in the thalamus), and the set usually includes the **Schachter-Singer two-factor theory** (1962: arousal plus a cognitive label), which Pinel does not cover. Pinel's contrast is simply sequential (James-Lange) versus parallel (Cannon-Bard).

> **Trap.** Dates. Pinel gives **1884** for James and Lange (Lange's monograph is often dated 1885) and **around 1915** for Cannon, but **Table 17.1 lists the pair as "about 1900"**, so that option is correct by the book.

#### Sham rage (Bard, 1929)

In the late 1920s **Bard (1929)** discovered that **decorticate** cats, cats whose cortex has been removed, respond aggressively to the slightest provocation: after a **light touch** they **arch their backs, erect their hair, hiss and expose their teeth**.

These responses are abnormal in **two respects**: they are **inappropriately severe**, and they are **not directed at particular targets**. Bard called these exaggerated, poorly directed aggressive responses **sham rage** (the name reflects the view that, without a cortex, the display is expression without the normally integrated experience behind it).

The decisive observation was a comparison between two lesion levels:

- Sham rage **can** be elicited in cats whose cerebral hemispheres have been removed **down to, but not including, the hypothalamus**.
- It **cannot** be elicited if the **hypothalamus is also removed**.

From this Bard drew a two-part conclusion: the **hypothalamus is critical for the expression of aggressive responses**, and the **function of the cortex is to inhibit and direct** them. Read the logic: removing the cortex *releases* and disorganises the response (so cortex normally restrains and aims it), while removing the hypothalamus *abolishes* it (so the hypothalamus is necessary to produce it at all).

> **Trap.** Sham rage follows removal of the **cortex** with the hypothalamus **intact**; hypothalamic removal **prevents** it.

#### The limbic system theory of emotion (Papez, 1937; MacLean, 1952)

In **1937, Papez** (pronounced "Payps") proposed that emotional expression is controlled by several interconnected nuclei and tracts that **ring the thalamus**. The key structures shown in Pinel's Figure 17.4 are the **amygdala, mammillary body, hippocampus, fornix, cingulate cortex, septum, olfactory bulb and hypothalamus**.

Papez's idea divided the labour in a way worth remembering: emotional states are **expressed** through the action of the other structures of the circuit **on the hypothalamus**, and they are **experienced** through their action **on the cortex**. Notice that this is a neural version of the Cannon-Bard split between expression and experience.

Papez's theory was **revised and expanded by Paul MacLean in 1952** and became the influential **limbic system theory of emotion**. Many of the structures in Papez's circuit are part of what is now called the **limbic system**.

> **Trap.** **Papez 1937** (the circuit) versus **MacLean 1952** (the limbic system theory, the 1952 table entry). Neuroanatomy texts usually give the *classic* Papez loop as hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → anterior thalamic nucleus → cingulate gyrus → hippocampus, with the amygdala emphasised later by MacLean; Pinel's figure includes the amygdala. Answer from Pinel's list for questions on this book; expect the anterior thalamic nucleus in neuroanatomy-style questions.

#### Klüver-Bucy syndrome (1939)

In **1939, Klüver and Bucy** described a striking syndrome (pattern of behaviour) in monkeys whose **anterior temporal lobes** had been removed. The **Klüver-Bucy syndrome** includes five behaviours:

1. **Consumption of almost anything that is edible.**
2. **Increased sexual activity, often directed at inappropriate objects.**
3. A tendency to **repeatedly investigate familiar objects**.
4. A tendency to **investigate objects with the mouth**.
5. A **lack of fear**.

The fear change is the most dramatic. Monkeys that could not be handled before surgery were transformed by **bilateral anterior temporal lobectomy** into tame animals that showed **no fear whatsoever, even of snakes**, which terrify normal monkeys. In primates, most of the symptoms have been attributed to **damage to the amygdala** (LeDoux, Michel, & Lau, 2020; Schröder, Moser, & Huggenberger, 2020), the structure that dominates the rest of this chapter.

> **Trap.** Clinical texts use labels Pinel does not: **dietary change/hyperphagia**, **hypersexuality**, **hypermetamorphosis** (re-investigating familiar objects), **hyperorality** and **placidity**, and many add **visual agnosia ("psychic blindness")**. Map them onto Pinel's list. The lesion is **bilateral anterior temporal**; the structure credited in primates is the **amygdala**, not the hippocampus.

#### A human case of Klüver-Bucy syndrome

The syndrome has been observed in several species. Pinel describes a human patient whose syndrome followed a **brain infection**:

> At first he was listless, but eventually he became very **placid with flat affect**. He reacted little to people or to other aspects of his environment. He spent much time **staring at the television, even when it was not turned on**. On occasion he would become extremely silly, **smiling inappropriately and mimicking the actions of others**, and once he began copying another person's movements he would persist for extended periods. He also tended to engage in **oral exploration, sucking, licking or chewing all small objects he could reach**.

Placidity maps onto the monkeys' loss of fear, oral exploration onto their mouthing; the persistent imitation and blank-screen staring resemble abnormal, repetitive investigation of the environment.

### Emotions and the autonomic nervous system

Research on the **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** in emotion has focused on two issues: how far **specific patterns of ANS activity** are associated with **specific emotions**, and how effective ANS measures are in **polygraphy** (lie detection).

#### Emotional specificity of the ANS

This is where James-Lange and Cannon-Bard make their sharpest testable disagreement. James-Lange says different emotional stimuli induce **different patterns** of ANS activity and that those different patterns *produce* the different emotional experiences. Cannon-Bard says all emotional stimuli produce the **same general pattern of sympathetic activation**, which prepares the organism for action:

- increased **heart rate**
- increased **blood pressure**
- **pupil dilation**
- increased **blood flow to the muscles**
- increased **respiration**
- increased release of **epinephrine and norepinephrine from the adrenal medulla**

The evidence places the truth **between the extremes of total specificity and total generality** (Kreibig, 2010; Quigley & Barrett, 2014). On one hand, ample evidence shows that **not all emotions are associated with the same pattern** of ANS activity, which is bad for Cannon-Bard. On the other, there is **no evidence that each emotion is characterised by a distinct pattern**, which is bad for James-Lange (Siegel et al., 2018).

#### Polygraphy

**Polygraphy**, commonly called the "lie detector test", is a method of interrogation that uses **ANS indexes of emotion** to infer the truthfulness of a person's responses. Pinel's verdict is balanced: administered by skilled examiners, polygraph tests can be **useful additions** to normal interrogation, but they are **far from infallible**.

The main problem in evaluating polygraphy is that in real-life cases it is rarely possible to know for certain who is guilty. Much research therefore uses the **mock-crime procedure**: volunteers take part in a staged crime and are then tested by an examiner who does not know whether they are "guilty" or "innocent". Ground truth is built into the design.

#### The control-question technique

The usual interrogation method is the **control-question technique**. The physiological response to the **target question** ("Did you steal that purse?") is compared with responses to **control questions** whose answers are known ("Have you ever been in jail before?"). The assumption is that **lying is associated with greater sympathetic activation**.

How well does it work? A review of the control-question technique in **real-life crime settings** produced an estimated success rate of **about 55 percent, only slightly better than chance (50 percent)** (Iacono & Ben-Shakhar, 2019).

The reason is conceptual, not technical: **polygraphy detects ANS activity, not lies**. In real life a question such as "Did you steal that purse?" is likely to provoke an emotional reaction in **every** suspect, guilty or innocent, because being accused is itself arousing (Ambach & Gamer, 2018). The innocent person's fear of being disbelieved looks, to the machine, exactly like the guilty person's fear of being caught. This is also why polygraphy is less successful in real life than in experiments, where the stakes for an innocent volunteer are low.

> **Trap.** Older notes and keys quote much higher control-question accuracy (around 80 percent, from mock-crime studies). The 11th edition gives **about 55 percent in real-life settings**, barely above **50 percent chance** (Iacono & Ben-Shakhar, 2019).

#### The guilty-knowledge technique

The **guilty-knowledge technique**, also known as the **concealed information test**, sidesteps the problem. The polygrapher must possess some piece of information about the crime **that only the guilty person would know**. Instead of trying to catch the suspect in a lie, the examiner simply assesses the suspect's reactions to a list of **actual and contrived details** of the crime. An **innocent** suspect, knowing nothing about the crime, reacts to all the details **in the same way**; the **guilty** suspect reacts **differentially** to the true one (Ambach & Gamer, 2018).

> **Key study, Lykken (1959).** Volunteers waited until the occupant of an office went to the washroom, entered her office, **stole her purse** from the desk, removed the money, and **left the purse in a locker**. The critical questioning went something like: "Where do you think we found the purse? In the washroom? ... In a locker? ... Hanging on a coat rack?" Although **electrodermal activity was the only ANS measure used**, **88 percent of the mock criminals were correctly identified**, and, more importantly, **none of the innocent control volunteers was judged guilty**.

Why does it protect the innocent? An innocent suspect cannot know which alternative is real, so however frightened, their arousal cannot systematically peak on the correct detail. The technique turns an arousal test into a **recognition** test, which a nervous innocent cannot fail for knowledge they lack. Its limitation is practical: investigators must hold details that have not leaked to the public.

### Emotions and facial expression

**Ekman** and his colleagues have been pre-eminent in the study of facial expression (Ekman, 2016). They began in the **1960s** by analysing **hundreds of films and photographs** of people experiencing real emotions, and from these compiled an **atlas of the facial expressions** normally associated with different emotions (**Ekman & Friesen, 1975**). The atlas specifies the movements muscle by muscle. For example, to produce the expression of **surprise**, models were told to pull their brows upward so as to wrinkle the forehead, open their eyes wide so as to reveal white above the iris, slacken the muscles around the mouth, and drop the jaw.

#### Universality of facial expression

Several early studies found that people of different cultures make **similar facial expressions in similar situations** and can correctly identify the emotional meaning of expressions displayed by members of **other** cultures. The most convincing was a study of members of an **isolated New Guinea tribe** who had had little or no contact with the outside world (**Ekman & Friesen, 1971**). The design matters: if people with no exposure to Western faces, films or photographs still read Western expressions correctly, the agreement cannot be explained by shared media or learned conventions, which points toward Darwin's evolutionary account.

#### Primary facial expressions

Ekman and Friesen concluded that the facial expressions of **six emotions are primary**: **surprise, anger, sadness, disgust, fear and happiness**. They further concluded that **all other facial expressions of genuine emotion are mixtures of these six primaries**.

> **Trap.** Learn the six exactly; the usual distractors are **contempt**, **shame**, **interest** or **pride**. Only happiness is clearly positive, and surprise is neither clearly positive nor negative.

#### The facial feedback hypothesis

Is there any truth to the old idea that putting on a happy face makes you feel better? The **facial feedback hypothesis** holds that **our facial expressions influence our emotional experience**. Notice that it is a direct descendant of James-Lange: bodily (here facial) responses feed back to shape the feeling.

> **Key study, Rutledge & Hupka (1985).** Volunteers were instructed to adopt one of two patterns of facial muscle contraction while viewing a series of slides. The patterns corresponded to **happy** or **angry** faces, but the **volunteers were not told that**. They reported that the slides made them feel **more happy and less angry** while they were making happy faces, and **less happy and more angry** while making angry faces, for both anger-inducing and happiness-inducing slides.

The cover story is the critical control: had participants known they were "smiling", their ratings could reflect what they thought the experimenter wanted.

A recent **meta-analysis** of the facial feedback hypothesis (**Coles, Larsen, & Lench, 2019**) confirmed the **reliability** of these and similar findings, but found the effects **smaller than originally believed**. So the hypothesis survives, in modest form.

Pinel's "Check It Out" exercise invites you to try it: pull your eyebrows down and together, raise your upper eyelids and tighten your lower eyelids, and narrow your lips and press them together. Hold it for a few seconds. If you feel slightly angry and uncomfortable, that is facial feedback.

> **Trap.** Facial feedback **supports a weak James-Lange** (feedback can influence feeling) and **counts against Cannon-Bard**. It does *not* show feedback is *necessary*; the broken-neck evidence says it is not.

#### Voluntary control of facial expression

Because we can control our facial muscles voluntarily, we can **inhibit** true expressions and **substitute** false ones. The reasons can be kind (a false smile to reassure a worried friend) or not (a false smile to disguise a lie). Either way, it is difficult to fool an expert.

**Ekman (1985)** described **two ways of distinguishing true from false expressions**:

1. **Microexpressions**: brief facial expressions of the real emotion often **break through** the false one (Wang et al., 2015). They last only **about 0.05 second**, but with practice they can be detected **without slow-motion photography**.
2. **Subtle differences** between genuine and false expressions that skilled observers can detect.

#### The Duchenne smile

The most widely studied difference between genuine and false expressions was first described by the French anatomist **Duchenne in 1862**. Duchenne said that the smile of enjoyment could be distinguished from deliberately produced smiles by two muscles that contract during **genuine** smiles:

| Muscle | What it does | Voluntary control |
|---|---|---|
| **Orbicularis oculi** | Encircles the eye; pulls skin from the cheeks and forehead toward the eyeball | Normally contracted **only by genuine pleasure**; its lateral portion is difficult for most people to contract voluntarily |
| **Zygomaticus major** | Pulls the lip corners up | **Can be controlled voluntarily** |

So a fake smile usually has the mouth component and lacks the eye component: in Pinel's phrase, the **inertia of the orbicularis oculi** in smiling unmasks a false friend. **Ekman named the genuine smile the Duchenne smile.** Figure 17.7 is a deliberately faked smile; the instruction is "look at her eyes".

> **Trap.** The muscle whose **absence** gives a fake smile away is the **orbicularis oculi** (eye); the **zygomaticus major** (mouth) is present in both because it is voluntary. Duchenne described it in **1862**; **Ekman** named it.

#### Facial expressions: current perspectives

Ekman's work began before video recording was commonplace. Video now provides almost unlimited access to **natural** expressions made in response to real situations, and this has produced **four important qualifications** to Ekman's original theory:

1. **The six primary expressions rarely occur in pure form.** They are ideals with many subtle variations.
2. **Other primary emotions have been recognised** (Whalen et al., 2013).
3. **Body cues, not just faces, play a major role** in emotional expression (Sznycer, 2019). **Pride**, for example, is expressed through a **small smile**, the **head tilted back slightly**, and the hands **on the hips, raised above the head, or clenched in fists with the arms crossed on the chest** (Witkower & Tracy, 2019; Figure 17.8).
4. **The six may not be as universal as originally believed.** There appear to be distinct differences in both the **expression and the recognition** of facial expressions between **Western Caucasian and East Asian** individuals (Calvo & Nummenmaa, 2015; Jack et al., 2012; Wood et al., 2016), and recent studies of **isolated tribes by Crivelli et al. (2016, 2017)** indicate that facial expressions of emotion are **not as universal as once thought**.

The fourth qualification is the one to weigh. Ekman's strongest evidence came from an isolated New Guinea tribe; Crivelli's newer work, also with isolated tribes, cuts the other way. There is substantial cross-cultural similarity, but strict universality of six fixed expressions is no longer the consensus.

> **Trap.** Older editions and keys treat Ekman's six universal primaries as settled. By the 11th edition, "Ekman's six expressions are fully universal" is **false**.

## 17.2 Fear, Defence, and Aggression

Most biopsychological research on emotion has focused on fear and defensive behaviour, so begin with three definitions that the rest of the chapter relies on:

- **Fear** is the emotional reaction to threat; it is the **motivating force for defensive behaviours**.
- **Defensive behaviours** are behaviours whose primary function is to **protect the organism from threat or harm**.
- **Aggressive behaviours** are behaviours whose primary function is to **threaten or harm**.

Notice that both behavioural categories are defined by their **function**, not by how violent they look. That choice turns out to be the key to the whole module.

### The problem of everyday emotion categories

This module has a second purpose besides describing fear, defence and aggression: to show a common problem in biopsychology and how researchers in this area got round it. **Barrett (2006)** pointed out that progress on the neural basis of emotion has been limited because neuroscientists have often been guided by **unsubstantiated cultural assumptions**. Because our language has words such as *fear*, *happiness* and *anger*, scientists have often assumed these emotions exist as **entities in the brain** and have searched for them, often with little success.

Pinel's Journal Prompt names the logical error involved: treating a psychological construct as if it were a real, concrete thing is an **error of reification**. "Intelligence" is the standard example, a construct developed by psychologists that people come to treat as an object because there is a word for it.

The way out, which the research below illustrates, is to base the search for neural mechanisms on **thorough descriptions of the relevant behaviours, the environments in which they occur, and their putative adaptive functions** (Kasai et al., 2015; LeDoux & Hofmann, 2018). In short: describe first, then look for the circuit.

### Types of aggressive and defensive behaviours

#### The colony-intruder model (Blanchard & Blanchard)

Considerable progress came from **Blanchard and Blanchard's** work on the **colony-intruder model** of aggression and defence in rats (Blanchard, Summers, & Blanchard, 2013; Koolhaas et al., 2013). They produced rich descriptions of rat **intraspecific** (within-species) aggression and defence by studying encounters between the **alpha male**, the **dominant male** of an established mixed-sex colony, and a **small male intruder**. The sequence is worth knowing move by move, because each move has a function:

1. On encountering the intruder, the alpha male typically **chases it away, repeatedly biting its back** during the pursuit.
2. The intruder eventually stops running and **turns to face** the alpha.
3. The intruder **rears up on its hind legs**, still facing its attacker, using its forelimbs to **ward off** the attack.
4. The alpha changes to a **lateral orientation**, the side of its body perpendicular to the front of the defender, and moves **sideways** toward it, crowding it and trying to push it off balance. This is the **"lateral attack"**.
5. If the defender stands firm, the alpha often makes a **quick lunge around the defender's body** to bite its back.
6. The defender **pivots on its hind feet** in the same direction as the attacker is moving, **keeping its front toward the attacker** to prevent the back bite.

Read the sequence as a contest over one body region: the attacker is trying to get to the **back**, and every defensive manoeuvre is an attempt to keep the back out of reach.

#### Pellis and colleagues (1988): do cats play with mice?

> **Key study, Pellis et al. (1988).** The researchers **videotaped interactions between cats and mice**. Different cats behaved differently: some were efficient mouse killers, some reacted defensively, and some seemed to **"play"** with the mice. Careful analysis of the "play" sequences led to two conclusions. First, contrary to common belief, **cats do not play with their prey**: the cats that appeared to be playing were simply **vacillating between attack and defence**. Second, each cat's behaviour toward mice could best be understood by placing it on a **linear scale**, with **total aggressiveness at one end, total defensiveness at the other**, and various mixtures in between.

They then **tested** the conclusion, which is what lifts this from description to experiment. They reduced the cats' defensiveness with an **antianxiety drug**. If "play" is a mixture of attack and defence, reducing defence should push every cat toward the aggressive end of the scale. That is exactly what happened:

| Before the drug | After the drug |
|---|---|
| Avoided mice | "Played with" them |
| "Played with" mice | Killed them |
| Killed mice | Killed them **more quickly** |

One manipulation moved *every* cat one step along the same scale, which is strong evidence that the scale is real.

> **Trap.** The antianxiety drug **reduced defensiveness**; it did not add aggression, it removed the defensive component that had been interrupting attack. And cats **do not** play with prey.

#### Categories of aggressive and defensive behaviour

Building on the detailed descriptions of the Blanchards, Pellis and others, most researchers now distinguish several categories of aggressive and defensive behaviour, using **three criteria**: (1) their **topography** (form), (2) the **situations that elicit them**, and (3) their **apparent function**. The rat categories in Table 17.2 (Blanchard et al., 2011; Jager et al., 2017; Kim & Jung, 2018):

| Category | Type | What it is (in rats) |
|---|---|---|
| **Predatory aggression** | Aggressive | Stalking and killing members of **other species** to eat them. Rats kill prey such as mice and frogs with **bites to the back of the neck** |
| **Social aggression** | Aggressive | **Unprovoked** aggression directed at a **conspecific** to establish, alter or maintain a **social hierarchy**. In mammals, occurs **primarily among males**. In rats: **piloerection, lateral attack, bites directed at the defender's back** |
| **Intraspecific defence** | Defensive | Defence against social aggression: **freezing and flight**, and behaviours such as **boxing** designed specifically to **protect the back** |
| **Defensive attacks** | Defensive | Attacks launched by animals **cornered** by threatening members of their own or other species: **lunging, shrieking and biting**, usually directed at the attacker's **face** |
| **Freezing and flight** | Defensive | Responses used to avoid attack. A wild rat approached by a human often **freezes** until the human penetrates its **safety zone**, then explodes into **flight** |
| **Maternal defensive behaviours** | Defensive | How mothers protect their young. Despite their defensive function, they **look similar to male social aggression** |
| **Risk assessment** | Defensive | Behaviours that gather information for more effective defence. Rats chased into their burrow by a cat do not emerge until they have spent considerable time **scanning** from the entrance |
| **Defensive burying** | Defensive | Rodents **spray sand and dirt ahead with their forepaws** to bury dangerous objects, drive off predators and build barriers in burrows |

> **Trap.** **Maternal defensive behaviour** and **defensive attack** look aggressive but are **defensive**: the criterion is function, not form. Predatory aggression targets **another species**; social aggression a **conspecific**.

#### The target-site concept

The behavioural analysis led to the **target-site concept**: the aggressive and defensive behaviours of an animal are often designed to **attack specific sites** on another animal's body while **protecting specific sites** on its own.

The rat colony shows it cleanly. A **socially aggressive** rat's lateral attack appears designed to deliver bites to the **defender's back** while **protecting its own face**, which is the likely target of a **defensive** attack. Conversely, most of the defending rat's manoeuvres, **boxing and pivoting**, appear designed to protect the target site on **its back**. So the two kinds of attack have different targets: **social aggression targets the back; defensive attack targets the face**. Once you know which site is being attacked, you know which category of behaviour you are watching.

#### Why the categories mattered: the myth of "septal rage"

The discovery that aggressive and defensive behaviours occur in a variety of **stereotypical, species-common forms** was the necessary first step toward identifying their neural bases. Because different categories are mediated by **different neural circuits**, little progress was possible before the categories were delineated.

The cautionary example is the **lateral septum**. It was once believed to **inhibit all aggression**, because lateral septal lesions made laboratory rats notoriously difficult to handle; the behaviour was called **septal aggression** or **septal rage**. We now know that **lateral septal lesions do not increase aggression**. Rats with these lesions **do not initiate more attacks**; they are **hyperdefensive when threatened**. Without the distinction between social aggression and defensive attack, a defensive change was misread as an aggressive one for years. That is Barrett's warning in a single example.

> **Trap.** "Septal rage" is **hyperdefensiveness**; septal-lesioned rats do **not** initiate more attacks. Contrast sham rage (decorticate cats), a genuinely exaggerated aggressive display.

### Aggression and testosterone

#### Organisational and activational effects

Social aggression in many species is more common in males, and this is usually explained by the **organisational and activational effects of testosterone** (Chapter 13). The brief period of testosterone release **around birth** in genetic males is thought to **organise** their nervous systems along masculine lines, creating the *potential* for male patterns of social aggression; the **high testosterone levels after puberty** then **activate** those patterns.

These effects have been demonstrated in some mammals. In **male mice**:

- **Neonatal castration** eliminates the ability of **testosterone injections** to induce social aggression in **adulthood** (no organisation, so nothing to activate).
- **Adult castration** eliminates social aggression in males that do **not** receive testosterone replacement injections (no activation).

Research in other species has not been so straightforward (Carré & Olmstead, 2015).

> **Trap.** **Neonatal** castration removes the **organisational** effect, so later testosterone cannot restore aggression; **adult** castration removes the **activational** effect, which replacement testosterone **can** restore.

#### The comparative evidence

The extensive comparative literature has been reviewed several times (Demas et al., 2005; Munley, Rendon, & Demas, 2018; Soma, 2006). The major conclusions:

1. **Testosterone increases social aggression in the males of many species**, and aggression is **largely abolished by castration** in these same species (Hashikawa et al., 2018).
2. In **some species castration has no effect** on social aggression; in still others castration reduces social aggression **during the breeding season but not at other times**.
3. The relation is hard to interpret because **engaging in aggressive activity can itself increase testosterone**. For example, **just playing with a gun** increased the testosterone levels of male college students (**Klinesmith, Kasser, & McAndrew, 2006**).
4. **Blood testosterone**, the only measure in many studies, is **not the best measure**. What matters more is testosterone in the **relevant brain areas**; studies of brain levels are rare, but testosterone **can be synthesised in particular brain sites** and not in others.

#### The human evidence

It is unlikely that humans are an exception to the usual mammalian involvement of testosterone in social aggression, but the evidence is far from clear. In **human males**:

- aggressive behaviour **does not increase at puberty** as blood testosterone rises;
- aggressive behaviour is **not eliminated by castration**;
- aggressive behaviour is **not increased by testosterone injections** that elevate blood levels.

A few studies have found that **violent male criminals** (Fragkaki, Cima, & Granic, 2018) and **aggressive male and female athletes** (Batrinos, 2012; Denson et al., 2018) tend to have **higher-than-normal testosterone**. But this is a **correlation**, and given conclusion 3 above it may mean that **aggressive behaviour increases testosterone rather than the reverse**.

#### Why the human evidence is weak

The lack of strong evidence for testosterone in human aggression has two possible explanations, and Pinel wants you to hold both open. Either the hormonal and neural regulation of aggression in humans **genuinely differs** from that in many other mammals, or the **human research is flawed**. Two flaws are identified:

1. **Blood instead of brain.** Human studies typically measure **blood** testosterone, often **inferred from saliva** because saliva is safer and easier to collect. Blood levels of a hormone are not necessarily indicative of how much reaches the brain.
2. **The wrong kind of aggression.** Researchers studying human aggression have often failed to distinguish **social aggression**, which is related to testosterone in many species, from **defensive attack**, which is **not** (Montoya et al., 2012; Sobolewski, Brown, & Mitani, 2013). **Most seemingly aggressive outbursts in humans are overreactions to real or perceived threat**, and so are more appropriately viewed as **defensive attack**, not social aggression.

The second flaw is the module's argument coming back round: lump a fight provoked by a perceived insult in with dominance-seeking aggression and you average a testosterone-sensitive behaviour with an insensitive one, so a weak, muddy relation is exactly what the rat categories predict.

> **Trap.** "Testosterone causes aggression in human males" is not supported by Pinel: puberty, castration and injection data fail to show it, and the criminal and athlete correlations may be **reverse causation**.

## 17.3 Neural Mechanisms of Fear Conditioning

Much of what is known about the neural mechanisms of fear has come from **fear conditioning**, the establishment of fear in response to a previously neutral stimulus (the **conditional stimulus**) by presenting it, usually several times, **before** delivery of an aversive stimulus (the **unconditional stimulus**).

### Fear conditioning: the paradigm

In the standard experiment the subject, often a rat, hears a **tone** (conditional stimulus) and then receives a **mild electric shock to its feet** (unconditional stimulus). After several tone-shock pairings, the tone alone elicits two families of response:

- **Defensive behaviours**, such as **freezing** and **increased susceptibility to startle**.
- **Sympathetic nervous system responses**, such as **increased heart rate and blood pressure**.

**LeDoux and his colleagues** mapped the neural mechanism of this **auditory fear conditioning**, fear conditioning that uses a **sound** as the conditional stimulus (Kim & Jung, 2018; LeDoux, 2014). The two response families matter because the circuit turns out to send separate outputs to each.

### The amygdala and fear conditioning

#### LeDoux's lesion strategy

LeDoux began by working **along the auditory pathway**, lesioning successive stages to find where the tone's signal had to go for conditioning to occur.

> **Key study, LeDoux and colleagues.** **Bilateral lesions of the medial geniculate nucleus** (the **auditory relay nucleus of the thalamus**) **blocked** fear conditioning to a tone. **Bilateral lesions of the auditory cortex did not.**

Two inferences follow, and the second is the clever one:

1. For auditory fear conditioning to occur, the tone's signal must **reach the medial geniculate nucleus** but **need not reach the auditory cortex**.
2. Therefore some pathway from the medial geniculate nucleus to a structure **other than the auditory cortex** must play a key role.

That pathway proved to be the projection from the **medial geniculate nucleus to the amygdala**. Consistent with this, **lesions of the amygdala, like lesions of the medial geniculate nucleus, blocked auditory fear conditioning**.

The amygdala receives input from **all sensory systems**, and it is believed to be the structure in which the **emotional significance of sensory signals is learned and retained**. That makes it the natural place for a tone to acquire the meaning "shock is coming".

#### Output pathways from the amygdala

Several pathways carry signals from the amygdala to brainstem structures that control the various emotional responses (Dampney, 2015). Two are named, one for each response family:

| Projection from the amygdala | Response it elicits |
|---|---|
| To the **periaqueductal gray (PAG)** of the midbrain | Appropriate **defensive (behavioural)** responses, e.g. freezing (Kim et al., 2013) |
| To the **lateral hypothalamus** | Appropriate **sympathetic** responses, e.g. raised heart rate and blood pressure |

> **Trap.** Behaviour goes through the **PAG**; sympathetic responses through the **(lateral) hypothalamus**. And the auditory relay is the **medial** geniculate; the **lateral** geniculate is visual.

#### Two routes from the medial geniculate nucleus

The fact that auditory cortex lesions do not disrupt conditioning to simple tones does **not** mean the auditory cortex plays no part. There are **two pathways** from the medial geniculate nucleus to the amygdala:

1. The **direct** route, medial geniculate nucleus → amygdala.
2. An **indirect** route, medial geniculate nucleus → **auditory cortex** → amygdala.

**Both routes can mediate fear conditioning to simple sounds**; if **only one is destroyed, conditioning proceeds normally**. However, **only the cortical route can mediate fear conditioning to complex sounds** (Chang & Grace, 2015).

Now you can see why the original lesion result came out the way it did. Cutting the auditory cortex removed only the indirect route; the direct route still carried the simple tone to the amygdala, so conditioning survived. The medial geniculate nucleus and the amygdala are the two places **both routes share** (their common start and common end), which is why lesioning either of them blocked conditioning.

> **Trap.** LeDoux's popular writing calls the direct route the **"low road"** (fast, crude) and the cortical route the **"high road"** (slower, detailed); Pinel says "direct" and "indirect". Either suffices for a **simple** tone; only the **cortical** route handles **complex** sounds.

#### The whole circuit

Put together, the circuit thought to mediate fear conditioning to an auditory conditional stimulus (Figure 17.9; Calhoun & Tye, 2015; Herry & Johansen, 2014) runs like this:

1. The threatening sound's signal travels from the **medial geniculate nucleus** of the thalamus to the **amygdala**, either **directly** or **indirectly via the auditory cortex**.
2. The amygdala **assesses the emotional significance** of the sound **on the basis of previous encounters** with it.
3. The amygdala **activates the appropriate response circuits**: **behavioural** circuits in the **periaqueductal gray** and **sympathetic** circuits in the **hypothalamus**.

### Contextual fear conditioning and the hippocampus

**Environments, or contexts**, in which fear-inducing stimuli are encountered can themselves come to elicit fear. If you repeatedly met a bear on a particular forest trail, the trail itself would begin to frighten you. The process by which benign contexts come to elicit fear through association with fear-inducing stimuli is **contextual fear conditioning**.

It has been produced in the laboratory in **two ways**:

1. By the **conventional fear-conditioning procedure**. A rat that repeatedly receives shock after a tone becomes fearful of the **conditioning context** (the test chamber) **as well as** the tone.
2. By delivering aversive stimuli in a particular context **in the absence of any other conditional stimulus**. A rat shocked in a distinctive chamber becomes fearful of that chamber.

Because the **hippocampus** plays a key role in **memory for spatial location**, it is reasonable to expect it to be involved in contextual fear conditioning, and this seems to be the case (Chaaya, Battle, & Johnson, 2018; Maren, Phan, & Liberzon, 2013):

> **Key finding.** **Bilateral hippocampal lesions block the subsequent development of a fear response to the context** **without** blocking the development of a fear response to the **explicit conditional stimulus** (e.g., the tone) (Moscarello & Maren, 2018).

The value of this result is that it is **selective**. A hippocampal lesion that blocked *all* fear learning would tell you little; one that removes context fear while sparing tone fear shows that the hippocampus contributes something specific, the representation of *where* the bad thing happened, while the amygdala remains the site where fear itself is learned.

> **Trap.** Hippocampal lesions abolish **context** fear but **spare** tone fear; blocking tone fear is what amygdala or medial geniculate lesions do.

### The amygdala complex and fear conditioning

So far the amygdala has been treated as one structure. It is not. It is a cluster of many nuclei, the **amygdala complex**: **a dozen or so major nuclei**, themselves divided into **subnuclei**. Each subnucleus is structurally distinct and has different connections, and so is likely to have **different functions** (Duvarci & Pare, 2014; Janak & Tye, 2015).

Fear conditioning provides a compelling demonstration that treating the amygdala as a single structure is a mistake. Evidence has accumulated that the **lateral nucleus of the amygdala**, not the entire amygdala, is critically involved in the **acquisition, storage and expression of conditioned fear** (Duvarci & Pare, 2014; Janak & Tye, 2015; Tovote, Fadok, & Lüthi, 2015).

The lateral nucleus also receives the two inputs that modulate fear learning:

| Input to the lateral nucleus | Proposed role |
|---|---|
| **Prefrontal cortex** | Acts on the lateral nucleus to **suppress conditioned fear** (Gilmartin, Balderston, & Helmstetter, 2014) |
| **Hippocampus** | Interacts with the lateral nucleus to mediate learning about the **context** of fear-related events |

And the output side is a different nucleus: the amygdala is thought to control **defensive behaviour via outputs from the central nucleus of the amygdala** (Janak & Tye, 2015; Kim & Jung, 2018; Pellman & Kim, 2016; Ressler & Maren, 2019).

So the internal logic of the amygdala is **lateral nucleus in, central nucleus out**: the lateral nucleus is where the tone and the shock meet and the association is stored, with the hippocampus adding context and the prefrontal cortex applying the brakes; the central nucleus sends the commands to the PAG and hypothalamus.

The prefrontal input is also the bridge to clinical work. A brake on conditioned fear from the prefrontal cortex is the kind of mechanism through which learned fear can be dampened without being erased, which is why this pathway is of interest for anxiety disorders and for the cognitive control of emotion in humans covered in 17.4.

> **Trap.** **Lateral** nucleus = acquisition, storage and expression, and target of prefrontal and hippocampal inputs; **central** nucleus = output. The **prefrontal cortex suppresses** conditioned fear; it does not create it.

## 17.4 Brain Mechanisms of Human Emotion

We still do not know how the human brain controls the experience or expression of emotion, or how it interprets emotion in others. But progress has been made, and this module takes it area by area: general findings from cognitive neuroscience, the amygdala, the medial prefrontal lobes, lateralisation, and a closing set of cautions from brain imaging.

### Cognitive neuroscience of emotion

**Cognitive neuroscience** is currently the dominant approach to the brain mechanisms of human emotion. Many functional imaging studies have scanned people **experiencing** emotions, **imagining** them, or **watching others** experience them. Together these studies have established **three points** that have changed thinking in fundamental ways (Neumann et al., 2014; Wood et al., 2016):

1. **Brain activity associated with each human emotion is diffuse.** There is **not a centre for each emotion** (Feinstein, 2013). Pinel's slogan: think **"mosaic", not "centre"**, for the locations of brain mechanisms of emotion.
2. **There is virtually always activity in motor and sensory cortices** when a person experiences an emotion.
3. **Similar patterns of brain activity** tend to be recorded when a person **experiences** an emotion, **imagines** it, or **sees somebody else** experience it.

Figure 17.10 (from **Carr et al., 2003**, a study of the neural mechanisms of empathy) illustrates the third point: horizontal, sagittal and coronal fMRI images show increased activity in the **primary motor cortex (M1)** and the **premotor cortex (PMC)** when volunteers **watched** facial expressions of emotion, and the **same areas were active when they made the expressions themselves**.

These findings have changed how the mechanism of emotional experience is conceived. The activity in sensory and motor cortex during emotion is no longer treated as a side effect; it is now believed to be **an important part of the mechanism by which emotions are experienced**. The re-experiencing of related patterns of motor, autonomic and sensory neural activity during emotional experience is called the **embodiment of emotions** (Wang et al., 2016).

Note the historical echo: James and Lange said emotion is the perception of the body's response; embodiment says emotional experience involves the brain *re-running* the emotion's motor, autonomic and sensory patterns. The body is back in the theory, but much of it is now represented inside the brain.

### The amygdala and human emotion

You have seen that the amygdala plays a central role in fear conditioning in rats. **Functional imaging** studies suggest that the role of the **human amygdalae** is **more general**. Although they respond **most robustly to fear**, they also respond to **other emotions** (Hsu et al., 2015; Koelsch & Skouras, 2014; Patin & Pause, 2015). Indeed, they appear to play a role in **any task with an emotional component, positive or negative** (Fastenrath et al., 2014; Stillman, Van Bavel, & Cunningham, 2015). This has led to the view that the amygdalae play a role in **evaluating the emotional significance of situations**.

But the **lesion** evidence pulls toward a more **specific** role in fear. The two kinds of evidence do not have to conflict: imaging shows where activity *occurs*, lesions show what is *necessary*. A structure can be active during many emotional tasks yet be indispensable mainly for one. The following case makes the point.

#### The Case of S.P., the Woman Who Couldn't Perceive Fear

Before reading the deficits, Pinel's Journal Prompt asks you to predict them from the rat work. (The rat work predicts a fear-specific problem; see how close that gets you.)

> **Case of S.P. (Anderson & Phelps, 2000).** At the age of **48**, S.P. had her **right amygdala and adjacent tissue removed** for the treatment of **epilepsy**. Because her **left amygdala had already been damaged**, she in effect had a **bilateral amygdalar lesion**. After surgery she had an **above-average IQ** and generally normal perceptual abilities. In particular she had **no difficulty identifying faces** or extracting information from them, such as **age or gender**. However, she had a **severe deficit in recognising facial expressions of fear**, with **less striking deficits** in recognising expressions of **disgust, sadness and happiness**. In contrast, she had **no difficulty specifying which emotion would go with particular sentences**, and **no difficulty producing facial expressions** of various emotions **on request**.

Work through the dissociations, because each one rules something out:

- **Identity and age/gender intact, fear expression impaired.** The problem is not face perception in general.
- **Fear severe, others milder.** The problem is relatively specific to fear, as the rat work predicts, though not perfectly so.
- **Sentences intact.** She still *knows about* fear conceptually; the deficit is in **reading** fear from the face, not in understanding the emotion.
- **Production intact.** She could **make** a fearful face on request, so the deficit is perceptual (recognition), not motor (expression).

#### Urbach-Wiethe disease

The case of S.P. resembles reported cases of **Urbach-Wiethe disease** (Meletti et al., 2014), a **genetic disorder** that often results in **calcification** (hardening by calcium deposition) of the **amygdala and surrounding anterior medial temporal lobe structures in both hemispheres**.

> **Key case, Adolphs (2006).** One Urbach-Wiethe patient with **bilateral amygdalar damage** had lost the ability to **recognise facial expressions of fear**. Indeed, she **could not describe fear-inducing situations or produce fearful expressions**, although she had **no difficulty on tests involving other emotions**. (This patient is widely known in the literature as **S.M.**)

> **Trap.** Do not merge the cases. **S.P.** (surgical; Anderson & Phelps, 2000) **could produce** fearful expressions and **match** emotions to sentences; she failed at **recognising** fear in faces. The **Urbach-Wiethe** patient (genetic calcification; Adolphs, 2006) **could not describe** fear-inducing situations **or produce** fearful expressions. Both deficits are **fear-specific** after **bilateral** damage.

### The medial prefrontal lobes and human emotion

Emotion and cognition are often studied separately, but they are now thought to be better studied as **different components of the same system** (Barrett & Satpute, 2013). The **medial portions of the prefrontal lobes**, including the medial portions of the **orbitofrontal cortex** and the **anterior cingulate cortex**, are the sites of **emotion-cognition interaction** that have received most attention (Etkin, Büchel, & Gross, 2015; Hiser & Koenigs, 2017; Kragel et al., 2018). These are the regions Damasio's reconstruction placed in the path of Gage's tamping iron.

#### Suppression and reappraisal paradigms

Functional imaging finds **activity in the medial prefrontal lobes when emotional reactions are being cognitively suppressed or re-evaluated** (Okon-Singer et al., 2015). Two experimental designs are used:

| Paradigm | What participants are told to do |
|---|---|
| **Suppression paradigm** | **Inhibit** their emotional reactions to unpleasant films or pictures |
| **Reappraisal paradigm** | **Reinterpret** a picture so as to change their emotional reaction to it |

The medial prefrontal lobes are active in **both**, and they seem to exert their cognitive control of emotion **by interacting with the amygdala** (Whalen et al., 2013). This is the human counterpart of the rat finding in 17.3 that the **prefrontal cortex acts on the lateral amygdala to suppress conditioned fear**: two methods, two species, the same top-down brake.

> **Trap.** **Suppression** = inhibit the reaction; **reappraisal** = reinterpret the stimulus. Both activate the **medial** prefrontal lobes.

#### What do the medial prefrontal lobes do?

Many theories of their specific function have been proposed. The medial prefrontal lobes have been hypothesised to:

1. **Monitor the difference between outcome and expectancy** (Diekhof et al., 2012).
2. **Encode stimulus value over time** (Tsetsos et al., 2014).
3. **Predict the likelihood of error** (Hoffmann & Beste, 2015).
4. **Mediate the conscious awareness of emotional stimuli** (Mitchell & Greening, 2011).
5. **Mediate social decision making** (Lee & Seo, 2016; Phelps, Lempert, & Sokol-Hessner, 2014).

Which is correct? Pinel's answer: **perhaps all are**. The medial prefrontal cortex is **large and complex** and likely performs many functions. The study that made this point is worth knowing in detail.

> **Key study, Kawasaki et al. (2005).** Using **microelectrodes**, the researchers recorded from **267 neurons** in the **anterior cingulate cortices** (part of the medial prefrontal cortex) of **four patients** before surgery, while the patients viewed photographs with emotional content. Of the 267 neurons, **56 responded most strongly and consistently to negative emotional content**.

Two conclusions. The result **confirms** earlier work linking the medial prefrontal lobes with **negative emotional reactions**. But it also shows that **not all neurons in the area do the same thing**: neurons directly involved in emotional processing appear to be **sparse and widely distributed** in the human medial prefrontal lobes. About a fifth of the sampled cells (56 of 267) carried the negative-emotion signal. That is the single-neuron version of the "mosaic, not centre" lesson.

### Lateralisation of emotion

There is evidence that emotional functions are **lateralised**, the left and right hemispheres being specialised for different emotional functions (as in Chapter 16). This has led to several theories, of which the two most prominent are (Gainotti, 2019):

| Model | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Right-hemisphere model** | The **right hemisphere is specialised for all aspects of emotional processing**: perception, expression and experience |
| **Valence model** | The **right hemisphere** is specialised for **negative** emotion; the **left hemisphere** for **positive** emotion |

Most studies of the lateralisation of emotion have used functional imaging, and the results have been **complex and variable**.

> **Key study, Wager et al. (2003).** A **meta-analysis of 65 functional imaging studies** of emotion. The main conclusion was that **current theories of emotional lateralisation are too general from a neuroanatomical perspective**. **Overall** comparisons of the two hemispheres showed **no interhemispheric differences** in either the **amount** of emotional processing or the **valence** of the emotions processed. But **structure-by-structure** comparisons revealed **substantial lateralisation**: some kinds of emotional processing were lateralised to the **left** in certain structures and to the **right** in others. Imaging studies have commonly observed lateralisation in the **amygdalae**, with **more activity often observed in the left amygdala**.

The verdict follows directly: **neither the right-hemisphere model nor the valence model is supported**; both are **too general**. Notice how the meta-analysis was able to say both "no difference" and "substantial lateralisation" without contradiction. If one structure leans left and another leans right, adding up whole hemispheres cancels them out. The lateralisation is real but local, and a theory pitched at the level of whole hemispheres cannot capture it.

> **Trap.** The **left** amygdala is often the more active one, awkward for the right-hemisphere model. Valence model: **right = negative, left = positive**. Pinel's verdict: **both models fail**.

#### Asymmetry of facial expressions

Another approach is to observe the **asymmetry of facial expressions**. In most people **each facial expression begins on the left side of the face** and, when fully expressed, is **more pronounced there**, which implies **right-hemisphere dominance for facial expression** (the left side of the face is controlled mainly by the right hemisphere). Remarkably, the **same asymmetry has been documented in monkeys** (Lindell, 2013). Figure 17.11 shows the expressions as more obvious on the left side of two well-known faces, those of the **Mona Lisa and Albert Einstein**.

Keep the scope of this finding in proportion. It supports a right-hemisphere advantage for the *expression* of emotion on the face. It does not rescue the right-hemisphere model's much broader claim that the right hemisphere handles *all* aspects of emotion, which the imaging meta-analysis rejected.

### Neural mechanisms of human emotion: current perspectives

There is general consensus that the **amygdalae and the medial prefrontal cortex** play major roles in the perception and experience of human emotion. But brain imaging has put that consensus into perspective (Pessoa, 2018; Todd et al., 2020), and Pinel lists **four important points**:

1. **Emotional situations produce widespread increases in cerebral activity**, not just in the amygdalae and prefrontal cortex.
2. **All brain areas activated by emotional stimuli are also activated during other psychological processes.**
3. **No brain structure has been invariably linked to a particular emotion.**
4. **The same emotional stimuli often activate different areas in different people.**

Each point undercuts a simple claim: point 1, "emotion lives in two structures"; point 2, emotion-*dedicated* areas; point 3, "one structure, one emotion"; point 4, that group-average maps describe any individual brain. Together they restate Barrett's warning for humans: emotion words do not map onto dedicated brain parts.

> **Trap.** Do not over-learn "amygdala = fear". Rats: the **lateral nucleus** is critical for **conditioned** fear. Human imaging: **most** response to fear, but also to **other emotions**. Human lesions: **fear-specific** deficits. Current perspectives: **no structure is invariably linked** to one emotion. Each is true at its own level of evidence.

## 17.5 Stress and Health

When the body is exposed to harm or threat, the result is a cluster of physiological changes generally called the **stress response**, or just **stress**. **Stressors** are the experiences that induce it. All stressors produce the **same core pattern** of physiological changes, whether they are **psychological** (dismay at losing one's job) or **physical** (long-term exposure to cold). But it is **chronic psychological stress** that has been most frequently implicated in ill health, and that is the focus of this module.

### The stress response

#### Selye and the anterior pituitary-adrenal cortex system

**Hans Selye** (pronounced "SELL-yay") first described the stress response in the **1950s**, and he emphasised its **dual nature**:

- In the **short term** it produces **adaptive** changes that help the animal respond to the stressor, for example **mobilisation of energy resources**.
- In the **long term** it produces **maladaptive** changes, for example **enlarged adrenal glands**.

Selye attributed the stress response to activation of the **anterior pituitary adrenal cortex system**. The chain he proposed:

1. Stressors acting on neural circuits stimulate release of **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** from the **anterior pituitary**.
2. ACTH triggers release of **glucocorticoids** from the **adrenal cortex**.
3. The glucocorticoids produce many of the components of the stress response (Russell & Lightman, 2019; Shirazi et al., 2015; Spiga et al., 2014).

The level of **circulating glucocorticoids is the most commonly employed physiological measure of stress**.

#### The sympathetic nervous system adrenal medulla system

Selye largely **ignored** the contribution of the **sympathetic nervous system**. Stressors activate it, thereby increasing the amounts of **epinephrine and norepinephrine** released from the **adrenal medulla**. Most modern theories acknowledge **both** systems (Carter & Goldstein, 2015), which is the **two-system view** of Figure 17.12:

| Route | Chain | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior pituitary adrenal cortex system** | Brain → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex | **Glucocorticoids** |
| **Sympathetic nervous system adrenal medulla system** | Brain → sympathetic nervous system → adrenal medulla | **Epinephrine and norepinephrine** |

> **Trap.** Adrenal **cortex** = **glucocorticoids** under **ACTH**; adrenal **medulla** = **epinephrine and norepinephrine** under **sympathetic** control; Selye featured only the cortex route. Other texts call Pinel's anterior pituitary adrenal cortex system the **HPA axis** (hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone triggers ACTH) and the medullary route **SAM**. The main glucocorticoid is **cortisol** in humans, **corticosterone** in rodents.

> **Trap.** Pinel dates Selye's description to "the 1950s" and omits his **general adaptation syndrome**, which Indian papers often ask about: **alarm reaction, resistance, exhaustion**, first reported in **1936**.

#### Is there one stress response?

The major feature of Selye's theory is the assertion that **both physical and psychological stressors induce the same general stress response**. This has proved **partly correct**. There is good evidence that common psychological stressors, **losing a job, taking a final exam, ending a relationship**, act like physical stressors. But Selye's contention that there is **only one stress response** is a **simplification**. Stress responses are complex and varied, the exact response depending on **the stressor, its timing, the nature of the stressed person, and how the person reacts** to the stressor (Hostinar, Sullivan, & Gunnar, 2014; Oken, Chamine, & Wakeland, 2015).

> **Key study, Katz et al. (1970).** In women **awaiting surgery for possible breast cancer**, stress levels were **lower** in those who had convinced themselves that they **could not possibly have cancer**, that their **prayers were certain to be answered**, or that it was **counterproductive to worry**.

The stressor was objectively the same; what differed was the appraisal, and the physiology followed the appraisal.

#### Cytokines: the 1990s advance

In the **1990s** came an important advance (Grippo & Scotti, 2013): stressors were found to produce physiological reactions that participate in the body's **inflammatory responses**. Most notably, stressors **increase blood levels of cytokines**, a group of **peptide hormones** released by many cells that take part in a variety of physiological and immunological responses, **causing inflammation and fever** (Padro & Sanders, 2014). This link between stress and inflammation becomes central in the psychoneuroimmunology section below.

### Animal models of stress

Most early stress research used non-humans, and even today most lines of stress research **begin with controlled experiments on non-humans** before moving to **correlational studies of humans**. Early non-human research tended to use **extreme** stressors, such as **repeated electric shock** or **long periods of physical restraint**. That approach had **two problems**:

1. **Ethics.** Any research that creates stressful situations is controversial, but many early studies were "over the top" and **would not be permitted today** in many countries.
2. **Questionable scientific value.** Responses to extreme stress tend to **mask normal variations** in the stress response, and it is difficult to relate the results to **common human stressors**.

#### Subordination stress

Better animal models study **social threat from conspecifics** (members of the same species). Virtually all mammals, particularly males, experience threats from conspecifics at some point in their lives. When conspecific threat becomes an **enduring feature of daily life**, the result is **subordination stress** (Rodriguez-Arias et al., 2016).

Subordination stress is most readily studied in social species that form **dominance hierarchies** (pecking orders; Chapter 2). Subordinate male rodents that are continually attacked by more dominant males (Barik et al., 2013):

- are **more likely to attack juveniles**;
- have **smaller testes**;
- have **shorter life spans**;
- have **lower blood levels of testosterone**;
- have **higher blood levels of glucocorticoids**.

The chronic social threat that induces subordination stress in many species has a familiar name in our own: **bullying**.

The advantage over shock and restraint is ecological validity: a stressor animals evolved to meet, at natural intensities, that maps onto a common human stressor.

> **Trap.** Subordinate males have **lower** testosterone and **higher** glucocorticoids, not the reverse; the five-item list is a frequent "which is NOT a feature" question.

### Psychosomatic disorders: the case of gastric ulcers

Interest in the pathological effects of stress has grown as more and more **psychosomatic disorders**, medical disorders in which **psychological factors play a causal role**, have been identified. So many adverse effects of stress on health have been documented (in **heart disease, asthma and skin disorders**, for example) that it is now more reasonable to think of **most, if not all, medical disorders as psychosomatic**.

**Gastric ulcers** were **one of the first** medical disorders classified as psychosomatic. They are **painful lesions to the lining of the stomach and duodenum**, which in extreme cases can be life-threatening. About **500,000 new cases** are reported each year in the **United States**.

The view of gastric ulcers as the prototypical psychosomatic disorder changed with the discovery that they seemed to be caused by **bacteria**. It was claimed that ***Helicobacter pylori*** (*H. pylori*) is responsible for **all** gastric ulcers except those caused by **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents such as aspirin**. That seemed to rule out stress. Pinel argues that the evidence says otherwise.

First, what is not in dispute: *H. pylori* **does damage the stomach wall**, and **antibiotic treatment helps many sufferers**. But the facts suggest that ***H. pylori* infection alone is insufficient** to produce the disorder in most people:

1. **Most patients** with gastric ulcers show signs of *H. pylori* infection, **but so do many healthy individuals** (Maixner et al., 2016; Testerman & Morris, 2014). An agent present in many people who never develop the disease cannot be the whole cause.
2. Antibiotics improve many patients, **but so do psychological treatments**, and they do so **without reducing signs of *H. pylori* infection**. If the bacteria were the whole story, a treatment that leaves the bacteria in place should not work.

So some other factor appears to increase the **susceptibility of the stomach wall** to damage from *H. pylori*, and that factor appears to be **stress**. Two more observations support it: gastric ulcers occur **more commonly in people living in stressful situations**, and **stressors can produce gastric ulcers in laboratory animals**.

The lesson generalises: finding a physical cause does not remove a psychological one, because the two can **interact**. "Psychosomatic" does not mean "imaginary"; it means psychological factors are among the causes.

> **Trap.** Older notes quote a specific percentage of healthy *H. pylori* carriers; the 11th edition says only "**many healthy individuals**". The logic: carriers without ulcers (not sufficient) and psychological treatment working without reducing infection (stress contributes). Marshall and Warren's 2005 Nobel Prize for the bacterial account, not mentioned by Pinel, does not contradict this.

### Psychoneuroimmunology: stress, the immune system, and the brain

A major change in the study of psychosomatic disorders came in the **1970s** with the discovery that **stress can increase susceptibility to infectious diseases**. Until then, infectious diseases had been regarded as "strictly physical". The discovery led, in the **early 1980s**, to a new field: **psychoneuroimmunology**, the study of **interactions among psychological factors, the nervous system and the immune system**. To follow its findings you need a working model of the immune system first.

#### Four lines of defence

Micro-organisms of every description thrive in the warm, damp, nutritive climate of your body. The body has **four lines of defence**:

1. **The behavioural immune system.** Humans are motivated to **avoid contact with individuals displaying symptoms of illness** (Murray & Schaller, 2016), and their bodies are **primed to respond more aggressively to infection when they perceive signs of infection in others** (Schaller et al., 2010).
2. **Surface barriers.** The major one is **skin**; other mechanisms protect the body's openings (respiratory tract, eyes, gastrointestinal tract), including **coughing, sneezing, tears, mucus and numerous chemical barriers**.
3. **The innate immune system.**
4. **The adaptive immune system.**

If micro-organisms breach the surface barriers, they meet the last two lines, which together constitute the **immune system** (Kipnis, 2018; Pringle, 2013).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition counts **four** lines of defence, with a **behavioural** line first; only lines 3 and 4 make up the **immune system** proper.

#### The innate immune system

The **innate immune system** is the **first component of the immune system to react**. It reacts **quickly**, generally **near the points of entry** of **pathogens** (disease-causing agents). It is triggered when receptors called **toll-like receptors** (named for their similarity to **toll**, a receptor previously discovered in **fruit flies**) bind to molecules on the surface of pathogens, or when injured cells send out alarm signals (De Nardo, 2015). Its reaction is a complex but **general** array of chemical and cellular responses: general in the sense that **the reaction to all pathogens is the same**.

One of the first innate reactions is **inflammation** (swelling), triggered by chemicals released from damaged cells. Particularly influential are the **cytokines**, which **attract leukocytes (white blood cells) and other phagocytes** into the infected area. **Phagocytes** are cells that **engulf and destroy pathogens**; **microglia** are the phagocytes specific to the **central nervous system** (Aguzzi, Barres, & Bennett, 2013; Su et al., 2016). Cytokines also **promote healing** of damaged tissue once the pathogens are destroyed (Kyritsis et al., 2012; Werneburg et al., 2017).

**Phagocytosis**, the destruction of pathogens by phagocytes, is thought to be **one of the first immune reactions to have evolved**; phagocytes have been identified in **all vertebrates and invertebrates** that have been examined. Figure 17.13 shows a phagocyte about to ingest bacteria.

#### The adaptive immune system

The **adaptive immune system** differs from the innate system in **four respects**:

| | **Innate** | **Adaptive** |
|---|---|---|
| Evolutionary age | Ancient (phagocytes in all vertebrates and invertebrates examined) | **More recent**, first appearing in **early vertebrates** |
| Speed | Fast; first to react | **Slower**; takes longer to be fully manifested |
| Specificity | **General**: the same reaction to all pathogens | **Specific**: reacts against **specific antigens** |
| Memory | None | **Has memory**: reacts more effectively to the same pathogen in future |

The main cells of the adaptive immune system are specialised leukocytes called **lymphocytes**. They are **produced in bone marrow and the thymus gland** and **stored in the lymphatic system** until activated. There are two major classes (Plesnila, 2016):

- **T cells** (T lymphocytes) direct **cell-mediated immunity**.
- **B cells** (B lymphocytes) direct **antibody-mediated immunity**.

#### Cell-mediated immunity

Follow the sequence step by step:

1. A **phagocyte ingests** a foreign micro-organism.
2. The phagocyte **displays the micro-organism's antigens** on the surface of its own cell membrane. **Antigens** are molecules, **usually proteins**, that can trigger an immune response.
3. The display **attracts T cells**. Each T cell carries **two kinds of receptor**: one for molecules **normally found on the surface of phagocytes and other body cells**, and one for **a specific foreign antigen**.
4. Across the T-cell population there are **millions of different receptors** for foreign antigens, but **only one kind on each T cell**, and **only a few T cells with each kind**.
5. When a T cell with a receptor for the displayed antigen **binds to the surface of the infected macrophage**, a series of reactions begins, including **multiplication of the bound T cell**.
6. The result is many more T cells with the specific receptor needed to **destroy all invaders carrying the target antigen and all body cells that have been infected** by them.

Step 4 explains why the adaptive system is slow: only a handful of matching T cells exist at first, and they must be found and multiplied before the response matters.

#### Antibody-mediated immunity

1. A **B cell binds to a foreign antigen** for which it has an appropriate receptor.
2. The B cell **multiplies** and **synthesises a lethal form of its receptor molecules**.
3. These lethal receptor molecules, **antibodies**, are released, **bind to the foreign antigens**, and **destroy or deactivate** the micro-organisms that carry them.
4. **Memory B cells** for that antigen are also produced. They are **long-lived** and **accelerate antibody-mediated immunity** if the same micro-organism invades again.

> **Trap.** **T cells = cell-mediated** (antigen **displayed on a phagocyte**); **B cells = antibody-mediated** (antigen **bound directly**; antibodies are a **lethal form of the B cell's receptor**). Memory belongs to the **adaptive** system.

#### Vaccination and immunisation

The memory of the adaptive immune system is what gives **vaccination** its **prophylactic** (preventive) effect. Vaccination involves administering a **weakened form of a virus**, so that if the virus later invades, the adaptive immune system is ready. **Smallpox**, for example, was eradicated by programmes of vaccination with its **largely benign relative, cowpox**. The process of creating immunity through vaccination is **immunisation**.

Until recently most immunological research focused on the adaptive system; the discovery of the role of **cytokines in the innate immune system** stimulated interest in it, and that is where the stress story picks up.

#### What effect does stress have on immune function: disruptive or beneficial?

It is widely believed, by family, friends and even physicians, that the main effect of stress on immune function is **disruptive**. Pinel asks whether this is true and raises a logical problem first: it is **inconsistent with evolution**. Virtually every organism encounters many stressors in its life, and it is hard to see how a **maladaptive** response to stress, such as immune disruption, could have evolved, or could have survived if it had arisen by genetic accident or as a **spandrel** (a **non-adaptive by-product of an adaptive evolutionary change**; Chapter 2).

Two events clarified the relation.

> **Key study, Segerstrom & Miller (2004).** A **meta-analysis of about 300 studies** of stress and immune function. The effects of stress **depended on the kind of stress**. **Acute (brief) stressors**, those lasting **less than 100 minutes**, such as **public speaking, an athletic competition or a musical performance**, actually **improved immune function**, mainly in the **innate** immune system, whose components can be marshalled quickly. **Chronic (long-lasting) stressors**, such as **caring for an ill relative** or **a period of unemployment**, **adversely affected the adaptive immune system**.

Stress that **disrupts** health or other aspects of functioning is **distress**; stress that **improves** health or functioning is **eustress**.

The second event was the discovery of the **bidirectional role of cytokines** in the innate immune system. **Short-term** cytokine-induced inflammatory responses **help the body combat infection**, whereas **long-term** cytokine release is associated with a variety of **adverse health consequences** (Dhabhar, 2014). This explains the Segerstrom and Miller pattern: a brief stressor triggers a burst of innate, cytokine-driven defence that is protective; a chronic stressor keeps that system switched on until the inflammation itself does harm, alongside suppression of adaptive immunity. And it removes the evolutionary puzzle: the stress response is adaptive for the brief threats it evolved to handle, and becomes harmful only when it is sustained.

> **Trap.** "Stress suppresses immunity" is the wrong blanket answer: **acute (under 100 min) improves, mainly innate; chronic impairs adaptive**. About **300** studies.

#### How does stress influence immune function?

The mechanisms are hard to pin down because there are so many possibilities. Stress acts through both the anterior pituitary adrenal cortex system and the sympathetic adrenal medulla system, and each has innumerable ways of influencing immunity. Some of the known links run in both directions between the nervous and immune systems:

- **T cells and B cells have receptors for glucocorticoids.**
- **Lymphocytes have receptors for epinephrine, norepinephrine and glucocorticoids.**
- Many **neuropeptides** released by neurons are **also released by cells of the immune system**.
- Conversely, **cytokines**, originally thought to be produced only by immune cells, are **also produced by cells of the nervous system** (Jin & Yamashita, 2016).

There are also **behavioural routes**. People under severe stress often change their **diet, exercise, sleep and drug use**, any of which can influence immune function. And the behaviour of a stressed or ill person can produce stress and illness **in others**:

> **Key study, Wolf et al. (2007).** **Stress in mothers aggravates asthmatic symptoms in their children**; conversely, **asthma in the children increases measures of stress in their mothers**.

That two-way loop is a warning that "stress causes illness" can conceal illness causing stress.

#### Does stress affect susceptibility to infectious disease?

Most people assume that if stress influences immune function, it must increase susceptibility to infectious disease. It does **not** follow, and Pinel gives **at least three reasons** why stress-produced decreases in immune function may **not** show up as increased infection:

1. The immune system has **many redundant components**, so disrupting one may have little or no effect on vulnerability.
2. Stress-produced changes in immune function may be **too short-lived** to affect the probability of infection substantially.
3. Declines in some aspects of immune function may induce **compensatory increases** in others.

Proving the link in humans is difficult because **only correlational studies are possible** (one cannot ethically assign people to chronic stress). Numerous studies have reported positive correlations between stress and ill health; for example, students reported **more respiratory infections during final exams** (**Glaser et al., 1987**). But such correlations are never straightforward. People may **report** more illness during stressful times because:

- they **expect** to be more ill;
- their **experience** of illness during stress is **more unpleasant**, so they notice and report it;
- the stress **changed their behaviour** in ways that increased their susceptibility to infection.

Despite these difficulties, the evidence for a link is strong, because **three types of evidence converge**:

1. **Correlational studies in humans** link stress levels to numerous measures of health.
2. **Controlled experiments in laboratory animals** show that stress can increase susceptibility to infectious disease.
3. **A few partially controlled studies in humans** add greatly to the weight of evidence.

> **Key study, Cohen et al. (1991).** Psychological stress was assessed by **questionnaire** in **394 healthy participants**. Each participant was then **randomly** given **saline nasal drops containing a respiratory virus, or saline only**. All participants were **quarantined** until the end of the study. A **higher proportion of the high-stress participants developed colds**.

Why "partially" controlled? Stress was **measured, not manipulated**, so a third variable could still link stress and susceptibility. But **exposure** was randomly assigned, and **quarantine** removed differences in exposure outside the study. That eliminates the biggest confound in studies such as Glaser's: the stressed participants could not simply have met more viruses, or behaved differently out in the world, because everyone was exposed in the same way and kept in the same environment. The saline-only group shows what happens without the virus.

### Early experience of stress

Early exposure to severe stress can have a variety of adverse effects on later development. Children subjected to **maltreatment** or other severe stress show a variety of **brain and endocrine abnormalities** (Klengel & Binder, 2015). For example, early stress often **increases the intensity of later stress responses**, such as the release of glucocorticoids in response to stressors.

The vulnerable developmental period **begins before birth**. Many animal experiments have exposed pregnant females to stressors and documented adverse effects on their **offspring** (e.g., Sowa et al., 2015).

#### Handling, maternal licking, and separation

One line of research began with the observation that **handling of rat pups by researchers for a few minutes per day during the first few weeks of life** has a variety of **salutary** (health-promoting) effects (Raineki, Lucion, & Weinberg, 2014). Most of these seemed to result from a **decrease in the magnitude of the handled pups' responses to stressful events**: as adults, rats handled as pups showed **smaller increases in circulating glucocorticoids** in response to stressors (Francis & Meaney, 1999).

It seemed remarkable that a few hours of handling in total could have such a large and lasting effect, and the evidence supports a different explanation:

> **Key study, Liu et al. (1997).** Handled rat pups are **groomed (licked) more by their mothers**. Liu and colleagues hypothesised that the salutary effects of handling came from the **extra maternal grooming**, not from the handling itself. They confirmed it: **unhandled pups that received a lot of grooming from their mothers** developed the **same profile of reduced glucocorticoid release** as handled pups (Champagne et al., 2008).

The logic of the confirmation is the classic way to test a mediator. If handling works *through* licking, then licking without handling should produce the same effect, and it did. The experimenter's hands were only the trigger; the mother's behaviour was the cause.

**Early separation** of pups from their mothers has the **opposite** effect to high levels of grooming (Zhang et al., 2013): rats separated from their mothers in infancy show **elevated behavioural and hormonal responses to stress as adults**.

Pinel's "themes revisited" adds that **epigenetic mechanisms appear to mediate many of the effects of stress on brain function**; the maternal-licking work is the best-known example (later studies linked high licking to lasting epigenetic changes in the offspring's glucocorticoid-receptor gene).

> **Trap.** Handling works through **maternal licking** (Liu et al., 1997). Handled or highly licked pups show **smaller** adult glucocorticoid responses; **separated** pups **larger** ones. Early severe stress **increases** later stress reactivity.

### Stress and the hippocampus

Stress affects the structure and function of the brain in many ways (Lupien et al., 2018; McEwen, Gray, & Nasca, 2015; Sandi & Haller, 2015), but the **hippocampus** appears **particularly susceptible** (Kim, Pellman, & Kim, 2015; McEwen, Nasca, & Gray, 2016). The likely reason is its **particularly dense population of glucocorticoid receptors**.

Stress has been shown to:

1. **reduce dendritic branching** in the hippocampus;
2. **reduce adult neurogenesis** in the hippocampus (Egeland, Zunszain, & Pariante, 2015);
3. **modify the structure of some hippocampal synapses**;
4. **disrupt performance of hippocampus-dependent tasks** (Kim, Pellman, & Kim, 2015).

These effects appear to be **mediated by elevated glucocorticoid levels**, and the evidence takes the classic two-sided form. They **can be induced by corticosterone** (a major glucocorticoid) given without the stressor, which shows glucocorticoids are **sufficient**; and they **can be blocked by adrenalectomy** (surgical removal of the adrenal glands), which removes the glucocorticoid source and shows glucocorticoids are **necessary** (de Quervain, Schwabe, & Roozendaal, 2017; Shirazi et al., 2015).

Pinel treats this as the chapter's main example of **neuroplasticity**: chronic stress reshapes the hippocampus. Recall from 17.3 that the hippocampus is also what allows fear to be attached to a **context**, so stress is acting on a structure that is itself part of the fear system.

> **Trap.** Stress **reduces** hippocampal dendritic branching and neurogenesis via **glucocorticoids** (induced by **corticosterone**, blocked by **adrenalectomy**); the vulnerability reflects dense **glucocorticoid receptors**.

## Conclusion: The Case of Charles Whitman, the Texas Tower Sniper

The chapter ends with a troubling case that reinforces the point that the amygdala plays a role in emotion.

> After having lunch with his wife and his mother, **Charles Whitman** went home and typed a **letter of farewell**. In it he said he was having many compelling and bizarre ideas, that psychiatric care had been no help, and he asked that **his brain be autopsied** after he was through; he was sure they would find the problem.
>
> By all reports Whitman had been a nice person: an **Eagle Scout at 12**, a high-school graduate at **17**, then a **Marine**, where he established himself as an **expert marksman**. After discharge he entered the **University of Texas** to study **architectural engineering**.
>
> Nevertheless, on the evening of **August 1, 1966**, he **killed his wife and his mother**. He professed love for both but did not want them to face the aftermath of what was to follow. The next morning at about **11:30** he went to the **Tower of the University of Texas** carrying **six guns**, ammunition, several knives, food and water. He clubbed the receptionist to death and shot four more people on his way to the observation deck, from which he opened fire on people crossing the campus and on nearby streets, killing people as far as **300 metres** away. At **1:24 p.m.** police reached the platform and shot him dead. In all, **17 people, including Whitman, were killed and 31 wounded** (Helmer, 1986).
>
> An autopsy was conducted. Whitman had been right: they found a **walnut-sized tumour in his right amygdala**.

Read the case with the chapter's discipline. It is a single case, and a tumour is not a clean, controlled lesion, so it cannot by itself prove that the tumour caused the violence; it fits the pattern that damage involving the amygdala transforms emotional behaviour. Pinel adds the qualification that matters: **not everybody reacts in the same way to amygdalar damage**. Klüver and Bucy's monkeys became placid; S.P. lost the ability to read fear in faces; Whitman became violent.

> **Trap.** Whitman: walnut-sized tumour in the **right amygdala**, **August 1 to 2, 1966**, **17 killed including Whitman, 31 wounded**. Gage is 1848 and **medial prefrontal**; Klüver-Bucy amygdala damage produced **tameness**, not aggression.

## How the chapter fits together

Seven threads run through the five modules. If you can follow each one from start to finish, you understand the chapter rather than just remembering it.

1. **Describe before you localise.** Barrett's (2006) warning about everyday emotion words, the reification error, the Blanchards' and Pellis's behavioural descriptions, the three criteria for classifying aggression and defence, the misread "septal rage" and the muddled human testosterone literature all make one point: brain mechanisms were found only after behaviour had been carved into its real categories.
2. **Body and brain influence each other.** James-Lange (body causes feeling) and Cannon-Bard (body and feeling independent) both failed; the modern view lets perception, bodily response and experience each influence the other two. Partial ANS specificity, facial feedback, embodiment, and stress hormones reshaping the hippocampus are all versions of that two-way traffic.
3. **The hypothalamus and the amygdala keep coming back.** The hypothalamus is necessary for sham rage, is the expression end of Papez's circuit, and delivers the sympathetic arm of conditioned fear. The amygdala carries most Klüver-Bucy symptoms, gives a tone its meaning in fear conditioning, responds to emotion in imaging, is needed to read fear in faces (S.P., Urbach-Wiethe), and held Whitman's tumour.
4. **Top-down control.** Bard's cortex inhibits and directs aggression; the prefrontal cortex suppresses conditioned fear via the lateral amygdala in rats; the medial prefrontal lobes are active in human suppression and reappraisal and act through the amygdala; Gage, with both medial prefrontal lobes damaged, lost that control.
5. **Mosaic, not centre.** The amygdala is a complex of nuclei (lateral in, central out); emotion neurons in the anterior cingulate are sparse (Kawasaki et al., 2005); lateralisation is structure-by-structure (Wager et al., 2003); no structure is invariably linked to one emotion.
6. **Evolution explains form and function**: Darwin's displays and antithesis, target sites, the rat defensive repertoire, and the argument against a generally harmful stress response, resolved by Segerstrom and Miller's acute-chronic split.
7. **Correlation, causation, necessity and sufficiency.** Testosterone in violent criminals, infections at exam time, *H. pylori* in ulcer patients and the single case of Whitman each look causal without being proved so; Cohen et al. (1991) shows how partial control strengthens a correlational case. Feedback is not necessary for emotion but influences it; *H. pylori* is not sufficient for ulcers; corticosterone is sufficient and adrenalectomy shows glucocorticoids are necessary for hippocampal stress damage.

## Worked example

*A 38-year-old man is referred eight months after herpes simplex encephalitis damaged both anterior medial temporal lobes. His wife says he puts small objects in his mouth, eats almost anything, sits placidly in front of the television, and last week walked calmly up to a snarling dog. On testing he recognises familiar faces and judges age and sex correctly but labels fearful faces "neutral"; he can explain what situations frighten people and can pull a frightened face when asked. His wife, his full-time carer since the illness, has had three colds this winter and a duodenal ulcer for which her GP found* H. pylori*, and says her memory "has gone". She asks: does her husband no longer feel fear; is the stress "destroying her immune system"; and is her ulcer "just the bacteria"?*

**His syndrome and its structure.** Mouthing objects, eating almost anything and loss of fear are the core of the **Klüver-Bucy syndrome** (1939), which follows **bilateral anterior temporal** damage and in primates is attributed mostly to the **amygdala**. Pinel's human case also followed a **brain infection** and showed the same placidity and oral exploration.

**His face-reading deficit.** Intact identity, age and sex with a selective failure on **fearful expressions** is the **S.P. pattern** (Anderson & Phelps, 2000). He can still describe fear-inducing situations (concept intact) and produce a fearful face (expression intact), so the deficit is in **recognition**. That makes him more like S.P. than like the **Urbach-Wiethe** patient (Adolphs, 2006), who could do neither.

**Would he acquire conditioned fear?** The rat circuit predicts not: conditioned fear to a tone needs the signal to pass from the **medial geniculate nucleus to the amygdala**, the **lateral nucleus** to store the association, and the **central nucleus** to drive the PAG and hypothalamus. If the **hippocampus** is also damaged, fear of **contexts** should be impaired too. This is a prediction from animal work, not a result the chapter reports in humans.

**Does he "no longer feel fear"?** Be careful. Lesion evidence ties bilateral amygdala damage to **fear-specific** deficits, and the dog episode fits. But imaging shows the amygdalae respond to many emotions, emotion-related activity is widespread, and **no structure is invariably linked to one emotion**. The defensible answer is that his threat detection and fear responding are severely reduced; whether every component of fear experience is gone cannot be read off one structure, and people do not all react the same way to amygdalar damage.

**Is her stress "destroying her immune system"?** Not as a blanket claim. **Segerstrom and Miller (2004)** found that **acute** stressors (under 100 minutes) tend to **improve** mainly innate immunity, whereas **chronic** stressors impair the **adaptive** system. Caring for an ill relative is their example of a chronic stressor, so her situation is the risky kind, and long-term **cytokine** release adds an inflammatory cost.

**Did the stress cause her colds?** Three colds are correlational, and Pinel's alternatives apply: she may expect illness, notice it more, or have changed her **sleep, diet and exercise** (a behavioural route). The causal case rests on converging evidence: animal experiments, correlational data, and **Cohen et al. (1991)**, where higher stress predicted more colds in 394 quarantined people randomly given virus or saline.

**Is her ulcer "just the bacteria"?** No, nor "just stress". *H. pylori* damages the stomach wall and antibiotics help, but **many healthy people carry it** and **psychological treatment helps without reducing infection**, so stress appears to raise the stomach wall's **susceptibility**. Treating the infection and reducing her stress are complementary.

**Her memory.** Chronic stress raises **glucocorticoids**, and the receptor-dense **hippocampus** shows reduced dendritic branching and neurogenesis and impaired performance (reproduced by **corticosterone**, blocked by **adrenalectomy** in animals). Her complaint fits, though poor sleep and low mood are also candidates.

**What you would not tell her:** that all stress is harmful, that her ulcer is purely psychological or purely bacterial, or that her husband's calm means he is "fine". His placidity is a symptom of losing the system that detects threat.

## MCQ traps

1. **James-Lange (1884)** is the **first physiological theory**: stimulus → bodily response → feeling. **Cannon-Bard (~1915**; Cannon, extended by Bard): feeling and expression **parallel and independent**. **Table 17.1** dates the pair "**about 1900**".
2. **Broken-neck patients** keep a **full range** of emotion with slight **dampening of fear and anger** (Pistoia et al., 2015): feedback is **not necessary**; facial feedback shows it **can influence** feeling. Modern view: each of the three components influences the other two.
3. **Sham rage (Bard, 1929)**: **decorticate** cats, **hypothalamus intact**; removing the hypothalamus **abolishes** it. Hypothalamus = expression; cortex **inhibits and directs**.
4. **Papez (1937)** circuit rings the thalamus (expression via **hypothalamus**, experience via **cortex**); **MacLean (1952)** made it the **limbic system theory**, the 1952 table entry.
5. **Klüver-Bucy (1939)**: **bilateral anterior temporal lobectomy**; no fear **even of snakes**; mainly **amygdala** damage.
6. **Landmark order**: Gage 1848, Darwin 1872, James-Lange/Cannon-Bard ~1900, sham rage 1929, Klüver-Bucy 1939, limbic system 1952.
7. **Principle of antithesis** = opposite messages, **opposite postures** (dogs: ears, back, hair, tail). Not the universality claim.
8. **ANS specificity** is **between** the extremes: emotions do not share one pattern, but none has a unique one.
9. **Polygraphy detects ANS activity, not lies.** The **control-question technique** scores about **55 percent** in real life vs **50 percent** chance (Iacono & Ben-Shakhar, 2019); older "80 percent" figures are outdated.
10. **Lykken (1959)**, guilty-knowledge technique (= **concealed information test**): **electrodermal** only, **88 percent** of mock criminals identified, **no** innocents judged guilty.
11. **Ekman & Friesen**: New Guinea study **1971**, atlas **1975**. Six primaries: surprise, anger, sadness, disgust, fear, happiness; **contempt** and **pride** are not among them.
12. **Rutledge & Hupka (1985)**: participants **unaware** they were making happy or angry faces. **Coles, Larsen & Lench (2019)**: effect reliable but **smaller** than believed.
13. **Microexpressions** last about **0.05 second** (Ekman, 1985). **Duchenne (1862)**: genuine smile = **orbicularis oculi** (not voluntary) + **zygomaticus major** (voluntary); a fake lacks the **eye** component; **Ekman** named it.
14. **Four qualifications** to Ekman: rarely pure, more primaries, **body cues** (pride), not fully universal (East Asian differences; **Crivelli et al., 2016, 2017**).
15. **Pellis et al. (1988)**: cats **do not play** with mice; "play" is attack-defence **vacillation**; an **antianxiety drug** moved every cat toward killing.
16. **Colony-intruder model (Blanchard & Blanchard)**: alpha's **lateral attack** aims at the **back**; defensive attack aims at the **face**; predatory rats bite the **back of the neck**.
17. **Maternal defence** and **defensive attack** look aggressive but are **defensive**: classification is by **function**.
18. **"Septal rage"** = **hyperdefensiveness**; lateral-septal rats do **not** initiate more attacks.
19. **Male mice**: **neonatal** castration prevents adult testosterone from inducing aggression (organisational); **adult** castration abolishes it unless testosterone is replaced (activational).
20. **Human testosterone**: no rise in aggression at puberty, none removed by castration, none added by injections; **playing with a gun** raises testosterone (Klinesmith et al., 2006), so correlations may be **reversed**. Flaws: **blood/saliva** not brain; **social aggression** confused with **defensive attack**.
21. **LeDoux**: **medial geniculate** lesions block auditory fear conditioning; **auditory cortex** lesions do **not**; **amygdala** lesions block.
22. **Amygdala → PAG** = defensive **behaviour**; **amygdala → lateral hypothalamus** = **sympathetic** response.
23. **Either route** (direct or via auditory cortex) suffices for **simple** tones; only the **cortical** route handles **complex** sounds.
24. **Hippocampal lesions** block **contextual** fear but **not** fear of the tone.
25. **Lateral nucleus** = acquisition, storage, expression of conditioned fear; **central nucleus** = output; **prefrontal cortex suppresses** conditioned fear.
26. **S.P.** (right amygdala removed at **48**, left already damaged) **could produce** expressions and match emotions to sentences; the **Urbach-Wiethe** patient (Adolphs, 2006) could **neither describe** fear situations **nor produce** fearful faces.
27. **Kawasaki et al. (2005)**: **267** anterior cingulate neurons, **4** patients, **56** responsive to **negative** content: sparse and distributed.
28. **Wager et al. (2003)**: **65** studies; no overall hemisphere difference; lateralisation **structure-by-structure**; **left amygdala** often more active; **both** the right-hemisphere and the valence (**right negative, left positive**) models rejected.
29. **Expressions** are stronger on the **left** of the face, implying **right**-hemisphere dominance; monkeys show it too.
30. **Selye (1950s)**: **ACTH** → **glucocorticoids** from the adrenal **cortex**; he **ignored** the sympathetic → adrenal **medulla** → epinephrine/norepinephrine route. Glucocorticoids are the **most common** stress measure.
31. **Subordinate male rodents**: attack juveniles more, **smaller testes**, **shorter lives**, **lower testosterone**, **higher glucocorticoids**.
32. **Gastric ulcers**: about **500,000** new US cases a year; *H. pylori* **not sufficient** (many healthy carriers; psychological treatment helps **without** reducing infection).
33. **Psychoneuroimmunology**: field in the **early 1980s**, after the **1970s** discovery; **four** lines of defence, starting with the **behavioural immune system**.
34. **Segerstrom & Miller (2004)**: about **300** studies; **acute (under 100 min)** stress **improves** mainly **innate** immunity; **chronic** stress **impairs adaptive** immunity.
35. **Cohen et al. (1991)**: **394** participants, virus or saline, **quarantined**; high stress → more colds. Stress **measured**, exposure **manipulated**.
36. **Liu et al. (1997)**: handling works through extra **maternal licking**; maternal **separation** has the opposite effect. Stress shrinks hippocampal **dendrites** and **neurogenesis** via glucocorticoids (**corticosterone** induces, **adrenalectomy** blocks). **Whitman (1966)**: tumour in the **right amygdala**.

| **Microexpressions** | Brief (about 0.05 s) expressions of the real emotion breaking through a false one |

| **Fear** | The emotional reaction to threat; motivates defensive behaviour |

| **Aggressive behaviours** | Behaviours whose primary function is to threaten or harm |

| **Social aggression** | Unprovoked aggression against a conspecific to establish, alter or maintain a hierarchy |
| **Defensive attack** | Attack by a cornered animal, usually aimed at the attacker's face |
| **Target-site concept** | Aggressive and defensive behaviours attack specific sites on another's body while protecting specific sites on one's own |

| **Amygdala complex** | The amygdala as a dozen or so major nuclei with subnuclei |

| **Central nucleus of the amygdala** | Output nucleus controlling defensive behaviour |

| **Suppression paradigms** | Participants inhibit emotional reactions to unpleasant films or pictures |
| **Reappraisal paradigms** | Participants reinterpret a picture to change their emotional reaction |
| **Right-hemisphere model / valence model** | Right hemisphere handles all emotion / right negative, left positive |

| **Stressors** | Experiences that induce the stress response |
| **Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** | Anterior pituitary hormone triggering glucocorticoid release |
| **Glucocorticoids** | Adrenal cortex stress hormones; the commonest physiological stress measure |
| **Adrenal cortex** | Releases glucocorticoids in response to ACTH |
| **Adrenal medulla** | Releases epinephrine and norepinephrine under sympathetic control |
| **Cytokines** | Peptide hormones from many cells causing inflammation and fever; raised by stress |
| **Subordination stress** | Stress from enduring conspecific threat in a dominance hierarchy |
| **Bullying** | The human form of chronic social threat |
| **Psychosomatic disorders** | Medical disorders in which psychological factors play a causal role |
| **Gastric ulcers** | Painful lesions to the lining of the stomach and duodenum |
| **Psychoneuroimmunology** | Study of interactions among psychological factors, the nervous system and the immune system |
| **Behavioural immune system** | Avoiding sick individuals and priming immune responses on seeing signs of infection |
| **Immune system** | The innate plus the adaptive immune systems |
| **Innate immune system** | Fast, general first immune response near points of pathogen entry |
| **Pathogens** | Disease-causing agents |

| **Leukocytes** | White blood cells |
| **Phagocytes / phagocytosis** | Cells that engulf and destroy pathogens / that process; among the first immune reactions to evolve |
| **Microglia** | Phagocytes of the central nervous system |
