# Chapter 14: Sleep, Dreaming, and Circadian Rhythms

**What this chapter is really about.** Most of us will sleep for well over **175,000 hours** in our lifetimes, more time than we give to eating and sex combined, and almost everybody assumes that this enormous commitment must mean we *need* all of it. The chapter is built around one deceptively simple question that students ask every year: **how much sleep do we need?** Pinel and Barnes set up two families of answer and then test them against everything that is known about sleep. **Recuperation theories** say that being awake wears something down (energy, a clean extracellular environment, synaptic plasticity) and that sleep exists to repair it, so the more you are awake, the more you must sleep. **Adaptation theories** say that sleep is driven mainly by an internal 24-hour clock that evolved to keep us out of trouble at night, so the timing of sleep is what is programmed, and the amount is more elastic than we think. Almost every experiment in the chapter can be read as a point scored for one side or the other, and by the end the authors have moved from a non-committal "it could be either" to a definite, provocative conclusion: most of us sleep more than we need.

Do not read this as a purely academic quarrel. The question decides how you interpret a sleep-deprived student's bad mood, whether you believe a patient who says he sleeps four hours a night, whether a doctor should prescribe a sleeping pill, and whether the advice "everyone needs eight hours" is evidence-based at all. The chapter's answer to the last question is no.

**How to read this chapter.** The 11th edition prints nine modules without numbers; they are numbered 14.1 to 14.9 here in the book's order. 14.1 gives you the measurement vocabulary (EEG, EOG, EMG, the three EEG stages, REM versus NREM) that every later module assumes. 14.2 is dreaming: what was believed, what was tested, and three theories of why we dream. 14.3 states the two kinds of sleep theory and the comparative (cross-species) evidence. 14.4 tests the theories against sleep deprivation and introduces the single most important idea in the chapter, that deprived sleepers become **more efficient** sleepers. 14.5 is the circadian clock, from free-running rhythms down to genes. 14.6 covers the four brain areas that produce or reduce sleep. 14.7 and 14.8 are the applied half: drugs and disorders. 14.9 returns to the opening question with long-term sleep-reduction studies and a personal experiment by one of the authors. As you read each finding, ask one question: does this fit a recuperation account or an adaptation account better?

## Opening: How Much Do You Need to Sleep?

Every time the authors lecture on sleep, somebody asks how much sleep we need, and every time they give the same unsatisfying answer: there are two fundamentally different answers and neither is a clear winner. The first stresses the presumed health-promoting and recuperative powers of sleep and says people need as much as they can comfortably get, the usual prescription being **at least 8 hours** a night. The second says that many of us sleep more than we need and are consequently sleeping part of our lives away. The arithmetic behind the second answer is worth having to hand: sleeping 5 hours a night instead of 8 gives you an extra **21 waking hours each week**, or **10,952 hours each decade**.

While preparing the chapter, Pinel (writing as JP) decided to test the idea on himself. He resolved to sleep no more than **5 hours a night, 11:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m.**, until the chapter was written. His first report comes at 4:50 on a Saturday morning: there had been a party, he had not made it to bed by 11:00, he had slept only **2 hours and 15 minutes**, and he felt quite good. He promises to report back at the end of the chapter, and he does (14.9). Hold the experiment in mind as a thread; the chapter is partly the story of whether it worked.

### The Case of the Woman Who Wouldn't Sleep

The opening case comes from **Meddis (1977)** and is meant to unsettle several common beliefs before any theory is introduced.

**Miss M.** usually slept only **1 hour per night**. She was retired but busy, painting, writing and volunteering in the community. She became tired *physically* but never reported feeling *sleepy*. During the night she sat on her bed reading, writing, crocheting or painting. At about 2:00 a.m. she would often fall asleep **without any preceding drowsiness**, and when she woke about an hour later she felt wide awake.

Testing her in a sleep laboratory was awkward at first. Miss M. explained that she never slept when there was something interesting to do, and a laboratory with company available all night counted as very interesting. So for two nights running she stayed up talking, while the researchers worked in shifts, one keeping her company as the others rested. Only on the **third night**, once she had agreed to make an effort, did she sleep, and the recording showed nothing unusual except the duration: she woke after **99 minutes** and could not get back to sleep.

The case challenges three beliefs: that everybody needs something close to 8 hours; that short sleep must bring sleepiness and impairment (Miss M. was active and never sleepy); and that sleep is a fixed physiological demand (she simply did not sleep when something more interesting was available). The last point returns in 14.9 as the claim that sleep has a high **positive-incentive value**, the concept that organised Chapter 12 on eating.

> **Trap.** Two numbers attach to Miss M. and are easily swapped. Her *usual* nightly sleep was **1 hour**; the sleep she produced on the **third laboratory night** lasted **99 minutes**. The source is **Meddis (1977)**, not Dement or Kleitman.

## 14.1 Stages of Sleep

Many changes occur in the body during sleep. This module introduces the three standard measures of those changes and how they shift across a night.

### Three standard psychophysiological measures of sleep

Start with the brain. Over a night, the human **electroencephalogram (EEG)**, the record of the brain's electrical activity picked up by electrodes on the scalp, changes a great deal. EEG waves during sleep are generally **high-voltage and slow**, but there are periods scattered through the night dominated by **low-voltage, fast** waves similar to those of a person who is awake. Two descriptive terms are worth fixing now, because the whole chapter uses them: *voltage* (or *amplitude*) is the height of the wave, and *frequency* is how many waves occur per second, measured in hertz (Hz). "High-voltage, slow" means tall waves, few per second; "low-voltage, fast" means small waves, many per second.

Two discoveries turned the EEG into a three-channel system:

1. In the **1950s** it was discovered that **rapid eye movements (REMs)** occur under the closed eyelids of sleepers during exactly those periods of low-voltage, fast EEG. Eye movements are recorded with the **electrooculogram (EOG)**, electrodes placed around the eyes.
2. In **1962, Berger and Oswald** discovered that during the same periods there is also a **loss of electromyographic activity in the neck muscles**. Muscle activity is recorded with the **electromyogram (EMG)**; the neck is used because it reflects the tone of the core (trunk) muscles.

From then on, the **EEG, the EOG and the neck EMG** became the **three standard psychophysiological bases for defining the stages of sleep**. Figure 14.1 shows a volunteer wired for exactly this.

A practical problem follows. A participant's first night in a sleep laboratory is often fitful, so the usual practice is to have each participant sleep **several nights** in the laboratory before the study proper begins. The disturbance of sleep on the first night in a laboratory is called the **first-night phenomenon**. Pinel notes that the term is well known to graders of introductory psychology examinations because of the creative definitions offered by students who forget that it is a sleep-related, rather than a sex-related, phenomenon.

### Three stages of sleep EEG

According to the **American Academy of Sleep Medicine**, there are **three stages of sleep EEG: stage 1, stage 2 and stage 3** (Grigg-Damberger, 2012). Build them up in the order a sleeper passes through them (Figure 14.2):

- **Alert wakefulness.** Low-voltage, high-frequency EEG.
- **Just before sleep.** After the eyes are shut and a person prepares to go to sleep, **alpha waves**, waxing and waning bursts of **8- to 12-Hz** waves, begin to punctuate the low-voltage, high-frequency waves of alert wakefulness.
- **Stage 1.** As the person falls asleep there is a **sudden transition** to stage 1 sleep EEG: a low-voltage, high-frequency signal **similar to, but slower than**, that of alert wakefulness.
- **Stage 2.** From stage 1 through stages 2 and 3 there is a **gradual increase in voltage and decrease in frequency**. The stage 2 EEG has slightly higher amplitude and lower frequency than stage 1, and it is punctuated by **two characteristic waveforms**:
  - **K complexes**: each is a single large **negative** wave (an **upward** deflection) followed immediately by a single large **positive** wave (a **downward** deflection) (Mak-McCully et al., 2014).
  - **Sleep spindles**: each is a **0.5- to 2-second** waxing and waning burst of **11- to 15-Hz** waves (Purcell et al., 2017).
- **Stage 3.** Defined by a **predominance of delta waves**, the **largest and slowest** EEG waves, with a frequency of **1 to 2 Hz**.

The logic of the progression is a single dial: as sleep deepens, the EEG gets taller and slower. Stage 2 is the only stage with named transient events, so if a question names K complexes or spindles, the answer is stage 2.

> **Trap.** **Three stages, not four.** The 11th edition follows the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and scores **three** NREM EEG stages. Older editions of Pinel (the 8th edition, for example) and the older Rechtschaffen and Kales (1968) scoring manual use **four**: they divide slow-wave sleep into **stage 3** (delta waves present but not dominant, conventionally 20 to 50 percent of the record) and **stage 4** (delta waves predominant, over 50 percent). Modern **NREM 3 is simply old stages 3 and 4 merged**. Translate accordingly: an old key's "stage 4 is the deepest sleep" or "sleepwalking occurs in stages 3 and 4" means **NREM 3 / slow-wave sleep** in this edition; an old key's "slow-wave sleep = stages 3 + 4" means **NREM 3**. If a question is explicitly framed on the current system, any option mentioning "stage 4" is wrong, because stage 4 no longer exists.

> **Trap.** The sleep-spindle numbers changed between editions. The 11th edition gives **0.5 to 2 seconds** and **11 to 15 Hz**; the 8th edition gave **1 to 2 seconds** and **12 to 14 Hz**. Both put spindles in the low teens of hertz, well above delta (1 to 2 Hz) and above alpha (8 to 12 Hz). Choose whichever range the options offer, and never confuse spindles (stage 2) with alpha (pre-sleep relaxation with eyes closed).

> **Trap.** In a K complex the **negative** wave is drawn as an **upward** deflection and the positive wave as a **downward** deflection. This is the electrophysiologists' convention, not a misprint. The order is negative first, then positive.

### The sleep cycle across a night: initial versus emergent stage 1

Once sleepers reach stage 3, they stay there for a time and then **retreat back through the stages to stage 1**. The critical point is that when they return to stage 1, things are not at all the same as the first time through:

- The **first** period of stage 1 EEG in a night, **initial stage 1 EEG**, is **not** marked by any striking EMG or EOG changes.
- **Subsequent** periods of stage 1 EEG, **emergent stage 1 EEG**, are accompanied by **rapid eye movements** and by a **loss of tone in the muscles of the body core**.

After the first cycle (initial stage 1 down to stage 3 and back up to emergent stage 1), the rest of the night is spent going **back and forth through the stages**. Figure 14.3 shows a typical night and the close relationship between emergent stage 1, REMs and the loss of core-muscle tone. Three regularities are visible in it and all three are examinable:

1. Each cycle tends to be about **90 minutes** long.
2. As the night progresses, **more and more** time is spent in emergent stage 1 sleep and **less and less** in the other stages, **particularly stage 3**. Deep slow-wave sleep is concentrated early in the night; REM periods lengthen towards morning.
3. There are **brief periods of wakefulness** during the night that the sleeper usually does not remember in the morning.

> **Trap.** "Stage 1 sleep" is not the same thing as "REM sleep". Only **emergent** stage 1 is REM sleep. **Initial** stage 1, the stage you pass through when you first fall asleep, has no REMs and no loss of core-muscle tone, and is classified as NREM 1. A statement such as "REM sleep is the first stage of sleep each night" is false.

### REM, NREM and slow-wave sleep: getting the terms straight

Now pin the vocabulary down, because exam options are built from it.

- The sleep associated with **emergent stage 1 EEG** is called **REM sleep** (pronounced "rehm"), after the associated rapid eye movements.
- **All other stages** of sleep together are called **NREM sleep** (non-REM sleep). Accordingly, **initial stage 1, stage 2 and stage 3** are sometimes called **NREM 1, NREM 2 and NREM 3** (Grigg-Damberger, 2012).
- **NREM 3** is often called **slow-wave sleep (SWS)**, after the delta waves that characterise it.

Table 14.1 is the book's own summary of these equivalences:

| EEG stage | Label | Broader category |
|---|---|---|
| Initial stage 1 | NREM 1 | **NREM sleep** |
| Stage 2 | NREM 2 | **NREM sleep** |
| Stage 3 | NREM 3, also called **slow-wave sleep** | **NREM sleep** |
| Emergent stage 1 | **REM sleep** | REM sleep (a category of its own) |

> **Trap.** Many older sources and question banks use the older label **paradoxical sleep** for REM sleep, because the EEG looks like waking while the core muscles are paralysed. If an option says "paradoxical sleep", read it as REM sleep. Equally, "slow-wave sleep" in this edition means **NREM 3 only**; in four-stage keys it meant stages 3 and 4.

### Other physiological correlates of REM sleep

REMs, loss of core-muscle tone and a low-amplitude, high-frequency EEG are not the only physiological correlates of REM sleep. Four more complete the picture:

- **Cerebral activity** (oxygen consumption, blood flow and neural firing) **increases to waking levels** in many brain structures.
- There is a general **increase in the variability of autonomic nervous system activity** (blood pressure, pulse and respiration).
- The muscles of the **extremities occasionally twitch**, even though the core muscles are atonic.
- There is almost always some degree of **penile or clitoral erection**.

Put this together and REM sleep is a strange state: a brain about as active as a waking brain, an autonomic system swinging about, eyes darting, limbs twitching, and a body core that is limp. That combination is exactly what made researchers in the 1950s suspect that REM sleep was emotion-charged, and that suspicion launched the study of dreaming in the next module.

## 14.2 Dreaming

Barnes (writing as SB) notes that few topics provoke more classroom discussion than dreaming, because people hold so many beliefs about it. The module tests those beliefs and surveys current theory, from the REM-dream link to the brain areas involved.

### Discovery of the relationship between REM sleep and dreaming

In **1953** REM sleep was a brand-new discovery, and **Nathaniel Kleitman's** laboratory was buzzing with the question of what it meant. Leave aside the drop in core-muscle tone and everything about a REM episode looked like the signature of an **emotion-charged** state: the brain was active, autonomic activity fluctuated and the eyes darted about. That pattern made the next step obvious to Kleitman and his students, who set out to test whether REM sleep was the **physiological correlate of dreaming**.

The support was striking:

> **Key finding, Kleitman's laboratory (1950s).** **80 percent** of awakenings from REM sleep, but only **7 percent** of awakenings from NREM sleep, led to dream recall. Dreams recalled from NREM sleep tended to be **isolated experiences** ("I was falling"); dreams recalled from REM sleep tended to take the form of **stories, or narratives**.

That ratio gave researchers an objective signal of *when* a person was probably dreaming, so beliefs about dreams could at last be tested by waking people at known moments.

### Testing common beliefs about dreaming

The REM-dream relationship gave Kleitman's group the opportunity to test common beliefs. Five were among the first addressed. For each, note the belief, the test and the verdict.

**Belief 1: external stimuli can be incorporated into dreams.** True, with qualifications.

> **Key study, Dement & Wolpert (1958).** Water was sprayed on sleeping volunteers after they had been in REM sleep for a few minutes, and they were awakened a few seconds later. In **14 of 33 cases** (about 42 percent) the water was incorporated into the dream report.

One participant, dreaming that he was acting in a play, reported that the leading lady collapsed with water dripping on her, water dripped on his own back and head, the roof was leaking, and he dragged her aside and began pulling the curtains before he woke. The water arrived as water, but the dreaming brain wove it into the ongoing story.

We now know that some external stimuli are more likely to be incorporated than others (Broughton, 1982; Schredl et al., 2009). Water droplets happen to be one of them. The stimulus **most readily incorporated** into a dream is the **feeling of pressure on a limb** (Schredl et al., 2009).

**Belief 2: dreams last only an instant.** False. Dreams run at roughly real time, and probably a little slower.

> **Key study, Dement & Kleitman (1957).** Participants were awakened either **5 or 15 minutes** after the beginning of a REM episode and asked to judge, from the duration of the events in their dreams, whether they had been dreaming for 5 or 15 minutes. They were correct in **92 of 111 cases** (about 83 percent).

If dreams were instantaneous flashes, people could not tell a 5-minute dream from a 15-minute one. Current research refines the conclusion: dreams run **slightly slower than real time**; a physical movement made in a dream can take **up to 40 percent longer** than the same movement made awake (Erlacher et al., 2014).

**Belief 3: some people do not dream.** Mostly false, but the recent evidence is mixed. Studies in the **1950s** showed that people who claimed never to dream had **just as much REM sleep** as normal dreamers, and most reported dreams if they were awakened during REM episodes (Goodenough et al., 1959), although they did so less frequently than normal dreamers. The non-dreamers were, on this evidence, non-*recallers*. More recent studies are mixed: some have found that such individuals do not appear to dream (Pagel, 2003), whereas others are more consistent with the earlier findings (Herlin et al., 2015).

**Belief 4: penile erections indicate dreams with sexual content.** False. Erections are **no more complete** during dreams with frank sexual content than during dreams without it (**Karacan et al., 1966**). Even **babies** have REM-related penile erections (Fanni et al., 2018). The erection is a physiological feature of REM sleep (you met it in 14.1), not a read-out of what the dream is about.

**Belief 5: sleeptalking and sleepwalking occur during REM sleep and are associated with dreaming.** False (Alfonsi et al., 2019).

- **Sleeptalking (somniloquy)** has **no special association with REM sleep**. It can occur during **any stage**, but often occurs during the **transition to wakefulness**.
- **Sleepwalking (somnambulism)** usually occurs during **slow-wave sleep**, and it **never occurs during REM sleep**, when the core muscles tend to be totally relaxed (Castelnovo et al., 2018; Januszko et al., 2015).

> **Trap.** **Sleepwalking: slow-wave sleep, never REM.** Older keys say "stage 4" or "stages 3 and 4"; in this edition that is **NREM 3**. **Sleeptalking: any stage**, often at the transition to waking. An option that pairs somnambulism with REM, or claims sleeptalking is confined to one stage, is wrong.

> **Trap.** Older editions concluded simply that "dreams run in real time". The 11th edition keeps the Dement and Kleitman (1957) result but adds that dreams probably run **slightly slower** than real time (movements up to **40 percent** longer; Erlacher et al., 2014). "Dreams happen in an instant" is wrong under both.

### Does REM sleep equal dreaming?

Some researchers still equate REM sleep with dreaming, but three key discoveries have complicated that equation:

1. **Dreaming occurs during NREM sleep.** This is now well established (Siclari & Tononi, 2017).
2. **Many NREM dreams are comparable in quality to REM dreams** (McNamara et al., 2010). This is especially true of NREM dreams **later in the night**: they are hallucinatory and have a storyline, just like REM dreams (Siclari & Tononi, 2017).
3. **REM sleep and dreaming can be dissociated** (Siegel, 2011), and the dissociation runs in both directions:
   - **Antidepressants** greatly reduce or abolish REM sleep **without affecting aspects of dream recall** (Oudiette et al., 2012). REM gone, dreaming present.
   - **Specific cortical lesions** can abolish dreaming **without affecting REM sleep** (Nir & Tononi, 2010). Dreaming gone, REM present.

The third point carries the argument. When each of two things can be removed while the other survives, you have a **double dissociation**, strong evidence that they depend on at least partly separate mechanisms: REM sleep is generated in the brain stem (14.6), dreaming depends on particular cortical areas (below). They usually co-occur, which is why the 80-versus-7 percent correlation was so striking, but they are not the same process.

### Lucid dreaming

Have you ever been aware that you were dreaming, and taken control of the dream? **Lucid dreaming** is the ability to be **consciously aware that one is dreaming** and, in some cases, to **control the content** of the dream (LaBerge, LaMarca, & Baird, 2018; Ribeiro, Gounden, & Quaglino, 2016). Barnes reports that he experiences lucid dreams from time to time.

For a long time, sleep researchers believed lucid dreaming was **impossible** (Baird, Mota-Rolim, & Dresler, 2019). In the **1980s, LaBerge** conducted a series of studies that left little doubt that it was a real phenomenon (Schredl, 2018). Earlier surveys had already indicated that many people have **at least one** lucid dream in their lives, some have them **regularly**, and a select few have them **every night** (Ribeiro, Gounden, & Quaglino, 2016; Saunders et al., 2016).

How do you prove that a sleeping person knows, *at that moment*, that they are dreaming? A report on waking could be confabulated. **LaBerge's technique** solved this. Self-proclaimed lucid dreamers slept in his laboratory, instructed beforehand to make a **specific bodily movement** in their dream when given a **specific signal**, for example **repeated left-right-left-right eye movements** when they saw **flashes of light on their eyelids** (Baird, Mota-Rolim, & Dresler, 2019; LaBerge, Baird, & Zimbardo, 2018). The design works because in REM sleep the core muscles are atonic but the **eyes are free to move**, so a pre-agreed eye-movement pattern appears on the EOG as an objective, timed signal sent from inside the dream.

#### The Case of the Levitating Teenager

Barnes describes a recurring teenage dream. He sits in an otherwise empty movie theatre between two people who argue loudly across him about the film's title (*Ferris Bueller's Day Off* or *The Breakfast Club*) until, exasperated, he yells "shut up!" He often woke at that point, sometimes **on a bus**, turning bright red at having told the whole bus to shut up.

Then one night he had the same dream, but this time, when the argument began, he **suddenly realised he was in a dream** and decided to take control. He slowly **levitated** from his seat, floated high above his two nemeses, smirked, leaned back and enjoyed the film.

Lucidity arrived when he recognised a familiar, recurring script, and what he then controlled was the dream's content, the "in some cases" part of the definition. His shouted "shut up!" on waking also fits the book's point that sleeptalking often occurs at the transition to wakefulness.

#### The Case of the Artistic Dreamer

**M.S.**, a guest at a dinner party, said they always dreamed lucidly. A sleep researcher who was present doubted the claim and invited M.S. to spend a few nights in her laboratory to test it. M.S. was fitted with **EEG electrodes on the scalp, EOG electrodes around the eyes and EMG electrodes on the arm**, and told that if a flashing light appeared in the dream, the signal was to **draw a star with the right arm**. M.S. joked that the researcher could have picked something more interesting to draw, then went to sleep.

Roughly **an hour and a half** later, the recordings showed REM sleep (low-amplitude, high-frequency EEG with rapid eye movements). The researcher then flicked a flashlight on and off at M.S.'s eyes while monitoring the arm EMG. The arm never visibly moved (**no overt movement**), yet the EMG recorded a sequence of muscle contractions **matching the pattern of drawing a star**, and that record turned the researcher into a believer in lucid dreams.

Why this convinces: the signal came at a moment chosen by the experimenter during objectively verified REM sleep; the response was an arbitrary act agreed in advance; and it appeared as patterned EMG activity without visible movement, as expected if the dreamer "moved" in the dream while the body stayed largely immobile. The ninety-minute wait also fits the roughly 90-minute sleep cycle.

### Inducing lucid dreams, and why they may be a mixed blessing

Several methods have been shown to **increase the likelihood** of a lucid dream (Bazzari, 2018):

- **Transcranial electrical stimulation** applied to the **frontal and temporal** areas of the skull (Bray, 2014; Voss et al., 2014).
- Certain **cognitive training techniques** (LaBerge, LaMarca, & Baird, 2018).
- Administration of **acetylcholine agonists** (LaBerge, LaMarca, & Baird, 2018).

Most techniques promoted on websites and in popular books, the authors warn, are ineffective or dangerous; they recommend *Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming* by **Stephen LaBerge**, the field's most respected researcher.

One would expect everybody to want to be a lucid dreamer. The next case shows why it can be a mixed blessing.

#### The Case of the Bored Lucid Dreamer

Barnes told a class how wonderful his lucid dreams were and said, "I wish I could lucid dream every night." From the back row, a student, **G.D.**, spoke for the first time that term: "Careful what you wish for." She had lucid dreamed **every night for as long as she could remember** and found her rare non-lucid dreams wonderful. It was like knowing the plot of every movie before watching it; for her, lucid dreaming was "about as boring as a lecture".

The case points to a real property of lucid dreams. Another reason they might be considered boring is that they tend to be **less bizarre** than non-lucid dreams (Yu & Shen, 2020). Concerns have also been raised that lucid dreaming might have a **negative impact on sleep quality** (Soffer-Dudek, 2020), although this is disputed (Schredl, Dyck, & Kühnel, 2020).

For researchers, lucid dreamers are a boon. Because they are aware within their dreams and **better able to recall** them on waking (Aspy et al., 2017), they are particularly well equipped to describe the sensory qualities of dreams. Two findings from **Kahan and LaBerge (2011)**:

- **Gustatory, olfactory and somatosensory** experiences are **uncommon** in their dreams.
- They are **unable to perceive fine-grained visual details** in their dreams.

As for the brain changes that accompany lucid dreaming, only **two studies** have examined them, one **case study** (Dresler et al., 2012) and one **quasi-experimental** study (Baird et al., 2018), and there was **little consistency** in their results (Baird, Mota-Rolim, & Dresler, 2019). This is an open question, and an answer that claims a known "lucid-dreaming area" goes beyond the evidence.

### Why do we dream what we do?

Most of us are fascinated by the content of our dreams, and people often seek out self-proclaimed dream interpreters. Is it possible to interpret dreams?

**Sigmund Freud** believed so (Freud, 1913, in the book's citation). Freud held that dreams are triggered by **unacceptable repressed wishes**, often of a **sexual** nature. Because these wishes are unacceptable, the dreams we experience, our **manifest dreams**, are merely **disguised versions** of our real dreams, our **latent dreams**. He hypothesised an **unconscious censor** that disguises and subtracts information from our real dreams so that we can endure them. Freud therefore concluded that one key to understanding people and treating their psychological problems is to expose the meaning of their latent dreams through the **interpretation of their manifest dreams**.

There is **no convincing scientific evidence** for the theory, and the **brain science of the 1890s** on which it rested is obsolete. Many people still believe that dreams express repressed wishes, but dream *interpretation* is no longer a focus of research; biopsychologists ask instead why we dream the *content* we do.

There is a methodological obstacle. To study dream content, a researcher must rely on the **detailed self-reports** of dreamers. Some question whether such reports can be trusted, since dream recall is often difficult or impossible for many people (Mangiaruga et al., 2018; Nir & Tononi, 2010). Others argue that self-reports are a legitimate window into dream content (Cipolli et al., 2017; Windt, 2013).

Despite these acknowledged limitations, studies of dream content have flourished, and **two key findings** have emerged:

1. Dream content is influenced by what we have experienced in the **prior period of wakefulness** (Cipolli et al., 2017; Fogel et al., 2018). Even the most mundane experiences, such as **taking out the garbage**, can enter a subsequent dream (Vallat et al., 2017).
2. The amount of **anxiety** experienced before a bout of dreaming affects the **emotional content** of dreams (Sikka, Pesonen, & Revonsuo, 2018; Vallat et al., 2017).

> **Trap.** **Manifest** content is the dream as experienced and remembered; **latent** content is the hidden "real" dream that, on Freud's view, the **unconscious censor** disguises. Do not reverse them. The book cites **Freud (1913)**, the date of the English translation it uses; the original *Interpretation of Dreams* appeared in **1900** (dated 1899 by some sources), and Indian papers usually ask for 1900.

### Why do we dream? Three theories

Rather than asking why we dream *what* we dream, many researchers ask why we dream *at all*. The book presents three of the most prominent theories.

**Hobson's activation-synthesis hypothesis (Hobson, 1989).** During sleep, many **brain-stem circuits become active** and **bombard the cerebral cortex** with neural signals (Palagini & Rosenlicht, 2011). The essence of the hypothesis is that the information supplied to the cortex during sleep is **largely random**, and the dream is the **cortex's effort to make sense** of these random signals. The book's analogy: staring up at clouds and seeing faces or figures in them. The clouds are randomly patterned, but your brain does its best to make sense of the pattern. On this view dreams are, in essence, **leftovers from a brain built for a day job**, and they serve **no particular function**.

**Revonsuo's evolutionary theory of dreams (Revonsuo, 2000).** Dreams serve an important **biological function** with implications for **fitness** in the Darwinian sense. Specifically, we dream to **simulate threatening events**, such as physical attack, threats to social relationships or threats to one's livelihood, and such simulation allows us to **better predict and respond to such threats when awake** (Gauchat et al., 2015). The theory is widely known as the threat-simulation theory.

**Hobson's protoconsciousness hypothesis (Hobson, 2009).** In the years after proposing activation-synthesis, Hobson came to believe that dreaming has a purpose and became increasingly critical of his own earlier hypothesis. Like Revonsuo, he now held that dreaming confers an **evolutionary advantage**. Unlike Revonsuo, he proposed that it does so by **simulating everything**, not just threats. Dreaming is important:

1. **During early development**, when sensory input is limited by underdeveloped sensory systems, the **visual system** in particular; and
2. **Throughout life**, by **anticipating and predicting** how events will unfold while awake.

In short, dreaming is a **training mechanism**, each dream a **virtual real-life scenario** (Boag, 2017; Hobson & Friston, 2012; Llewellyn, 2016; Scarpelli et al., 2019). Hence the term **protoconsciousness**: a virtual prototype of our conscious experiences.

The authors use Hobson's reversal as a lesson in critical thinking: to think critically, one must be as critical of one's own theories as of anybody else's.

| Theory | Proposer (year) | Do dreams have a function? | What dreams are |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Activation-synthesis** | Hobson (1989) | **No** | The cortex's attempt to make sense of random brain-stem signals |
| **Evolutionary (threat simulation)** | Revonsuo (2000) | **Yes** | Simulations of **threats**, rehearsing responses |
| **Protoconsciousness** | Hobson (2009) | **Yes** | Simulations of **everything**; training in development and prediction throughout life |

> **Trap.** **Both activation-synthesis and protoconsciousness are Hobson's**, and they contradict each other: the first (1989 in this book) says dreams have no function; the second (2009) says dreaming is a training simulation of everything. **Revonsuo (2000)** simulates **threats only**. Many older keys date activation-synthesis to **Hobson and McCarley (1977)**, the original paper; this edition cites **Hobson (1989)**. Accept either date if only one appears among the options.

### The dreaming brain

Studies of patients with brain lesions and neuroimaging studies of dreaming individuals tell a clear story (Figure 14.4).

**Lesion evidence.** Two types of brain lesion produce a **cessation of dreaming** (as determined by self-report, by laboratory sleep studies, or both) (Domhoff & Fox, 2015; Vallat et al., 2018):

1. **Bilateral lesions of the temporo-parieto junction**, the place in the cerebral cortex where the temporal and parietal lobes meet.
2. **Bilateral lesions of the medial prefrontal cortex.**

In addition, lesions of the areas of **secondary visual cortex in the medial occipital lobe** lead to a **loss of visual imagery in dreams**, not to a loss of dreaming as such.

**Imaging evidence.** Many fMRI studies of healthy participants in REM sleep converge with the lesion work: the **temporo-parieto junction, medial prefrontal cortex and medial occipital cortex** all display **increased neural activity during REM sleep** (Domhoff & Fox, 2015).

**NREM dreaming.** Whether NREM dreams involve comparable patterns of activation remains to be seen (Mutz & Javadi, 2017). The authors know of only **one** study of the neuroanatomical correlates of NREM dreaming: **Siclari et al. (2017)** showed that activity in the **temporo-parieto junction** was associated with **both REM and NREM dreaming**, suggesting that this region is **critical for dreaming in general**.

The logic is converging methods: lesions show an area is *necessary*, imaging shows it is *active* when dreaming occurs, and agreement between the two is much stronger than either alone. The lesion data also close the dissociation argument: cortical lesions that abolish dreaming leave REM sleep, generated elsewhere, intact.

## 14.3 Why Do We Sleep, and Why Do We Sleep When We Do?

With the properties of sleep and dreaming in place, the chapter turns to two fundamental questions: **why do we sleep**, and **why do we sleep when we do**? The two kinds of theory introduced here frame the rest of the chapter.

### Two kinds of theories of sleep: recuperation and adaptation

**Recuperation theories of sleep** hold that **being awake disrupts the homeostasis** (internal physiological stability) of the body in some way, and **sleep is required to restore it**. They differ in the disruption they propose. The three most common say that sleep serves to:

1. **restore energy levels** that decline during wakefulness (Porkka-Heiskanen, 2013);
2. **clear toxins** that accumulate during wakefulness, such as **beta-amyloid** (the protein of Alzheimer's plaques, Chapter 10) (Da Mesquita et al., 2018; Holth et al., 2019); or
3. **restore synaptic plasticity** that might dissipate during wakefulness (Cirelli & Tononi, 2015; Tononi & Cirelli, 2019).

Whatever the function proposed, all recuperation theories imply that **sleepiness is triggered by a deviation from homeostasis caused by wakefulness** and that **sleep ends when homeostasis returns**. This is the same deficit-and-repair logic that Chapter 12 examined for hunger.

**Adaptation theories of sleep** hold that sleep is **not a reaction to the disruptive effects of being awake** but the result of an **internal 24-hour timing mechanism**: we are programmed to sleep at night **regardless of what happens to us during the day**. They focus more on **when** we sleep than on its function, and some even propose that sleep plays **no role in the efficient physiological functioning** of the body. On these theories, early humans finished their eating, drinking and reproducing in daylight, and a strong motivation to sleep at night evolved to **conserve energy**, to make them **less susceptible to mishaps in the dark** such as predation (Joiner, 2016), and to carry out certain **brain functions not possible during wakefulness** (Frank, 2017; Stickgold, 2015). The book's analogy is the key: sleep is like **reproductive behaviour**, in that we are **highly motivated to engage in it, but we do not need it to stay healthy**.

The two are not mutually exclusive. A theory might say we **evolved to sleep at night** but that the **duration** of sleep is set by **recuperative mechanisms** (Eban-Rothschild, Giardino, & de Lecea, 2017; Yadav et al., 2017).

| Question | Recuperation theories | Adaptation theories |
|---|---|---|
| **Why do we sleep?** | To restore what wakefulness depletes (energy, toxin clearance, synaptic plasticity) | Because we evolved a strong motivation to; large amounts may not be needed |
| **Why when we do?** | When the deficit is large enough; sleep ends when homeostasis returns | An internal 24-hour clock programmes night-time sleep, for safety and energy conservation |
| **Prediction for deprivation** | Disturbances that worsen, and lost sleep regained | Effects tied to time of day; little need to repay lost sleep |
| **Analogy** | Refuelling | Reproductive behaviour |

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel call the adaptation theories **circadian theories** of sleep, and some question banks still use that name. Note also that the recent **toxin-clearance (beta-amyloid)** and **synaptic-plasticity** proposals are **recuperation** theories: the label refers to the deficit-repair logic, not to the age of the idea.

### Comparative analysis of sleep

Sleep has been studied in only a small number of species, but the evidence suggests that **all mammals and most birds sleep** (Manger & Siegel, 2020), with **high-amplitude, low-frequency EEG** punctuated by periods of **low-amplitude, high-frequency** waves, as in humans. The evidence for sleep in **amphibians, reptiles, fish and insects** is less clear: some show periods of inactivity and unresponsiveness, but their relation to mammalian sleep **has not been established** (Siegel, 2008; Zimmerman et al., 2008). The book's journal prompt (how would you *define* sleep across organisms?) exposes why: an EEG definition excludes animals without a mammal-like EEG, while a behavioural definition casts a much wider net.

### Four conclusions from the comparative evidence

**First: sleep probably serves some important physiological function**, rather than merely protecting animals from mishap and conserving energy (Siegel, 2012). The evidence is strongest in species at **increased risk of predation when asleep** (for example, antelopes) and in species that have evolved **complex mechanisms that enable them to sleep**: some **birds and marine mammals sleep with only half of their brain at a time**, so that the other half can control resurfacing for air (Lyamin et al., 2018; Mascetti, 2016), usually called unihemispheric sleep. It would run against the logic of natural selection for animals to risk predation to sleep, or to evolve elaborate machinery to sleep safely, unless sleep itself served some critical function. Note which way this cuts: a pure "sleep keeps you out of trouble" theory struggles, because sleep sometimes creates trouble.

**Second: the primary function of sleep is not some special, higher-order human function.** Because most mammals and birds sleep, suggestions that sleep helps humans **reprogram our complex brains** or permits an **emotional release** that maintains mental health are **improbable**.

**Third: sleep may be essential, but it is not necessarily needed in large quantities.** **Horses** and many other animals manage on **2 or 3 hours** a day. And sleep in the **wild** can differ substantially from sleep in **captivity**, where animals are usually studied: some that sleep a great deal in captivity **sleep little in the wild** when food is short or during **migration or mating** (Lesku et al., 2012; Siegel, 2012).

**Fourth: sleep time is predicted by ecology, not by energy.** Why do **cats** sleep about **14 hours** a day and **horses** only about **2**? Guided by recuperation theories, researchers looked at energy-related factors, but there is **no strong relationship** between a species' sleep time and its **level of activity, body size or body temperature** (Siegel, 2005). **Giant sloths sleep 20 hours** a day, a strong argument against sleep as compensation for energy expenditure, and energy expenditure has **little effect on subsequent sleep in humans** (Kelley & Kelley, 2017). **Adaptation theories correctly predict** that sleep time is related to **how vulnerable a species is while asleep** and **how much time it must spend feeding** and meeting other survival needs. **Zebras** graze almost continuously and are extremely vulnerable to predators: they sleep about **2 hours** a day. **African lions** often sleep more or less continuously for **2 or 3 days** after gorging on a kill (Figure 14.5), and they sleep anywhere they want.

> **Trap.** "Species that expend more energy sleep more" is the **recuperation** prediction, and it **fails** (**Siegel, 2005**; the **giant sloth**, 20 h). The successful predictors, **vulnerability while asleep** and **feeding time**, are **adaptation**-theory variables.

## 14.4 Effects of Sleep Deprivation

One way to identify the functions of sleep is to see what happens without it. The module opens with a caution about interpretation, states what recuperation theories predict, gives two classic cases, summarises human and animal research, and ends with the chapter's most important finding.

### Interpreting sleep-deprivation effects: the stress problem

After a short night you feel out of sorts and function less well. Such experiences are compelling but must be interpreted with caution. In Western cultures, **most people who sleep little or irregularly do so because they are under stress** (illness, excessive work, shift work, drugs, examinations), and stress has adverse effects **independent of sleep loss**. Even healthy laboratory volunteers often find the deprivation procedure itself stressful. Sleep loss and stress are therefore **confounded**: a student who sleeps four hours before an examination and feels terrible next day cannot tell whether the cause is the lost sleep or the examination anxiety that caused it.

News reports linking short sleep in overworked people to health and accident problems have value in identifying a **public health issue** (Chattu et al., 2018), but because low sleep is **hopelessly confounded with high stress** (Anafi, Kayser, & Raizen, 2019), most such studies tell us **little about the functions of sleep and how much we need**.

### What recuperation theories predict about sleep deprivation

Because recuperation theories treat sleep as a response to the accumulation of some debilitating effect of wakefulness, they make **three predictions**:

1. **Long periods of wakefulness will produce physiological and behavioural disturbances.**
2. **These disturbances will grow worse as deprivation continues.**
3. **After deprivation ends, much of the missed sleep will be regained.**

Score each result in this module against them. The pattern that emerges: the first is only partly confirmed, and the second and third largely fail.

### Two classic sleep-deprivation case studies

The groundbreaking study of sleep-deprived students (**Kleitman, 1963**) and the case of **Randy Gardner** (**Dement, 1978**).

#### The Case of the Sleep-Deprived Students

Most of Kleitman's students, deprived of all sleep, showed the same pattern. On the **first night** they read or studied easily until **after 3:00 a.m.**, when an attack of sleepiness forced their watchers to take care that they did not sleep. Next day they felt alert **as long as they were active**. On the **second night**, reading or studying was next to impossible, and again sleepiness became overpowering **after 3:00 a.m.**; yet later in the morning it eased, and they could work around the laboratory as long as they were standing and moving. The **third and fourth nights** resembled the second, with even more severe sleepiness. Surprisingly, **things did not grow worse after the fourth night**: those who persisted went through the same daily cycle.

Sleepiness did not climb steadily with hours awake; it **waxed and waned on a daily cycle**, and after the fourth night it **stopped getting worse**. Both features suit a circadian (adaptation) account, and the second contradicts prediction 2.

#### The Case of Randy Gardner

Randy Gardner and two classmates, entrusted with keeping him awake, planned to break the then world record of **260 hours** of continuous wakefulness. **Dement** joined the team to collect data, much to the comfort of Randy's parents. Randy complained vigorously when not allowed to close his eyes, but **in no sense was his behaviour disturbed**; near the end he held a **press conference** and conducted himself impeccably. He went to sleep exactly **264 hours and 12 minutes** after waking **11 days** before, slept only **14 hours** the first night, and then returned to his usual **8-hour** schedule.

This lack of substantial recovery sleep is **typical** of such cases. Rough arithmetic: eleven days awake cost him something like 80 to 90 hours of his usual sleep, and he repaid about 6 (14 minus 8). Prediction 3 fails badly; prediction 1 is weakly supported at best.

> **Trap.** Randy Gardner: **264 h 12 min (11 days)**, beating a record of **260 h**; slept **14 h**, then back to **8 h**; reported by **Dement (1978)**. The sleep-deprived **students** are **Kleitman (1963)**; do not confuse them with Kleitman's **1953** laboratory work on REM and dreaming.

### Studies of sleep deprivation in humans

Since the first studies by Dement and Kleitman in the mid-20th century, **hundreds** of studies have assessed schedules from slightly reduced sleep on one night to total deprivation for several nights, measuring sleepiness, mood, cognition, motor performance, physiology and even molecular function (Krause et al., 2017).

Even **moderate** deprivation, such as **sleeping 3 or 4 hours less than normal for one night**, has **three consistent effects**:

1. **Increased sleepiness**: people report being sleepier and **fall asleep faster** given the chance.
2. **Negative affect** on tests of mood.
3. **Poor performance on tests of sustained attention**, such as watching for a moving light on a computer screen (Kirszenblat & van Swinderen, 2015).

### Sleep deprivation and cognition: why executive function

Effects on **complex cognitive functions** have been **less consistent** (Basner et al., 2013), with **marked individual variability** from none to severe (Krause et al., 2017; Satterfield & Killgore, 2019), so researchers have preferred **simple, dull, monotonous** sustained-attention tasks, which are the most sensitive (Hudson, Van Dongen, & Honn, 2019). Even so, many studies show disruption of complex tasks.

The inconsistency was clarified by the discovery that **only some cognitive functions are susceptible**. Early studies used tests of **logical deduction or critical thinking**, which proved **largely immune**. Tests of **executive function** proved more susceptible. **Executive function** is a group of abilities including **problem solving, working memory, decision making, and assimilating new information to update plans and strategies** (Miller & Wallis, 2009).

The book adds a caution: the hypothesis **requires more systematic investigation**, because deficits in **vigilance and motivation** caused by sleep loss may be **mistaken for cognitive deficits** (Axelsson et al., 2019; Basner et al., 2013; Massar, Lim, & Huettel, 2019). For example, a sleepy participant who lapses for a moment, or stops trying on a dull trial, misses an item on a working-memory test and is scored as having a memory failure although memory itself may be intact.

> **Trap.** Sleep loss spares **logical deduction and critical thinking** but impairs **executive function**. Part of the apparent cognitive deficit may really be a **vigilance or motivation** deficit, which is why the book calls the conclusion provisional.

### Physical performance, physiology and susceptibility to infection

**Physical performance.** Given the general belief that good sleep is essential for motor performance, the effects have been **surprisingly inconsistent**: only a few measures tend to be affected (Patrick et al., 2017), even after lengthy deprivation, those effects may be unreliable (Knufinke et al., 2018), and the topic remains **controversial** (Vaara et al., 2018).

**Physiological consequences.** Deprivation produces **reduced body temperature, increased blood pressure, decreases in some aspects of immune function, hormonal changes and metabolic changes** (Cedernaes, Schiöth, & Benedict, 2015; Irwin, Olmstead, & Carroll, 2016). But there is **little evidence that these changes affect health or performance**. A decline in one immune measure need not increase susceptibility to infection, because the immune system is complex and other changes often compensate. That is why the authors commend a study that measured susceptibility directly:

> **Key study, Prather et al. (2015).** The researchers deliberately exposed **164** healthy volunteers to a **cold virus**. Short sleepers (**less than 6 hours** a night) were **no less likely** than the others to catch the infection; where they stood out was in going on to **develop an actual cold** more often.

Two cautions. The study is **correlational**: sleep duration was measured, not manipulated, so it cannot show that short sleep was the cause (short sleepers might differ in stress, the confound above). And the outcome that singled out the short sleepers was **developing a cold**, not infection. The suggestion of a causal link warrants further research.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition's infection study is **Prather et al. (2015): 164 volunteers, under 6 hours**, more likely to **develop a cold**, correlational. Older editions described an earlier, similar study (Cohen and colleagues, 2009, with 153 volunteers); do not mix the two sample sizes.

### Microsleeps and the puzzle of subtle effects

After **2 or 3 days** of continuous deprivation, most volunteers experience **microsleeps**, unless they are in a laboratory where these are interrupted as soon as they begin. **Microsleeps** are brief periods of sleep, typically **2 or 3 seconds** long, during which the **eyelids droop** and the person becomes **less responsive to external stimuli** while still **sitting or standing** (Toppi et al., 2016). They severely disrupt vigilance tests, but even sleep-deprived people without microsleeps show some vigilance problems.

Now compare deprivation of other motivated behaviours. Deprive people of **food** and starvation and death follow; deprive them of **sex** and reproductive capacity is lost entirely. Despite our powerful drive to sleep, the effects of sleep deprivation are **comparatively subtle, selective and variable** (Satterfield & Killgore, 2019), which is puzzling if sleep is a vital recuperative process. A second puzzle: deficits vanish easily. In one study, **4 hours of sleep eliminated the performance deficits produced by 64 hours of deprivation** (Rosa, Bonnet, & Warm, 2007).

### Sleep-deprivation studies of laboratory animals: the carousel apparatus

In the **carousel apparatus** (Figure 14.6), an **experimental rat** and its **yoked control** sit in separate chambers whose floors are partly formed by one disk. Whenever the **experimental rat's EEG** indicates sleep, the disk rotates slowly; if the experimental rat does not wake immediately, it is shoved off into a **shallow pool of water**. The yoked control receives **exactly the same rotations**, but if awake it can avoid a dunking by walking against the rotation. The **experimental rats typically died after about 12 days**, while the **yoked controls stayed reasonably healthy** (Rechtschaffen & Bergmann, 1995; apparatus after Rechtschaffen, Gilliland, Bergmann, & Winter, 1983).

The yoked control equates the **physical stimulation**, but not the **contingency**: the experimental rat is disturbed precisely whenever it falls asleep, whereas the control can sleep whenever the disk is still. The two rats differ in sleep, but they may also differ in stress. That is the critique. Humans and rats have been deprived **by other means** for similar periods **without dire consequences** (Rial et al., 2007; Siegel, 2009, 2012), so the carousel may kill **because it is stressful, not because it prevents sleep**. Post-mortem findings support this: **swollen adrenal glands, gastric ulcers and internal bleeding** (Geissmann, Beckwith, & Gilestro, 2019), the classic signature of chronic stress. The book concludes that sleep deprivation may be one phenomenon that **cannot be productively studied in nonhumans** because of the unavoidable confound of extreme stress (McEwen & Karatsoreos, 2015).

### REM-sleep deprivation

Because of its association with dreaming, REM sleep has been studied intensively, by **waking volunteers each time a REM bout begins**. REM-sleep deprivation has **two consistent effects** (Figure 14.7):

1. **REM rebound**: after deprivation, participants have **more than their usual amount of REM sleep** for the first **two or three nights** (Lyamin et al., 2018; McCarthy et al., 2016). In Figure 14.7, REM is about 20 percent of total sleep before deprivation and about 30 percent on the first night after, declining towards baseline.
2. **Increasing pressure to enter REM**: with each successive night, participants **initiate REM more readily** and must be awakened **more and more often** (McCarthy et al., 2016).

> **Key study, Webb & Agnew (1967).** On the **first night** of REM deprivation, participants had to be awakened **17 times**; by the **seventh night**, **67 times**.

The compensatory rebound suggests that **the amount of REM sleep is regulated separately from the amount of slow-wave sleep** and that REM serves a **special function** (Hayashi et al., 2015; Vyazovskiy, 2015), which has prompted much speculation about what that function is.

> **Trap.** Three Webb and Agnew studies appear in this chapter. **1967**: REM deprivation, **17 to 67** awakenings. **1974**: **16 volunteers** at **5.5 hours a night for 60 days** (14.9). **1975**: reduced sleepers lose NREM 1 and 2 but keep slow-wave sleep (efficiency evidence, below).

### REM sleep and memory

Opinion on sleep and memory divides three ways. Some believe **REM sleep strengthens explicit memories** (Diekelmann & Born, 2010), especially **emotional** ones (Wiesner et al., 2015); others that **slow-wave sleep promotes consolidation** (Inostroza & Born, 2013; Tononi & Cirelli, 2013); others that memories of daily experience are **processed (modified) before consolidation** during sleep (Dudai, Karni, & Born, 2015; Stickgold & Walker, 2013). Overall, the results are **still not convincing**: some studies find **no relationship** between memory and sleep (Ackermann et al., 2015). And **depressed patients** on **antidepressants** may have **no REM sleep for years** with **no memory deficits** (Genzel et al., 2014; Tribl, Wetter, & Schredl, 2013).

> **Trap.** Popular sources and some keys state flatly that "REM sleep consolidates memory". This book's evaluation is that the evidence is **not convincing**: researchers disagree on REM versus slow-wave sleep, some studies find no link, and patients can go **years without REM** with no memory deficit.

### The default theory of REM sleep

The **default theory of REM sleep** (Horne, 2013) starts from the observation that it is **difficult to stay continuously in NREM sleep**, so the brain periodically switches to one of two states: **wakefulness** if there are **immediate bodily needs** (such as food or water), and **REM sleep** if there are **none**. REM is thus **more adaptive than wakefulness when no needs are pressing**, and it also **prepares the organism for wakefulness** in environments where effective action may be needed on waking (Horne, 2013; Klemm, 2011).

Most support is **indirect**, from the many similarities between REM sleep and wakefulness. One study is more direct:

> **Key study, Nykamp et al. (1998).** Young adults were awakened every time they entered REM sleep, and a **15-minute period of wakefulness was substituted for each lost REM period**. They were **not tired the next day**, despite only **5 hours of sleep**, and showed **no REM rebound**.

Why this matters: in ordinary REM deprivation each lost REM period is replaced by more NREM sleep, and rebound follows. If REM had a unique restorative function, substituting *wakefulness* should leave the deficit and the rebound intact. Instead the rebound disappeared: there seemed to be **no need for REM if wakefulness was substituted for it**, as the default theory predicts if REM and waking are interchangeable exits from NREM. Consistent with this, as antidepressants reduce REM sleep, **nighttime awakenings increase** (Wichniak et al., 2017): block the REM exit and the brain takes the waking exit.

> **Trap.** In the default theory the **default** is **REM sleep**, chosen when there are **no** bodily needs; **wakefulness** is chosen when there **are**. The 11th edition cites **Horne (2013)**; older editions cite Horne (2000) for the same theory.

### Sleep deprivation increases the efficiency of sleep

In the book's words, one of the most important findings of human deprivation research is that **sleep-deprived individuals become more efficient sleepers**: their sleep has a **higher proportion of slow-wave sleep (NREM 3)**, which seems to serve the main restorative function. Six pieces of evidence:

1. People regain only a **small proportion of total lost sleep** after deprivation, but **most of their lost slow-wave sleep** (Léger et al., 2018).
2. After deprivation, slow-wave sleep has an **even higher proportion of slow waves** than usual (Léger et al., 2018).
3. People who sleep **6 hours or less** normally get **as much or more slow-wave sleep** as those who sleep **8 hours or more** (Åkerstedt et al., 2019).
4. Even **during REM sleep**, slow-wave activity occurs at **different times and in different locations** in the brain (Bernardi et al., 2019; Siclari & Tononi, 2017).
5. People who **reduce their usual sleep** get **less NREM 1 and NREM 2**, but their **slow-wave sleep stays about the same** (Mullaney et al., 1977; Webb & Agnew, 1975).
6. **Repeatedly waking people during REM sleep** produces **little increase in next-day sleepiness**, whereas waking them during **slow-wave sleep** has **major effects** (Nykamp et al., 1998).

Every item says that slow-wave sleep is protected and the lighter stages are what get trimmed. The implication, which the authors call extremely important: because sleep becomes more efficient when it is shortened, **conventional sleep-deprivation studies are virtually useless for discovering how much sleep people need**. People certainly respond badly to less sleep than they are used to, but the ill effects in **inefficient** sleepers do not show that the lost sleep was **needed**. True need can be assessed only by reducing sleep **regularly for many weeks**, so that participants adapt by maximising efficiency. Those studies are the subject of 14.9.

At this point Pinel files a progress report. After **2 weeks** on 5 hours a night, things are going well: progress on the chapter is faster than usual, and getting up and working are no problem. But staying awake in the evening takes a major effort; reading or watching television **after 10:30** brings **microsleeps**, and his "so-called friends" delight in punishing his lapses.

> **Trap.** The six efficiency items changed between editions. In the 11th edition item 4 is **slow-wave activity during REM sleep**; older editions had a different item (about morning naps), and older keys say "most lost **stage 4** is regained" where this edition says **slow-wave sleep (NREM 3)**. The conclusion is unchanged: deprived sleepers become **more efficient**, so short-term deprivation cannot measure **need**.

## 14.5 Circadian Sleep Cycles

This module explains what circadian rhythms are, what happens when they are disrupted, and the neural and genetic machinery that generates them. It supplies the adaptation side of the chapter with its strongest evidence.

### Circadian rhythms and zeitgebers

The world cycles from light to dark once every 24 hours, and most surface-dwelling species have adapted with **circadian rhythms** (*circadian* means "lasting about a day"). The most obvious is the **circadian sleep-wake cycle**: humans use the day for their biological needs and sleep for much of the night, while **nocturnal** animals such as **rats** sleep for much of the day. It is difficult to find any physiological, biochemical or behavioural process that does **not** show some circadian rhythmicity (McGinnis & Young, 2016; Turek, 2016); each day the body adjusts to the two environments we live in, light and dark.

Circadian cycles are kept on their 24-hour schedule by environmental cues, the most important for mammals being the **light-dark cycle**. Cues that can **entrain** circadian rhythms (control their timing) are called **zeitgebers** (pronounced "ZITE-gay-bers"), German for **"time givers"**. Three facts about them:

- **The period can be stretched or squeezed a little.** With alternating **11.5-hour** periods of light and dark, subjects' circadian cycles begin to conform to a **23-hour day**.
- **Light is not the only zeitgeber.** **Hamsters** in continuous darkness or continuous light can be entrained by regular daily bouts of **social interaction, hoarding, eating or exercise** (Mistlberger, 2011).
- **Hamsters** show particularly clear circadian cycles and are favourite subjects; they reappear in the SCN transplant and *tau* experiments.

### Free-running circadian sleep-wake cycles

Under conditions with absolutely **no temporal cues**, humans and other animals **maintain all of their circadian rhythms**. Rhythms in constant environments are **free-running rhythms**, and their duration is the **free-running period**. Free-running periods **vary between individuals**, are **relatively constant within an individual**, and are **usually longer than 24 hours**: about **24.2 hours** is typical for humans under **constant moderate illumination**. We all seem to have an **internal clock that habitually runs a little slow** unless entrained.

Figure 14.8 (Wever, 1979) shows a man who, without any external cues, fell asleep at intervals of about **25.3 hours** for a whole month, about **1.3 hours later** each day. The **regularity** of such cycles **despite variations in physical and mental activity** supports the **dominance of circadian over recuperative factors**: a deficit-driven sleep onset should come earlier after an exhausting day and later after an idle one, not at clockwork intervals.

Free-running cycles **are not learned**: **rats born and raised** in constant light or constant darkness show regular free-running cycles slightly longer than 24 hours (**Richter, 1971**).

One more free-running observation directly contradicts recuperation theories. When volunteers **stay awake longer than usual**, the following sleep is **shorter, not longer** (**Wever, 1979**). Recuperation predicts the reverse. The circadian explanation: we are programmed for sleep-wake cycles of about 24 hours, so **the more wakefulness in a cycle, the less time there is for sleep**.

> **Trap.** **24.2 hours** is the **typical human** free-running period; **25.3 hours** is the **one man** in Figure 14.8 (Wever, 1979), sleeping about 1.3 hours later each day. Older sources and keys say "about 25 hours". Either way the clock runs **slow** (longer than 24 hours), not fast.

### Internal desynchronisation

Many animals show a circadian **body-temperature** cycle linked to sleep: they **sleep during its falling phase** and **wake during its rising phase**. In constant laboratory environments, however, the sleep-wake and temperature cycles sometimes **break away from one another**: **internal desynchronisation** (Daan, Honma, & Honma, 2013).

> **Key observation.** In one human volunteer, both cycles initially free-ran at **25.7 hours**. Then, for unknown reasons, the **sleep-wake cycle lengthened to 33.4 hours** while the **body-temperature cycle shortened to 25.1 hours**.

Two conclusions: two simultaneous free-running periods were the **first evidence for more than one circadian timing mechanism** (one clock cannot run at two speeds); and sleep is **not causally related to the fall in body temperature** normally associated with it, since the two can drift apart.

### Jet lag and shift work

Modern industrialised societies impose two disruptions:

- **Jet lag**: the zeitgebers controlling circadian phase are **accelerated on east-bound flights (phase advances)** or **decelerated on west-bound flights (phase delays)**.
- **Shift work**: the zeitgebers stay the same, but workers must **adjust their natural sleep-wake cycles** to changing schedules.

Both produce **sleep disturbances, fatigue, general malaise, and deficits on tests of physical and cognitive function** (Lewis, 2015; van Ee et al., 2016), which can last many days: full adjustment to the **8-hour phase advance** of a **Seattle-to-London** flight typically takes about **8 days**.

**Remedies for jet lag.** **Pharmacological**: **melatonin or melatonin agonists** taken **before bedtime at the new location** are **effective** (Reid & Abbott, 2015). **Nonpharmacological**: **pre-travel light exposure** timed to the desired wake time at the destination has only **limited support** (Bin, Postnova, & Cistulli, 2019).

**Remedies for shift work.** Companies have improved productivity and job satisfaction by scheduling **phase delays rather than advances**, moving workers to schedules that **begin later in the day**. Going to sleep and getting up 4 hours **earlier** (an advance) is much harder than doing both 4 hours **later** (a delay). A natural way to understand the asymmetry: the internal clock already runs slow, drifting later each day, so a delay goes with its grain.

> **Trap.** **East-bound = phase advance = harder; west-bound = phase delay = easier.** Shift rotations should move to **later** starts. The 11th edition's figure is about **8 days for an 8-hour advance (Seattle to London)**; older editions used a different example (about 10 days), so anchor on the current numbers. Melatonin at bedtime at the destination is the **supported** remedy.

### A circadian clock in the suprachiasmatic nuclei

Rhythms that persist without cues imply an internal **circadian clock**. The first clue was **Richter's 1967** finding that **large medial hypothalamic lesions** disrupt circadian cycles of **eating, drinking and activity** in rats. Then specific lesions of the **suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN)** of the medial hypothalamus, which sit just **above the optic chiasm**, were shown to disrupt circadian cycles including the sleep-wake cycle (Figure 14.9).

The examinable detail is what SCN lesions do and do not do: they **do not greatly affect the amount of time mammals sleep**, but they **abolish its circadian periodicity**, so sleep is scattered across the 24 hours instead of collected into a daily block. The SCN themselves show **circadian cycles of electrical, metabolic and biochemical activity** that can be **entrained by the light-dark cycle** (Pauls et al., 2016; Sollars & Pickard, 2015).

> **Trap.** SCN lesions abolish the **timing** of sleep, not the **amount**; "SCN lesions cause insomnia" is wrong. **Richter (1967)**: medial hypothalamic lesions disrupt rhythms. **Richter (1971)**: rats raised in constant conditions still free-run. Different findings, same author.

### The SCN transplant experiment (Ralph and colleagues, 1990)

Any lingering doubt about the clock's location was removed by **Ralph and colleagues (1990)**.

> **Key study, Ralph et al. (1990).** SCN from the **fetuses of mutant hamsters** with an abnormally short **20-hour** free-running cycle were transplanted into **normal adult hamsters** whose **25-hour** free-running cycles had been **abolished by SCN lesions**. The transplants **restored** free-running cycles, but of about **20 hours**. Transplants the **other way**, normal fetal SCN into SCN-lesioned mutant adults, restored cycles of about **25 hours**.

Why this is decisive: a lesion shows only that the SCN are *necessary*; it cannot show that the rhythm is *generated* there rather than relayed. The transplant shows that the restored rhythm carries the **donor's** period, not the host's, so the period is a property of the SCN tissue itself. The **reciprocal** design rules out any generic effect of transplantation, because the outcome flips exactly when the donor flips. (The **25 hours** here is the normal **hamster** period, and the 20-hour mutants are the ***tau*** hamsters described below.)

### Other circadian clocks outside the SCN

The SCN are the **major** mammalian clocks but **not the only ones** (Rosenwasser & Turek, 2015). Three lines of evidence, mostly from the 1980s and 1990s:

1. Under certain conditions, bilateral SCN lesions **leave some rhythms intact** while abolishing others.
2. SCN lesions **do not eliminate all entrainment** (Saper, 2013): they **block entrainment by light but not by regular food or water availability** (Mistlberger, 2011), so a food-entrainable timer must exist elsewhere.
3. **Cells from other parts of the body** often show **free-running circadian cycles in tissue culture**, just as SCN neurons do.

### Neural mechanisms of entrainment

**SCN firing.** The SCN's timing depends on the firing of its neurons: many are **inactive at night, start firing at dawn, and fire at a slow, steady pace all day** (Pauls et al., 2016; Hastings, Maywood, & Brancaccio, 2018).

**The pathway from eye to clock** was found by cutting the visual pathway at two levels (Figure 14.10):

1. **Cutting the optic nerves before the optic chiasm eliminated** light-dark entrainment.
2. **Cutting the optic tracts where they leave the chiasm left entrainment intact.**

So the critical axons must leave the visual pathway **near the optic chiasm**. This led to the discovery of the **retinohypothalamic tracts**, which leave the chiasm and project to the **adjacent SCN** (Junko et al., 2019); **neurons from each retina project to both SCN**.

**The photoreceptors.** Surprisingly, **neither rods nor cones are necessary** for entrainment. The critical photoreceptors are **retinal ganglion cells** with distinctive properties (Lazzerini Ospri, Prusky, & Hattar, 2017; Roecklein et al., 2013): in evolution they have **sacrificed rapid, brief responses to sudden light changes** in favour of **consistent responses to slowly changing background illumination**, which is what a clock needs. Their photopigment is **melanopsin**.

> **Trap.** **Optic nerve cut (before the chiasm): entrainment lost. Optic tract cut (after the chiasm): entrainment intact.** The pathway is the **retinohypothalamic tract**; the entraining photoreceptors are **melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells**, not rods or cones.

### Genetics of circadian rhythms

In **1988**, routine screening of a shipment of hamsters revealed some with abnormally short **20-hour** free-running rhythms. Breeding showed a **genetic mutation**, and the gene was named ***tau*** (**Ralph & Menaker, 1988**). *Tau* was the **first mammalian circadian gene identified**, but **not the first characterised**: that was ***clock***, discovered in **mice** and characterised in **1997**; *tau* was characterised in **2000** (Lowrey et al., 2000). Several others have since been specified (Cox & Takahashi, 2019).

The circadian genes led to **three important discoveries**:

1. **Conservation.** The same or similar genes occur in species of very different evolutionary ages (**bacteria, flies, fish, frogs, mice, humans**), so they **evolved early** and have been **conserved** (Sun et al., 2019).
2. **Mechanism.** The key mechanism seems to be **gene expression**: the **transcription of proteins by circadian genes shows a circadian cycle** (Brancaccio et al., 2014; Musiek & Holtzman, 2016).
3. **Clocks in most cells.** **Molecular timing mechanisms like those of the SCN exist in most cells of the body** (Masri & Sassone-Corsi, 2013; Patke, Young, & Axelrod, 2019), but these cellular clocks are **normally regulated by neural and hormonal signals from the SCN**.

The third discovery explains the tissue-culture evidence above: cells free-run because they carry the clockwork themselves, while the SCN keeps them in phase.

> **Trap.** ***Tau*** (hamsters, **Ralph & Menaker, 1988**) was the **first mammalian circadian gene identified**; ***clock*** (mice) was the **first characterised** (**1997**); *tau* was characterised in **2000**. "First identified" and "first characterised" have different answers.

## 14.6 Four Areas of the Brain Involved in Sleep

Module 14.5 dealt with the *timing* of sleep. This module covers **four areas** directly involved in **producing or reducing** sleep: the **anterior hypothalamus**, the **posterior hypothalamus**, the **reticular formation**, and the **reticular REM-sleep nuclei**.

### Two areas of the hypothalamus involved in sleep

Remarkably, two of these areas were identified early in the 20th century, long before modern behavioural neuroscience, by **Baron Constantin von Economo**, a **Viennese neurologist** (Scammell, Arrigoni, & Lipton, 2017; Weber & Dan, 2016).

#### The Case of Constantin von Economo, the Insightful Neurologist

During **World War I**, a serious **viral infection of the brain, encephalitis lethargica**, swept the world, and many victims **slept almost continuously**. Von Economo found that the brains of deceased victims with **excessive sleep** all had damage in the **posterior hypothalamus and adjacent midbrain**. He then examined a small group of victims with the **opposite** problem, **difficulty sleeping**, and found that their brains always had damage in the **anterior hypothalamus and adjacent basal forebrain**. He concluded that the **posterior hypothalamus promotes wakefulness** and the **anterior hypothalamus promotes sleep**, a conclusion since confirmed by **lesion and recording studies in experimental animals** (Schwartz & Kilduff, 2015). Figure 14.11 shows the two regions.

The direction is where students slip, so apply lesion logic: damage removes a function. Patients who slept too much had lost the region that **keeps us awake** (posterior); patients who could not sleep had lost the region that **puts us to sleep** (anterior). Keep this in mind for 14.8, where the **orexin** neurons whose loss causes narcolepsy turn out to lie in the **posterior** hypothalamus.

> **Trap.** **Posterior hypothalamic damage produced too much sleep**, so the posterior hypothalamus is **wake-promoting**; **anterior hypothalamic (and basal forebrain) damage produced insomnia**, so the anterior hypothalamus is **sleep-promoting**. The disease was **encephalitis lethargica**, a **viral** epidemic around **World War I**.

### Reticular formation and sleep

A third area was found by comparing two brain-stem transections in cats.

**Cerveau isolé (Bremer, 1936).** **Bremer** severed cats' brain stems **between the inferior and superior colliculi** (a midcollicular cut) to disconnect the forebrain from ascending sensory input (Figure 14.12). This is the **cerveau isolé** preparation ("ser-VOE ees-o-LAY"), literally **"isolated forebrain"**. The cortical EEG showed **almost continuous slow-wave sleep**. Only **strong visual or olfactory stimuli** (whose nerves enter above the cut, so they remain intact) could change it to a **desynchronised EEG**, a **low-amplitude, high-frequency** EEG, and the arousal barely outlasted the stimuli. Bremer's own interpretation, usually called the **passive sensory theory of sleep**, was that the forebrain sleeps when deprived of sensory input.

**Encéphale isolé (Bremer, 1937).** For comparison, Bremer transected the **caudal brain stem** of other cats, disconnecting the brain from the rest of the nervous system: the **encéphale isolé** preparation ("on-say-FELL ees-o-LAY"). Although it cut **most of the same sensory fibres**, it **did not disrupt the normal cycle of sleep and wakefulness EEG**.

Reason it through: both cuts deprive the forebrain of most sensory input, so if sensory deprivation caused sleep, both preparations should sleep continuously. Only the higher cut does. What matters must be **something in the brain stem between the two cuts**, which the lower cut leaves connected to the forebrain. Two 1949 findings identified it as the **reticular formation**:

1. **Partial transections** at the cerveau isolé level disrupted cortical sleep-wake cycles **only when they severed the reticular formation core**; partial cuts restricted to the **lateral** areas containing the **ascending sensory tracts** had **little effect** (**Lindsey, Bowden, & Magoun, 1949**).
2. **Electrical stimulation of the reticular formation** of sleeping cats **woke them** and produced a **lengthy period of EEG desynchronisation** (**Moruzzi & Magoun, 1949**); Figure 14.12 specifies the **pontine** reticular formation.

In **1949, Moruzzi and Magoun** put the four findings together (cerveau isolé, encéphale isolé, reticular lesions, reticular stimulation) and proposed that **low reticular activity produces sleep and high reticular activity produces wakefulness** (Larson-Prior, Ju, & Galvin, 2014). The idea is so widely accepted that the reticular formation is commonly called the **reticular activating system**, although maintaining wakefulness is only one function of its many nuclei.

| Preparation or manipulation | Who (year) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Cerveau isolé** (midcollicular cut) | Bremer (1936) | Almost continuous **slow-wave** EEG; strong visual or olfactory stimuli desynchronise only briefly |
| **Encéphale isolé** (caudal brain-stem cut) | Bremer (1937) | **Normal sleep-wake** EEG cycle |
| Partial midcollicular cuts | Lindsey, Bowden & Magoun (1949) | Sleep-wake EEG disrupted only if the **reticular core** is cut |
| Stimulation of the pontine reticular formation | Moruzzi & Magoun (1949) | Sleeping cats **wake**, with lengthy EEG **desynchronisation** |

> **Trap.** **Cerveau isolé** (isolated forebrain; higher, midcollicular cut between the **inferior and superior colliculi**) gives **continuous slow-wave sleep**. **Encéphale isolé** (isolated brain; lower, caudal cut) gives a **normal sleep-wake cycle**. "Desynchronised EEG" means **low-amplitude, high-frequency** (waking-like), not "disorganised".

### Reticular REM-sleep nuclei

The fourth area controls **REM sleep** and lies within the reticular formation, in its **caudal** part. Given how similar REM sleep and wakefulness are, it makes sense that an area involved in wakefulness would also produce REM sleep. REM is controlled not by one nucleus but by **a variety of nuclei scattered throughout the caudal reticular formation**, each controlling **one index of REM sleep**: one for reduced core-muscle tone, one for EEG desynchronisation, one for rapid eye movements, and so on (Liu & Dan, 2019; Peever, Luppi, & Montplaisir, 2014; Weber et al., 2015). Figure 14.13 (after Vertes, 1983) maps them in the cat's pons and medulla:

- **PGO spikes** (EEG spikes recorded in the **pons, lateral geniculate and occipital cortex**);
- **cortical EEG desynchronisation**;
- **hippocampal theta waves** (hippocampal EEG waves of **5 to 8 Hz**);
- **twitches of the extremities**;
- **rapid eye movements**;
- **core-muscle relaxation**; and
- **cardiorespiratory changes**.

### Why there is no single REM-sleep centre

The authors draw a general lesson. Many people assume that **if a behaviour has one name, there must be a single brain structure for it**, as if evolution had shaped the brain according to our language and theories. REM sleep shows the flaw: it occurs only when a **network of independent structures becomes active together**. The evidence that the components are genuinely independent is that the changes defining a state **sometimes come apart**:

- During **REM-sleep deprivation**, **penile erections**, normally a REM feature, begin to occur during **slow-wave sleep**.
- During **total sleep deprivation**, **slow waves**, normally confined to slow-wave sleep, begin to occur during **wakefulness**.

So REM sleep, slow-wave sleep and wakefulness are **not each controlled by a single mechanism**; each results from the **interaction of several mechanisms** that can, under certain conditions, operate **independently**. The book's journal prompt applies the lesson to the claim that "the prefrontal cortex is the brain structure responsible for higher cognition": the same one-word, one-structure error. The disorders module shows the principle clinically, with single REM components detached from the rest.

## 14.7 Drugs That Affect Sleep

Most drugs that influence sleep fall into two classes. **Hypnotic drugs** increase sleep; **antihypnotic drugs** reduce sleep. A third class comprises drugs that influence the **circadian rhythmicity** of sleep; the main one is **melatonin**.

### Hypnotic drugs: benzodiazepines

The **benzodiazepines** (for example, **diazepam** and **clonazepam**) are **GABA-A agonists**. They were developed and tested for the treatment of **anxiety**, yet they are among the most commonly prescribed hypnotics. In the **short term** they increase drowsiness, **decrease the time it takes to fall asleep**, **reduce the number of awakenings** during the night, and **increase total sleep time** (DeKosky & Williamson, 2020). They can therefore be effective for **occasional** difficulties in sleeping.

Their prescription for **chronic** sleep difficulties, though common, is **ill-advised** (Atkin, Comai, & Gobbi, 2018; Bourgeois et al., 2014; Mignot, 2013). Chronic use as a hypnotic carries **five complications**:

1. **Tolerance** develops to the hypnotic effects, so patients must take larger and larger doses to maintain efficacy (Atkin, Comai, & Gobbi, 2018; Frase et al., 2018).
2. **Cessation after chronic use causes insomnia**, which can exacerbate the very problem the drug was meant to correct (Guina & Merrill, 2018).
3. They **distort the normal pattern of sleep**: they **increase the duration of NREM 2** while **decreasing both slow-wave sleep and REM sleep**.
4. They produce **next-day drowsiness** (Ware, 2008) and are associated with an increased risk of **traffic accidents** (Atkin, Comai, & Gobbi, 2018).
5. Most troubling, chronic use has been shown to **substantially reduce life expectancy** (Votaw et al., 2019).

Note the link to 14.4: the stage benzodiazepines suppress, slow-wave sleep, seems to do the main restorative work, while the stage they inflate, NREM 2, is among those trimmed first in efficient sleep. A drug can lengthen sleep while making it less normal.

> **Trap.** Benzodiazepines **increase NREM 2** and **decrease slow-wave sleep and REM**. The book calls them **GABA-A agonists**; pharmacology texts describe them more precisely as positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor, and either description points to the same receptor. Their short-term effectiveness is real; the five problems belong to **chronic** use.

### Hypnotic drugs: imidazopyridines and 5-HTP

Problems with the benzodiazepines led to a search for drugs with the same hypnotic effects but fewer side effects. In the **early 1990s**, a new class of GABA-A agonists, the **imidazopyridines**, was marketed for insomnia, with claims of fewer adverse side effects and less potential for addiction. One of the most widely prescribed is **zolpidem** (Krystal, 2015; Neubauer, 2014). Yet zolpidem has been found to be **no safer or more effective than the benzodiazepines** (Arbon, Knurowska, & Dijk, 2015).

A different approach came from evidence that the **raphé nuclei**, which are **serotonergic**, play a role in sleep, suggesting that serotonergic drugs might be effective hypnotics. Efforts focused on **5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP)**, the **precursor of serotonin**, because 5-HTP, **but not serotonin itself, readily passes through the blood-brain barrier**. Injections of 5-HTP do **reverse the insomnia produced in cats and rats** by the serotonin antagonist **PCPA**; however, they appear to be of **no therapeutic benefit in human insomnia**.

### Antihypnotic drugs

The mechanisms of three classes of antihypnotic drug are well understood: **cocaine-derived stimulants**, **amphetamine-derived stimulants**, and **tricyclic antidepressants**. All three seem to promote wakefulness by **boosting the activity of catecholamines** (**norepinephrine, epinephrine and dopamine**), by increasing their **release** into synapses, by **blocking their reuptake**, or both.

Regular use is risky. Antihypnotics tend to produce **loss of appetite, anxiety, tremor, addiction, and disturbance of normal sleep patterns**. Moreover, they may **mask the pathology** that is causing the excessive sleepiness in the first place.

### Melatonin and the pineal gland

**Melatonin** is a hormone **synthesised from the neurotransmitter serotonin** in the **pineal gland** (Alghamdi, 2018; Cecon et al., 2015). The pineal gland is an inconspicuous gland that **René Descartes** once believed to be the **seat of the soul**. It lies on the **midline** of the brain, **just ventral to the rear portion of the corpus callosum** (Figure 14.14).

In **birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish**, the pineal gland has important functions (Sapède & Cau, 2013): it has **inherent timing properties** and regulates **circadian rhythms and seasonal changes in reproductive behaviour** through its release of melatonin. In **humans and other mammals**, its functions are not as apparent.

In mammals, circulating melatonin levels display **circadian rhythms under the control of the SCN** (Videnovic et al., 2014; Zisapel, 2018), with the **highest levels associated with darkness and sleep** (Morris, Aeschbach, & Scheer, 2012). On the basis of this correlation, it has long been assumed that melatonin promotes sleep or regulates its timing in mammals.

One fact should temper that assumption. Taking the pineal gland out of an **adult mammal** (**pinealectomy**), and with it the animal's melatonin, **seems to change very little**. The gland does contribute to the **development of sexual maturity** in mammals, but once **puberty** is over, what it does is far from clear. A hormone whose removal changes little in adults is a weak candidate for an essential sleep signal.

### Does melatonin improve sleep? Soporific versus chronobiotic

Does **exogenous** (externally produced) melatonin improve sleep, as widely believed? A **meta-analysis** (a combined analysis of the results of more than one study) of **19 studies** found that exogenous melatonin has a **slight, but statistically significant, soporific** (sleep-promoting) effect (**Ferracioli-Oda, Qawasmi, & Bloch, 2013**).

Melatonin's more convincing action concerns timing: good evidence shows that it can **move mammalian circadian cycles** to a different point in the day. For that reason several researchers (Golombek et al., 2015) prefer to call it a **chronobiotic**, meaning a substance that **resets when internal biological rhythms run**, rather than a **soporific**. This is consistent with its effectiveness against **jet lag** (14.5), where the problem is mistimed sleep, not a shortage of sleepiness.

## 14.8 Sleep Disorders

Many sleep disorders fall into one of two complementary categories. **Insomnia** includes all disorders of **initiating and maintaining sleep**. **Hypersomnia** includes disorders of **excessive sleep or sleepiness**. A third major class includes all disorders specifically related to **REM-sleep dysfunction**. Both insomnia and hypersomnia are **common symptoms of many psychiatric disorders** (Rumble, White, & Benca, 2015), which is one reason the topic matters clinically.

### Complaints versus laboratory findings

In various surveys, about **30 percent** of respondents report significant sleep-related problems, yet complaints often come from people whose sleep looks **normal in laboratory tests**. Many people sleep **6 hours or less** and do well, but pressure from doctors, friends and their own expectations to get 8 hours makes them **spend too long in bed**, and **anxiety** about not sleeping makes sleep harder still (Harvey & Tang, 2012; Khoury & Doghramji, 2015). Such patients are often helped by **counselling them to go to bed only when they are very sleepy**. Others have more serious problems.

The book's clearest demonstration of the gap between complaint and record:

> **Key finding, the self-report gap.** Individuals with insomnia claimed to take an average of **1 hour** to fall asleep and to sleep an average of only **4.5 hours** a night. Tested in a sleep laboratory, they had an average **sleep latency** (time to fall asleep) of only **15 minutes** and an average nightly sleep duration of **6.5 hours**.

It was once common practice to call people who complained of insomnia but slept more than 6.5 hours **neurotic**. This stopped when some patients diagnosed with **neurotic pseudoinsomnia** turned out to have **sleep apnoea, nocturnal myoclonus** (an older name for periodic limb movement disorder) or other sleep-disturbing problems. **Insomnia is not necessarily too little sleep; it is often too little undisturbed sleep.**

### Insomnia: iatrogenic causes

Many cases of insomnia are **iatrogenic** (physician-created), largely because **sleeping pills** such as benzodiazepines, usually prescribed by physicians, are a **major cause of insomnia**. At first the hypnotic may increase sleep, but soon the patient is trapped in a rising spiral: **tolerance** develops, progressively more drug is needed for the original effect, and the patient cannot stop without risking **withdrawal symptoms, which include insomnia**. The case of Mr. B. (Dement, 1978) illustrates it.

#### Mr. B., the Case of Iatrogenic Insomnia

Under stress while studying for an important examination, Mr. B. could not sleep, and his physician prescribed a moderate bedtime dose of a **barbiturate (sodium amytal)**. It worked for a few nights; **after a week** he slept badly again and took **two** pills. The cycle repeated **twice more**, so that on the night before the examination he was taking **four times the original dose**. The next night, pressure off, he took none and could not sleep, concluded he had serious insomnia, and returned to the pills. When he reached a sleep clinic **several years later**, he was taking about **1,000 mg of sodium amytal nightly**, and his sleep was **more disturbed than ever**.

Such patients may go on for years with escalating doses, never realising that the pills are the problem. The sleepless night after the examination, which Mr. B. read as proof of an underlying disorder, was **withdrawal insomnia** produced by the drug itself.

> **Trap.** Mr. B. was prescribed **sodium amytal, a barbiturate** (not a benzodiazepine), and escalated to about **1,000 mg** a night over several years; the case is from **Dement (1978)**, the same source as Randy Gardner. Sodium amytal is also the drug of the carotid-injection speech-lateralisation test in Chapter 16, a link examiners occasionally exploit.

### Sleep apnoea

The insomnia associated with **sleep apnoea** is well documented. The patient **stops breathing many times each night**; each time, the patient **awakens**, begins to breathe again, and drifts back to sleep. Sleep apnoea usually leaves a sense of having slept poorly and is therefore often diagnosed as **insomnia**. But some patients are **totally unaware** of their multiple awakenings and complain instead of **excessive daytime sleepiness**, which can lead to a diagnosis of **hypersomnia**.

There are two types:

- **Obstructive sleep apnoea**: obstruction of the respiratory passages by **muscle spasms or atonia** (lack of muscle tone); it often occurs in **vigorous snorers** (Yenigun et al., 2020).
- **Central sleep apnoea**: failure of the **central nervous system to stimulate respiration** (Dempsey, 2019).

Sleep apnoea is more common in **males**, in people who are **overweight**, and in the **elderly** (Badran et al., 2015; Mansur et al., 2019).

> **Trap.** Sleep apnoea can present as **either insomnia or hypersomnia**, depending on whether the patient notices the awakenings. **Obstructive** = airway blocked (spasm or atonia; snorers). **Central** = the brain fails to drive breathing. Risk groups: **males, overweight, elderly**.

### Periodic limb movement disorder and restless legs syndrome

Two other specific causes of insomnia involve the legs:

- **Periodic limb movement disorder** is characterised by periodic, **involuntary movements of the limbs**, often twitches of the legs, **during sleep**. Most patients complain of poor sleep and daytime sleepiness but are **unaware of the nature of their problem**.
- **Restless legs syndrome** is the reverse as far as awareness goes: patients know **only too well** what is wrong. As they try to drop off, their legs fill with a **tension or uneasiness** they find hard to put into words, and it **prevents sleep onset** (Lai et al., 2017).

Once established, **both disorders are chronic** (Ondo, 2019; Stevens, 2015). There are **no effective treatments**, although **L-dopa** (Chapter 10) can help some patients (Ondo, 2019).

> **Trap.** The discriminating feature is **awareness and timing**. **Periodic limb movement disorder**: movements **during sleep**, patient **unaware** (bed partner may notice). **Restless legs syndrome**: uneasy sensation **before sleep onset**, patient **acutely aware**. Neither has an effective treatment; **L-dopa** helps some.

### Sleep restriction therapy

**Sleep restriction therapy** ranks among the most effective treatments for insomnia (Kay-Stacey & Attarian, 2016; Trauer et al., 2015), and it runs in two phases. The patient's permitted time in bed is first **cut back substantially**. After a spell on that restricted schedule, it is **lengthened again in small steps**, each step allowed only while **sleep latency stays within the normal range**. The method helps even those whose insomnia is **severe**.

Why does giving an insomniac *less* time in bed help? Restricted sleep becomes more **efficient** (14.4), and the anxious hours of lying awake are removed, which systematises the "go to bed only when very sleepy" advice.

### Hypersomnia: narcolepsy

**Narcolepsy** is the most widely studied hypersomnia. It occurs in about **1 in 2,000** individuals (Arango, Kivity, & Shoenfeld, 2015; Mahoney et al., 2019) and has **two prominent symptoms**:

1. **Severe sleepiness during the day, with repeated short (10- to 15-minute) sleep episodes.** Total sleep is barely raised: a person with narcolepsy typically sleeps only about **an hour a day more than average**. What really marks the condition is the **inappropriateness** of the episodes, that is, when they strike. Anyone may nod off now and then on the beach, while watching television or in a big, stuffy, dimly lit lecture hall; people with narcolepsy drop off mid-conversation, during a meal, while scuba diving and even during sex.
2. **Cataplexy** (Dauvilliers et al., 2014): repeated episodes in which a waking patient suddenly **loses muscle tone**, frequently **set off by strong emotion**. A mild attack may do no more than make the person sit down for a few seconds. A severe one drops them to the floor **as if shot**, where they lie for a minute or two while staying **fully conscious**.

People with narcolepsy often experience **two other symptoms**:

- **Sleep paralysis**: the **inability to move just as one is falling asleep or waking up** (Sharpless & Kliková, 2019).
- **Hypnagogic hallucinations**: **dreamlike experiences during wakefulness**.

Many healthy people occasionally experience sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations (Denis, French, & Gregory, 2018; Larøi et al., 2019); it is the full pattern that is diagnostic. The four together (sleep attacks, cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations) are often called the narcolepsy tetrad.

**Why narcolepsy was thought to be a REM disorder.** Three lines of evidence suggested to early researchers that narcolepsy results from an abnormality in the mechanisms that trigger REM sleep:

1. Unlike other people, those with narcolepsy often go **directly into REM sleep** when they fall asleep (normally, as 14.1 showed, the first REM period comes only after a full descent to stage 3 and back).
2. They experience **dreamlike states** during wakefulness (hypnagogic hallucinations).
3. They experience **loss of muscle tone** during wakefulness (cataplexy, and sleep paralysis at transitions) (Mahoney et al., 2019).

Each of the last two is a single REM-sleep component intruding into waking, which is the dissociation principle from 14.6 at work.

> **Trap.** Narcolepsy is defined by the **inappropriateness** of sleep, not its quantity: patients sleep only about **1 hour a day more than average**, in **10- to 15-minute** episodes; prevalence about **1 in 2,000**. **Cataplexy** is loss of muscle tone **while awake and conscious**, often **triggered by emotion**; it is not a seizure and not fainting. **Hypnagogic hallucinations** are dreamlike experiences **during wakefulness**.

### Orexin and the neural basis of narcolepsy

A strain of **narcoleptic dogs** launched some of the most exciting research on sleep mechanisms. **Lin and colleagues (1999)** spent **10 years** on the dogs' genetics before they pinned down the gene responsible for the disorder. Its product is a **receptor protein** for **orexin** (also known as **hypocretin**; Mieda, 2017; Richter, Woods, & Schier, 2014), a neuropeptide that comes in two forms, **orexin-A and orexin-B**.

The finding pointed researchers towards genetic causes, yet in **monozygotic twins the concordance rate for narcolepsy is only about 25 percent** (Liblau et al., 2015). Identical twins share all their genes, so if one twin has narcolepsy and the other usually does not, genes cannot be the whole story.

Evidence from humans converges on orexin, but at a different point in the system from the dogs:

- **Reduced levels of orexin** have been found in the **cerebrospinal fluid** of people living with narcolepsy and in the **brains** of deceased people who had it (Nishino & Kanbayashi, 2005).
- The **number of orexin-releasing neurons is reduced** in the brains of people with narcolepsy (Mahoney et al., 2019; Shan, Dauvilliers, & Siegel, 2015).
- One popular explanation for the loss of these neurons is an **autoimmune response** (Arango, Kivity, & Shoenfeld, 2015; Liblau et al., 2015; Mahoney et al., 2019), which would also fit the low twin concordance.

**Where is orexin made?** By neurons in the region of the hypothalamus linked to the promotion of wakefulness: the **posterior hypothalamus**, von Economo's wake-promoting area. The orexin neurons **project diffusely** throughout the brain but have **many connections with the other wakefulness-promoting area, the reticular formation**. There is considerable current interest in the role of orexin circuits in wakefulness and REM sleep (Mignot, 2013; Mahoney et al., 2019).

Three strands thus converge: von Economo's lesions (the posterior hypothalamus keeps us awake), the dog gene (orexin signalling) and the human pathology (orexin neurons lost). Losing the wake-promoting orexin signal produces what you would predict: sleep, and REM components, intruding into the waking day.

**Treatment.** Narcolepsy has traditionally been treated with **stimulants** (for example, **amphetamine** and **methylphenidate**), but these have addiction potential and many undesirable side effects. The antihypnotic stimulant **modafinil** has been shown to be effective in some cases, and certain **antidepressants** can be effective against **cataplexy** (Scammell, 2015).

> **Trap.** **Orexin = hypocretin** (**orexin-A and orexin-B**). In the **dogs (Lin et al., 1999)** the mutant gene encoded the **orexin receptor**; in **humans** the finding is **low orexin and fewer orexin neurons**, possibly **autoimmune**; MZ concordance only **about 25 percent**. The book places orexin neurons in the **posterior hypothalamus**; many texts say **lateral hypothalamus** (the orexin field spans both), so accept whichever is offered.

### REM-sleep-related disorders

Several sleep disorders are specific to REM sleep and are classed as **REM-sleep-related disorders**. Even narcolepsy, usually classed as a hypersomnia, can be considered REM-related for the reasons just given.

**Patients with little or no REM sleep** are rare but theoretically important. **Lavie and colleagues (1984)** described a man whose brain injury presumably damaged the **REM-sleep controllers in the caudal reticular formation**. He **did not appear to be adversely affected** by the lack of REM: after the injury he completed **high school, college and law school** and built a **thriving law practice**. Together with the antidepressant evidence of 14.4, this makes it hard to argue that REM sleep is indispensable.

**REM-sleep behaviour disorder.** In **REM-sleep behaviour disorder** (Schenck et al., 2013; Högl, Stefani, & Videnovic, 2017), REM sleep occurs **without the usual core-muscle atonia**. The disorder is **common among people with Parkinson's disease** (Högl, Stefani, & Videnovic, 2017; Neikrug et al., 2014; Tekriwal et al., 2016), and it offers a natural test of one proposed **function of REM-sleep atonia: to stop us acting out our dreams**. Case reports bear that idea out, as the following one from Schenck and colleagues (1986) shows.

#### The Case of the Sleeper Who Ran Over Tackle

The patient, a man, dreamed he was in a football game. In the dream he took a lateral pass from the quarterback and was meant to cut back over tackle, but a **280-pound** opposing tackle stood in his path, so he lowered his shoulder and barged the man aside; he remembered this part of the dream very vividly. He woke to find himself **standing in front of his dresser**. Everything on top of it, lamps and mirrors included, had been knocked to the floor, and he had hit his head on the wall and banged his knee on the dresser.

The case is the mirror image of cataplexy: in cataplexy REM **atonia** intrudes into **wakefulness**; in REM-sleep behaviour disorder REM sleep runs **without its atonia**, so the dream's movements reach the muscles. Both are possible only because REM's components have separate controllers (14.6).

> **Trap.** **REM-sleep behaviour disorder** occurs in **REM** sleep (REM **without atonia**; dream enactment; associated with **Parkinson's disease**). **Sleepwalking** occurs in **slow-wave sleep** and is **not** dream enactment. The patient with **no REM sleep** who became a lawyer is **Lavie et al. (1984)**; the football case is **Schenck et al. (1986)**.

## 14.9 Effects of Long-Term Sleep Reduction

This final module returns to the opening question with the kind of evidence that 14.4 said was needed: studies of people who sleep little, either because they always have or because they have deliberately reduced their sleep for weeks or months.

### Two interpretations of the effects of sleep loss

When people sleep less than usual, they do not feel or function well, and most people, experts included, conclude that **we need the sleep we typically get**. An alternative, more consistent with the **plasticity of the adult brain**, is that the brain needs only a **small amount** of sleep but will take much more under ideal conditions because sleep has a **high positive-incentive value**; it then **adapts** to the amount it habitually gets, even if that is far more than it needs, and is disturbed by a **sudden reduction**.

The positive-incentive idea is the same one Chapter 12 used for eating: we are drawn to do things that are pleasurable, not only pushed by deficits. On this view, sleep is like a large, rich meal: very attractive, and missed when withdrawn, but not therefore required in that quantity.

The two interpretations make different predictions about **short sleepers**. If people need at least 8 hours a night, short sleepers should be suffering from a variety of health and performance problems. The authors add one caution before the results: because they are so time-consuming, **few long-term studies** have been done, and some are not sufficiently thorough. Nevertheless, a clear pattern has emerged.

### Differences between short and long sleepers

Numerous studies have compared **short sleepers** (**6 hours or less** a night) with **long sleepers** (**8 hours or more**). The book focuses on the **2004 study of Fichten and colleagues** because it is the most thorough. It had three strong features:

1. A relatively **large sample: 239** adult short and long sleepers.
2. A comparison on **48 different measures**, including daytime sleepiness, daytime naps, regularity of sleep times, busyness, regularity of meal times, stress, anxiety, depression, life satisfaction and worrying.
3. Careful **screening out** of volunteers who were **ill or under stress or pressure**, so that the participants were healthy people sleeping the amount that felt right for them.

The findings are captured by the paper's title: **"Long sleepers sleep more and short sleepers sleep less."** Apart from sleep time, there were **no differences** between the groups on any of the other measures, no sign that the short sleepers were suffering in any way. Fichten and colleagues report that this agrees with most previous comparisons (e.g., Monk et al., 2001), **except** for a few studies that **did not screen out** people sleeping little because of stress (worry, illness or demanding work). Those studies did find negative characteristics in their short-sleep groups, which likely reflected the stress some of those participants were under.

This is the stress confound of 14.4 turning up again, and the screening step is the methodological feature that makes the Fichten study credible.

### Long-term reduction of nightly sleep

Are short sleepers simply genetically predisposed, or can average people adapt to a short schedule? Only **two** published studies have had healthy volunteers reduce their nightly sleep for several weeks or longer.

> **Key study, Webb & Agnew (1974).** **16** volunteers slept only **5.5 hours a night for 60 days**. On an extensive battery of mood, medical and performance tests there was **only one detectable deficit**: a **slight deficit on a test of vigilance**.

> **Key study, Friedman et al. (1977); Mullaney et al. (1977).** **Eight** volunteers reduced their nightly sleep gradually:
>
> - by **30 minutes every 2 weeks** until they reached **6.5 hours**;
> - then by **30 minutes every 3 weeks** until they reached **5 hours**;
> - then by **30 minutes every 4 weeks** thereafter.
>
> When a participant indicated no desire to reduce further, he or she spent **1 month** at the shortest duration achieved, then slept at **that duration plus 30 minutes for 1 year**.

Results:

- **Minimum nightly sleep achieved**: **5.5 hours** for **2** participants, **5.0 hours** for **4**, and an impressive **4.5 hours** for **2**.
- In every participant, reduced sleep time came with **increased sleep efficiency**: a **shorter time to fall asleep**, **fewer nighttime awakenings**, and a **higher proportion of slow-wave sleep**. This is the efficiency principle of 14.4, now observed prospectively.
- Once participants reached **6 hours**, they began to experience **daytime sleepiness**, which became a problem as sleep was reduced further.
- Nevertheless, there were **no deficits on any of the mood, medical or performance tests** throughout.
- The most encouraging result: an unexpected **follow-up 1 year after the study** found that **all participants were sleeping less than before the study**, between **7 and 18 hours less each week**, with **no excessive sleepiness**.

> **Trap.** Keep the two studies apart. **Webb & Agnew (1974)**: **16** people, fixed **5.5 h**, **60 days**, one slight **vigilance** deficit. **Friedman / Mullaney (1977)**: **8** people, **stepwise** 30-minute reductions (every **2**, then **3**, then **4** weeks), minima **5.5 / 5.0 / 4.5 h** (2 / 4 / 2 people), follow-up **7 to 18 hours less per week**, not per night.

### Long-term sleep reduction by napping

Most mammals and human infants show **polyphasic sleep cycles**: they regularly sleep **more than once per day**. Most adult humans show **monophasic sleep cycles**: they sleep **once per day**. Even so, most adults display **polyphasic cycles of sleepiness**, with periods of sleepiness in the **late afternoon and late morning**.

Do adults need to sleep in one continuous block, or could they sleep effectively in several naps, as infants and other mammals do? Research shows that **naps have recuperative powers out of proportion to their brevity** (Ru et al., 2019), suggesting that polyphasic sleep might be particularly efficient.

Interest was stimulated by the **legend** that **Leonardo da Vinci** sustained a stream of artistic and engineering accomplishments by **napping for 15 minutes every 4 hours**, limiting his sleep to **1.5 hours a day** (six naps of 15 minutes). As unbelievable as this seems, the schedule has been **replicated in several studies** (**Stampi, 1992**). The main findings:

1. Participants required **several weeks to adapt** to a polyphasic schedule.
2. Once adapted, they were **content** and showed **no deficits** on the performance tests given.
3. Leonardo's **4-hour** schedule worked quite well, but in unstructured working situations (for example, **around-the-world solo sailboat races**) individuals often **varied the cycle duration** without negative consequences.
4. Most people showed a strong preference for particular nap durations (for example, **25 minutes**) and avoided sleeping too little, which left them unrefreshed, or too much, which left them **groggy for several minutes on waking**, an effect called **sleep inertia** (Hilditch & McHill, 2019).
5. When people first adopted polyphasic sleep, **most of their sleep was slow-wave sleep**, but eventually they returned to a **mix of REM and slow-wave sleep**.

The fifth finding is the efficiency principle again: when sleep is scarce, slow-wave sleep is taken first.

The artist **Giancarlo Sbragia (1992)**, who adopted Leonardo's purported schedule, reported (paraphrased) that at first it was difficult and took about **3 weeks** to get used to, but he soon reached a point of comfort and found it thrilling: "I discovered dawns, silence, and concentration", with far more time for painting and his career.

### Effects of shorter sleep times on health

For decades it was believed that sleeping **8 hours or more** a night was ideal for health and longevity. A series of large epidemiological studies around the world challenged this (Liu et al., 2017). These studies **excluded** potential sources of bias, such as people who slept little because they were ill, depressed or under stress; they started with **healthy volunteers** and followed their health for years.

> **Key study, Liu et al. (2017).** A **meta-analysis of 40 studies** that collectively followed **2,200,425 participants** for an average of **12 years**. Mortality at **7 hours** a night was set at **100 percent** and other durations expressed relative to it (Figure 14.15): **4 h 105%, 5 h 106%, 6 h 105%, 7 h 100%, 8 h 104%, 9 h 115%, 10 h 125%, 11 h 138%**.

The authors' reading: **sleeping less than 8 hours a night does not increase the risk of death**, but **sleeping more than 8 hours does, and dramatically**. Look at the bars to see what that means: the short-sleep values sit only 4 to 6 points above the 7-hour reference, a small difference the authors treat as no meaningful increase, while the long-sleep values climb steeply to 138 percent at 11 hours.

Now the essential caveat. These data are **correlational**, so they must not be read causally. They do **not** prove that sleeping 9 or more hours causes health problems; perhaps something about people who sleep that long leads them to die sooner. Nor do they prove that reducing your sleep will make you live longer. What they **do** provide is strong evidence that **sleeping less than 8 hours is not the risk to life and health it is often made out to be**.

Consistent with this, adults in **pre-industrial hunter-gatherer societies** tend to sleep **less than 8 hours**, between **5.7 and 7.1 hours** a day, yet have a **lower incidence of many illnesses** and **higher physical fitness** than people in industrial societies (**Yetish et al., 2015**). The chapter's closing summary puts it plainly: epidemiological studies indicate that people who sleep **between 5 and 7 hours** a night live the longest.

> **Trap.** In Liu et al. (2017) the **reference** is **7 hours = 100 percent**, not 8. The highest bar is **11 hours = 138 percent**. The data are **correlational**: they show short sleep is **not** the danger usually claimed, but they do **not** prove that long sleep causes death or that cutting sleep extends life. Hunter-gatherers: **5.7 to 7.1 hours** (Yetish et al., 2015).

### Long-term sleep reduction: a personal case study

The chapter ends where it began. Pinel started the chapter **4 weeks** earlier with zeal and some trepidation: he hoped to wring 2 or 3 extra hours out of each day, and knew the evidence, but worried about cutting his sleep by **3 hours a night**.

#### The Case of the Author Who Reduced His Sleep

Rather than a **gradual, stepwise** reduction (the Friedman and Mullaney method), Pinel **jumped directly** into a 5-hours-a-night schedule, which proved less difficult than expected. He used a **trip from his home on the West Coast to the East Coast** to reset his circadian clock: in the East he got up at **7:00 a.m.**, which is **4:00 a.m. West Coast time**, and simply kept the same schedule when he got home. He added his extra waking hours to the **beginning** of his day rather than the end, so there would be no temptation to waste them; there are few distractions around a university at 5:00 a.m.

Figure 14.16 records his sleep over the **4 weeks** it took to write a first draft of the chapter. He did not quite meet his goal of less than 5 hours every night, but he did not miss by much: his **overall mean was 5.05 hours** a night. In the **last week** his circadian clock tended to **run a bit slow**: he began sleeping in until **4:30 a.m.** and staying up until **11:30 p.m.**

The positives: the **added time**, an extra **21 hours a week**; and, because his routine was out of step with everybody else's, little time spent in traffic or queues. The only negative was **sleepiness**. It was no problem during the day while he was active, but staying awake in the **last hour before bed**, usually spent in sedentary activities such as reading, was at times a problem. That is when he became personally familiar with **microsleeps** and needed some help to stay awake. Each night of sleep became a highly satisfying but all-too-brief experience.

Notice how the case uses the chapter's own machinery. The eastward trip is a **phase advance**, the hard direction, but he used it to his advantage by simply keeping the advanced schedule at home. The drift later in the final week is what you would expect from a clock whose free-running period is **longer than 24 hours** (14.5). And the evening microsleeps fit the pattern from 14.4: sleepiness concentrated at a particular circadian time, not a general collapse of function.

**The authors' conclusion.** At the start they gave a professorial "it could be this, it could be that" answer to the question of how much sleep we need. After one of them experienced sleep reduction first-hand and they reviewed the evidence, they were less inclined to be non-committal. The fact that **most committed volunteers who are active during the day can reduce their sleep to about 5.5 hours a night** without great difficulty or major adverse consequences suggested to them that the answer is **5.5 hours**, not far from the **6-hour daily requirement** advocated by one esteemed sleep expert (**Horne, 2010, 2011**). However, they reached this conclusion **before** learning about polyphasic sleep schedules. Having done so, they **revise their estimate downward**.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all nine modules. If you can trace each one, you understand the chapter rather than merely remembering it.

1. **Recuperation versus adaptation is the scorecard.** Recuperation theories predict that deprivation produces disturbances that worsen and that lost sleep is repaid. The students' sleepiness cycled and plateaued after the fourth night; Randy Gardner repaid about 6 of his lost hours; free-running sleep comes at clockwork intervals regardless of activity; staying up longer shortens the next sleep (Wever, 1979); sleep time across species tracks vulnerability and feeding time, not energy use. Against that, sleep is nearly universal in mammals and birds and some species pay heavily to get it, so it probably does *something*. The chapter's settled position is a hybrid with an adaptation tilt: a modest core need, with timing and most of the amount set by the clock and by circumstance.
2. **Slow-wave sleep is the protected core; the rest is elastic.** Deprived sleepers regain most lost slow-wave sleep but little else; short sleepers have as much slow-wave sleep as long sleepers; reduced sleepers lose NREM 1 and 2; new polyphasic sleepers take mostly slow-wave sleep. Because sleep becomes more efficient when it is short, short-term deprivation studies cannot measure need, and only long-term reduction studies can.
3. **Sleep behaves like a motivated behaviour with high positive-incentive value.** Miss M. skipped sleep when life was interesting; deprivation effects are subtle compared with deprivation of food or sex; the brain adapts to the amount it habitually gets. This is the same reframing Chapter 12 applied to hunger.
4. **The clock is built in, from genes to the SCN to every cell.** Free-running rhythms without cues, rats raised in constant conditions, the SCN transplant that carries the donor's period, the retinohypothalamic tract with melanopsin ganglion cells, *tau* and *clock*, and molecular clocks in most cells kept in phase by the SCN.
5. **No single centre for a named state.** REM sleep is the coordinated output of separate caudal reticular nuclei, and its parts can come apart: dreaming without REM (antidepressants) and REM without dreaming (cortical lesions); atonia in wakefulness (cataplexy); REM without atonia (REM-sleep behaviour disorder); erections in slow-wave sleep and slow waves in wakefulness during deprivation. Two hypothalamic regions push towards sleep (anterior) and wakefulness (posterior, the home of orexin), and the reticular formation sets the overall level of arousal.
6. **Confounds and correlations everywhere.** Stress confounds human deprivation studies, the carousel experiments and short-sleeper comparisons that fail to screen; Prather's cold study and Liu's mortality meta-analysis are correlational; dream research rests on self-report; insomnia complaints diverge from laboratory records. Much of the chapter is training in asking "what else could explain this?"

The book's own "themes revisited" maps onto these: **thinking creatively** (the 8-hour belief overturned), the **evolutionary perspective** (comparative analysis, adaptation theories), **clinical implications** (the disorders module, especially narcolepsy's genetics and physiology), **neuroplasticity** (the adult brain may adapt to a shorter schedule), and the emerging theme of **consciousness** (sleep as a set of altered states, dreaming and lucid dreaming).

## Worked example

*A 46-year-old schoolteacher is referred to a clinical psychologist for "chronic insomnia". For three years she has taken a benzodiazepine nightly, and her dose has doubled. She says she lies awake "about an hour" and sleeps "maybe four hours". Her husband says she snores loudly, sometimes seems to stop breathing, and kicks him in her sleep, which she does not remember. She is overweight, feels tired by mid-afternoon, and her doctor has told her that anyone sleeping under 8 hours is damaging her health. She asks whether she should increase the dose.*

Reason through it with the chapter rather than reciting facts.

**Is her sleep as short as she says?** Possibly not. In the self-report study, insomnia patients claimed 1 hour to fall asleep and 4.5 hours of sleep but were measured at 15 minutes and 6.5 hours. A laboratory recording (EEG, EOG, EMG) is the first step. But do not repeat the old mistake of labelling a mismatch "neurotic pseudoinsomnia": the patients once given that label often had sleep apnoea or nocturnal myoclonus, and this patient's history points to both.

**What is the drug doing?** Three things, all bad in chronic use. Tolerance explains the doubled dose. Stopping abruptly would cause withdrawal insomnia, which she would read as proof of her disorder, exactly as Mr. B. did with sodium amytal. And benzodiazepines distort sleep, increasing NREM 2 while reducing slow-wave and REM sleep. Raising the dose is the wrong direction; her insomnia may now be partly iatrogenic. Any reduction belongs with her physician, not with a sudden stop.

**What do the snoring and pauses suggest?** Obstructive sleep apnoea: airway obstruction by atonia or spasm, typical of vigorous snorers, more common in overweight people. Each apnoea wakes her briefly. That fits poor-quality sleep at night and, if she is unaware of the awakenings, daytime sleepiness, which is why apnoea can be diagnosed as either insomnia or hypersomnia.

**What about the kicking?** Involuntary leg movements during sleep that she does not remember point to periodic limb movement disorder (formerly nocturnal myoclonus), not restless legs syndrome, whose sufferers are acutely aware of an uneasy feeling that keeps them from falling asleep.

**Is the doctor right that under 8 hours is harmful?** The chapter's evidence says no. Healthy, screened short sleepers do not differ from long sleepers on 48 measures (Fichten et al., 2004); in Liu et al. (2017), mortality is lowest at 7 hours and rises steeply above 8; hunter-gatherers sleep 5.7 to 7.1 hours. Pressure to reach 8 hours makes people spend too long in bed, and the anxiety makes sleep harder.

**What would the chapter recommend?** Evaluate for apnoea and limb movements; review the hypnotic with her physician; advise her to go to bed only when very sleepy; and consider sleep restriction therapy, which cuts time in bed and then extends it in small steps while sleep latency stays normal, using the efficiency principle of 14.4. Melatonin would not be a strong substitute, since its soporific effect is slight; its real use is as a chronobiotic.

## MCQ traps

1. **Three NREM stages, not four.** The 11th edition (AASM; Grigg-Damberger, 2012) scores stages 1 to 3; **old stages 3 + 4 = NREM 3 = slow-wave sleep**. Any "stage 4" in an old key means NREM 3.
2. **Standard measures:** **EEG, EOG, neck EMG**; EMG loss in REM found by **Berger & Oswald (1962)**; REMs discovered in the **1950s**.
3. **Alpha 8 to 12 Hz** (eyes closed, pre-sleep); **spindles 0.5 to 2 s, 11 to 15 Hz** (old editions 12 to 14 Hz); **delta 1 to 2 Hz**; **hippocampal theta 5 to 8 Hz**.
4. **K complexes and spindles = stage 2**; K complex = large **negative (upward)** then large **positive (downward)** wave.
5. **Initial stage 1 is not REM**; only **emergent** stage 1 has REMs and core-muscle atonia.
6. **REM: core muscles atonic, extremities twitch**; cerebral activity at waking levels; penile or clitoral erection.
7. **Dream recall: 80 percent from REM, 7 percent from NREM** (Kleitman's laboratory, 1953 onwards); NREM recall is not zero.
8. **Dement & Wolpert (1958):** water incorporated in **14 of 33**; the most readily incorporated stimulus is **pressure on a limb**.
9. **Dement & Kleitman (1957):** 5 versus 15 minutes judged correctly in **92 of 111**; dreams run in real time or **slightly slower** (movements up to **40 percent** longer; Erlacher et al., 2014).
10. **Sleepwalking: slow-wave sleep, never REM; sleeptalking: any stage**, often at the transition to waking.
11. **REM sleep is not equal to dreaming:** NREM dreams exist; antidepressants abolish REM but not dream recall; cortical lesions abolish dreams but not REM (double dissociation).
12. **Freud:** **manifest** = experienced dream, **latent** = disguised real dream, hidden by the **unconscious censor**; no convincing evidence.
13. **Hobson has two theories:** **activation-synthesis (1989)**, no function, cortex interprets random brain-stem signals; **protoconsciousness (2009)**, dreams simulate everything. **Revonsuo (2000)** simulates **threats** only.
14. **Dreaming brain:** bilateral **temporo-parieto junction** or **medial prefrontal** lesions abolish dreaming; **medial occipital** lesions abolish visual imagery; TPJ linked to REM **and** NREM dreaming (**Siclari et al., 2017**).
15. **Beta-amyloid clearance and synaptic-plasticity restoration are recuperation theories**; adaptation theories (older name: circadian theories) say sleep is clock-timed, like reproductive behaviour.
16. **Energy does not predict species' sleep time** (Siegel, 2005); **giant sloth 20 h**; **horse, zebra, roe deer 2 h**; cat 14; human 8.
17. **Randy Gardner:** **264 h 12 min** (11 days), record had been **260 h**; slept **14 h**, then usual 8; **Dement (1978)**.
18. **Moderate deprivation (3 to 4 h less):** sleepiness, negative mood, impaired **sustained attention**. **Executive function** is susceptible; **logical deduction and critical thinking** are largely immune.
19. **Prather et al. (2015):** **164** volunteers, **under 6 h**, more likely to **develop a cold**; correlational.
20. **Carousel apparatus:** experimental rats died in about **12 days**, yoked controls did not; adrenal swelling, ulcers and bleeding point to **stress**.
21. **REM deprivation:** **REM rebound** for 2 to 3 nights; awakenings needed rose from **17 (night 1) to 67 (night 7)** (**Webb & Agnew, 1967**).
22. **Default theory (Horne, 2013):** REM is the default exit from NREM when **no** bodily needs exist; **Nykamp et al. (1998)**: 15 min of waking per lost REM period gave **no rebound** and no tiredness on 5 h.
23. **Efficiency:** deprived sleepers regain **most lost slow-wave sleep** but little total sleep; short-term deprivation studies cannot measure need.
24. **Free-running period:** typical human **24.2 h**; the Figure 14.8 man **25.3 h** (1.3 h later daily); clock runs **slow**.
25. **Internal desynchronisation:** both cycles **25.7 h**, then sleep-wake **33.4 h**, temperature **25.1 h**: more than one clock.
26. **Wever (1979):** staying awake longer makes the **next sleep shorter**, against recuperation theory.
27. **East = phase advance = harder**; **west = phase delay = easier**; about **8 days** for an 8-hour advance (Seattle to London); shift rotations should **delay**.
28. **SCN lesions abolish timing, not amount**; first clue **Richter (1967)**; rats raised in constant conditions still free-run (**Richter, 1971**).
29. **Ralph et al. (1990):** transplanted SCN impose the **donor's** period (20 h mutant into 25 h normal hamster gives about 20 h, and vice versa).
30. **Optic nerve cut kills entrainment; optic tract cut does not**; pathway = **retinohypothalamic tract**; photoreceptors = **melanopsin retinal ganglion cells**, not rods or cones.
31. ***Tau*** (hamsters, **1988**) first **identified**; ***clock*** (mice) first **characterised (1997)**; *tau* characterised **2000**.
32. **Von Economo:** **posterior** hypothalamus damage gives **excessive sleep** (wake area); **anterior** hypothalamus/basal forebrain damage gives **insomnia** (sleep area); **encephalitis lethargica**, WWI.
33. **Cerveau isolé** (Bremer **1936**, between **inferior and superior colliculi**) = continuous slow-wave sleep; **encéphale isolé** (Bremer **1937**, caudal brain stem) = normal cycles.
34. **Benzodiazepines** (GABA-A agonists): **increase NREM 2, decrease slow-wave sleep and REM**; tolerance, withdrawal insomnia, next-day drowsiness, reduced life expectancy.
35. **Zolpidem** (imidazopyridine) is **not** safer or more effective; **5-HTP** reverses **PCPA** insomnia in animals but **not** human insomnia.
36. **Melatonin** is made from **serotonin** in the **pineal gland**, peaks in **darkness**, has a **slight** soporific effect (19-study meta-analysis) and is best classed as a **chronobiotic**; pinealectomy in adult mammals has **little effect**.
37. **Insomnia self-report gap:** claimed 1 h latency and 4.5 h sleep; measured **15 min** and **6.5 h**.
38. **Mr. B.:** **sodium amytal (barbiturate)**, up to about **1,000 mg** a night; **iatrogenic** insomnia.
39. **Narcolepsy:** about **1 in 2,000**; **10 to 15 min** sleep attacks; only about **1 h** more sleep a day; **cataplexy** while conscious, often emotion-triggered; direct entry into REM.
40. **Orexin = hypocretin**; dog gene (**Lin et al., 1999**) codes the **receptor**; human narcolepsy shows **fewer orexin neurons**, possibly autoimmune; **MZ concordance about 25 percent**.
41. **REM-sleep behaviour disorder** = REM **without atonia**, dream enactment, linked to **Parkinson's disease**; **Lavie et al. (1984)** patient with no REM became a lawyer.
42. **Long-term reduction:** **Webb & Agnew (1974)**, 16 people at 5.5 h for 60 days; **Friedman/Mullaney (1977)**, 8 people down to 5.5/5.0/4.5 h, sleeping **7 to 18 h less per week** a year later.
43. **Liu et al. (2017):** 40 studies, **2,200,425** people, 12 years; reference **7 h = 100 percent**; **11 h = 138 percent**; correlational.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Electroencephalogram (EEG)** | Record of the brain's electrical activity from scalp electrodes; the primary basis for staging sleep |
| **Electrooculogram (EOG)** | Record of eye movements from electrodes around the eyes |
| **Electromyogram (EMG)** | Record of muscle activity; neck EMG indexes core-muscle tone |
| **First-night phenomenon** | Disturbed sleep on a participant's first night in a sleep laboratory |
| **Rapid eye movements (REMs)** | Darting eye movements under closed lids during emergent stage 1 sleep |
| **Alpha waves** | Waxing and waning 8- to 12-Hz bursts as a person relaxes with eyes shut before sleep |
| **K complex** | Stage 2 waveform: a large negative (upward) wave followed by a large positive (downward) wave |
| **Sleep spindle** | Stage 2 waveform: a 0.5- to 2-second burst of 11- to 15-Hz waves |
| **Delta waves** | The largest, slowest EEG waves (1 to 2 Hz); predominate in stage 3 |
| **Initial stage 1 EEG** | The first stage 1 period of the night; no REMs, no loss of core-muscle tone (NREM 1) |
| **Emergent stage 1 EEG** | Later stage 1 periods, accompanied by REMs and core-muscle atonia |
| **REM sleep** | Sleep associated with emergent stage 1 EEG |
| **NREM sleep** | All other sleep: initial stage 1, stage 2 and stage 3 (NREM 1 to 3) |
| **Slow-wave sleep (SWS)** | Stage 3 (NREM 3), characterised by delta waves; the main restorative stage |
| **Somniloquy** | Sleeptalking; any stage, often at the transition to waking |
| **Somnambulism** | Sleepwalking; usually slow-wave sleep, never REM |
| **Lucid dreaming** | Being consciously aware that one is dreaming, sometimes with control over the dream |
| **Manifest / latent dream** | Freud: the dream as experienced / the disguised real dream beneath it |
| **Activation-synthesis hypothesis** | Hobson (1989): dreams are the cortex's attempt to make sense of random brain-stem signals |
| **Evolutionary theory of dreams** | Revonsuo (2000): dreams simulate threats to improve waking responses |
| **Protoconsciousness hypothesis** | Hobson (2009): dreams simulate all experience, training the brain in development and prediction |
| **Temporo-parieto junction** | Cortex where temporal and parietal lobes meet; bilateral lesions abolish dreaming; active in REM and NREM dreams |
| **Recuperation theories of sleep** | Wakefulness disrupts homeostasis and sleep restores it |
| **Adaptation theories of sleep** | Sleep is timed by an internal 24-hour clock that evolved for safety and energy conservation |
| **Executive function** | Problem solving, working memory, decision making and updating plans; susceptible to sleep loss |
| **Microsleeps** | Sleep episodes of about 2 to 3 seconds, with drooping eyelids, during prolonged deprivation |
| **Carousel apparatus** | Rotating-disk device that deprives an experimental rat of sleep while a yoked control receives the same rotations |
| **REM rebound** | More than the usual amount of REM sleep for 2 to 3 nights after REM deprivation |
| **Default theory of REM sleep** | Horne (2013): from NREM the brain switches to waking if needs exist, otherwise to REM |
| **Circadian rhythms** | Biological cycles lasting about a day |
| **Zeitgebers** | Environmental cues ("time givers") that entrain circadian rhythms; chiefly the light-dark cycle |
| **Free-running rhythms** | Circadian rhythms maintained in an environment without zeitgebers |
| **Free-running period** | The duration of a free-running rhythm; about 24.2 hours in humans |
| **Internal desynchronisation** | Breaking apart of two free-running rhythms (e.g., sleep-wake and body temperature) |
| **Jet lag** | Circadian disruption from accelerated (east-bound) or decelerated (west-bound) zeitgebers |
| **Phase advance / phase delay** | Shifting a rhythm earlier (east-bound; harder) / later (west-bound; easier) |
| **Circadian clock** | The internal timing mechanism that controls circadian rhythms |
| **Suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN)** | Medial hypothalamic nuclei above the optic chiasm; the major mammalian circadian clock |
| **Retinohypothalamic tracts** | Axons leaving the optic chiasm for the SCN; carry light information for entrainment |
| **Melanopsin** | Photopigment of the retinal ganglion cells that entrain circadian rhythms |
| **Tau** | Hamster circadian gene (Ralph & Menaker, 1988); first mammalian circadian gene identified |
| **Clock** | Mouse circadian gene; first mammalian circadian gene to be characterised (1997) |
| **Encephalitis lethargica** | Viral brain infection around World War I; von Economo's source of hypothalamic sleep evidence |
| **Cerveau isolé preparation** | Midcollicular transection isolating the forebrain; produces almost continuous slow-wave EEG |
| **Encéphale isolé preparation** | Caudal brain-stem transection; normal sleep-wake EEG cycles preserved |
| **Desynchronised EEG** | Low-amplitude, high-frequency EEG, as in waking and REM sleep |
| **Reticular activating system** | The reticular formation, so named for its role in maintaining wakefulness |
| **PGO spikes** | REM-related EEG spikes recorded in the pons, lateral geniculate and occipital cortex |
| **Hippocampal theta waves** | Hippocampal EEG waves of 5 to 8 Hz during REM sleep |
| **Hypnotic drugs** | Drugs that increase sleep |
| **Antihypnotic drugs** | Drugs that reduce sleep |
| **Benzodiazepines** | GABA-A agonist anxiolytics used as hypnotics (e.g., diazepam, clonazepam) |
| **Imidazopyridines** | GABA-A agonist hypnotics from the early 1990s (e.g., zolpidem); no safer than benzodiazepines |
| **5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP)** | Serotonin precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier; no benefit in human insomnia |
| **Melatonin** | Pineal hormone synthesised from serotonin; peaks in darkness; a chronobiotic |
| **Pineal gland** | Midline gland just ventral to the rear of the corpus callosum; Descartes's "seat of the soul" |
| **Chronobiotic** | A substance that adjusts the timing of internal biological rhythms |
| **Meta-analysis** | A combined analysis of the results of more than one study |
| **Insomnia** | Disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep |
| **Hypersomnia** | Disorders of excessive sleep or sleepiness |
| **Iatrogenic** | Physician-created; e.g., insomnia caused by prescribed hypnotics |
| **Neurotic pseudoinsomnia** | Abandoned label for insomnia complaints in people who sleep over 6.5 hours |
| **Sleep apnoea** | Repeated cessation of breathing during sleep, each ending in a brief awakening |
| **Obstructive / central sleep apnoea** | Airway blocked by spasm or atonia / CNS fails to stimulate breathing |
| **Periodic limb movement disorder** | Involuntary limb (usually leg) movements during sleep; patient unaware (formerly nocturnal myoclonus) |
| **Restless legs syndrome** | Uneasy tension in the legs that prevents falling asleep; patient acutely aware |
| **Sleep restriction therapy** | Reducing time in bed, then increasing it in small steps while sleep latency stays normal |
| **Narcolepsy** | Hypersomnia with brief, inappropriate daytime sleep attacks; about 1 in 2,000 |
| **Cataplexy** | Loss of muscle tone during wakefulness, often emotion-triggered, with consciousness retained |
| **Sleep paralysis** | Inability to move just as one is falling asleep or waking |
| **Hypnagogic hallucinations** | Dreamlike experiences during wakefulness |
| **Orexin (hypocretin)** | Posterior hypothalamic neuropeptide (orexin-A and orexin-B); deficient in narcolepsy |
| **REM-sleep behaviour disorder** | REM sleep without core-muscle atonia; dream enactment; common in Parkinson's disease |
| **Short sleepers / long sleepers** | People sleeping 6 hours or less / 8 hours or more a night |
| **Polyphasic sleep cycles** | Sleeping more than once a day (most mammals, human infants) |
| **Monophasic sleep cycles** | Sleeping once a day (most adult humans) |
| **Sleep inertia** | Grogginess for several minutes after waking from too long a sleep |
