# Chapter 15: Drug Use, Drug Addiction, and the Brain's Reward Circuits

**What this chapter is really about.** Most people arrive at this chapter already holding a theory of addiction. Legal drugs are basically safe and illegal ones dangerous; addicted people are trapped on a chemical treadmill of dose, withdrawal and another dose to stop the misery; cure the withdrawal and you cure the addiction. Pinel and Barnes take that theory apart. Legal status tells you almost nothing about a drug's hazards: the two drugs that kill by far the most people are the two that are legal almost everywhere. Withdrawal is real, but it is not what keeps most addicted people using, which is why detoxification alone has such a dismal record. And tolerance, which looks purely pharmacological, turns out to be shaped by learning: by what the user does while drugged and by where the drug is taken.

What replaces the treadmill is the move the book made for hunger in Chapter 12: people are not mainly *driven* to take drugs by an internal deficit but *drawn* to them by their anticipated effects, their **positive-incentive value**. The second half of the chapter follows that idea into the brain (**intracranial self-stimulation**, the **mesocorticolimbic dopamine pathway**, the **nucleus accumbens**) and then out again, to the modern view that addiction develops in **three stages**, that *wanting* a drug can grow while *liking* it shrinks (**incentive-sensitisation theory**), and that relapse is triggered by stress, by a single dose and, above all, by drug-associated cues.

**How to read this chapter.** 15.1 gives the pharmacological vocabulary (routes, metabolism, tolerance, withdrawal, physical dependence, addiction) that every later section uses; learn the definitions exactly. 15.2 is the conceptual heart of the first half: contingent and conditioned tolerance, Siegel's conditioned compensatory responses, and Ramsay and Woods's reinterpretation. 15.3 is a fact-dense tour of five drugs and is where most numerical MCQs come from. 15.4 asks what the health data can and cannot show. 15.5 covers early brain research on reward, and 15.6 the current stage-based approach, ending with the case of Sigmund Freud. One habit carries you through: whenever a claim about a drug is made, ask whether the evidence is **experimental or correlational**, because the book asks that on almost every page.

## The Case of the Drugged High School Teachers

The chapter opens with scale. Globally, more than **1 billion** people are addicted to nicotine, more than **100 million** to alcohol, more than **5 million** to marijuana, more than **28 million** to illegal drugs, and tens of millions to prescription drugs (Degenhardt et al., 2018; Sinha et al., 2018; Farrell et al., 2019). With figures like these, it is almost certain that the reader, or someone dear to them, will be adversely affected by drugs.

Then comes a warning about preconceptions. Do not assume that a drug's **legal status** says much about its **safety** (Nutt, King, & Nichols, 2013). Most of the laws governing drug use around the world were enacted in the **early part of the 20th century, long before there was any scientific research on the topic**.

The case, told by Pinel (JP) in the first person, makes the point with irony. Invited to address a convention of high school teachers on drug misuse, he was escorted on arrival to a special suite and encouraged to join the executive committee in a round of drug taking, the drug being a special single-malt whiskey. Stepping to the podium under the influence of a psychoactive drug, he looked out through a haze of **cigarette smoke** at educators who had invited him because they were worried about the impact of drugs on their students. The applause gave way to the tinkling of ice cubes in liquor glasses. They did not like what he had to say. The teachers had absorbed the equation of *legal* with *safe*, so their own two drugs, alcohol and nicotine, did not register as drugs at all; 15.4 shows that these are, across populations, the two most harmful drugs in the chapter.

## 15.1 Basic Principles of Drug Action

This module concerns **psychoactive drugs**: drugs that influence subjective experience and behaviour by acting on the nervous system. **Pharmacological** means pertaining to the scientific study of drugs.

### Routes of drug administration

Drugs are usually administered in one of **four** ways: **oral ingestion**, **injection**, **inhalation**, or **absorption through the mucous membranes** of the nose, mouth or rectum. The route determines the *rate* at which and the *degree* to which the drug reaches its sites of action.

**Oral ingestion** is the preferred route for many drugs. Swallowed drugs dissolve in the stomach fluids and are carried to the intestine, where they are absorbed into the blood. Some, **alcohol** being the standard example, pass readily through the **stomach wall** and so act sooner. Drugs not readily absorbed from the gut, or broken down into inactive **metabolites** (breakdown products of the body's chemical reactions) before absorption, must be given another way. Advantages: **ease and relative safety**. Main disadvantage: **unpredictability**, because absorption depends on hard-to-gauge factors such as the amount and type of food in the stomach.

**Injection** is common in medicine because its effects are **strong, fast and predictable**.

| Route | Abbreviation | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Subcutaneous | SC | Into the fatty tissue just beneath the skin |
| Intramuscular | IM | Into the large muscles |
| Intravenous | IV | Directly into veins where they run just beneath the skin |

Many drug-addicted persons prefer **IV** injection because the bloodstream delivers the drug straight to the brain. The speed is a mixed blessing: there is **little or no opportunity to counteract** an overdose, an impurity or an allergic reaction, and repeated injection at the few accessible veins produces **scar tissue, infections and collapsed veins**.

**Inhalation** uses the rich capillary network of the **lungs**; many anaesthetics, tobacco and marijuana are taken this way. Its shortcomings: the dose is **difficult to regulate precisely**, and many substances **damage the lungs** when inhaled chronically.

**Mucous membranes** of the nose, mouth and rectum also absorb drugs. **Cocaine** is commonly snorted through the nasal membranes, which it damages (Walker, Joshi, & D'Souza, 2017).

### Drug penetration, action and elimination

**Penetration of the CNS.** A drug in the bloodstream reaches the blood vessels of the CNS, where the **blood-brain barrier** (Chapter 3) makes it hard for many bloodborne chemicals to pass into the extracellular space around neurons and glia (Sweeney et al., 2019).

**Mechanisms of action.** Some drugs, such as **alcohol** and many **general anaesthetics**, act **diffusely on neural membranes throughout the CNS**. Others act specifically: by binding to particular synaptic receptors; by influencing the synthesis, transport, release or deactivation of particular neurotransmitters; or by influencing the chain of chemical reactions that receptor activation sets off in postsynaptic neurons (Chapter 4).

**Metabolism and elimination.** Most drugs' actions are ended by **enzymes synthesised by the liver**, which convert active drugs to non-active forms: **drug metabolism**. Metabolism often removes a drug's ability to pass through the lipid membranes of cells, so it can **no longer penetrate the blood-brain barrier**. Small amounts of some psychoactive drugs also leave the body in urine, sweat, faeces, breath and **mother's milk**.

### Drug tolerance

**Drug tolerance** is a state of **decreased sensitivity to a drug that develops as a result of exposure to it**. It can be shown in two ways: a given dose has **less effect** than before, or it takes **more drug** to produce the same effect. These are one fact seen from two directions, which the **dose-response curve** (a graph of the magnitude of effect at different doses) makes clear: tolerance is a **shift of the dose-response curve to the right** (Bespalov et al., 2016). Read the shifted curve vertically at a fixed dose and the effect is smaller; read it horizontally at a fixed effect and the required dose is larger.

> **Trap.** Tolerance shifts the dose-response curve to the **right**, not to the left and not downward. A leftward shift (a smaller dose producing the same effect) would be **sensitisation**.

### Three things to know about the specificity of tolerance

1. **Cross tolerance.** One drug can produce tolerance to **other drugs that act by the same mechanism** (e.g., Schiavi et al., 2019).
2. **Tolerance is effect-specific.** It often develops to **some effects of a drug but not to others** (e.g., Castelló et al., 2014). People tolerant to some effects, for example the **nauseating** effects of alcohol, may wrongly assume they are tolerant to all of them, with tragic consequences. Sensitivity to some effects can even **increase** while tolerance develops to others; increasing sensitivity to a drug is **drug sensitisation**. (Cocaine users, in 15.3, become tolerant to its euphoria but sensitised to its motor effects.)
3. **Tolerance is not unitary.** **No single mechanism** underlies all examples of it (Koshimizu et al., 2018; Siciliano et al., 2016); many kinds of adaptive change can reduce a drug's effects.

### Metabolic versus functional tolerance

| Type | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Metabolic tolerance** | Changes that reduce the **amount of drug reaching its sites of action** |
| **Functional tolerance** | Changes that reduce the **reactivity of the sites of action** to the drug |

**Tolerance to psychoactive drugs is largely functional** (Martyn, Mao, & Bittner, 2019). Exposure can **reduce the number of receptors** for the drug, **decrease the efficiency of its binding** to existing receptors, or **diminish the impact of binding** on the cell's activity. At least some of these changes are **epigenetic** (Ghezzi et al., 2013; Liang et al., 2013), the first appearance of a theme that runs through the whole chapter. Alcohol is the worked example (15.3): heavy drinkers' livers metabolise alcohol faster, but this contributes **only slightly**; **most alcohol tolerance is functional**.

### Drug withdrawal effects and physical dependence

After significant amounts of a drug have been in the body for a period (e.g., **several days**), its sudden elimination can trigger an adverse physiological reaction called a **withdrawal syndrome**. Withdrawal effects are **virtually always opposite to the drug's initial effects**: withdrawal of **anticonvulsants** often triggers **convulsions**; withdrawal of **sleeping pills** often produces **insomnia**. Individuals who suffer withdrawal reactions on stopping a drug are **physically dependent** on it.

Why the mirror image? The theory ties withdrawal to tolerance:

1. A drug disturbs nervous-system function in some direction.
2. Exposure produces **compensatory changes** that **offset** the drug's effects; the same dose now does less: **tolerance**.
3. When the drug is eliminated, the compensatory changes **remain**, with no drug left to counteract.
4. Unopposed, they push the system **opposite to the drug**: **withdrawal symptoms**.

So as the adaptations develop, tolerance increases; after the drug is removed, as they subside, withdrawal declines. Tolerance and withdrawal are two faces of one set of adaptations. Withdrawal **severity** depends on the **particular drug**, the **duration and degree** of prior exposure, and the **speed of elimination**: **longer exposure to greater doses followed by more rapid elimination** produces greater withdrawal.

### Drug addiction: what is it?

Learn the definition word for word. **Drug-addicted individuals are habitual drug users, but not all habitual drug users are drug-addicted individuals.** Drug-addicted individuals are habitual users who **continue to use a drug despite its adverse effects on their health and social life** (Heilig et al., 2016; Zakiniaeiz & Potenza, 2018) **and despite their repeated efforts to stop** (Keramati, Ahmed, & Gutkin, 2017).

The definition says nothing about withdrawal, and deliberately so. The **greatest confusion** about addiction, the book says, concerns its relation to **physical dependence**. Many people picture addicted persons on a merry-go-round of drug taking, withdrawal and more drug taking to combat the withdrawal. The picture is simple and inconsistent with the evidence. Addicted people do sometimes take drugs to prevent or relieve withdrawal, but this is often **not the major motive**. If it were, addiction could be cured by **hospitalising people for a few days** until withdrawal subsided; in fact **most addicted individuals renew drug taking even after months of enforced abstinence** (Meye et al., 2017). The argument returns in 15.5 as the reason physical-dependence theories were abandoned.

> **Trap.** **Physical dependence is not addiction.** A patient on long-term medication can be physically dependent without being addicted, and an addicted person can relapse months after all withdrawal has ended. An option equating addiction with withdrawal avoidance reflects the older physical-dependence view the book rejects.

> **Trap (terminology across editions and manuals).** The 11th edition speaks of **drug-addicted individuals** and **drug use**; older editions said **"addict"**, called the five-drug module "Five Commonly **Abused** Drugs" (now "Five Commonly **Used** Drugs"), and titled the chapter without "Drug Use". Separately, DSM-IV split "substance abuse" from "substance dependence", while DSM-5 merges them into **substance use disorder**. Pinel's "physical dependence" is a pharmacological term (withdrawal on stopping), not the DSM-IV diagnosis.

### Addictions beyond drugs

People who risk their health by continually **bingeing on high-calorie foods** (Majuri et al., 2017; Westwater, Fletcher, & Ziauddeen, 2016), or their economic stability through **compulsive gambling** (Clark, 2014; Robbins & Clark, 2015), clearly have an addiction in the same sense. Although the chapter focuses on drugs, **food, gambling and Internet addictions may be based on similar neural mechanisms**, an idea 15.6 develops.

## 15.2 Role of Learning in Drug Tolerance

An important line of psychopharmacological research has shown that **learning plays a major role in drug tolerance**, and that efforts to understand psychoactive drugs **without considering the experience and behaviour of the subjects** can give only partial answers. The research has focused on two phenomena:

| | **Contingent drug tolerance** | **Conditioned drug tolerance** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | What subjects **do** while under the influence | The **situation** in which the drug is taken |
| Claim | Tolerance develops only to drug effects **actually experienced** | Tolerance is maximally expressed only in the **situation where the drug was previously given** |
| Classic study | Pinel, Mana, & Kim (1989) | Crowell, Hinson, & Siegel (1981) |

### Contingent drug tolerance

**Contingent drug tolerance** refers to demonstrations that tolerance develops **only to drug effects that are actually experienced**. Most studies use the **before-and-after design**: two groups receive the **same series of drug injections** and the **same series of tests**, but one group gets the drug **before** each test and the other **after** each test. At the end, all subjects get the same dose followed by a final test, and the drug's disruption of performance is compared. Because pharmacological exposure is identical, any difference in tolerance must come from whether the drug's effect on the tested behaviour was **experienced**.

> **Key study, Pinel, Mana, & Kim (1989).** Two groups of rats received **exactly the same alcohol regimen: one injection every 2 days**. During tolerance development, one group got each injection **1 hour before a mild convulsive amygdala stimulation**, so alcohol's **anticonvulsant** effect was experienced on every trial; the other got each injection **1 hour after** the stimulation, so the effect was never experienced. Finally, all rats got alcohol followed 1 hour later by stimulation. The **before** group had become **almost completely tolerant** to the anticonvulsant effect; the **after** group, with identical injections and stimulations in the **reverse order**, showed **no tolerance whatsoever**.

In the book's figure, the before group's convulsions start almost completely suppressed by alcohol and grow back towards baseline length over the treatment trials as tolerance develops, while the after group convulses at baseline throughout. On the test trial, with alcohol given first to both, the before group convulses nearly as long as at baseline and the after group's convulsions are almost abolished. Contingent tolerance has since been shown for many drug effects and in many species, **including humans** (Wolgin & Jakubow, 2003).

### Conditioned drug tolerance: Crowell, Hinson and Siegel (1981)

**Conditioned drug tolerance** refers to demonstrations that tolerance is **maximally expressed only when a drug is given in the same situation in which it has previously been given** (Castelló, Molina, & Arias, 2017; Siegel, 2011).

> **Key study, Crowell, Hinson, & Siegel (1981).** Two groups of rats received **20 alcohol and 20 saline injections**, alternating, **one injection every other day**. One group got all its alcohol in a **distinctive test room** and its saline in the **colony room**; the other group the reverse. Tolerance to alcohol's **hypothermic** (temperature-reducing) effect was then tested in **both** environments. **Tolerance appeared only in the environment previously paired with alcohol.**

The figure gives the numbers: first injections lowered body temperature by about **1.36 degrees**; after 20 injections, rats tested in the **same** environment showed essentially **no** drop (complete tolerance), while rats tested in a **different** environment dropped about **1.31 degrees**, as if it were their first injection. The swap of rooms across groups rules out any effect of one room as such; the groups differ only in which **cues** have been paired with alcohol. Dozens of further demonstrations show the situational specificity of tolerance to be **large, reliable and general**.

### Siegel's overdose hypothesis

Situational specificity led **Siegel** and colleagues to a hypothesis about **overdose**. Users who repeatedly take a drug **in the same environment** become tolerant there and escalate to **larger and larger doses**. If they take their usual massive dose **in an unusual situation**, the tolerance that normally offsets it is **absent**, and the risk of death rises.

> **Key study, Siegel et al. (1982).** Many more **heroin-tolerant rats died** after a high dose of heroin given in a **novel environment** than after the same dose in their **usual injection environment**. (Heroin kills by **suppressing respiration**.)

### Conditioned compensatory responses

**Siegel** treats each drug administration as a **Pavlovian conditioning trial** (Chapter 5):

| Pavlovian element | In drug taking |
|---|---|
| **Conditional stimuli** | Stimuli that regularly predict the drug: particular rooms, drug paraphernalia, other drug users |
| **Unconditional stimulus** | The drug effects |
| **Conditional responses** | Responses **opposite** to the drug's unconditional effects |

The theory's **central assumption** is that drug-predictive stimuli come to elicit conditional responses **opposite to the drug's unconditional effects**, which Siegel called **conditioned compensatory responses**. With repeated pairings these grow, increasingly counteract the drug, and produce **situationally specific tolerance** (González et al., 2019). In the Crowell study, the alcohol room comes to elicit a conditioned *rise* in temperature that cancels the alcohol's cooling; in Siegel's overdose rats, the novel room fails to elicit the compensatory responses that would have protected breathing.

The book points out that this is the same idea as **Woods's theory of mealtime hunger** (Chapter 12). Stimuli predicting the homeostasis-disrupting effects of a **meal** trigger conditioned compensatory responses that minimise its disruption, just as stimuli predicting a **drug** do.

### Exteroceptive and interoceptive conditional stimuli

Most demonstrations have used **exteroceptive stimuli** (external, public stimuli such as the drug-administration environment) as conditional stimuli. But **interoceptive stimuli** (internal, private stimuli) are **just as effective**: the **thoughts and feelings produced by the drug-taking ritual** and the **drug effects experienced soon after administration** can, through conditioning, come to reduce a drug's impact (Siegel, 2008). Hence **just thinking about a drug can evoke conditioned compensatory responses**, a point that returns in 15.6 as a trigger of craving and relapse.

### Withdrawal effects versus conditioned compensatory responses

Both are **responses opposite to the drug's unconditioned effect**. **Withdrawal effects** are produced by **elimination of the drug from the body**; **conditioned compensatory responses** are elicited by **drug-predictive cues in the absence of the drug**. In complex real-life situations they are **nearly impossible to tell apart**.

### Conditioned drug sensitisation: Anagnostaras and Robinson (1996)

Drug **sensitisation**, like tolerance, can be **situationally specific** (e.g., Carey, 2020; Singer et al., 2014).

> **Key study, Anagnostaras & Robinson (1996).** **10 amphetamine injections, one every 3 or 4 days**, greatly increased amphetamine's ability to activate **motor activity** in rats, but **only when the rats were injected and tested in the same environment** as their previous injections.

Here a drug-paired environment produced a response in the **same** direction as the drug, which Siegel's theory cannot predict.

### Thinking about drug conditioning: Ramsay and Woods (1997)

Conditioned effects are **inevitable** wherever drugs are given repeatedly, but most theories of drug conditioning **have difficulty predicting the direction** of the conditioned effect. Siegel's theory predicts conditioned effects **always opposite** to the drug's, yet conditional stimuli often elicit drug-**like** responses (conditioned sensitisation).

**Ramsay and Woods (1997)** trace the confusion to a misunderstanding of Pavlovian conditioning. The usual assumption is that the **unconditional stimulus is the drug**, and the **unconditional responses are whatever changes the experimenter happens to record**. They argue instead that the **unconditional stimulus is the disruption of neural functioning directly produced by the drug**, and the **unconditional responses are the neurally mediated compensatory reactions** to it, which the experimenter may or may not be recording.

Re-analyse Crowell et al. (1981). Conventionally, **alcohol** is the US and **hypothermia** the UR, so the conditioned warming looks like a response opposite to the UR, which is odd for Pavlovian conditioning. For Ramsay and Woods, the US is the **hypothermia directly produced by alcohol**, and the URs are the **compensatory changes that counteract the fall in temperature**. The conditioned warming is then **similar** to the UR, exactly as salivation to a bell resembles salivation to food. Hence their rule: **once the US and UR are correctly identified, the conditional response is always similar to the unconditional response.**

> **Trap.** Two rules, each correct in its own framework, are often set against each other. **Siegel:** the conditioned response is **opposite to the drug effect**. **Ramsay & Woods:** the conditioned response is **similar to the unconditional response**, because the true UR is the body's compensatory reaction, not the drug effect. "Ramsay and Woods showed conditioned responses are always opposite to the UR" has the rule backwards.

## 15.3 Five Commonly Used Drugs

This module covers the health hazards of chronic use of five commonly used drugs: **nicotine, alcohol, marijuana, cocaine (and other stimulants), and the opioids**. Keep two running comparisons in mind for 15.4: how **addictive** each drug is (the proportion of triers who become addicted), and how **directly harmful** it is to an individual user.

### Nicotine: smoking and vaping

**Nicotine**, the major psychoactive ingredient of tobacco, is most commonly taken by **inhalation** (other routes exist, e.g., nicotine gum). The two methods of inhalation are **smoking** (inhaling the smoke of burning tobacco, as in cigarettes and cigars) and **vaping** (inhaling a nicotine-containing vapour, as from e-cigarettes). Smoking is the more common, but vaping is quickly catching up.

### Tobacco smoking: what is inhaled and what it does

A smoked cigarette delivers nicotine and some **4,000 other chemicals**, collectively called **tar**, through the lungs. Nicotine acts on **nicotinic cholinergic receptors** in the brain (Nees, 2014; Pistillo et al., 2014). Tobacco is the **leading cause of preventable death and disease in developed nations** (Tuesta et al., 2017), contributing to more than **6.5 million deaths a year** worldwide, about **1 in every 10 deaths** (Britton, 2017; Reitsma et al., 2017).

Because considerable tolerance develops to some immediate effects, a cigarette does different things to different people. **Non-smokers** often respond to a few puffs with nausea, vomiting, coughing, sweating, abdominal cramps, dizziness, flushing and diarrhoea. **Smokers** report feeling **more relaxed, more alert and less hungry** (Tuesta et al., 2017). This is the effect-specificity of tolerance from 15.1: tolerance has removed the aversive effects and left the valued ones.

### Long-term hazards of tobacco

- **Smoker's syndrome**: chest pain, laboured breathing, wheezing, coughing, and heightened susceptibility to respiratory infections.
- **Lung disorders**, potentially lethal: **pneumonia**, **bronchitis** (chronic inflammation of the bronchioles), **emphysema** (loss of lung elasticity from chronic irritation) and **lung cancer**.
- **Other cancers**: of the **larynx**, **mouth, oesophagus, kidneys, pancreas, bladder and stomach**.
- **Cardiovascular disease**, which may culminate in **heart attack or stroke**.

### Buerger's disease

**Buerger's disease** occurs in about **15 of every 100,000** people, **mostly male smokers**: the blood vessels, **especially those supplying the legs**, become **constricted**. The book quotes Brecher (1972, pp. 215 to 216). If the patient keeps smoking, **gangrene** may set in: first some toes are amputated, then the foot at the ankle, then the leg at the knee and ultimately at the hip, and gangrene may attack the other leg too. Patients are told that stopping smoking will almost certainly halt this march. Yet surgeons report that it is **not at all uncommon to find a patient with Buerger's disease vigorously puffing away in his hospital bed after a second or third amputation**. That is the definition of addiction from 15.1, continued use despite catastrophic adverse effects, at its starkest.

### Second-hand smoke, pregnancy and the father's nicotine

- **Second-hand exposure.** People who live or work with smokers are more likely to develop **heart disease and cancer**.
- **Pregnancy.** Even the unborn are vulnerable (Abbott & Winzer-Serhan, 2012). Nicotine is a **teratogen**, an agent that can disturb normal fetal development (Jung et al., 2016). Smoking in pregnancy increases the likelihood of **miscarriage, stillbirth, early death of the child, psychiatric disorders in adolescence** (e.g., Hunter et al., 2020) and other consequences (e.g., Baskaran et al., 2019).
- **The father.** Nicotine given to **male rats** can increase **depression and anxiety** in their offspring, in animal models of those conditions (Goldberg & Gould, 2019). This is **transgenerational epigenetics**: the transmission of epigenetic changes to later generations (Chapter 2).

> **Trap.** A **teratogen** harms the fetus *during pregnancy*. A **transgenerational epigenetic** effect passes drug-induced epigenetic changes to later generations; the chapter's examples are mostly through the **father** (male rats), which cannot be teratogenic since the father never carries the fetus. The book gives a paternal-transmission example for **every one** of the five drugs.

### Quitting and nicotine replacement

**Bad news:** treatments for nicotine addiction are only **marginally effective** (Zwar, Mendelsohn, & Richmond, 2014). **Good news:** many people do stop, with **major health benefits**, and even **replacing tobacco with another form of nicotine** (gum, skin patch, mouth spray) is likely to bring major benefits (Fagerström & Bridgman, 2014; Kupferschmidt, 2014).

### Nicotine vaping

In **2003** the pharmacist **Hon Lik** presented the **first e-cigarette** (Löhler & Wallenberg, 2019), marketed for over a decade as a **safer alternative** to tobacco. Current evidence supports the idea that e-cigarettes **can help people quit smoking** (Hajek et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018); but many who start vaping have **difficulty quitting**, and some **move on to smoking tobacco** (Chapman, Bareham, & Maziak, 2019). Is vaping safer? There have **not been enough studies** to say. Reports of **respiratory ailments in heavy vapers** are increasing, and the authors know of **no controlled studies of long-term health outcomes** (Pisinger, Godtfredsen, & Bender, 2019). Youth use has risen fast: in **2016**, **11 percent of American high school students** and **4 percent of middle school students** reported past-month e-cigarette use (Warner & Mendez, 2019).

### Addiction and nicotine

Heavy smokers and vapers are addicted in every sense of the word; the book asks what other psychoactive drug is **self-administered almost continually**, even while walking along the street. **Drug craving**, an affective state of **strong desire for the drug**, is a **major defining feature of addiction**, readily seen in a smoker who has run out of cigarettes or a vaper deprived of nicotine for a few hours. Those who stop experience withdrawal: **depression, anxiety, restlessness, irritability, constipation, and difficulties in sleeping and concentrating**.

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Experimenters with smoking who become addicted | about **68 percent** |
| Compare: alcohol / marijuana | **23** / **9 percent** |
| Speed of onset of nicotine addiction | typically **within a few weeks** |
| Attempts to stop smoking that succeed for **2 years or more** | only about **20 percent** |

(Curran et al., 2016.) The marijuana section adds the matching figure for cocaine: **21 percent**.

> **Trap (older editions).** Older Pinel editions gave different addiction-rate figures (about **70 percent** of experimenters for tobacco, with much lower figures for alcohol, and a heroin figure). The 11th edition uses Curran et al. (2016): **tobacco 68, alcohol 23, cocaine 21, marijuana 9 percent**. The ranking (tobacco far ahead) is unchanged; the numbers and drug list are not.

### Alcohol: scale and basic pharmacology

Alcohol is involved in more than **3 million deaths a year** worldwide (birth defects, ill health, accidents, violence; Degenhardt et al., 2018; Parrott & Eckhardt, 2018), and about **100 million people are heavy users**. Its molecules are **small and soluble in both fat and water**, so they **invade all parts of the body**, which is why the list of alcohol's harms is so long. It is classed as a **depressant** because **at moderate-to-high doses it depresses neural firing**, but at **low doses** it can **stimulate** firing (Alasmari et al., 2018) and facilitate social interaction. **Alcohol addiction has a major genetic component.**

### Acute effects, tolerance and the hangover

- **Moderate doses:** cognitive, perceptual, verbal and motor impairment, and a loss of control that can lead to socially unacceptable actions.
- **High doses:** unconsciousness; at blood levels of **0.5 percent**, risk of **death from respiratory depression**.
- **The red facial flush** comes from **dilation of skin blood vessels**, which increases heat loss and lowers body temperature (**hypothermia**), the effect measured by Crowell and colleagues in 15.2.
- **Diuretic:** alcohol **increases urine production** by the kidneys.

Alcohol produces both **tolerance and physical dependence**. Heavy drinkers' livers metabolise it faster, but this contributes **only slightly**; **most alcohol tolerance is functional**. Even a **single bout** of drinking can produce a withdrawal syndrome of **headache, nausea, vomiting and tremors**, euphemistically called a **hangover**: a hangover is a withdrawal syndrome.

### The full-blown alcohol withdrawal syndrome

Withdrawal after a **long bout of heavy drinking** produces a **full-blown alcohol withdrawal syndrome** with **four phases** (Perry, 2014):

| Phase | Onset after cessation | Defining features |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **6 to 8 hours** | Anxiety, **tremor**, nausea, **tachycardia** (rapid heartbeat) |
| **2** | **10 to 30 hours** | Hyperactivity, insomnia, **hallucinations** |
| **3** | typically **12 to 48 hours** | **Convulsive activity** (the defining feature) |
| **4** | usually begins **3 to 5 days** after; lasts **up to a week** | **Delirium tremens (DTs)**: disturbing hallucinations, bizarre delusions, disorientation, agitation, confusion, **hyperthermia**, tachycardia |

**The convulsions and the DTs can be lethal.** Note the direction: a depressant that calms and cools produces, on withdrawal, hyperexcitability, convulsions and *hyper*thermia.

> **Trap (older editions and keys).** Older Pinel editions described a **three-phase** syndrome (tremor and related signs within hours, convulsions in the second phase, DTs as the third phase starting a day or two after cessation). The **11th edition has four phases**, with **hallucinations first in phase 2** and **DTs as phase 4** (from 3 to 5 days, lasting up to a week). Also keep apart **hypothermia** (alcohol intoxication) and **hyperthermia** (DTs).

### Alcohol and the brain

Alcohol **attacks almost every tissue** (González-Reimers et al., 2014), and chronic use produces **extensive brain damage** (Zahr & Pfefferbaum, 2017), both **directly** (Zahr, Kaufman, & Harper, 2011) and **indirectly** (Weil, Corrigan, & Karelina, 2018).

- **Korsakoff's syndrome** (Chapter 1), a neuropsychological disorder of **memory loss, sensory and motor dysfunction and, in advanced stages, severe dementia**, is caused **indirectly**, by alcohol inducing and interacting with **thiamine deficiency** (Moretti et al., 2017).
- Heavy users **without** Korsakoff's still show brain changes, most commonly a **general loss of cortical white and grey matter** (Everitt & Robbins, 2016).
- Within neurons, alcohol interferes with **second messengers**, disrupts **GABAergic and glutamatergic** transmission, leads to **DNA methylation**, and triggers **apoptosis** (Berkel & Pandey, 2017). It is a **neurotoxin** (Henriques et al., 2018).

### Alcohol and the rest of the body

- **Cirrhosis** (extensive scarring of the liver) is **the major cause of death among heavy users**.
- Alcohol **erodes heart muscle**, raising heart-attack risk.
- It **irritates the digestive tract lining**, raising the risk of **oral and liver cancer, stomach ulcers, pancreatitis** (inflammation of the pancreas) and **gastritis** (inflammation of the stomach).
- **Accidents**: in the United States, more than **10,000 people die each year in alcohol-related traffic accidents** alone.
- **Light drinking is not exempt.** People tend to define drinking "a lot" as drinking much more than they themselves do. Large studies show that even **low-to-moderate regular drinking (a drink or two per day)** is associated with more **breast, oral cavity and colorectal** cancer (Bagnardi et al., 2013; Stone et al., 2014).

### Fetal alcohol syndrome and paternal drinking

Heavy drinking in pregnancy can produce **fetal alcohol syndrome (FAS)**, worldwide prevalence **0.7 percent** (Lange et al., 2017). Its features include some or all of **brain damage, intellectual disability, poor coordination, poor muscle tone, low birth weight, delayed growth and physical deformity** (Landgraf et al., 2014). Because alcohol disrupts brain development in so many ways (e.g., production of **cell-adhesion molecules**, normal patterns of **apoptosis**), there is **no safe time during pregnancy** (Paintner, Williams, & Burd, 2012) and seemingly **no safe amount** (Charness, Riley, & Sowell, 2016). Full-blown FAS is **rare** in babies of mothers who never had more than **one drink a day**, but children of moderate drinkers sometimes show **cognitive problems** without an FAS diagnosis (Hendricks et al., 2019; Koren et al., 2014).

**Paternal effects.** Alcohol also produces **transgenerational epigenetic effects** through the **father** (Kippin, 2014; Vassoler, Byrnes, & Pierce, 2014): offspring of alcohol-consuming **male rats** are impaired on various **cognitive tasks** (Goldberg & Gould, 2019), and there are reports of **children with FAS characteristics whose mothers did not drink but whose fathers were alcoholics** (Chastain & Sarkar, 2017).

### Does moderate drinking protect the heart?

This is the book's best worked example of the trouble with correlational health data.

1. **The claim.** Moderate drinking reduces the risk of **coronary heart disease**, because the disease is **less common among moderate drinkers than among abstainers**.
2. **The first checks.** Knowing that correlation does not establish causation, researchers tried to rule out other factors that might protect moderate drinkers, and seemed to rule out every one.
3. **The flaw.** Where drinking is the norm, **any large group of abstainers includes people who stopped drinking because they are ill**. That may be why abstainers have more heart attacks (Roerecke & Rehm, 2011).
4. **The test.** A meta-analysis used **only studies whose abstainers had never consumed alcohol**. Against **lifelong abstainers**, moderate drinkers had **no less coronary heart disease** (Fillmore et al., 2006; Stockwell, 2012). A later meta-analysis found that moderate drinking **did not reduce mortality** either (Stockwell et al., 2016).

The hypothesis was tested not by a new experiment but by **choosing the right control group**: it predicted that the "protective" effect would vanish against never-drinkers, and it did.

### Marijuana: the plant and its chemistry

**Marijuana** is the common name for the **dried flower buds of female *Cannabis* plants**. **Cannabis**, the common hemp plant, has **three species**: *Cannabis sativa*, *Cannabis indica* and *Cannabis ruderalis*. It is usually **smoked** in a **joint** (marijuana cigarette) or pipe, but it is also effective **orally** if first baked into an **oil-rich substrate** (e.g., a chocolate brownie) to promote absorption from the gut.

Its psychoactive effects are largely attributable to **THC (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol)**, but marijuana contains **more than 80 cannabinoids** (chemicals of the same class as THC), which may also be psychoactive. One, **THCV (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabivarin)**, has **antipsychotic** effects (Chapter 18; Cascio et al., 2014). Most cannabinoids are in a **sticky resin** covering the leaves and flowers; extracted and dried, the resin forms a dark, cork-like material called **hashish**.

### A short history of cannabis and its laws

- **About 11,000 years ago:** archaeological evidence of *Cannabis sativa* use for its psychological effects (Bonini et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2017).
- **6,000 years of written records in China**: stems for **rope**, seeds as **grain**, leaves and flowers for **psychoactive and medicinal** effects.
- **Middle Ages:** cultivation spread into **Europe**, mainly for **rope**; during European imperialism the **American colonies** grew it as a **cash crop** for ships' rope. **George Washington** was one of the more notable growers.
- **Early 20th century:** smoking the flower buds, and the word *marijuana*, reached the **southern United States**.
- **1926:** a **New Orleans** newspaper article exposed the "**menace of marijuana**", and similar stories across the country claimed it turned people into violent, drug-crazed criminals.

The misrepresentation led to the **rapid enactment of laws**. In many states marijuana was legally classified as a **narcotic**, a legal term generally used for **opioids**, and punished accordingly, although **marijuana bears no resemblance to opioid narcotics**. Its legal category was assigned by newspapers and legislators, not by pharmacology: the opening case's point again.

### Effects of marijuana at social and high doses

Popularisation among the **middle and upper classes in the 1960s** triggered a massive research programme. Marijuana's effects are **subtle, hard to measure, and greatly influenced by the social situation**. The book quotes the **National Commission on Marijuana and Drug Abuse (1972, p. 68)**: at low, usual "social" doses there may be an increased sense of well-being, initial restlessness and hilarity followed by a dreamy, carefree relaxation, **expansion of space and time**, more vivid senses, **hunger, especially a craving for sweets**, and subtle changes in thought, yet to an unknowing observer the person would not appear noticeably different.

**High doses** impair **short-term memory** and the ability to carry out **multi-step tasks**; speech becomes **slurred**; a sense of **unreality**, emotional intensification, sensory distortion, **paranoia** and **motor impairment** are common. **Driving** under its influence is obviously dangerous (Neavyn et al., 2014).

### Marijuana: addiction potential, tolerance and withdrawal

Marijuana's **addiction potential is low**: about **9 percent** of users become addicted, against **cocaine 21, alcohol 23 and tobacco 68 percent** (Curran et al., 2016). Most users use **occasionally**, only about **10 percent daily**, and most try it in their **teens** and curtail use by their **30s or 40s**. **Tolerance** develops with sustained use, and **withdrawal** (irritability, nausea, nightmares and other sleep disturbances) appears in about **50 percent of daily users** who stop (Curran et al., 2016).

> **Trap.** Two figures near 10 percent: **9 percent** of users become **addicted**; about **10 percent** use **daily**. And withdrawal is **not** rare in heavy users: about **50 percent of daily users** who stop show it. Older notes calling marijuana withdrawal "rare" do not match the 11th edition.

### Marijuana: health hazards

- **Respiratory:** **bronchitis and coughing** in heavy smokers.
- **Cardiovascular:** increased likelihood of **heart attack** at all ages, **most pronounced in older** users (Singh et al., 2018).
- **Cancer:** **no evidence** of increased risk (Cohen, Weizmann, & Weinstein, 2019).

### Does marijuana cause brain damage?

The claim has been spread by governmental and social agencies trying to discourage use. **No damage reasonably attributable to marijuana** has been found in the brains of living or deceased users (Hall & Degenhardt, 2014). Four lines of **indirect, correlational** evidence bear on the question, each with a weakness:

| Finding | Why it does not establish damage |
|---|---|
| **Hippocampal volume** slightly **reduced** in some heavy users (Batalla et al., 2013) | May reflect **pre-existing differences** between users and non-users (Pagliaccio et al., 2015) |
| **Functional imaging (e.g., fMRI)** shows reliable differences across many tasks | No indication whether the changes are **harmful or beneficial** (Zehra et al., 2018) |
| Heavy users have **memory problems** (Crane et al., 2013) | Deficits **dissipate within about 72 hours** of stopping (Scott et al., 2018) |
| Heavy users are **slightly more likely to be diagnosed with schizophrenia**, especially with **adolescent** onset of use (Radhakrishnan et al., 2014) | Correlational; reasons not yet sorted out (Pasman et al., 2018) |

Until the schizophrenia link is understood, **youths with a family history of schizophrenia should refrain from marijuana** (Burns, 2013): a correlation is enough to justify caution in a high-risk group even though it does not establish cause. The book's conclusion: **there is no convincing evidence that marijuana causes brain damage**. It may even be **neuroprotective**:

> **Key study, Nguyen et al. (2014).** Among adults treated for **traumatic brain injury** (Chapter 10), those who **tested positive for marijuana** were **80 percent less likely to die** from the injury than non-users.

Also, **old mice** perform better in a variety of learning paradigms after a **low dose of THC** (Bilkei-Gorzo et al., 2017). Both findings are suggestive, not conclusive; the TBI result is itself correlational.

### Cannabinoid receptors and anandamide

THC research changed irrevocably in the **early 1990s** with the discovery of **two THC receptors, CB1 and CB2** (Scherma et al., 2019). **CB1** is **one of the most prevalent metabotropic receptors in the brain** (Chapter 4; Parsons & Hurd, 2015) and occurs elsewhere in the body too. **CB2** is found **throughout the CNS and in immune-system cells** (Mechoulam & Parker, 2013).

Why would the brain have receptors for THC? They could hardly have evolved to mediate marijuana smoking. The answer was the discovery of **endogenous cannabinoid neurotransmitters**, the **endocannabinoids** (McPartland et al., 2014). The first to be isolated and characterised was named **anandamide**, from a word meaning "**internal bliss**" (Piomelli, 2014). The logic is the same as for opioids and endorphins below: a plant drug acts on the brain because it mimics a natural neurotransmitter whose receptors were there all along.

> **Trap (older editions).** Older notes place CB2 in the **brainstem** and immune cells. The 11th edition says CB2 is found **throughout the CNS** and in **immune** cells; **CB1** is the one described as among the **most prevalent metabotropic receptors in the brain**.

### Therapeutic effects of THC

- Since the **early 1990s**: suppressing **nausea and vomiting in cancer patients** and stimulating **appetite in AIDS patients** (Robson, 2014).
- Also shown to **block seizures**, **dilate the bronchioles of asthmatics**, **reduce the severity of glaucoma** (raised pressure of the fluid inside the eye), and reduce **anxiety, some kinds of pain and the symptoms of multiple sclerosis** (Koppel et al., 2014).
- **2010:** **Sativex**, a **mouth spray** of THC and other cannabinoids, introduced in several countries for **multiple sclerosis** symptoms (Cressey, 2015), for which it seems effective (Chiurchiù et al., 2018).

### Marijuana: transgenerational effects and a story

Marijuana too produces **transgenerational epigenetic effects** through the father (Szutorisz & Hurd, 2018).

> **Key study, Andaloussi, Taghzouti, & Abboussi (2019).** Offspring of **male rats** given a **CB1-receptor agonist for 20 days** showed **increased vulnerability to stress-induced anxiety**.

The section ends with a story. At a colleague's home, Pinel heard him call his dog to dinner: "**Sativa**, here Sativa." The children had chosen the name, the colleague explained, probably from school, "a Greek goddess or something", and it was catchy: every kid on the street seemed to remember it. Pinel agreed it was very pretty.

### Cocaine and other stimulants: what stimulants are

**Stimulants** are drugs whose primary effect is to produce **general increases in neural and behavioural activity**; they share a profile of effects but differ greatly in **potency**. **Coca-Cola** is a mild commercial stimulant: its action now comes from **caffeine**, but when first introduced it contained small amounts of **cocaine**.

### Forms of cocaine

Cocaine comes from the leaves of the **coca shrub**, which grows mainly in **western South America**.

| Form | How it is made | How it is used |
|---|---|---|
| **Coca paste** | Crude extract made directly from the leaves | **Eaten**, for centuries |
| **Cocaine hydrochloride** | Extracted from treated coca paste; the white powder called simply cocaine | **Snorted** or **injected** |
| **Crack** | Cocaine hydrochloride converted to its **base form** by boiling in **baking soda** solution until the water evaporates; the **impure residue** | **Smoked**; potent and cheap |

Because crack is **impure and smoked**, it is hard to study, so most research uses **pure cocaine hydrochloride**. More than **18 million** people worldwide used cocaine in the past year (Farrell et al., 2019). Cocaine hydrochloride is an effective **local anaesthetic**, once widely prescribed until replaced by synthetic analogues such as **procaine and lidocaine**; but users are not interested in its anaesthetic action.

### Effects of cocaine, sprees and cocaine psychosis

Users report a **wave of well-being**: they feel **self-confident, alert, energetic, friendly, outgoing, fidgety and talkative**, with **less than their usual desire for food and sleep**.

Addicted users tend to go on **cocaine sprees**, binges of extremely high intake lasting **a day or two**. During a spree they become increasingly **tolerant to the euphoria**, so they take **larger and larger doses**; the spree ends when the **cocaine runs out** or **serious toxic effects** begin.

- **Effects of sprees:** sleeplessness, tremors, nausea, **hyperthermia**, and in rare cases psychotic symptoms, **cocaine psychosis**, sometimes **mistaken for schizophrenia** (Chapter 18).
- **Risks:** loss of consciousness, **seizures**, **respiratory arrest**, **heart attack** or **stroke** (Stankowski, Kloner, & Rezkalla, 2014).
- **Tolerance and sensitisation together:** tolerance develops to most effects (e.g., **euphoria**), but repeated exposure **sensitises** users to cocaine's **motor** effects (Li & Wolf, 2012).
- **Withdrawal** after a spree is **relatively mild**: a **negative mood swing** and **insomnia**. A highly addictive drug with mild withdrawal is the first argument against physical-dependence theories in 15.5.

### Amphetamine and its relatives

**Amphetamine and its relatives are currently the most widely misused stimulants** (UN Global ATS Assessment, 2011), more so than cocaine.

- **Amphetamine** has been in wide use since the **1960s**, usually taken **orally** as the potent **d-amphetamine (dextroamphetamine)**; like cocaine it can produce a psychosis, **amphetamine psychosis**.
- In the **1990s** it was supplanted by more potent relatives: **methamphetamine** ("meth"; Hsieh et al., 2014), often in its even more potent **smokeable crystalline** form (**crystal meth**); and **MDMA** (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine, **ecstasy**), taken **orally** (Cole, 2014). MDMA is also an **empathogen**: a psychoactive drug that produces **feelings of empathy** (Bedi, Hyman, & de Wit, 2010).

### How stimulants work: dopamine transporters

Cocaine and its derivatives act mainly by **altering the activity of dopamine transporters**, molecules in the **presynaptic membrane** that normally **remove dopamine from synapses** and return it to presynaptic neurons. (In standard pharmacological terms cocaine **blocks** them, so dopamine lingers in the synapse.) **Other stimulants** act differently, by **increasing the release of monoamines** into synapses (Sitte & Freissmuth, 2015). Reuptake versus release is the distinction a mechanism question will test.

### Long-term health effects of stimulants

- **Cognitive impairments** reported in **methamphetamine and MDMA** users (Parrott, 2013; Moratalla et al., 2017), but often **small or hard to reproduce** (Amoroso, 2015).
- **Methamphetamine and amphetamine users, but not cocaine users**, have a greater risk of **Parkinson's disease** (Callaghan et al., 2012; Curtin et al., 2015).
- **Electrocardiographic abnormalities** in many cocaine-, amphetamine- and methamphetamine-dependent patients (Carvalho et al., 2012).

**All these results are correlational**, so other explanations cannot be excluded (Hart et al., 2012; Krebs & Johansen, 2012).

### Cocaine across generations

> **Key study, Le et al. (2017).** Offspring of **male rats** that had **heavily self-administered cocaine** were more likely to show **increased cocaine self-administration** themselves.

Offspring of cocaine-exposed male rats are also more likely to show **anxiety- and depression-like behaviours** (Goldberg & Gould, 2019).

### The opioids: opium, morphine, codeine and the endogenous opioids

**Opium** is the dried **sap exuded by the seedpods of the opium poppy**. Its most notable psychoactive ingredients are **morphine** and its weaker relative **codeine**; these and other drugs of similar structure or effect are **opioids**. Opioids bind to receptors whose normal function is to bind **endogenous opioids** (Stein, 2016), of two classes: **endorphins and enkephalins** (Chapter 4).

The opioids have a **Jekyll-and-Hyde character**. Dr Jekyll: they are effective **analgesics** (painkillers; Weibel et al., 2013) and extremely effective against **cough and diarrhoea**. Mr Hyde: **the risk of addiction**.

> **Trap (older editions).** Older Pinel editions headed this section "**The Opiates**: Heroin and Morphine". The 11th uses **opioids** throughout, the umbrella term for natural (morphine, codeine), semisynthetic (heroin) and synthetic (fentanyl, oxycodone, methadone) drugs acting at opioid receptors. **Morphine** is the **most potent** constituent of opium; **codeine** the **weaker**.

### A history of opioid addiction

Opioids have been used for euphoria and pain relief for **over 4,000 years** (Darcq & Kieffer, 2018). Three historic events fanned opioid addiction:

1. **1644:** the **Emperor of China banned tobacco smoking**, contributing to a rise in **opium smoking**, spurred by the **British East India Company's** smuggling of opium into China. **Smoking opium affects the brain more than eating it**, so many more people became addicted.
2. **1803:** **morphine** was **isolated**, becoming commercially available in the **1830s**.
3. **1856:** the **hypodermic needle** was invented, and the injured were soon introduced to morphine by injection.

Until the early 20th century opium was legal in much of the world, including Europe and North America: an ingredient of **cakes, candies and wines** and of over-the-counter remedies such as **laudanum** (opium and alcohol), **Godfrey's Cordial** and **Dalby's Carminative**. (Pinel awards *carminative*, a drug that expels gas from the digestive tract, first prize for making a sow's ear sound like a silk purse; *flatulence* takes second.) There were even potions for babies, such as **Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup** and **Street's Infant Quietness**. Pure morphine needed a prescription, but it was prescribed for so many maladies that **morphine addiction was common among those who could afford a doctor**.

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1870** | **Heroin** synthesised by adding **two acetyl groups to morphine**, greatly increasing its **penetration of the blood-brain barrier** |
| **1898** | **Bayer** markets heroin without prescription as a **superior kind of aspirin**: a **more potent analgesic than morphine**, **less likely to cause nausea and vomiting**, and, Bayer claimed on **flimsy evidence**, **not addictive** |
| **1914** | **Harrison Narcotics Act** makes it illegal to sell or use **opium, morphine or cocaine** in the US (morphine still prescribable). **Heroin is not included**, because of the non-addiction claim |
| **1924** | Congress makes it illegal to **possess, sell or use heroin** |

Leaving heroin out of the 1914 Act sent opioid-dependent Americans, barred from opium and morphine, to the **readily available and much more potent heroin**: a law meant to curb addiction pushed users to a stronger drug.

**The modern epidemic.** The laws have been far from successful. US **deaths from opioid overdose now exceed deaths from car accidents** (Manchikanti et al., 2012); more than **53,000 people died of opioid overdose in 2016** (high doses of heroin kill by **suppressing breathing**). Part of the cause is **synthetic opioids such as fentanyl and oxycodone**, **more potent and more addictive than heroin**, many of them marketed as low-risk (Volkow & Koroshetz, 2019). By all accounts we are in an **epidemic of opioid abuse**.

### The heroin rush, tolerance and dependence

The effect users value most is the **rush** after **intravenous injection**: a wave of **intense abdominal, orgasmic pleasure** that evolves into **serene, drowsy euphoria**. Drawn by this, many use more and more often; once they are drugged much of the time, **tolerance and physical dependence develop** and compound the problem. **Tolerance** pushes users to **higher doses**, **more potent drugs** (heroin, fentanyl) and **more direct routes** (IV); **physical dependence** adds to an already high motivation. The pleasure comes first; dependence follows.

### The opioid withdrawal syndrome

- **Onset:** usually **6 to 12 hours** after the last dose; first sign is **restlessness** (pacing, fidgeting).
- **Early:** **watering eyes, running nose, yawning, sweating**; then a **fitful sleep** of several hours.
- **On waking**, in extreme cases: chills, shivering, profuse sweating, **gooseflesh**, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, cramps, **dilated pupils**, tremor, muscle pains and **spasms**.
- **Course:** **most severe on the second or third day**; **all but gone by the seventh**.

The **gooseflesh** and **leg spasms** gave us "**going cold turkey**" and "**kicking the habit**". Overall, opioid withdrawal is **about as serious as a bad case of the flu**. **McKim (1986, p. 199)**: it is one of the most misunderstood aspects of drug use because of its portrayal in films; few users take enough drug to produce the severe symptoms shown; and even at its worst it is **not as dangerous or terrifying as withdrawal from barbiturates or alcohol**.

> **Trap.** **Alcohol** withdrawal (convulsions, DTs) **can be lethal**; **opioid** withdrawal is **about as serious as a bad flu** (McKim, 1986). Students who rely on films reverse this. Note too that opioids **constrict** the pupils while opioid **withdrawal dilates** them: the opposite-direction rule again.

### Direct versus indirect hazards of opioids

**Direct hazards are surprisingly minor**: **constipation, pupil constriction, menstrual irregularity and reduced sex drive**. Many users have taken pure heroin or morphine for years without serious ill effects, and **opioid addiction is more prevalent among doctors, nurses and dentists than among other professionals** (Brewster, 1986). **Julien (1981, p. 117)**: a tolerant, dependent user able to obtain good-quality drug, sterile syringes and needles may keep up social and occupational functioning, stay in fairly good health and suffer little incapacitation. **McKim (1986, p. 197)** gives the example of **Dr William Steward Halsted**, a founder of **Johns Hopkins Medical School** and "**the father of modern surgery**", who was **addicted to morphine** throughout his career, kept it secret from all but his closest friends, and had trouble from his habit **only when trying to reduce his dosage**.

**Indirect hazards are the real danger.** Most medical risks are **not attributable to the drug itself** but arise from the battle between opioids' addictive power and governments' attempts to eradicate addiction by making opioids **illegal**. Treatment programmes report **success rates of only about 10 percent**, so many users cannot quit, and must buy from **illicit dealers at inflated prices**. Those who are not wealthy are trapped in **poverty and petty crime**; they are poor, undernourished and poorly cared for medically, and at great risk of **HIV and other infections from unsterile needles**. They never know what they are injecting: street drugs are poorly processed and almost always **cut** (stretched with some other substance) to an unknown degree. **Strict laws against opioid sales are associated with more overdose deaths**, largely through heroin **cut with more powerful synthetic opioids like fentanyl** (Csete et al., 2016).

### Opioids across generations

Offspring of **male rats** given opioids (1) show **more severe opioid withdrawal** and (2) show **decreased synaptic plasticity** (Goldberg & Gould, 2019).

### Treating opioid addiction: methadone and buprenorphine

The primary treatments for heroin addiction in most countries are **methadone and buprenorphine**, both with a **high and long-lasting affinity for opioid receptors**. Ironically, **both are opioids**, with many of heroin's adverse effects; but because they **produce less pleasure**, the strategy is to block heroin withdrawal with one of them and **maintain** the person on it until they can be **weaned** off.

| | **Methadone** | **Buprenorphine** |
|---|---|---|
| Effectiveness | Has improved the success rate of some programmes | **Considered less effective than methadone** (Mattick et al., 2014) |
| Side effects | Adverse effects **problematic** (Nutt, 2015) | **Fewer** adverse effects (Nutt, 2015) |

> **Trap (older editions).** Older notes call buprenorphine "**as effective as** methadone". The 11th edition: **fewer side effects** but **considered less effective** (Mattick et al., 2014). Both are **opioids**, not opioid antagonists.

### The Swiss heroin-maintenance experiment

In **1994** the **Swiss government** set up clinics where, **as part of a total treatment package**, heroin users could **receive heroin injections from a physician for a small fee**, and it funded a **major research programme** to evaluate them (Khan et al., 2014). The results were **uniformly positive**:

- With a **cheap, reliable source**, most users **gave up their criminal lifestyles**; their **health improved** through contact with the clinics' medical and counselling staff; many **returned to family and jobs**, and many **reduced or stopped** heroin use.
- Heroin use **vanished from Swiss streets and parks**; **drug-related crime fell substantially**; and the **number of new cases of heroin addiction declined** (Csete et al., 2016).

Similar programmes in **Canada, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland and the United Kingdom** have had similar success. In a large **randomised trial**, supervised heroin treatment was **more effective and less costly than methadone** (Nosyk et al., 2012), and **safe injection facilities** have reduced the **spread of infection** (Southwell et al., 2019) and **overdose deaths** (Csete et al., 2016). The result fits the chapter's argument: if most opioid harm is **indirect**, removing the conditions of illegal use while leaving the drug in place should reduce harm, and it did, without the feared rise in new addiction. The book leaves you to consider why some governments have not adopted such programmes.

## 15.4 Comparing the Health Hazards of Commonly Used Drugs

This module compares the hazards of the five drugs directly, but first asks how far any of the hazard claims can be trusted.

### Interpreting studies of the health hazards of drugs

The repeated disclaimer of 15.3 is that the evidence is **correlational**, and two problems follow.

**Problem 1: users differ from non-users in more than drug use.** Most studies compare known users with non-users, so one can **never be certain** that a health difference is due to the drug rather than some other difference between the groups (recall the hippocampus and moderate-drinking examples).

**Problem 2: who gets recruited.** Most studies recruit users from **addiction treatment clinics**, and these are typically **the most severely addicted**. Samples from the **general population** give different answers (Krebs & Johansen, 2012):

| Sample recruited from | Typical conclusion |
|---|---|
| **Addiction treatment clinics** | Drug addiction is **difficult to treat** |
| **General population** | Many users **successfully treat their own addictions without professional help** and live productive lives (Blanco et al., 2013; Chapman & MacKenzie, 2010) |

This is a **selection bias**: people who reach clinics are, almost by definition, those who could not stop alone. The book illustrates it with an anonymous excerpt from a former student of Barnes (SB) who had dropped out of high school 17 years earlier and, for **3 years, used methamphetamine almost daily, mostly by injection**. He never lost his job or house, had no psychiatric symptoms and received no drug-related medical treatment, though the drug permeated his life. Falling in love with his future wife gave him the motivation to **stop using regularly**; until they decided to have a child about **5 years later**, he used meth at parties **no more than twice a year**. More than a decade after dropping out he enrolled at university and got good grades. Someone like him never appears in a clinic sample.

## 15.5 Early Biopsychological Research on Addiction

The module opens with a question asked twice before in the book, about **eating** (Chapter 12) and **sleep** (Chapter 14): are addicted individuals **driven** to take drugs by an internal need, or **drawn** to them by anticipated positive effects?

### Physical-dependence theories of addiction

Early explanations attributed addiction to **physical dependence**. According to **physical-dependence theories of addiction**, dependence traps users in a **vicious circle** of drug taking and withdrawal: once intake is high enough to induce dependence, users are **driven by their withdrawal symptoms** to take the drug whenever they try to cut down. Early treatment programmes therefore tried to break the circle by **gradually withdrawing drugs** in a **hospital** (**detoxification**). Unfortunately, **once discharged, almost all detoxified users return to their former habits**.

### Why detoxification failed

The book gives **two reasons** the failure is unsurprising:

1. **Some highly addictive drugs, such as cocaine and amphetamines, do not produce severe withdrawal distress.** If withdrawal drove addiction, these should be weakly addictive.
2. **Many habitual users already cycle through binges and detoxification.** Some choose **weekend binges** to fit their work schedules; some **cannot afford** continuous use; some have the pattern forced on them by **repeated failed attempts to quit**. Whether by choice or necessity, **detoxification does not stop them renewing the habit**.

The second reason is the decisive one. Detoxification removes physical dependence; if dependence caused addiction, every binge-and-detox user would be cured at the end of every dry spell. They are not.

### Positive-incentive theories of addiction

In the **1970s and 1980s** a different approach came to predominate (Higgins, Heil, & Lussier, 2004): most addicted individuals take drugs **not to escape or avoid withdrawal but to obtain the drugs' positive effects**. **Positive-incentive theories of addiction** hold that the primary factor in most addiction is **the craving for the drug's positive-incentive (expected pleasure-producing) properties**. Physical dependence **does play a role**, since users sometimes take a drug to relieve withdrawal; but most researchers now assume that the **more important factor is the drugs' hedonic (pleasurable) effects** (Cardinal & Everitt, 2004; Everitt, Dickinson, & Robbins, 2001). The early brain research that follows was largely built on this view.

### Intracranial self-stimulation

When addiction research turned to positive incentives in the 1970s and 1980s, its starting point was what had been learned about the neural basis of pleasure from **intracranial self-stimulation (ICSS)**: rats, humans and many other species will administer **brief bursts of weak electrical stimulation to specific sites in their own brains**. The sites that support it are often called **pleasure centres**.

> **Key study, Olds & Milner (1954).** The **discoverers of ICSS** argued that the sites mediating self-stimulation are those that **normally mediate the pleasurable effects of natural rewards** (food, water, sex). Researchers then used self-stimulation of various sites to **map the neural circuits of pleasure**.

ICSS was **initially assumed to be unitary**, with the same properties wherever the electrode was placed. Most early studies stimulated the **septum** or **lateral hypothalamus**, because rates there are spectacularly high: rats typically press **thousands of times per hour**, stopping only when **exhausted**; self-stimulation of many other structures has also been documented. In the apparatus, a lever press activates a stimulator that delivers a brief pulse through an implanted electrode, the lead suspended by an elastic band so the rat moves freely.

### The mesotelencephalic dopamine system

The **mesotelencephalic dopamine system**, dopaminergic neurons projecting from the **mesencephalon (midbrain)** into regions of the **telencephalon**, plays an important role in ICSS. Its cell bodies lie in **two midbrain nuclei**, the **substantia nigra** and the **ventral tegmental area (VTA)**. Its axons project to specific regions of the **prefrontal cortex**, the **limbic cortex**, the **olfactory tubercle**, the **amygdala**, the **septum**, the **dorsal striatum** and, in particular, the **nucleus accumbens**, a nucleus of the **ventral striatum** (Kringelbach & Berridge, 2012).

| Pathway | Cell bodies | Main target | Known for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nigrostriatal pathway** | **Substantia nigra** | **Dorsal striatum** | Degeneration associated with **Parkinson's disease** (Chapter 10) |
| **Mesocorticolimbic pathway** | **Ventral tegmental area** | Various **cortical and limbic** sites | Mediating **ICSS** and reward |

There is **some intermingling** of neurons between the two pathways.

> **Trap.** **Substantia nigra → dorsal striatum = nigrostriatal** (Parkinson's). **VTA → cortical and limbic sites, including nucleus accumbens = mesocorticolimbic** (reward). Swapping the two nuclei is the classic error. The **nucleus accumbens is ventral striatum**, which matters in 15.6 when control shifts to the **dorsal** striatum.

> **Trap (terminology).** Older texts and keys often call the reward pathway the **"mesolimbic"** pathway (VTA to nucleus accumbens) and treat the **"mesocortical"** pathway (VTA to prefrontal cortex) separately. The 11th edition uses one term, **mesocorticolimbic pathway**, for VTA projections to both cortical and limbic targets. "Mesolimbic" in an option names the same core circuit in older language.

### Four lines of evidence linking the mesocorticolimbic pathway to self-stimulation

1. **Many brain sites that support self-stimulation are part of the mesocorticolimbic pathway.**
2. **ICSS is often associated with increased dopamine release in the pathway.**
3. **Dopamine agonists tend to increase ICSS, and dopamine antagonists tend to decrease it.**
4. **Lesions of the mesocorticolimbic pathway tend to disrupt ICSS.**

The four are of four kinds, **anatomical, neurochemical, pharmacological and lesion**, and converging evidence of different types is stronger than repeated evidence of one type, because each method has different weaknesses.

### Measuring the rewarding effects of drugs

In the **1970s**, after much research on dopamine and ICSS, experiments began to implicate dopamine in the rewarding effects of **natural reinforcers and addictive drugs (mainly stimulants)**. They used, mostly in non-humans, one of **two methods** still in use today (Badiani et al., 2011; Kuhn, Kalivas, & Bobadilla, 2019).

**Drug self-administration paradigm.** Animals **press a lever to inject drugs into themselves** through implanted **cannulas** (thin tubes). They readily learn to self-administer **intravenous** injections of drugs to which **humans become addicted** (O'Connor et al., 2011). Self-administered **microinjections directly into particular brain structures** have been particularly enlightening, because they show *where* a drug is rewarding.

**Conditioned place-preference paradigm.** Animals repeatedly receive a drug in **one compartment** of a **two-compartment box**. At test, the **drug-free** rat is placed in the box and the **proportion of time it spends in the drug compartment**, versus the **equal-sized but distinctive control compartment**, is measured. Subjects usually prefer compartments paired with drugs to which humans become addicted. The **main advantage**: testing is **drug-free**, so the measure of incentive value is **not confounded by other effects of the drug on behaviour** (a drug that stimulated or sedated movement could alter lever-pressing for reasons unrelated to reward).

### Early evidence that dopamine mediates drug reward

These methods showed that **dopamine plays an important role in the rewarding effects of addictive drugs and natural reinforcers**. In rats, **dopamine antagonists blocked self-administration of, or conditioned place preference for, several addictive drugs**, and **reduced the reinforcing effects of food**. Dopamine seemed to signal **something akin to reward value or pleasure**, a claim 15.6 revises.

### The nucleus accumbens and drug addiction

Exploring particular sites in the mesocorticolimbic pathway soon focused attention on the **nucleus accumbens**: events there, and in its **dopaminergic input from the VTA**, seemed most clearly related to reward and pleasure. Four kinds of findings in laboratory animals pointed there (Deadwyler et al., 2004; Nestler, 2005; Pierce & Kumaresan, 2006), most from studies of **stimulants**:

1. Animals **self-administered microinjections of addictive drugs directly into the nucleus accumbens**.
2. **Microinjections of addictive drugs into the nucleus accumbens produced a conditioned place preference.**
3. **Lesions of the nucleus accumbens or the VTA blocked** self-administration of drugs into **general circulation**, or the development of drug-associated place preferences.
4. **Both drug self-administration and natural reinforcers were associated with elevated extracellular dopamine in the nucleus accumbens.**

Findings 1 and 2 show the accumbens is **sufficient** (drug delivered there alone is rewarding by both measures); finding 3 shows it and its VTA input are **necessary** (destroy them and drug delivered anywhere stops being rewarding); finding 4 shows **drug and natural reward share a neurochemical signature** there. Together they made the VTA-to-accumbens dopamine projection the leading candidate for the brain's reward circuit, with the caveat, which 15.6 takes up, that the evidence came mostly from stimulants.

## 15.6 Current Approaches to the Mechanisms of Addiction

### How current thinking evolved

Figure 15.9 summarises the major shifts in thinking about the brain mechanisms of addiction. Modern theory came from **two lines of research**, both rooted in the **1950s** work on **drug tolerance and physical dependence**.

- **Line 1 (reward).** Tolerance and dependence research developed (1960s) into **physical-dependence theories of addiction**, simple but inconsistent with the evidence. The inconsistencies produced (1970s) **positive-incentive theories**, which combined with research on **dopamine and intracranial self-stimulation** to yield (1980s and 1990s) the focus on the **mesocorticolimbic pathway and the mechanisms of reward**.
- **Line 2 (conditioning).** The same starting point led (1970s) to the discovery that drug cues elicit **conditioned compensatory responses** through Pavlovian conditioning, and that these are largely responsible for **functional tolerance**. This gained prominence (1980s and 1990s) when conditioned responses to drug cues proved to be **major factors in drug craving and relapse**. **Relapse** is the **return to one's drug-taking habit after a period of voluntary abstinence**.

Together the two lines shaped **21st-century theories** of the brain mechanisms of addiction. Line 1 is 15.5 extended; Line 2 is 15.2 extended. The current approach needs both.

### Three stages in the development of an addiction

Modern approaches increasingly try to model each of **three stages** in the development of an addiction: **(1) initial drug taking, (2) habitual drug taking, and (3) drug craving and repeated relapse**. The framework replaces one question ("what causes addiction?") with three, each with different answers: why does someone try a drug, why do some triers become habitual users, and why do people who have stopped start again?

### Stage 1: Initial drug taking

Not everyone given access to a drug takes it, and many who do **never take it more than once**. **Price and availability, peer pressure and prior life experiences** are well-known factors in initial use, and rat self-administration studies point to others:

| Facilitate acquisition of self-administration | Protect against it |
|---|---|
| **Food restriction** (Carroll & Meisch, 1984) | **Environmental enrichment** (Puhl et al., 2012) |
| **Social stress** (Ahmed, 2005) | **Social interaction** (Raz & Berger, 2010) |
| **Environmental stress** (Ambroggi et al., 2009; Lu et al., 2003) | **Access to non-drug reinforcers** (Ahmed, Lenoir, & Guillem, 2013) |

Deprivation and stress on one side, richness of alternatives on the other: the same symmetry returns for relapse and for the critique of the self-administration paradigm.

**Traits.** Rats that **prefer sweetened water** and rats **more active in a novel environment** are both more likely to **self-administer cocaine**, traits likened to **novelty seeking**, which is associated with initial drug taking in humans.

**Drugs as instruments.** Viewing drugs as **tools or instruments** (Müller & Schumann, 2011) makes the question simple: people first take a drug to see **whether it is useful** to them (or to confirm rumours that it is), and continue if they **find some use in it** (Boys & Marsden, 2003). Barnes (SB) has **coffee** before driving to campus to stay alert: **caffeine** as an instrument, and other **stimulants** and **nicotine** are used the same way. Low-dose **alcohol** is used to ease **social interaction and sexual intercourse** (Patrick & Maggs, 2009), and alcohol and **marijuana** to relieve **stress and anxiety**. People with medical conditions **self-medicate**: many with **schizophrenia** use **nicotine** to relieve **cognitive impairments** and **anhedonia** (a general inability to experience pleasure) (Rezvani & Levin, 2001). And many use drugs **simply for pleasure** (Hart, 2013).

### Stage 2: Habitual drug taking

Habitual use is necessary for addiction but **not the inevitable outcome** of taking a drug; many people use addictive drugs periodically and enjoy their hedonic effects **without becoming habitual users** (Badiani et al., 2011). Positive-incentive theories face two problems here:

1. They have trouble explaining **why some users become habitual and others do not**.
2. They cannot explain the **big discrepancy between the hedonic value (liking) and the positive-incentive value (wanting)** of the drug in many addicted people.

| Term | Definition | Everyday word |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive-incentive value** | The **anticipated** pleasure associated with an action (e.g., taking a drug) | **Wanting** |
| **Hedonic value** | The amount of pleasure **actually experienced** | **Liking** |

Addicted individuals often report being **compulsively driven** by the drug's positive-incentive value (they **want** it) although taking it is no longer as pleasurable as it was (they no longer **like** it) (Everitt & Robbins, 2016).

### Incentive-sensitisation theory

**Incentive-sensitisation theory** answers both problems (Berridge, Robinson, & Aldridge, 2009). Its **central tenet**: **the positive-incentive value of addictive drugs increases (becomes sensitised) with repeated use in addiction-prone individuals** (Miles et al., 2004; Robinson & Berridge, 2008), making them highly motivated to seek and take the drug. Its **key point**: it is **not the pleasure (liking)** of the drug that underlies habitual use and addiction but the **anticipated pleasure, the wanting or craving**.

How it resolves the two problems:

1. **At first**, a drug's positive-incentive value is closely tied to its pleasurable effects.
2. With repeated use, **tolerance often develops to the pleasurable effects** (liking falls).
3. Meanwhile, in addiction-prone individuals, **craving is sensitised** (wanting rises).
4. So the drug's positive-incentive value becomes **out of proportion to the pleasure actually derived**: many addicted people are miserable, their lives in ruin, the drug effects no longer great, **yet they crave the drug more than ever**. That is the **liking-wanting discrepancy**.
5. Because sensitisation occurs in **addiction-prone** individuals, the theory also explains **why only some users become habitual**.

> **Trap.** In incentive-sensitisation theory **wanting** (positive-incentive value) **sensitises** while **liking** (hedonic value) tends to show **tolerance**. An option saying addiction is maintained by increasing **pleasure** from the drug reverses the theory.

> **Trap (dates in older keys).** The theory is credited to **Robinson and Berridge**, whose original paper dates from **1993**, the date older notes give. The 11th edition cites it via **Robinson & Berridge (2008)** and **Berridge, Robinson, & Aldridge (2009)**. Keep it distinct from Berridge's positive-incentive theory of **eating** (Chapter 12).

### The neural circuitry of wanting and liking

There is now **general agreement that wanting and liking have different circuitry**, and that **dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens (via the mesocorticolimbic pathway) is more closely associated with *wanting* a drug than with *liking* it** (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015; Kringelbach & Berridge, 2012). For example, **neutral stimuli that signal impending reward** (food or an addictive drug) are **sufficient to trigger dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens** (Floresco, 2015): dopamine is released by anticipation, before any pleasure is experienced. The circuitry of liking is still being mapped, but **dopamine is less important for it than once believed**, and its structures **only partially overlap** with those for wanting (Liu et al., 2011).

> **Trap.** The 15.5 conclusion was that dopamine signals "something akin to reward value or pleasure". The 11th-edition position is that accumbens dopamine tracks **wanting** (anticipation, incentive value) **more than liking** (pleasure). "Dopamine is the pleasure chemical" is the outdated answer.

### Brain changes in the transition to habitual use

1. **A shift within the striatum.** In addicted individuals, striatal control of drug taking **shifts from the nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum) to the dorsal striatum** (Pierce & Vanderschuren, 2010), an area involved in **habit formation and retention** (Chapter 11).
2. **Prefrontal impairment.** At the same time, impaired **prefrontal cortex** function likely relates to the **loss of self-control** in addiction (George & Koob, 2010; Goldstein & Volkow, 2011; Volkow & Warren, 2011).

These differences were originally thought merely to **accompany** the transition; **Ersche and colleagues (2012)** showed that they **predispose** an individual to it (Volkow & Baler, 2012). (The book does not describe the design; the study is usually summarised as comparing stimulant-dependent people with their non-dependent biological siblings, who shared some of the same brain abnormalities, which points to predisposition rather than drug damage.)

### Addiction as a general problem of self-control

Drug addiction is increasingly seen as **one expression of a more general behavioural problem: the inability to refrain from a behaviour despite its adverse effects**. Addicted individuals make **poor decisions**, take **excessive risks** and show **deficits in self-control** (Volkow et al., 2013). **Overeating, compulsive gambling, kleptomania (compulsive shoplifting) and compulsive shopping** have received attention as addictive behaviours (Jabr, 2013; Potenza, 2015; Robbins & Clark, 2015).

### Anhedonia in habitual users

Habitual users often develop **anhedonia**, a general inability to experience pleasure **from natural reinforcers** (Ahmed, 2005; Robbins, 2016): **sex, eating and sleeping** become less pleasurable. This devaluation of natural reinforcers **persists after the drug is stopped**, and without natural pleasures many abstinent users experience **craving** and **relapse**. Anhedonia is the bridge from stage 2 to stage 3.

### Stage 3: Drug craving and relapse

**Addiction is not necessarily a life sentence.** Many addicted people stop or reduce their intake on their own (Klingemann, Sobell, & Sobell, 2009), others with a treatment programme. But weeks, months or years later some abstainers experience **craving**, which can lead to **relapse**. **The propensity to relapse, even after long voluntary abstinence, is a hallmark of addiction**, so understanding relapse is a key to understanding addiction and its treatment.

### Three causes of relapse

Three causes have been identified (Badiani et al., 2011; Pickens et al., 2011):

1. **Stress** (Moschak et al., 2018). Illustration: **marked increases in cigarette, alcohol and marijuana use by New Yorkers after the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001** (Vlahov et al., 2004).
2. **Drug priming**: **a single exposure to the formerly misused drug**. Abstainers who feel their addiction is under control sample the drug **just once** and are plunged back into **full-blown addiction**.
3. **Drug-associated cues** (people, times, places, objects) (Everitt & Robbins, 2016; Milton & Everitt, 2012; Steketee & Kalivas, 2011). Illustration: **many US soldiers addicted to heroin in the Vietnam War easily shed the addiction on returning home**, attributed to **removal from their drug-associated environment**.

### How cues trigger craving: back to conditioned compensatory responses

Cues that predict a drug come to elicit **conditioned compensatory responses**, which, being opposite to the drug's effects, produce **tolerance** (15.2). The new point is that these same responses **seem to increase craving in abstinent addicted individuals and so trigger relapse**; and since interoceptive cues work too, **just thinking about a drug can induce craving and relapse**. The Vietnam finding follows: in the war zone a whole environment of cues had been paired with heroin; at home those cues, and the responses they elicit, were absent.

### The incubation of drug craving

**Timing** matters: cues presented **soon after withdrawal** are **less likely** to elicit craving and relapse than cues presented **later**. This **time-dependent increase in cue-induced craving and relapse** is the **incubation of drug craving** (Pickens et al., 2011).

> **Key finding, incubation of cocaine craving (Figure 15.11; data from Pickens et al., 2011).** Rats that had self-administered cocaine were presented with a **drug-associated cue** at intervals after withdrawal, and presses on the lever that had always delivered cocaine were counted. **Shortly after withdrawal** the cue elicited **few** presses (under 50 on day 1); pressing **rose steadily** across days 2, 4, 7, 15 and 30, reaching well over **200 presses by day 60**.

Incubation has also been shown with **heroin, methamphetamine, alcohol and nicotine** (Pickens et al., 2011), and may explain why some apparently recovered people experience craving and relapse **even after years of abstinence** (Bedi et al., 2011). It overturns a natural assumption: withdrawal peaks and fades within days, but cue-induced craving **grows** with abstinence, so the most dangerous period for relapse can come much later. That is one more reason physical-dependence theories fail.

### Enrichment and non-drug reinforcers protect against relapse

1. **Environmental enrichment after withdrawal** reduced **cue- and stress-induced** relapse of drug self-administration, **but not drug-priming-induced** relapse (Chauvet et al., 2009, 2012).
2. **Even a few brief exposures to non-drug reinforcers** reliably reduce relapse of **cocaine** self-administration (Liu & Grigson, 2005; Quick et al., 2011).

Perhaps the best way to prevent relapse is therefore to **improve recovered individuals' opportunities and surroundings**, and such interventions have succeeded (Cao et al., 2011; Dutra et al., 2008; Friedmann et al., 2004).

### Current concerns about the drug self-administration paradigm

Much of the neurobiology of addiction comes from animal self-administration studies. Two sets of recent findings challenge their relevance.

**1. Unnatural housing and testing conditions.** Conventionally, rats are **housed individually** and tested in a **barren chamber** where the only rewarding thing to do is press a lever for drug. Rats that were **group housed** (Raz & Berger, 2010), given **enriched environments** (Nader et al., 2008; Solinas et al., 2010), offered **non-drug reinforcers** (Ahmed, 2010, 2012), or housed in a **large naturalistic environment** (Alexander et al., 1981, the experiments widely known as "Rat Park") were **much less likely to self-administer drugs**.

> **Key study, Lenoir et al. (2007).** Given a **choice between consuming sucrose and self-administering cocaine**, only **10 percent** of rats preferred cocaine.

Some researchers therefore question the relevance of conventional self-administration research to human addiction (Ahmed, Lenoir, & Guillem, 2013). Viewed another way, the findings suggest that human addiction **might be prevented by improving the environment and life choices** of the most vulnerable.

**2. Excessive focus on stimulants.** Addiction mechanisms were long assumed to be **independent of the specific drug** (Badiani et al., 2011), so most self-administration studies used **stimulants**, and two major conclusions followed: **(1) all addictive drugs activate the mesocorticolimbic pathway**, and **(2) dopamine is important for the reinforcing properties of all addictive drugs**. **Opioid** studies led to **different conclusions** (Nutt et al., 2015): **mesocorticolimbic lesions or dopamine antagonists disrupt habitual cocaine self-administration but did not disrupt habitual heroin self-administration** (Badiani et al., 2011).

> **Trap (older editions and keys).** The classic claim, still in older notes, is that **all addictive drugs act by raising dopamine in the nucleus accumbens**. The 11th edition flags this as built mostly on **stimulant** studies: mesocorticolimbic lesions and dopamine antagonists **disrupt cocaine but not heroin** self-administration. "Dopamine is essential for the reinforcing effects of all addictive drugs" is no longer a safe answer.

### A noteworthy case of addiction: Sigmund Freud

The chapter ends with one drug-addicted individual, **Sigmund Freud**, whose case matters for **two reasons**: it shows that **nobody, however powerful their intellect, is immune** to addiction, and it allows a **comparison between the two drugs** he had problems with, **cocaine** and **nicotine**.

### The case of Sigmund Freud

**Cocaine.** In **1883** a German army physician prescribed cocaine, then recently isolated, to **Bavarian soldiers** to help them cope with military manoeuvres. Reading of this, Freud obtained some, took it himself and **pressed it on friends and associates**, for themselves and their patients; he even sent some to his **fiancée**. By today's standards, the book says, he was **a public menace**. His essay on cocaine, "**Song of Praise**", published in **July 1884**, was so glowing that it created a **wave of interest** in the drug. **Within a year** came a critical reaction: as evidence mounted that cocaine was **highly addictive** and produced a **psychosis-like state at high doses**, so did criticism of Freud. He kept praising cocaine until the **summer of 1887**, then **suddenly stopped all use**, personal and professional. **After 3 years of use, he seems to have had no difficulty stopping.**

**Nicotine.**

| Year / age | Event |
|---|---|
| **1894, age 38** | His physician and close friend ordered him to stop smoking because it was causing a **heart arrhythmia**. A heavy smoker of about **20 cigars a day**, he stopped, then **started again 7 weeks later** |
| Another occasion | Stopped for **14 months** |
| **Age 58** | Still smoking **20 cigars a day**, still struggling; wrote that smoking harmed his heart and his work, yet kept smoking |
| **1923, age 67** | **Sores in his mouth** proved **cancerous**; after oral surgery he wrote that smoking caused his cancer, yet kept smoking; severe heart pains (**tobacco angina**) whenever he smoked, still he kept smoking |
| **Age 73** | **Hospitalised** for his heart; stopped and recovered immediately; **started again 23 days later** |
| **1936, age 79** | More heart trouble; **33 operations** for recurring oral cancer; **jaw entirely removed and replaced by an artificial one**; in constant pain, able to swallow, chew and talk only with difficulty, yet he kept smoking |
| **1939** | **Died of cancer** (Sheth, Bhagwate, & Sharma, 2005) |

**What the case shows.** The drug Freud stopped easily (cocaine) is the one now illegal and feared; the drug that disfigured and killed him (tobacco) is legal: the opening case's lesson in one life. The difference matches the 15.3 addiction rates (**68 percent** for tobacco, **21 percent** for cocaine). His relapses after **7 weeks**, **14 months** and **23 days** came long after any nicotine withdrawal had passed, so withdrawal was not what held him. And continued use despite arrhythmia, angina, cancer, 33 operations and the loss of his jaw is the **definition of addiction** from 15.1, demonstrated more starkly than any definition could.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the six modules. Trace each one and the chapter becomes one argument rather than a list of drugs.

1. **Legal status is not pharmacology.** The opening case, marijuana's classification as a "narcotic", the Harrison Act's heroin loophole, Figure 15.5 and Freud's two drugs all make the same point: drug laws were written before the science, and the legal drugs (tobacco, alcohol) do the most harm.
2. **Physical dependence is not addiction.** Relapse after months of enforced abstinence, the failure of detoxification, highly addictive drugs with mild withdrawal (cocaine, amphetamines), opioid withdrawal no worse than flu, and Freud's relapses all show that withdrawal avoidance is a minor motive.
3. **The body compensates, and learns to compensate in advance.** Withdrawal is the unopposed mirror image of tolerance; conditioned compensatory responses make tolerance situation-specific (Crowell et al., 1981), make overdose likelier in new settings (Siegel et al., 1982), and, as cues and thoughts, drive craving and relapse. Woods's mealtime hunger (Chapter 12) is the same mechanism.
4. **Driven versus drawn.** As with eating and sleep, the positive-incentive view replaced the deficit view; incentive-sensitisation theory then split positive-incentive value (wanting) from hedonic value (liking), and accumbens dopamine turned out to track wanting more than liking.
5. **Correlation is not causation.** Moderate drinking and the heart, marijuana and the hippocampus, stimulants and cognition, clinic samples versus population samples: almost every human hazard claim is correlational, and the book shows how to test one (the never-drinker control group).
6. **Environment and evolution.** Brain reward mechanisms evolved for natural reinforcers and have been co-opted by drugs. Enrichment, social contact and non-drug rewards protect against initial use and relapse, the Swiss clinics reduced harm by changing users' circumstances, and the transgenerational epigenetic effects of every drug show that a user's environment can reach the next generation.

## Worked example

*A 30-year-old man has injected heroin daily for four years. He has been through hospital detoxification twice and relapsed within weeks each time, both times soon after returning to the flat where he used to use with friends. Six months ago he nearly died after injecting his usual dose in a cousin's house in another city. He says heroin "barely does anything now" but he thinks about it constantly. He also smokes about 20 cigarettes a day and drinks heavily at weekends. His family want him kept in hospital "until the withdrawal is completely out of his system", and they regard his smoking as harmless "because at least it's legal".*

**Will a longer detoxification cure him?** No. Detoxification removes physical dependence, and the book's case against physical-dependence theories is that most addicted people **renew drug taking even after months of enforced abstinence**. Opioid withdrawal peaks on day 2 or 3 and is all but gone by day 7; his relapses happened after it had ended.

**Why did he relapse at the old flat?** **Drug-associated cues**, one of the three causes of relapse. Siegel's account explains how: cues repeatedly paired with heroin elicit **conditioned compensatory responses**, which in an abstinent person **increase craving**. The Vietnam veterans who shed heroin addiction on coming home show the converse. Incubation adds a warning: cue-induced craving **grows** over weeks and months of abstinence, so the risk does not fade when withdrawal does.

**Why did he nearly die in his cousin's house?** **Siegel's overdose hypothesis.** His tolerance was partly conditioned to his usual setting. In a novel setting the conditioned compensatory responses were absent, so his usual (large, tolerance-escalated) dose met less opposition, and heroin kills by **suppressing respiration** (Siegel et al., 1982: more heroin-tolerant rats died after a high dose in a novel environment).

**Why does he crave a drug that "barely does anything"?** **Incentive-sensitisation theory.** Tolerance has reduced the drug's **hedonic value** (liking) while its **positive-incentive value** (wanting) has been sensitised. Accumbens dopamine tracks the wanting, so drug cues trigger strong motivation even when the drug no longer pleases.

**What treatment does the chapter support?** **Methadone** or **buprenorphine** maintenance (both opioids that block withdrawal and give less pleasure; buprenorphine has fewer side effects but is considered less effective), or, where available, **supervised heroin treatment**, which Nosyk et al. (2012) found more effective and less costly than methadone; the Swiss programme cut crime and new addiction by removing the **indirect** hazards of illegal use. Relapse prevention should change his surroundings: **environmental enrichment** reduced cue- and stress-induced relapse in rats, and non-drug reinforcers reduce relapse of cocaine self-administration.

**Is his smoking harmless because it is legal?** No. Tobacco is the most addictive drug in the chapter (**68 percent** of experimenters) and causes about **6.5 million deaths a year**. Legal status reflects early 20th-century law, not research.

**Anything urgent about the weekend drinking?** If he drinks heavily for a long bout and stops abruptly, the **alcohol withdrawal syndrome** (convulsions at 12 to 48 hours, DTs from 3 to 5 days) **can be lethal**, unlike opioid withdrawal. Any supervised withdrawal must account for the alcohol, not only the heroin.

## MCQ traps

1. **Legal status ≠ safety**: most drug laws predate scientific research (early 20th century); tobacco (6.5 million deaths/year) and alcohol (3 million) are legal.
2. **Tolerance = rightward shift** of the dose-response curve (same dose less effect, or more dose for same effect); leftward = sensitisation.
3. **Cross tolerance** = tolerance to other drugs acting by the **same mechanism**; tolerance can develop to some effects while **sensitisation** develops to others (cocaine: tolerance to euphoria, sensitisation to motor effects).
4. **Metabolic** (less drug reaches site) vs **functional** (site less reactive); psychoactive drug tolerance is **largely functional**, including alcohol's.
5. **Withdrawal effects are opposite** to drug effects: anticonvulsant withdrawal → convulsions; sleeping pills → insomnia; opioids constrict pupils, withdrawal dilates them.
6. **Physical dependence ≠ addiction**; addiction = continued use despite adverse health and social effects **and** repeated efforts to stop.
7. **Pinel, Mana & Kim (1989)**: alcohol **anticonvulsant** effect, amygdala stimulation, injection every 2 days, 1 h before vs 1 h after; identical exposure; only the **before** group became tolerant (contingent tolerance, before-and-after design).
8. **Crowell, Hinson & Siegel (1981)**: 20 alcohol + 20 saline, every other day, distinctive room vs colony room; **hypothermic** effect; tolerance only in the alcohol-paired room (first injections about 1.36 degrees of cooling; after 20 injections about 0 in the same room vs 1.31 in a different room).
9. **Siegel et al. (1982)**: more heroin-tolerant rats died after a high dose in a **novel** environment.
10. **Conditioned compensatory responses** (Siegel) are opposite to the drug effect; **Ramsay & Woods (1997)**: US = drug-induced neural disruption, UR = compensatory reaction, so the CR is always **similar to the UR**.
11. **Interoceptive** (thoughts, early drug effects) cues work as well as **exteroceptive** (rooms, paraphernalia) ones: just thinking about a drug can elicit CCRs.
12. **Anagnostaras & Robinson (1996)**: 10 amphetamine injections, every 3 or 4 days; **sensitisation** of motor activity is situation-specific.
13. **Nicotine** acts on **nicotinic cholinergic receptors**; tar = about **4,000** other chemicals; about **1 in 10** deaths worldwide.
14. Addiction rates (Curran et al., 2016): **tobacco 68, alcohol 23, cocaine 21, marijuana 9 percent**; only about **20 percent** of quit attempts last 2 years; older editions used about 70 percent for tobacco.
15. **Buerger's disease**: about 15 per 100,000, mostly **male smokers**, leg vessels constrict, gangrene; patients smoke after amputations (Brecher, 1972).
16. **Hon Lik, 2003**, first e-cigarette; **2016**: 11 percent of US high school and 4 percent of middle school students vaped in the past month.
17. **Alcohol withdrawal, 11th ed. = four phases**: 6 to 8 h (tremor, tachycardia), 10 to 30 h (hallucinations), 12 to 48 h (convulsions), DTs from 3 to 5 days lasting up to a week (hyperthermia); older editions had three.
18. Alcohol is a **depressant** at moderate-to-high doses but **stimulates** at low doses; **0.5 percent** blood level risks death by respiratory depression; flush → **hypothermia**; **diuretic**.
19. **Korsakoff's** is caused **indirectly** via **thiamine deficiency**; **cirrhosis** is the major cause of death in heavy drinkers.
20. **FAS** prevalence **0.7 percent**; no safe time, no safe amount; FAS-like features reported with alcoholic **fathers**.
21. Moderate drinking does **not** protect the heart: abstainer groups include **ill former drinkers**; against **lifelong abstainers**, no benefit (Fillmore et al., 2006).
22. **THC** = delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol; **THCV** is antipsychotic; **hashish** = dried resin; **narcotic** is a legal term for opioids.
23. Marijuana: memory deficits fade in about **72 hours**; withdrawal in about **50 percent** of daily users; **no** cancer link; **no convincing** brain damage; TBI patients positive for marijuana **80 percent** less likely to die (Nguyen et al., 2014).
24. **CB1** = among the most prevalent metabotropic receptors in the brain; **CB2** = throughout CNS and immune cells; **anandamide** = "internal bliss"; **Sativex** (2010) for MS.
25. **Cocaine** acts on **dopamine transporters** (reuptake); other stimulants increase **release**; **amphetamines** are the most widely misused stimulants; **MDMA** is an **empathogen**.
26. **Parkinson's** risk is raised in **methamphetamine/amphetamine** users, **not cocaine** users.
27. Heroin: synthesised **1870**, marketed by **Bayer 1898**, omitted from the **Harrison Act 1914**, banned **1924**; morphine isolated **1803**; hypodermic needle **1856**; China tobacco ban **1644**.
28. **Opioid withdrawal** starts 6 to 12 h, peaks day **2 to 3**, gone by day **7**; like **flu**, less dangerous than alcohol or barbiturate withdrawal (McKim, 1986).
29. Opioid **direct** hazards minor; addiction more common in **doctors, nurses, dentists**; **Halsted** ("father of modern surgery") was a secret morphine addict; treatment success about **10 percent**.
30. **Buprenorphine**: fewer side effects but **less effective** than methadone (11th ed.); both are **opioids**.
31. **Swiss heroin clinics (1994)**: crime fell, health improved, **new cases declined**; Nosyk et al. (2012): supervised heroin more effective and cheaper than methadone.
32. **Nigrostriatal** = substantia nigra → dorsal striatum (Parkinson's); **mesocorticolimbic** = VTA → cortical/limbic sites incl. **nucleus accumbens** (ventral striatum); older keys say "mesolimbic".
33. **Olds & Milner (1954)** discovered ICSS; early sites **septum** and **lateral hypothalamus**, thousands of presses per hour.
34. **Conditioned place preference** tests the animal **drug-free**, so incentive value is not confounded by other drug effects.
35. Incentive-sensitisation: **wanting sensitises, liking shows tolerance**; accumbens dopamine tracks **wanting** more than liking.
36. Habitual use: control shifts **ventral → dorsal striatum**; prefrontal impairment; **Ersche et al. (2012)**: differences **predispose**.
37. Relapse causes: **stress** (9/11 New Yorkers), **drug priming** (single dose), **cues** (Vietnam veterans); **incubation**: cue-induced craving **increases** with time (cocaine, heroin, meth, alcohol, nicotine).
38. Enrichment after withdrawal reduces **cue- and stress-induced** relapse, **not priming-induced** (Chauvet et al.); **Lenoir et al. (2007)**: only **10 percent** of rats chose cocaine over sucrose.
39. Mesocorticolimbic lesions or dopamine antagonists disrupt **cocaine** but **not heroin** self-administration: dopamine is not essential for all addictive drugs.
40. **Freud**: stopped **cocaine** easily after 3 years ("Song of Praise", July 1884); could not stop **cigars** (20 a day) despite cancer from 1923, **33 operations** and an artificial jaw; died **1939**.
