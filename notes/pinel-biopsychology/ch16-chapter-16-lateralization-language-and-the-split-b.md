# Chapter 16: Lateralization, Language, and the Split Brain

**What this chapter is really about.** You almost certainly experience yourself as one person with one mind. This chapter asks you to look at that experience very carefully, because the organ that produces it is built in two halves. The two cerebral hemispheres look alike but do not do the same things; the study of those differences is **lateralisation of function**. The chapter has two great stories. The first is the **split brain**: patients whose hemispheres have been surgically disconnected (by **commissurotomy**) behave, in the laboratory, as if two separate minds were living in one skull, each with its own perceptions, memories and intentions. The second is **language**, the most strongly lateralised of all cognitive abilities,, and the effort to locate it within the left hemisphere, which produced the **Wernicke-Geschwind model**, still prominent in classrooms and clinics but largely abandoned by researchers. The chapter shows you why.

The common thread is a warning about popular psychology's tidy "left-brained versus right-brained" story. The evidence says that lateralisation is real but **statistical rather than absolute**, that what is lateralised is probably small individual cognitive processes rather than big clusters of abilities, and that language is carried out by many small, scattered, variable patches of cortex rather than by a few large centres joined by arrows.

**How to read this chapter.** 16.1 covers discovery (Dax, Broca, Liepmann), measurement (lesions, amytal, dichotic listening, imaging), handedness and sex differences. 16.2 is the split brain; read it slowly. 16.3 lists hemispheric differences and anatomical asymmetries; 16.4 asks why lateralisation and language evolved. 16.5 and 16.6 build and then test the Wernicke-Geschwind model; 16.7 and 16.8 present the cognitive neuroscience approach, ending with dyslexia and a girl who reads with only her right hemisphere. The single most useful habit for this chapter: whenever a stimulus or a response is mentioned, ask yourself **which hemisphere** it reaches or comes from.

## Opening: One Skull, Two Hemispheres

Apart from a few midline openings, the body has two of almost everything, and this **bilateral duplication** holds even for the brain. In its upper reaches the brain consists of two structures, the left and right **cerebral hemispheres**, and these are completely separate except for the **cerebral commissures** that connect them.

The commissures you should be able to name are those labelled in the book's figure: the **corpus callosum** (by far the largest, the one that matters most in this chapter), the **anterior commissure**, the **posterior commissure**, the **hippocampal commissure**, and the **massa intermedia**. The **optic chiasm** is shown too because it is a second route by which visual information reaches the opposite hemisphere, crucial in the split-brain experiments.

Two foci dominate: **split-brain patients**, whose hemispheres have been separated by **commissurotomy**, and the localisation of language, the most highly lateralised ability, within the left hemisphere. The authors warn that your hemispheres can have different thoughts, memories and emotions, which challenges your sense of being a unitary self.

> **Trap.** Therapeutic commissurotomy typically cuts the **corpus callosum** and spares the smaller commissures, and the hemispheres can still communicate indirectly through the brainstem. "Cuts all connections between the hemispheres" is wrong.

## 16.1 Cerebral Lateralisation of Function: Introduction

### Marc Dax (1836): the report nobody noticed

In **1836**, **Marc Dax** presented a short report at a medical society meeting in France, his first and only scientific presentation. He had noticed something striking in his own clinical experience: of the **40 or so** brain-damaged patients with speech problems he had seen during his career, **not a single one** had damage restricted to the **right** hemisphere. The report aroused little interest, and Dax died the following year without knowing he had anticipated one of the most important areas of modern neuropsychology.

Why was it ignored? Not because the observation was wrong, but because of the intellectual climate. Most of Dax's contemporaries believed that **the brain acted as a whole** and that specific functions could not be attributed to particular parts of it. A one-sided speech centre had nowhere to fit.

### Paul Broca (1861 and 1864): aphasia and the left frontal lobe

The holistic view began to change **25 years later**, in **1861**, when **Paul Broca** reported his post-mortem examinations of **two aphasic patients**. **Aphasia** is a deficit, produced by brain damage, in the ability to produce or comprehend language.

Both patients had a lesion in the **left** hemisphere that involved an area of the **frontal cortex just in front of the face area of the primary motor cortex**. Notice what Broca did *not* see at first: he did not recognise any relation between aphasia and the **side** of the damage, and he did not know of Dax's report. His first claim was about *where along the front-back axis* the damage lay, not *which side*. By **1864**, however, he had examined **seven more** aphasic patients post-mortem, and he was struck by the fact that, like the first two, all had damage to the **inferior prefrontal cortex of the left hemisphere**. That region had by then become known as **Broca's area**: inferior left prefrontal cortex, just anterior to the face area of the left primary motor cortex.

> **Trap.** Dax (1836) first noted the left-hemisphere link; Broca (1861, two patients; 1864, seven more) gets the credit, and his 1861 report was about **location within the frontal lobe**, not side.

### Hugo-Karl Liepmann: apraxia is also a left-hemisphere matter

A second example of lateralisation followed in the **early 1900s**. **Hugo-Karl Liepmann** found that **apraxia**, like aphasia, is almost always associated with **left-hemisphere damage**, despite the fact that its symptoms are **bilateral** (they involve both sides of the body). Apraxic patients have difficulty performing movements when asked to perform them **out of context**, even though they often have no difficulty performing the same movements when they are not thinking about doing so (the book refers you back to Chapter 8 for detail).

The bilateral symptoms matter: since most motor control is **contralateral**, a hemisphere whose damage disturbs **both** sides must play a higher-level role in organising voluntary movement. This returns in 16.3 (ipsilateral movement) and 16.4 (Kimura's motor theory).

### The theory of cerebral dominance

Put Broca and Liepmann together and you have evidence that the left hemisphere plays a special role in **both language and voluntary movement**. The combined impact led to the **theory of cerebral dominance**: one hemisphere, usually the left, assumes the dominant role in the control of **all** complex behavioural and cognitive processes, and the other plays only a minor role. From this came the habit of calling the left hemisphere the **dominant hemisphere** and the right hemisphere the **minor hemisphere**.

The theory was too strong, and much of the chapter is a correction of it. But it did something valuable: the discovery that language and motor abilities are lateralised to the left hemisphere triggered a search for **other** lateralised functions, and in effect established lateralisation of function as a major area of neuroscientific research.

> **Trap.** "Dominant" and "minor" are labels of the outdated cerebral dominance theory; the "minor" right hemisphere is superior at some spatial, emotional, musical and nonverbal-memory tasks.

### Tests of cerebral lateralisation: the toolkit

Early research compared the effects of **left-hemisphere and right-hemisphere lesions**. Other techniques are now also used. The book describes three in this module: the **sodium amytal test**, the **dichotic listening test**, and **functional brain imaging**. A fifth method, the study of **split-brain patients**, is the subject of 16.2. By the start of 16.3 you should be able to list all five.

### The sodium amytal test (the Wada test)

The **sodium amytal test** of language lateralisation was introduced by **Wada (1949)**, which is why you will also hear it called the **Wada test**. It is often given to patients **before neurosurgery** (Bauer et al., 2014). The neurosurgeon uses the result to plan the operation so as to avoid damaging cortex likely to be involved in language.

The procedure works by temporarily switching off one hemisphere. A small amount of **sodium amytal** (a fast-acting anaesthetic drug) is injected into the **carotid artery on one side of the neck**. The carotid on each side supplies mainly the hemisphere on the same side, so the injection **anaesthetises the hemisphere on that side for a few minutes**, which lets the examiner assess what the **other** hemisphere can do on its own. During that window the patient is asked to recite well-known series (letters of the alphabet, days of the week, months of the year) and to name pictures of common objects. Then the other carotid is injected and the test is repeated.

- When the hemisphere **specialised for speech** (usually the left) is anaesthetised, the patient is rendered **completely mute for a minute or two**, and once speech returns there are **errors of serial order and naming**.
- When the **other** hemisphere (usually the right) is anaesthetised, **mutism often does not occur at all**, and errors are few.

The test is **invasive** and used only in patients facing surgery.

> **Trap.** Sodium amytal is injected into the **carotid artery** on **one** side, and it anaesthetises the hemisphere on the **same** side (ipsilateral to the injection), not the opposite one. The tell-tale sign of injecting the speech-dominant side is **mutism lasting a minute or two**, followed by serial-order and naming errors.

### The dichotic listening test

Unlike the sodium amytal test, the **dichotic listening test** is **non-invasive**, so it can be given to healthy people. In the standard version, **three pairs of spoken digits** are presented through earphones, and the two digits of each pair are presented **simultaneously, one to each ear** (Blass et al., 2015; Kimura, 2011). For example, a person might hear 3, 9, 2 through one ear and at the same time 1, 6, 4 through the other. The person is then asked to report all the digits.

**Kimura** found that most people report **slightly more digits from the right ear** than from the left. That pattern indicates **left-hemisphere** specialisation for language. The crucial validation came from patients who had already had the sodium amytal test: Kimura found that **all** the patients identified by the amytal test as having **right-hemisphere** specialisation for language performed better with the **left** ear. In other words, the ear advantage tracks the language hemisphere, on the opposite side, and it flips when the language hemisphere flips.

Why does the opposite ear win? Kimura argued that sounds from each ear project to **both** hemispheres, but the **contralateral** connections are **stronger** and **take precedence** when two sounds compete simultaneously for the same cortical auditory centres. The advantage is small (about **55 to 45 percent**, 16.3) and reverses for **melodies** (Kimura, 1964).

> **Trap.** The dichotic right-ear advantage does **not** mean the right ear is connected only to the left hemisphere. Each ear projects to **both** hemispheres; the contralateral projection is merely **stronger** and **wins under simultaneous competition**. If an option says "because auditory pathways are completely crossed", reject it.

### Functional brain imaging

Lateralisation can also be studied with **functional brain-imaging** techniques. While a volunteer engages in some activity, such as reading, brain activity is monitored by **positron emission tomography (PET)** or **functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)**. On language tests these techniques typically reveal **far greater activity in the left hemisphere than in the right** (Bauer et al., 2014). Like dichotic listening, imaging can be used in healthy people. Its limitation (16.7) is that it shows **correlation**, not causation.

| Method | Invasive? | Who can be tested | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unilateral lesions (left vs right) | Not applicable (natural or surgical damage) | Brain-damaged patients | Which side's damage disrupts a function |
| Sodium amytal (Wada) test | Yes (carotid injection) | Pre-surgical patients | Which hemisphere is necessary for speech |
| Dichotic listening | No | Anyone | Ear advantage reflecting the contralateral hemisphere |
| Functional imaging (PET, fMRI) | No (fMRI); PET uses a radioactive tracer | Anyone | Which hemisphere is more active during a task |
| Split-brain studies (16.2) | Surgery already done for epilepsy | Commissurotomy patients | What each disconnected hemisphere can do alone |

### Speech laterality and handedness

Once lateralisation could be measured, an obvious question was whether it relates to **handedness**. Two large **lesion studies** clarified the relation. One studied military personnel who had suffered brain damage in **World War II** (**Russell & Espir, 1961**). The other studied neurological patients who had undergone **unilateral excisions** for the treatment of neurological disorders (**Penfield & Roberts, 1959**). Both produced remarkably similar figures. The book uses **dextrals** for right-handers and **sinestrals** for left-handers.

| Group | Aphasic after left-hemisphere lesion | Aphasic after right-hemisphere lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Dextrals (right-handers) | about 60% | about 2% |
| Sinestrals (left-handers) | about 30% | about 24% |

So the left hemisphere is dominant for language in **almost all dextrals** and in **the majority of sinestrals**, but for sinestrals the margin is narrow. The key conclusion is that sinestrals are **more variable (less predictable)** than dextrals in the hemisphere to which language is lateralised. This greater variability is not limited to language: sinestrals also show greater variability in the lateralisation of **attention** and **face recognition** (Vingerhoets, 2019).

> **Trap.** Left-handers are **not** mirror-reversed: most are still **left**-hemisphere dominant for language; they are simply **more variable**. The 30 and 24 percent figures are aphasia rates after left vs right lesions, not proportions with left-hemisphere speech.

**Results of the sodium amytal test** have confirmed the lesion findings. **Milner (1974)** reported three key figures:

- Almost all **right-handed** patients without early left-hemisphere damage had **left-hemisphere** specialisation for speech: **92 percent**.
- Most **left-handed and ambidextrous** patients without early left-hemisphere damage also had left-hemisphere specialisation for speech: **69 percent**.
- **Early left-hemisphere damage** decreased left-hemisphere specialisation for speech in left-handed and ambidextrous patients: only **30 percent**.

The third figure shows plasticity: early left damage makes left-hemisphere speech much less likely. (The book's review quiz rounds 69 to "70 percent of left-handers".)

> **Trap.** Memorise Milner's triplet as **92 / 69 / 30**: right-handers / left-handers and ambidextrous without early damage / left-handers and ambidextrous **with** early left damage. A favourite distractor swaps 69 and 30, or attributes the figures to Kimura or Wada.

### Sex differences in brain lateralisation

In **1972**, **Levy** proposed that female and male brains differ in their **degree** of lateralisation. Interest in the hypothesis was boosted by **McGlone's (1977, 1980)** observation that **male** victims of unilateral strokes were **three times more likely to suffer aphasia** than female victims. The reasoning: if one-sided damage knocks out language more often in men, men must rely more on one hemisphere. McGlone concluded that **male brains are more lateralised than female brains**.

The hypothesis has been used to explain almost every imaginable sex difference, but the evidence has not held up:

- Some researchers **failed to confirm** McGlone's sex difference in the effects of unilateral lesions (**Inglis & Lawson, 1982**).
- More damaging still, a **meta-analysis of 17 functional brain-imaging studies** found **no significant effect of sex** on lateralisation of brain function (**Hirnstein, Hugdahl, & Hausmann, 2019**).

Accordingly, the authors conclude that **Levy's hypothesis of sex differences in brain lateralisation is no longer tenable**.

> **Trap.** "Males are more lateralised" is a **rejected** hypothesis: **proposed by Levy (1972)**, supported by McGlone (1977, 1980), not replicated by Inglis & Lawson (1982), and undermined by the 17-study meta-analysis (Hirnstein, Hugdahl & Hausmann, 2019).

## 16.2 The Split Brain

So far you have four methods of studying lateralisation: unilateral lesions, the sodium amytal test, dichotic listening and functional imaging. This module is about a fifth, and by far the most dramatic: studying what each hemisphere does when it has been disconnected from the other.

### The paradox of the corpus callosum

In the early 1950s the **corpus callosum**, the largest cerebral commissure, was a paradox. Its size (an estimated **200 million axons**) and its central position, right between the hemispheres, implied that it did something extremely important. Yet research in the **1930s and 1940s** seemed to show that it did **nothing at all**. The corpus callosum had been cut in monkeys and several other laboratory species, and the animals seemed no different after surgery. Similarly, human patients **born without a corpus callosum**, or whose callosum had been damaged, seemed quite normal.

**Roger Sperry** (of the eye-rotation experiments in Chapter 9) and his colleagues were intrigued by this paradox (Aboitiz, 2017). As you will see, the earlier studies were testing the wrong thing: if both hemispheres receive the same information from the world, neither needs the callosum. Its function becomes visible only when information reaches **one** hemisphere and the **other** is tested.

### Myers and Sperry (1953): the design

The solution came in **1953**, from an experiment on **cats** by **Myers and Sperry**. It made **two astounding theoretical points**. First, it showed that one function of the corpus callosum is to **transfer learned information from one hemisphere to the other**. Second, it showed that when the corpus callosum is cut, **each hemisphere can function independently**: each split-brain cat appeared to have two brains.

The task was a simple **visual discrimination**. On each trial a cat faced two panels, one with a **circle** and one with a **square**. Their positions (left or right) varied randomly from trial to trial, and the cat had to learn which symbol to press to get food. Myers and Sperry saw that the key to split-brain research was to **teach and test one hemisphere at a time**.

To do that with vision, you have to understand the routes by which visual information from one eye reaches the **contralateral** hemisphere. There are two:

1. via the **optic chiasm**, where fibres from the medial (nasal) half of each retina cross to the opposite side; and
2. via the **corpus callosum**, which can pass information from the hemisphere that received it to the other hemisphere.

So in their **key experimental group**, Myers and Sperry **transected** (cut completely through) **both** the optic chiasm **and** the corpus callosum, and put a **patch over one eye**. With the chiasm cut, each eye now sends information only to the hemisphere on its own side; with the callosum cut, that hemisphere cannot pass it on. The result is that all incoming visual information is restricted to the hemisphere **ipsilateral** to the uncovered eye.

The design needed controls, and it had three: cats with **only the optic chiasm** transected, cats with **only the corpus callosum** transected, and **unlesioned** cats. The three control groups performed identically, so the book shows them together.

### Myers and Sperry (1953): the results

**Phase 1: learning with a patch on one eye.** All cats learned the task with one eye patched. The key experimental cats learned the discrimination **as rapidly** as the unlesioned controls and the cats with only one structure cut. This is surprising in itself, because cutting the optic chiasm produced a **scotoma** (an area of blindness) covering the **entire medial half of each retina**. So the first conclusion is that **one hemisphere working alone can learn a simple task as rapidly as two hemispheres working together**.

**Phase 2: switching the patch to the other eye.** This is the decisive manipulation. For the three control groups, moving the patch had **no effect**: they continued to perform at close to **100 percent** correct. Think about why. In the chiasm-only group the callosum could pass the learning across; in the callosum-only group the intact chiasm had delivered the training information to both hemispheres in the first place; in unlesioned cats both routes were available.

For the experimental cats, moving the patch was **devastating**. In effect it blindfolded the hemisphere that had learned the task and tested the hemisphere that had been blindfolded during training. Performance dropped **immediately to baseline (50 percent correct, chance for a two-choice task)**, and the cats then relearned the task with **no savings whatsoever**, as if they had never seen it before.

Myers and Sperry concluded that **the cat brain has the capacity to act as two separate brains** and that **the function of the corpus callosum is to transmit information between them**.

Notice how each control rules something out. The chiasm-only group rules out the idea that the deficit comes simply from the scotoma or from losing crossed visual input. The callosum-only group rules out the idea that cutting the callosum is in itself harmful to learning or memory. Only when **both** routes are cut does transfer fail, which isolates transfer as the callosum's job.

> **Trap.** The experimental cats learned **as fast** as intact cats in phase 1. The deficit appeared only when the **untrained** hemisphere was tested: **50 percent**, **no savings**. Cutting the callosum alone produced **no** deficit.

### Confirmation in other species: split-brain monkeys

Myers and Sperry's conclusions about the duality of the cat brain and the information-transfer function of the callosum have been confirmed in many species with many test procedures. For example, **split-brain monkeys** cannot perform tasks requiring **fine tactual discriminations** (such as rough versus smooth) or **fine motor responses** (such as unlocking a puzzle) with one hand if they learned them with the other hand, **provided they are not allowed to watch their hands**, since watching would let the information enter both hemispheres through vision.

The explanation is anatomical. The somatosensory and motor fibres involved in **fine** sensory and motor discriminations are all **contralateral**, so what the left hand learns is stored in the right hemisphere, and with the commissures cut the hemispheres have lost their ability to communicate directly. This is called a failure of **intermanual transfer**.

### Commissurotomy in humans with epilepsy

In the first half of the twentieth century, while the normal function of the corpus callosum was still a mystery, it was known that **epileptic discharges often spread from one hemisphere to the other through the corpus callosum**. Combine that with the repeated finding that cutting the callosum had no obvious effect on performance outside the contrived conditions of Sperry's laboratory, and you have the rationale that led two neurosurgeons, **Vogel and Bogen**, to begin a programme of **commissurotomy** for **severe, intractable epilepsy**. They did so even though a previous similar attempt had failed, presumably because the transections were **incomplete** (**Van Wagenen & Herren, 1940**).

The rationale was that the severity of convulsions might be reduced if the discharges could be **limited to the hemisphere in which they originated** (Mancuso et al., 2019). Therapeutic commissurotomy typically involves **transecting the corpus callosum** and **leaving the smaller commissures intact**. The benefits turned out to be **even greater than anticipated**: although the operation was performed only in the most severe cases, many commissurotomised patients **never experienced another major convulsion**.

> **Trap.** Vogel and Bogen were the **neurosurgeons** who performed the operations. **Sperry and Gazzaniga** were the researchers who **tested** the patients. The earlier failed attempt was by **Van Wagenen and Herren (1940)**, and it failed presumably because the cuts were **incomplete**, not because the idea was wrong.

### Testing the human split brain: Sperry and Gazzaniga's method

The neuropsychological evaluation of Vogel and Bogen's patients was carried out by **Sperry and his associate Gazzaniga**, and this work was a major factor in Sperry's receiving a **Nobel Prize in 1981**. They began by building a test battery on the same strategy that had worked in animals: **deliver information to one hemisphere while keeping it out of the other** (Mancuso et al., 2019; Uddin, 2011).

They could not cut the optic chiasm, because that produces a **scotoma**. Instead they used normal visual anatomy: everything in the **left visual field** (whichever eye you use) goes to the **right** visual cortex, and everything in the **right visual field** goes to the **left**. The patient fixated the centre of a screen and stimuli were flashed to the left or right for **0.1 second**: long enough to be perceived, too short for an eye movement that would bring the stimulus into both fields. This brief lateralised flash is the split-brain form of **visual field** (tachistoscopic) presentation.

**Fine tactual and motor tasks were performed by each hand under a ledge**, so that the **ipsilateral** (non-performing) hemisphere could not monitor the hand by sight.

The rules you must keep in your head are those in the book's figure: **visual input goes from each visual field to the contralateral hemisphere; fine tactile input goes from each hand to the contralateral hemisphere; and each hemisphere controls the fine motor movements of the contralateral hand.**

> **Trap.** Left **visual field** is not the same as left **eye**. Each eye sees both visual fields; it is the **left visual field** of both eyes that projects to the **right** hemisphere. That is why the split-brain method uses central fixation and a **0.1-second** flash rather than an eye patch. Only the cat experiments used patches (combined with chiasm section).

### Two brains, but not equal: "Right Hemisphere Ray" and "Left Hemisphere Logan"

The human results confirmed the animal findings in one major respect but not in another. **Like** split-brain animals, human split-brain patients seem in some respects to have **two independent brains**, each with its own stream of consciousness, abilities, memories and emotions (Gazzaniga, 1967; Gazzaniga & Sperry, 1967; Sperry, 1964). **Unlike** the hemispheres of split-brain animals, however, the two human hemispheres are **far from equal** in what they can do. Most notably, the **left hemisphere of most split-brain patients can speak, whereas the right cannot**.

The authors advise: think of each split-brain patient as two separate people:

- **Right Hemisphere Ray** understands a few simple instructions but **cannot speak**; he receives sensory information from the **left visual field and the left hand**, and controls the fine motor responses of the **left hand**.
- **Left Hemisphere Logan** is **verbally adept**; he receives sensory information from the **right visual field and the right hand**, and controls the fine motor responses of the **right hand**.

In everyday life split-brain patients behave fairly normally because both hemispheres acquire much the same information; in the laboratory, large discrepancies can be created.

### Evidence that the hemispheres can function independently

Walk through the standard test with the book's example of an **apple**.

**Information to the left hemisphere.** Flash an apple in the **right** visual field (or place it in the **right** hand). The left hemisphere can simply **say** "apple", or the **right** hand can pick out an apple from the objects under the ledge. But the right hemisphere cannot identify it: performance with the **left** hand is **no better than chance**.

**Information to the right hemisphere.** Flash the apple in the **left** visual field and the patient (the left hemisphere, which saw nothing) **claims that nothing appeared**. Place it in the **left** hand and the patient is usually **aware that something is there**, presumably through **crude ipsilateral somatosensory fibres**, but **cannot say what it is**. All the while, the **left hand** (right hemisphere) can **identify the correct object**, fetching a matching one from under the ledge while the left hemisphere vehemently insists it does not know what the object is.

> **Trap.** A split-brain patient who "cannot name" an object in the left visual field has **not** failed to perceive it. The right hemisphere perceived it perfectly well and can prove it **non-verbally** with the **left hand**. The failure is one of **access by the speaking hemisphere**. Answers that say the patient "is blind in the left visual field" or "has visual agnosia" are wrong.

> **Trap.** Object in the **left hand**: the patient knows *that* something is there (ipsilateral fibres) but not *what*. Picture in the **left visual field**: the patient says **nothing** was there.

### Cross-cuing

The two hemispheres of a split-brain patient have no means of **direct** neural communication, but they are not completely cut off. They can communicate neurally through **indirect pathways through the brainstem**, and they can communicate by an **external route** called **cross-cuing**.

The book's example comes from tests of whether the left hemisphere could respond to **colours** presented in the **left** visual field. A **red** or a **green** stimulus was flashed in the left visual field and the patient was asked to say which colour it was. At first, performance was at **chance (50 percent correct)**, as you would expect if the speaking hemisphere had no access to the colour. But after a while performance **improved appreciably**, which seemed to suggest that colour information was somehow being transferred over neural pathways from the right hemisphere to the left.

It was not. What was happening was this. If a green light was shown and the patient happened to guess "green", she was correct and the trial ended. But if a green light was shown and she guessed "red", then after a pause she would **frown, shake her head**, and change her answer: "Oh no, I meant green." The **right** hemisphere had seen the green light and **heard** the left hemisphere guess "red". Knowing that red was wrong, the right hemisphere initiated a **frown and a head shake**, and that external signal told the left hemisphere that its answer was wrong and needed correcting. Information passed between the hemispheres not through the brain but through the patient's own **face and body**, perceived by the other hemisphere.

The clinical lesson: patients can perform the same task by **different cognitive strategies**, which often **masks their deficits** and complicates neurological assessment.

> **Trap.** Cross-cuing is an **external** (behavioural) route between hemispheres, distinct from indirect **neural** routes through the brainstem.

### Doing two things at once

A sarcastic student once asked the authors whether splitting his brain would let him study for two exams at once. A fair question: independent hemispheres should be able to **learn** two different things simultaneously. They can.

**The two-bag test.** Two different pictures appear simultaneously, say a **pencil in the left visual field** and an **orange in the right visual field**. The patient is asked to reach into two bags at once, one with each hand, and to grasp in each hand the object that was on the screen. After grasping but before withdrawing the hands, the patient is asked what is in them. The patient (the left hemisphere) replies, "**Two oranges.**" Much to the bewilderment of the verbal left hemisphere, when the hands are withdrawn there is an **orange in the right hand and a pencil in the left**. Each hemisphere had learned a different thing at exactly the same time and acted on it with the hand it controls. Notice too that the left hemisphere, knowing only about the orange, reported **two** oranges: it confidently described the whole situation in terms of the only information it had.

### The helping-hand phenomenon

In a related test the same two stimuli are presented (pencil left, orange right), but the patient is asked to pick up the presented object from an assortment on a table **in full view**. As the **right** hand reaches for the **orange** under the direction of the left hemisphere, the right hemisphere **sees** what is happening and thinks an error is being made (it saw a pencil). On some trials the right hemisphere dealt with the problem in the only way it could: the **left hand shot out, grabbed the right hand away from the orange, and redirected it to the pencil**. This is the **helping-hand phenomenon**.

> **Trap.** Helping hand = **left** hand intervening in a **full-view** task. Two-bag test = hidden hands, verbal report "two oranges".

### Independent attention and faster visual search

The split brain's ability to do two things at once also shows up in **attention**. Each hemisphere of a split-brain patient appears able to maintain an **independent focus of attention** (Gazzaniga, 2005). That produces an ironic result: split-brain patients can search for and identify a visual target in an array of similar items **more quickly than healthy controls** (**Luck et al., 1989**), presumably because the two disconnected hemispheres are conducting **two independent searches** in parallel.

### Visual completion and the chimeric figures test

The last example of doing two things at once involves **visual completion**. From Chapter 6 you may recall that people with **scotomas** are often unaware of them, because the brain fills in (completes) the blind area using information from the surrounding visual field. In a sense, **each hemisphere of a split-brain patient is like a person with a scotoma covering the entire ipsilateral visual field**: the left hemisphere sees nothing of the left visual field, and vice versa. If each hemisphere completes its own half independently, then a single stimulus straddling the midline should be completed in **two different ways at once**.

This was demonstrated with the **chimeric figures test**, named after the **Chimera**, a mythical monster made of parts of different animals. **Levy, Trevarthen, and Sperry (1972)** flashed photographs made of **fused half-faces of two different people** onto the centre of a screen in front of split-brain patients. The patients were then asked to describe what they saw, or to point to it among a series of photographs of intact faces.

Amazingly, each patient (that is, each **left** hemisphere) reported seeing a **complete, bilaterally symmetrical face**, even when asked leading questions such as "Did you notice anything peculiar about what you just saw?" When asked to **describe** what they saw, patients usually described a completed version of the half-face that had been presented to the **right visual field** (the left hemisphere's half). The interpretation is that the left hemisphere sees a single normal face that is a completed version of the right half-face, while **at the same time** the right hemisphere sees a single normal face that is a completed version of the left half-face.

> **Trap.** In the chimeric figures test (Levy, Trevarthen & Sperry, 1972), **verbal description** matches the **right visual field** half-face, completed into a whole face; neither hemisphere notices anything odd.

### A note on two terms you may meet elsewhere

Two terms from older editions and other textbooks are not in the 11th edition's text, so no study is attributed here. The **Z lens** is a contact-lens device that restricts one eye's input to one visual field, allowing longer lateralised viewing than a 0.1-second flash. The **left-hemisphere interpreter** is the idea that the speaking hemisphere constructs verbal explanations for behaviour whose real cause it cannot access; the confident "Two oranges" is the kind of behaviour the idea refers to.

### Dual mental functioning and conflict in split-brain patients

In **most** split-brain patients the right hemisphere does not seem to have a strong will of its own; the **left hemisphere seems to control most everyday activities**. In a **few** patients, however, the right hemisphere takes a more active role, and then there can be serious conflict between the hemispheres. The book's example is a patient it calls Peter.

### The Case of Peter, the Split-Brain Patient Tormented by Conflict

At the age of **8**, Peter began to suffer **complex partial seizures**. Antiepileptic medication was ineffective, and at **20** he received a **commissurotomy**, which greatly improved his condition but did not completely stop his seizures. A **sodium amytal test** given before surgery showed that he was **left-hemisphere dominant for language**.

After surgery, the independent and mischievous behaviour of his right hemisphere often frustrated him (his left hemisphere). He complained that his **left hand would turn off television shows he was enjoying**, that his **left leg would not always walk in the intended direction**, and that his **left arm would sometimes perform embarrassing, socially unacceptable acts**, such as **striking a relative**. In the laboratory, Peter (his left hemisphere) sometimes became angry with his left hand, **swearing at it, striking it, and trying to force it with his right hand** to do what he wanted (**Joseph, 1988**).

Apply the Ray-and-Logan rule: every complaint is spoken (left hemisphere); every rebellious act is by the **left** side of the body (right hemisphere).

> **Trap.** Peter is the exception. In **most** split-brain patients the left hemisphere controls everyday behaviour and the right shows **no strong will** of its own.

### Independence of split hemispheres: the current perspective

Discussions of split-brain patients, including this one, tend to dwell on cases of **complete** separation. But **complete hemispheric independence is not an inevitable consequence of split-brain surgery**. In most split-brain patients some communication between the hemispheres can be demonstrated, depending on **the particular surgery, the time since surgery, the particular information, and the method of testing**.

**Emotion crosses easily.** Feelings of emotion appear to pass readily between the hemispheres of most split-brain patients. Present emotionally loaded images to the right hemisphere and ask the patient to respond verbally: the verbal left hemisphere often responds with the **appropriate emotion**, even when it is **unaware of the image** (**Sperry, Zaidel, & Zaidel, 1979**).

The book quotes a remarkable exchange from that study. The patient's **right** hemisphere was shown an array of photographs, and he was asked whether any was familiar. He pointed to the photo of his **aunt**. Asked whether this was a neutral, thumbs-up or thumbs-down person, he smiled, made a thumbs-up sign, and said, "This is a happy person." Asked whether he knew *him* personally, he said, "Oh, it's not a him, it's a her." He could not say whether she was an entertainer or a historical figure. Then he **traced something with his left index finger on the back of his right hand**, and exclaimed, "My aunt, my Aunt Edie." Asked how he knew, he said, "**By the E on the back of my hand.**"

Two mechanisms are combined. The **emotional tone** and even the person's sex reached the speaking hemisphere; the **name** did not, until the right hemisphere used the **left hand** to write an **E** on the **right hand**, whose touch goes to the left hemisphere. That is **cross-cuing** through the skin.

**Task difficulty matters.** Another factor that contributes substantially to hemispheric independence is **task difficulty** (**Weissman & Banich, 2000**). As tasks become more difficult, they are more likely to involve **both** hemispheres of split-brain patients. It appears that **simple tasks are best processed in one hemisphere**, the one specialised for the activity, but **complex tasks require the cognitive power of both**.

> **Trap.** Two easily missed facts: (1) **emotion** transfers readily between the hemispheres of most split-brain patients even when the image itself does not (Sperry, Zaidel & Zaidel, 1979); (2) **harder** tasks produce **more** interhemispheric involvement, not less (Weissman & Banich, 2000). The "Aunt Edie" episode is an example of **cross-cuing** combined with emotional transfer.

## 16.3 Differences Between Left and Right Hemispheres

You now have five methods: unilateral lesions, the sodium amytal test, dichotic listening, functional imaging and split-brain studies. This module surveys what they have revealed. Because the verbal and motor abilities of the left hemisphere are obvious, most research has gone into uncovering the less obvious special abilities of the **right** hemisphere.

### Lateralisation is statistical, not absolute

Before listing differences, the book clears up a common misconception. For **many functions there are no substantial differences** between the hemispheres, and where differences do exist they tend to be **slight biases** in favour of one hemisphere, **not absolute differences**. The popular media ignore this and portray left-right differences as absolute, so it is widely believed that, for example, the left hemisphere has **exclusive** control of language and the right hemisphere exclusive control of emotion and creativity.

Language makes the point best, precisely because it is the **most lateralised** of all cognitive abilities. Even here lateralisation is far from total, and there is substantial language-related activity in the right hemisphere. Two pieces of evidence:

- On the dichotic listening test, people who are left-hemisphere dominant for language identify more digits with the right ear, but the right-ear advantage is only **55 to 45 percent**.
- The **right hemispheres of most left-hemisphere-dominant split-brain patients can understand many spoken or written words and simple sentences** (Gazzaniga, 2013).

> **Trap.** "The right hemisphere has no language ability" is false. It understands many words and simple sentences in most split-brain patients; what it typically lacks is **speech**. And the dichotic right-ear advantage is a modest **55:45**, a bias, not a monopoly.

### Abilities that show some lateralisation (Table 16.1)

The book's Table 16.1 lists abilities that display **some degree** of lateralisation, sorted by which hemisphere tends to dominate. Learn it as a whole; MCQs frequently lift single cells from it.

### Superiority of the left hemisphere in controlling ipsilateral movement

An unexpected left-hemisphere specialisation came from **functional imaging**. When complex, cognitively driven movements are made by **one hand**, most activation is in the **contralateral** hemisphere, as expected. But some activation also appears in the **ipsilateral** hemisphere, and these ipsilateral effects are **substantially greater in the left hemisphere than in the right** (Bundy & Leuthardt, 2019; Hervé et al., 2013). In other words, the left hemisphere participates in controlling the **left** hand more than the right hemisphere participates in controlling the right hand.

Lesion data agree. **Left-hemisphere lesions are more likely than right-hemisphere lesions to produce ipsilateral motor problems**: left lesions are more likely to reduce the accuracy of **left-hand** movements than right lesions are to reduce the accuracy of right-hand movements. This fits neatly with Liepmann's observation that left-hemisphere damage produces **bilateral** apraxia.

### Superiority of the right hemisphere in spatial ability

In a classic early study, **Levy (1969)** placed a three-dimensional block of a particular shape in either the right or the left hand of split-brain patients. After they had thoroughly **palpated** it (explored it by touch), she asked them to point to the two-dimensional drawing that best showed what the block would look like if it were made of cardboard and **unfolded**. Levy found a **right-hemisphere superiority**, and she noticed that the two hemispheres went about the task differently. The **left hand (right hemisphere)** performed **rapidly and silently**. The **right hand (left hemisphere)** performed **hesitantly**, often accompanied by a **running verbal commentary** that patients found hard to inhibit. Levy concluded that the right hemisphere is superior to the left at spatial tasks. That conclusion has been frequently confirmed (Dietz et al., 2014; Hirnstein, Hugdahl, & Hausmann, 2019; Zaidel, 2013), and it is consistent with the finding that disorders of spatial perception, such as **contralateral neglect** (Chapters 7 and 8), tend to be associated with **right-hemisphere damage**.

> **Trap.** Two different Levy studies appear in this chapter and a third Levy proposal: **Levy (1969)** is the unfolded-block spatial study; **Levy, Trevarthen and Sperry (1972)** is the chimeric figures test; and **Levy (1972)** proposed the sex-difference hypothesis. Match year to study.

### Specialisation of the right hemisphere for emotion

The old concept of left-hemisphere dominance held that the minor right hemisphere is **not involved in emotion**. That presumption has been proven **false**. Analysis of **unilateral lesions** indicates that the right hemisphere may be **superior** to the left on some tests of emotion, for example in accurately **identifying facial expressions of emotion** (Bernard et al., 2018; Gainotti, 2018, 2019a, 2019b; Mitchell & Phillips, 2015; Prete et al., 2015).

Now the qualification, which the book is careful to make. Although lesion studies suggest a **general right-hemisphere dominance for some aspects of emotional processing**, **functional brain-imaging studies have not provided unambiguous support** for this view (Costanzo et al., 2015; Gainotti, 2019a). So the right-hemisphere emotion story is supported by one method and only partly by another.

> **Trap.** Right-hemisphere emotion dominance rests mainly on **lesion** evidence; **imaging** evidence is **ambiguous**.

### Superior musical ability of the right hemisphere

**Kimura (1964)** compared the performance of **20 right-handers** on the standard digit dichotic listening test with their performance on a **melody** version. In the melody version she played two different melodies simultaneously, one to each ear, and then asked the participants to pick out the two they had heard from **four** melodies subsequently played to both ears. The **right ear (left hemisphere)** was superior for **digits**, whereas the **left ear (right hemisphere)** was superior for **melodies**.

Because the same people and apparatus give **opposite** ear advantages depending only on the material, a general right-ear bias is ruled out. It is consistent with the clinical observation that **right temporal lobe lesions** are more likely to disrupt music discriminations than left temporal lobe lesions (Casey, 2013).

> **Trap.** Kimura (1964): digits, **right** ear; melodies, **left** ear. The book's own review quiz asks exactly this ("the right ear was superior in the perception of ___, while the left ear was superior in the perception of ___": digits, melodies).

### Hemispheric differences in memory

Early lateralisation research assumed that particular abilities **reside in** one hemisphere or the other. Research has pushed towards a different idea: **the two hemispheres have similar abilities that tend to be expressed in different ways**. Memory was one of the first areas to force this change. Both hemispheres can perform memory tests, but the left is better on some and the right on others.

There are two approaches to the lateralisation of memory:

1. **Link memory processes to hemispheres.** For example, it has been argued that the **left hemisphere is specialised for encoding episodic memory** (Chapter 11).
2. **Link memory to materials.** In general the **left hemisphere plays the greater role in memory for verbal material**, and the **right hemisphere in memory for nonverbal material** (e.g., Willment & Golby, 2013).

Whichever approach proves more fruitful, both are an advance over the idea that memory is totally lateralised to one hemisphere.

### What is lateralised: broad clusters of abilities or individual cognitive processes?

Early theories ascribed complex **clusters** of abilities to one hemisphere. Because the left hemisphere tended to do better on language tests, it was presumed dominant for "language-related abilities"; because the right did better on some spatial tests, it was presumed dominant for "space-related abilities"; and so on. That may have been a reasonable first step, but the **consensus is now that it is simplistic**.

The problem is that labels such as language, emotion, musical ability and spatial ability each cover **dozens of different cognitive activities**, and there is no reason why all the activities grouped under one label should be lateralised to the same hemisphere. Indeed, **major exceptions to all broad categories** have emerged (Cai & Van der Haegen, 2015; Crepaldi et al., 2013; Turner et al., 2015). How can all language abilities be "in" the left hemisphere when the **right hemisphere has been shown to be involved in speech perception and in understanding word meaning** (Kreitewolf, Friederici, & von Kriegstein, 2014; Poeppel, 2014)?

So many researchers now take a different approach. They build on cognitive psychology, which has broken complex tasks (reading, judging space, remembering) into their **constituent cognitive processes**. Once the laterality of each individual process has been established, it should be possible to **predict** the laterality of any task from the processes that compose it. This same move, from big functions to small constituent processes, reappears as Premise 1 of the cognitive neuroscience approach to language in 16.7.

### Anatomical asymmetries of the brain

The discovery of functional lateralisation led to a search for **anatomical** asymmetries, especially ones that might explain the functional differences. Do anatomical differences make the left hemisphere better suited to language?

Most efforts have focused on the size of **three cortical areas important for language**:

- The **frontal operculum**: frontal cortex just in front of the face area of the primary motor cortex. In the **left** hemisphere it is the location of **Broca's area**.
- The **planum temporale**: temporal cortex in the **posterior region of the lateral fissure**. It is thought to play a role in language comprehension and is often referred to as **Wernicke's area**.
- **Heschl's gyrus**: in the lateral fissure **just anterior to the planum temporale**, in the temporal lobe. It is the location of **primary auditory cortex**.

Many anatomical differences between the average left and right hemispheres have been reported, and their nature depends on **sex and age** (Kong et al., 2018). There is no question that the average hemispheres differ anatomically, but the **functional consequences** of the differences have not been apparent.

> **Trap.** Get the front-to-back order right along the lateral fissure: **frontal operculum** (front, Broca's area on the left), then **Heschl's gyrus** (primary auditory cortex), then **planum temporale** (posterior, Wernicke's area). Heschl's gyrus is **anterior** to the planum temporale, not behind it.

### Why anatomical asymmetry has been hard to link to function

There are **two serious difficulties** in studying asymmetry of the language areas. First, their **boundaries are unclear**, with no consensus on how to define them (Hagoort, 2014; Poeppel, 2014). Second, there are **large individual differences** in the structure of these areas among healthy people (Amunts & Zilles, 2012). Given these difficulties it is no surprise that reports have been **variable**: often the predicted left-side size advantage is found, but sometimes there is no asymmetry, or even a **right**-hemisphere advantage (Amunts & Zilles, 2012; Kong et al., 2018).

There is also a problem of inference. Whenever one of the areas is reported to be larger on the left, someone suggests that the asymmetry caused, or was caused by, left lateralisation of language. There is **little support** for such conjectures (Bishop, 2013). The fact that an area is **on average** larger on the left does not show a causal link to language lateralisation, even if the area is involved in language. **At a bare minimum**, it must be shown that anatomical and functional asymmetries are **correlated within individuals**: that the degree of anatomical asymmetry in a given person reflects the degree of language lateralisation in **that same person**. To the authors' knowledge, **no such correlations have been found**.

(The book's review quiz adds: in **musicians with perfect pitch**, the left **planum temporale** is larger than the right.)

### Kong and colleagues (2018): 17,000 brains

An important recent study bears directly on the question. In **2018**, **Kong et al.** analysed **MRI data from over 17,000 healthy individuals**. They found **large asymmetries in the size of the frontal operculum and Heschl's gyrus**. **Heschl's gyrus was larger on the left**, as predicted. But **different parts of the frontal operculum showed different directions** of asymmetry: its **anterior** portion was **larger on the right**, and its **posterior** portion was **larger on the left**. Clearly, if anatomical asymmetry relates to language function, the relationship is **far more complex than previously thought**.

In short, the search has been **only partially successful**: many anatomical asymmetries have been found, but few have been clearly related to functional asymmetries (Kong et al., 2019). Several researchers suggest that differences in **microstructure** (cell types, synapses, neural circuitry) may prove more informative than comparisons of the size of vaguely defined areas (Chance, 2014).

> **Trap.** Kong et al. (2018): over **17,000** healthy people; **Heschl's gyrus larger on the left**; **frontal operculum split** (anterior larger on the right, posterior larger on the left). A statement that "the whole frontal operculum is larger on the left" is wrong. And the general verdict is that anatomical asymmetries have **not** been shown to cause functional ones.

## 16.4 Evolution of Cerebral Lateralisation and Language

Discussion of lateralisation keeps leading back to language, because language is the most lateralised cognitive function. This module asks why lateralisation evolved, when it evolved, and how human language might have evolved.

### Two general premises behind theories of lateralisation

Many theories have been proposed for why lateralisation of function evolved. Most rest on one of **two general premises** (Corballis, 2017):

1. It is advantageous for brain areas that perform **similar functions** to be located in the **same hemisphere**.
2. It is advantageous to put some functions on one side and others on the other side so as to **minimise redundancy** between the hemispheres.

Each theory then proposes a **different fundamental distinction** between left and right hemisphere function. The book considers three.

### The analytic-synthetic theory

The **analytic-synthetic theory** holds that there are two basic **modes of thinking**, an **analytic** mode and a **synthetic** mode, which have become segregated during evolution into the **left** and **right** hemispheres respectively. The left hemisphere works in a **logical, analytical, computer-like** way, analysing and abstracting input **sequentially** and attaching **verbal labels**. The right hemisphere is primarily a **synthesiser**, organising and processing information in terms of **gestalts**, or wholes.

It has been the darling of pop psychology ("You are so good with maths, you must be left-brained!"). Its weakness is **vagueness** (Karolis, Corbetta, & de Schotten, 2019). Because no one can specify how much any given task requires analytic versus synthetic processing, the theory has been **difficult to test empirically**.

### The motor theory of cerebral asymmetry

The **motor theory** (**Kimura, 1979**) holds that the left hemisphere is specialised not for speech as such but for the control of **fine movements**, of which **speech is only one category** (Neubauer et al., 2020). Support comes from reports that lesions producing aphasia often produce **other motor deficits** too (Kimura, 2011), which fits Liepmann's apraxia findings and the left hemisphere's role in ipsilateral movement. Its shortcoming is that it **does not explain why motor function became lateralised in the first place** (Beaton, 2003).

### The linguistic theory of cerebral asymmetry

The **linguistic theory** holds that the **primary** role of the left hemisphere **is language**. Contrast it with the other two: the analytic-synthetic and motor theories treat language as a **secondary** specialisation, located in the left hemisphere only because that hemisphere is primarily specialised for analytic thought or for skilled movement respectively.

The linguistic theory rests largely on studies of **deaf people who use American Sign Language** (a sign language with a structure similar to spoken language) and who suffer unilateral brain damage (Campbell, MacSweeney, & Waters, 2008; Rogalsky et al., 2013). The argument is a dissociation: sign and pantomime are both complex manual movements, so if left damage disrupts **sign language but not pantomime gestures** (gestures that express meaning), the left hemisphere's specialisation must be **language itself**, not movement. W.L. shows this.

### The Case of W.L., the Man Who Experienced Aphasia for Sign Language

W.L. is a **congenitally deaf, right-handed** man who grew up using **American Sign Language**. **Seven months** before testing, he was admitted to hospital complaining of **right-side weakness** and motor problems. A **CT scan** revealed a large **left frontotemporoparietal stroke**. At that time his wife noticed that he was making many uncharacteristic **errors in signing** and was having difficulty **understanding the signs of others**.

His neuropsychologists obtained a **2-hour videotape** of an interview recorded **10 months before** the stroke, which gave them valuable pre-stroke performance measures. Formal post-stroke testing confirmed a **specific loss in the ability to use and understand sign language**. Because he could still **produce and understand complex pantomime gestures**, his sign-language aphasia appeared to be **specific to language** (**Corina et al., 1992**).

> **Trap.** W.L. supports the **linguistic** theory, not the motor theory. His problem was not with manual movement in general (pantomime was spared) but with **language** expressed manually. Note that his deficit was both **expressive and receptive** (signing errors and difficulty understanding others' signs), showing that aphasia is not tied to the auditory-vocal channel.

| Theory | Left hemisphere is primarily specialised for | Main support | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analytic-synthetic | Analytic, sequential, verbal-label processing (right: synthetic, gestalt) | Popular appeal | Too vague to test |
| Motor (Kimura, 1979) | Fine movement, speech being one kind | Aphasic lesions often cause other motor deficits | Does not explain why motor control lateralised |
| Linguistic | Language itself | Sign-language aphasia with spared pantomime (W.L.) | (none stated in the book) |

### When did cerebral lateralisation evolve?

Until recently lateralisation was assumed to be **exclusive to hominins**. One version of the motor theory, for example, said that left-hemisphere motor specialisation evolved in early hominins in response to **tool use**, and that vocal language later evolved in the left hemisphere because of its greater motor dexterity. But lateralisation of function has now been found in species from **all five classes of vertebrates** (fishes, reptiles, birds, amphibians and mammals) and in **some invertebrates**, species that evolved long before humans (Prieur et al., 2019). So lateralisation is **ancient**, far older than humans, tools or language.

That it is so widespread suggests it confers **survival advantages**. The book identifies two (Corballis, 2015):

1. It may be **more efficient** for the neurons performing a particular function to be **concentrated in one hemisphere**. For example, it is usually better to have **one highly skilled hand** than two moderately skilled hands.
2. Two different kinds of cognitive process may be more readily performed **simultaneously** if they are lateralised to **different** hemispheres.

As an illustration, **right-handedness may have evolved from a preference for using the right side of the body for feeding**, a preference demonstrated in species of all five vertebrate classes. Once hands evolved, species with hands (monkeys and apes) showed a hand preference for feeding and for other complex responses such as **tool use and communication**, which in turn led to lateralisation of those functions as well (Prieur et al., 2019).

> **Trap.** Lateralisation is **not** uniquely human or uniquely hominin. It occurs in all five vertebrate classes and some invertebrates. An option claiming lateralisation "evolved with tool use in early hominins" states the older view that the comparative evidence overturned.

### Evolution of human language: what makes it different

Human language is a system that allows a **virtually limitless number of ideas** to be expressed by **combining a finite set of elements** (Hauser et al., 2014). Other species communicate, but not like this. Monkeys have distinct warning calls for different threats but **do not combine** them to express new ideas; birds and whales sing complex songs but show **no creative recombination** to express new ideas (Hage & Nieder, 2016).

Human infants show how quickly the capacity unfolds. Before **10 months** of age infants only **babble** (Laake & Bridgett, 2018), but by **30 months** they speak in **complete sentences** and use **more than 500 words**. Over the same 20-month period the plastic infant brain **reorganises** to learn its parents' languages. At **10 months** infants can **distinguish the sounds of all human languages**; by **30 months** they readily discriminate **only** the sounds of the languages they have been exposed to. Once the ability to discriminate a particular speech sound is lost, it is **difficult to regain**, which is one reason adults usually have trouble learning to speak a new language **without an accent**.

Words leave no fossils, so the evolution of language can only be studied by **comparing existing species**. Naturally, researchers turned first to the vocal communication of our primate relatives.

### Vocal communication in nonhuman primates

No other species has a language comparable to ours, but each nonhuman primate species has a variety of **calls**, each with a **specific meaning** understood by conspecifics. These calls are **not simple reflexes**: they depend on **social context** (Seyfarth & Cheney, 2014). **Vervet monkeys** do not make alarm calls unless other vervets are nearby, and are most likely to call if the nearby vervets are **relatives** (Oller & Griebel, 2014). **Chimpanzees** vary the screams they produce in aggressive encounters according to the **severity** of the encounter, their **role** in it, and **which other chimpanzees can hear them** (Slocombe et al., 2010).

A consistent pattern has emerged: there is typically a **substantial gap between vocal production and auditory comprehension**. Even the most vocal nonhumans produce relatively **few** calls, yet they can **interpret a wide range** of other sounds in their environment (Fitch, 2018; Hauser et al., 2014). Two implications follow. First, nonhumans may be limited in producing vocal language not by an inability to **interpret** sounds but by an inability to exert **fine motor control over the voice** of the kind humans have (Fitch, 2018; Ghazanfar & Takahashi, 2014). Second, **human language may have evolved from a competence in comprehending sounds that already existed in our primate ancestors**.

### The motor theory of speech perception

It was reasonable for Broca to think his area had a specific role in **speech production**, since Broca's area is part of the left **premotor** cortex. The **motor theory of speech perception** goes a step further: it proposes that **perceiving and comprehending speech depends on the heard words activating the same neural circuits in the motor system that would have been activated if the listener had said the words** (Cogan et al., 2014; but see Arsenault & Buchsbaum, 2015). Do not confuse it with Kimura's motor theory of cerebral **asymmetry** in the previous subsection.

The evidence for and against, in order:

- **General support.** Just **thinking** about an action often activates the same brain areas as performing it, and **mirror neurons** (Chapter 8), motor cortex neurons that fire when a particular response is either **performed or observed**, have been discovered (Cook et al., 2014).
- **Imaging support.** Many functional imaging studies show activity in **primary or secondary motor cortex** during language **comprehension** tests that involve no speaking or writing. **Scott, McGettigan, and Eisner (2009)** compiled these studies and concluded that motor cortex is particularly active during the perception of **conversational exchanges**.
- **More direct (causal) support.** Several studies applied **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** to motor cortex areas involved in **speech articulation** while volunteers listened to syllables and words. As predicted, stimulation **disrupted perception** of the syllables and words (D'Ausilio et al., 2014; Schomers et al., 2015; Smalle, Rogers, & Möttönen, 2015).
- **Contradicting evidence.** **Case studies of patients with motor cortex damage** have **failed to reveal the predicted deficits in speech perception** (Stasenko, Garcea, & Mahon, 2013; Stasenko et al., 2015).

So this theory is genuinely **contested**: imaging and TMS support it, clinical lesion cases do not.

> **Trap.** Two different "motor theories": Kimura's (1979) **motor theory of cerebral asymmetry** explains **why the left hemisphere is lateralised**; the **motor theory of speech perception** explains **how speech is understood** (via the listener's own articulatory circuits and mirror neurons).

### Gestural language

Because only humans have a high degree of motor control over the vocal apparatus, perhaps language in nonhuman primates is mainly **gestural** rather than vocal. **Pollick and de Waal (2007)** tested this by comparing the **gestures** and **vocalisations** of **chimpanzees**. They found a **highly nuanced vocabulary of hand gestures** used in many situations and in various **combinations**. In short, the chimpanzees' gestures were **much more like human language than their vocalisations**. The book leaves open whether primate gestures were a critical stage in the evolution of human language (de Boer, 2019; Gillespie-Lynch et al., 2014; Prieur et al., 2018).

> **Trap.** In Pollick and de Waal (2007), it was chimpanzee **gestures**, not vocalisations, that resembled human language (nuanced, used in many contexts, combinable). Combination is the key property, because it is exactly what monkey alarm calls and bird song lack.

## 16.5 Cortical Localisation of Language: The Wernicke-Geschwind Model

First, a distinction that exams test directly. **Language lateralisation** refers to the relative control of language functions by the **left versus right** hemisphere. **Language localisation** refers to **where within** the hemispheres the circuits for language are located. This module is about localisation, and it starts with the **Wernicke-Geschwind model**, the predominant theory of language localisation. Most research on the topic has been conducted and interpreted within this model, so reading about it without knowing the model would be, in the authors' words, like watching chess without knowing the rules.

### Broca's hypothesis: programmes of articulation

The history of localisation began at the same point as the history of lateralisation: with **Broca's** claim that a small area in the **inferior left prefrontal cortex** (Broca's area) is the **centre for speech production**. Broca hypothesised that **programmes of articulation** are stored in this area, and that speech is produced when these programmes activate the **adjacent precentral gyrus**, which controls the muscles of the face and oral cavity. On this view, damage **restricted** to Broca's area should disrupt **speech production** without producing deficits in **comprehension**.

### Wernicke (1874) and Wernicke's area

The next major event came in **1874**, when **Carl Wernicke** (pronounced "VER-ni-key") concluded from **10 clinical cases** that there is a language area in the **left temporal lobe just posterior to the primary auditory cortex**, that is, in the **left planum temporale**. Wernicke argued that this second area was the cortical area of **language comprehension**, and it became known as **Wernicke's area**.

### Broca's aphasia and Wernicke's aphasia

Wernicke then proposed two contrasting syndromes, both of which were **hypothetical** predictions from selective lesions:

- **Broca's aphasia** (from selective lesions of Broca's area) is primarily **expressive**: **normal comprehension** of both written and spoken language, and speech that **retains its meaningfulness** despite being **slow, laboured, disjointed and poorly articulated**.
- **Wernicke's aphasia** (from selective lesions of Wernicke's area) is primarily **receptive**: **poor comprehension** of both written and spoken language, and speech that is **meaningless** but still retains the superficial **structure, rhythm and intonation** of normal speech. This normal-sounding but nonsensical speech is called **word salad**.

The book's examples (from **Geschwind, 1979, p. 183**) make the contrast vivid. A Broca's patient asked about a dental appointment replied haltingly and indistinctly: "Yes ... Monday ... Dad and Dick ... Wednesday nine o'clock ... 10 o'clock ... doctors ... and ... teeth." The content words carry the message; the grammar and fluency are gone. A Wernicke's patient asked to describe a picture of two boys stealing cookies replied smoothly: "Mother is away here working her work to get her better, but when she's looking the two boys looking in the other part. She's working another time." Fluent, grammatical-sounding, and almost empty of meaning.

> **Trap.** Expressive = **Broca's** = **non-fluent**, meaningful, comprehension largely intact. Receptive = **Wernicke's** = **fluent** word salad, comprehension poor. Note the word "hypothetical": the book stresses these were **predicted** syndromes, and 16.6 shows that pure forms are rarely if ever seen.

### Conduction aphasia

Wernicke reasoned further. If Wernicke's area comprehends and Broca's area produces, there must be a pathway between them, the **arcuate fasciculus**. Damage to it should produce a third type of aphasia, which he called **conduction aphasia**: **comprehension and spontaneous speech** would be **largely intact**, but patients would have difficulty **repeating words they had just heard**, because the heard words could be understood but could not be passed forward to the speech-production area. This is a prediction derived from a box-and-arrow model, and it shows how the model works: every box and every arrow implies a syndrome.

### Dejerine (1892): the angular gyrus, alexia and agraphia

The **left angular gyrus**, the area of left temporal and parietal cortex **just posterior to Wernicke's area**, was implicated in language in **1892** by the neurologist **Joseph Jules Dejerine**, on the basis of a post-mortem examination of **one special patient**. The patient had **alexia** (inability to **read**) and **agraphia** (inability to **write**), and what made the case special was that they were exceptionally **pure**: he could not read or write, yet had **no difficulty speaking or understanding speech**. Dejerine's post-mortem showed damage in the **pathways connecting the visual cortex with the left angular gyrus**. He concluded that the left angular gyrus is responsible for **comprehending language-related visual input**, received directly from the adjacent **left** visual cortex and indirectly from the **right** visual cortex via the **corpus callosum**.

> **Trap.** **Alexia** = cannot read; **agraphia** = cannot write. Dejerine's patient (1892) had both but **normal speech and comprehension**, and the damage was in the **pathways between visual cortex and the left angular gyrus**. Do not confuse alexia (acquired, in a previous reader) with dyslexia, or agraphia with apraxia.

### Holistic opposition and Geschwind's revival

In the era of Broca, Wernicke and Dejerine, many influential scholars (the book names **Freud, Head, and Marie**) opposed attempts to localise language abilities to specific neocortical areas. Advocates of the **holistic** approach gradually gained the upper hand, and interest in localisation waned. Then in the **mid-1960s**, **Norman Geschwind (1970)** revived the localisationist ideas of Broca, Wernicke and Dejerine, added new data and interpretation, and melded them into a powerful theory: the **Wernicke-Geschwind model**.

### The Wernicke-Geschwind model: seven components

The model has **seven components**, all in the **left hemisphere**: **primary visual cortex, angular gyrus, primary auditory cortex, Wernicke's area, arcuate fasciculus, Broca's area, and primary motor cortex**. The book's review exercise gives each a one-line job, which is the most efficient way to learn them:

| Component | Job in the model |
|---|---|
| Primary auditory cortex | Perceives the spoken word |
| Primary visual cortex | Perceives the written word |
| Angular gyrus | Translates the visual form of a read word into an auditory code |
| Wernicke's area | Centre for language comprehension |
| Arcuate fasciculus | Carries signals from Wernicke's area to Broca's area |
| Broca's area | Contains the programmes of articulation |
| Primary motor cortex | Controls the muscles of articulation |

### How the model works: two circuits

The book traces two examples. **Responding to a heard question:** auditory signals from the other person's speech are received by **primary auditory cortex** and conducted to **Wernicke's area**, where they are **comprehended**. If a reply is needed, Wernicke's area generates the neural representation of the thought underlying it, which is sent through the left **arcuate fasciculus** to **Broca's area**. There it activates the appropriate **programme of articulation**, which drives the appropriate neurons of **primary motor cortex** and ultimately the muscles of articulation.

**Reading aloud:** the signal received by **primary visual cortex** goes to the **left angular gyrus**, which **translates the visual form of the word into its auditory code** and sends it to **Wernicke's area** for comprehension. Wernicke's area then triggers responses in the **arcuate fasciculus, Broca's area and motor cortex**, in that order, producing the speech sounds.

> **Trap.** In the model, reading aloud goes **visual cortex → angular gyrus → Wernicke's area → arcuate fasciculus → Broca's area → motor cortex**. Written words are routed **through Wernicke's area** after being recoded into auditory form; they do not go straight from visual cortex to Broca's area. And all seven components are in the **left** hemisphere.

## 16.6 The Wernicke-Geschwind Model: The Evidence

### Reasons for scepticism before looking at data

By this point in the book you should be sceptical of any model of a complex cognitive process that consists of a few localised neocortical centres joined in series by a few arrows. The neocortex is not divided into neat compartments whose functions match vague concepts like "language comprehension", "speech motor programmes" and "conversion of written language to auditory language" (Cahana-Amitay & Albert, 2014). Still, the ultimate test of a theory is whether its **predictions** match the **evidence**.

One point first. The model was originally based on case studies of aphasic patients with **strokes, tumours and penetrating brain injuries**. Damage in such cases is usually **diffuse**, and it inevitably encroaches on **subcortical nerve fibres** that connect the lesion site to other areas. The book shows the extent of the damage in **one of Broca's two original patients** (Mohr, 1976): it is so widespread, extending well beyond Broca's area, that the case provides **little evidence that Broca's area plays a role in speech** at all.

> **Trap.** The founding evidence for Broca's area was itself weak: one of Broca's original two patients had damage **far larger than Broca's area** (Mohr, 1976). Most aphasic patients have **diffuse** damage, which is exactly why lesion cases cannot pin down precise language areas.

### Evidence from surgical excisions

Since the model grew out of lesion studies, the first test is whether it predicts the effects of cortical damage. **Neurosurgical patients** are especially informative because the location and extent of their lesions can be read with reasonable accuracy from the **surgeons' reports**. The results have **not confirmed** the model "by any stretch of the imagination".

- Surgery that destroys **all of Broca's area** but little surrounding tissue typically has **no lasting effects on speech** (Penfield & Roberts, 1959; Rasmussen & Milner, 1975; Zangwill, 1975). Some speech problems did follow removal of Broca's area, but their **time course** pointed to **post-surgical oedema** (swelling) in surrounding tissue, not to the **excision** itself. Before effective anti-inflammatory drugs, patients often woke with language **fully intact**, developed serious problems **over the next few hours**, and recovered over the following **weeks**. Damage from the excision would be present immediately and would not go away; swelling builds up and then subsides, which is exactly the pattern seen.
- Discrete surgical lesions of the **arcuate fasciculus** did **not** produce permanent speech difficulties, and lesions restricted to the cortex of the **angular gyrus** did **not** produce permanent alexia and agraphia (Rasmussen & Milner, 1975).
- Removal of **Wernicke's area** is less well documented, because surgeons have been reluctant to remove it given Wernicke's dire predictions. Nevertheless, in some cases a good portion has been removed **without lasting language deficits** (e.g., Ojemann, 1979; Penfield & Roberts, 1959).

The book's six cases from Penfield and Roberts (1959) show the oedema signature (little immediate problem, aphasia after hours or days, recovery within weeks). For example, **J.M.** was almost totally aphasic by day 3 but by day 18 had no difficulty in speech, naming or reading (spelling and writing remained poor); **J.C.** became completely aphasic 18 hours after surgery, with only mild aphasia at 21 days; **D.H.** and **A.D.** had essentially no lasting problems.

### Hécaen and Angelergues (1964): accidental and disease-related damage

**Hécaen and Angelergues (1964)** published the first **large-scale study** of accidental or disease-related brain damage and aphasia. They rated articulation, fluency, comprehension, naming, repetition of spoken sentences, reading and writing in **214 right-handed patients with left-hemisphere damage**, estimating the location and extent of damage by post-mortem examination or visual inspection during later surgery. Their findings:

- **Small** lesions of Broca's area **seldom** produced lasting language deficits, and small lesions restricted to Wernicke's area **did not always** do so.
- **Larger** lesions produced more lasting deficits; but, contrary to the model, **problems of articulation were just as likely after parietal or temporal lesions** as after comparable lesions near Broca's area.
- **None** of the 214 patients showed aphasia that was **totally expressive** (Broca's) or **totally receptive** (Wernicke's).

### Evidence from structural neuroimaging

Since their development in the **1970s**, **CT and MRI** have been used extensively to analyse the damage associated with aphasia. Several large studies of aphasic patients with accidental or disease-related damage (e.g., Yourganov et al., 2015) have confirmed and extended the earlier findings, and "have not been kind" to the model:

- **No** aphasic patients have damage **restricted** to Broca's area or Wernicke's area.
- Aphasic patients almost always have significant damage to **subcortical white matter**.
- **Large anterior** lesions are more likely to produce **expressive** symptoms; **large posterior** lesions more likely to produce **receptive** symptoms.
- **Global aphasia** (severe disruption of **all** language abilities) is usually related to **massive lesions of anterior cortex, posterior cortex and underlying white matter**.
- Aphasia sometimes occurs with damage that does **not** touch the Wernicke-Geschwind areas: with visible damage only to the **medial frontal lobe, subcortical white matter, basal ganglia or thalamus**.

In summary, large-scale objective studies, whether by autopsy, observation during surgery or brain scans, have **not confirmed the model's major predictions** (Nasios, Dardiotis, & Messinis, 2019; Rahimpour et al., 2019).

### Evidence from electrical stimulation of the cortex

The first large-scale human brain-stimulation studies were carried out by **Wilder Penfield** and colleagues in the **1940s** at the **Montreal Neurological Institute** (Rahimpour et al., 2019). One purpose was to **map each patient's language areas** so they could be avoided during surgery. Conscious patients under **local anaesthetic** were stimulated at points on the cortical surface; the effect of each stimulation was dictated to a **stenographer** (this was before tape recorders), and a tiny **numbered card** was dropped on the site for later photography.

The book illustrates the responses of a **37-year-old right-handed** patient with epilepsy whose seizures began about **3 months after a blow to the head**; at the time of his operation, in **1948**, he had suffered seizures for **6 years** despite medication. Sites around the central fissure produced simple sensory and motor effects (tingling in the right thumb, jaw movements, sensations in lips and tongue), since cortex just **posterior** to it is **somatosensory** and just **anterior** is **motor**. Other sites affected language: stimulation **stopped his speech**; at one site his mouth moved but **no sound** came; at others he had **naming** difficulty (shown a foot: "That is what you put in your shoes," saying "foot" only after stimulation stopped). At one site he could not name pictures while the EEG showed **seizure activity**; when it stopped he said, "Now I can talk."

### Penfield and Roberts (1959) and Ojemann: scattered sites and a mosaic

Because stimulation is **much more localised** than a lesion, it is a useful test of the model. **Penfield and Roberts (1959)** published the first large-scale study of cortical stimulation and speech. They found that sites at which stimulation **blocked or disrupted speech** were **scattered throughout a large expanse of frontal, temporal and parietal cortex**, not restricted to the Wernicke-Geschwind areas. There was **no tendency** for particular kinds of disturbance (mispronunciation, confused counting, inability to name, misnaming) to come from particular areas; such sites were **intermingled**. **Right-hemisphere** stimulation almost **never** disrupted speech.

**Ojemann** and colleagues (Corina et al., 2010; McDermott, Watson, & Ojemann, 2005) assessed **naming**, **reading of simple sentences**, **short-term verbal memory**, ability to **mimic movements of the face and mouth**, and ability to recognise **phonemes** during stimulation. (A **phoneme** is the smallest unit of sound that distinguishes words in a language; its pronunciation varies slightly depending on the sounds next to it.) Their findings relevant to the model:

- Stimulation **far beyond** the Wernicke-Geschwind areas could disrupt language.
- Each language test was disrupted by stimulation at **widely scattered** sites.
- There were **major individual differences** in the organisation of language abilities.

Because the effect at a given site was often **very specific** (disrupting only one test), Ojemann suggested that language cortex is organised like a **mosaic**, with **discrete columns** of tissue performing particular functions **widely distributed** throughout the language areas.

> **Trap.** Penfield and Roberts (1959) appear twice: in the **handedness lesion** data (unilateral excisions) and in the **stimulation** study (scattered speech-disruption sites, right-hemisphere stimulation almost never disrupting speech). The **mosaic** idea belongs to **Ojemann**, not Penfield.

### Current status of the Wernicke-Geschwind model

The evidence supports the model in **two general respects**. First, **Broca's and Wernicke's areas do play important roles** in language; many aphasics have diffuse damage that involves one or both. Second, there is a **tendency** for **anterior** damage to produce more **expressive** deficits and **posterior** damage more **receptive** deficits. But four major predictions fail:

1. Damage **restricted** to the Wernicke-Geschwind areas often has **little lasting effect**; aphasia is typically associated with **widespread** damage.
2. Damage that includes **none** of the Wernicke-Geschwind areas can produce aphasia (Lorch, 2019).
3. Broca's and Wernicke's aphasias **rarely exist in pure form**; aphasia virtually always involves **both expressive and receptive** symptoms (O'Sullivan, Brownsett, & Copland, 2019).
4. There are **major differences between people** in the locations of cortical language areas.

Even so, the model has been extremely important. It guided the study and clinical diagnosis of aphasia for **more than four decades**, and clinical neuropsychologists **still use Broca's aphasia and Wernicke's aphasia as diagnostic categories**, with the understanding that the syndromes are less selective and the damage more diffuse and variable than the model implies. Because its major predictions lack empirical support, the model has been **largely abandoned by researchers**, but it remains **prominent in the classroom and clinic** (Dick, Bernal, & Tremblay, 2014; Nasios, Dardiotis, & Messinis, 2019; Poeppel et al., 2012).

> **Trap.** "Abandoned by researchers" does **not** mean "abandoned by clinicians". The book's precise verdict: largely abandoned in research, still prominent in classroom and clinic, and its two partial successes are (1) Broca's and Wernicke's areas matter, and (2) the **anterior-expressive / posterior-receptive tendency**.

## 16.7 Cognitive Neuroscience of Language

The **cognitive neuroscience approach** currently dominates language research. It is defined by three premises, each of which contradicts an assumption of the Wernicke-Geschwind model.

### Three premises of the cognitive neuroscience approach

**Premise 1: Language is mediated by widespread activity in all the brain areas that take part in the cognitive processes involved in a particular language behaviour.** The Wernicke-Geschwind model assumes each language area is dedicated to a **complex** process such as speech production, comprehension or reading. Cognitive neuroscience assumes each complex process results from a **combination of constituent cognitive processes**, which may be organised separately in different parts of the brain (Bouchard, 2015). So the **constituent processes**, not the general Wernicke-Geschwind processes, are the right level of analysis. Cognitive neuroscientists usually divide them into three categories: **phonological analysis** (the **sound** of language), **grammatical analysis** (the **structure** of language), and **semantic analysis** (the **meaning** of language).

**Premise 2: The brain areas involved in language are not dedicated solely to language** (Friederici & Singer, 2015). The Wernicke-Geschwind model treated large areas of left cortex as language-only; cognitive neuroscience assumes many constituent processes also serve **other kinds of behaviour**.

**Premise 3: Because many language areas are also parts of other functional systems, they are likely to be small, widely distributed and specialised** (Friederici & Singer, 2015). By contrast, the Wernicke-Geschwind areas are assumed to be **large, circumscribed and homogeneous** (Friederici & Gierhan, 2013).

| Assumption | Wernicke-Geschwind model | Cognitive neuroscience approach |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | Complex processes (comprehension, production, reading) | Constituent processes (phonological, grammatical, semantic) |
| Dedicated to language? | Yes, large areas language-only | No, areas shared with other functions |
| Size and layout of areas | Large, circumscribed, homogeneous | Small, widely distributed, specialised |
| Main method | Brain-damaged patients | Also functional imaging in healthy volunteers |

### Method, and the causation caveat

The approach also differs in **method**: the Wernicke-Geschwind model rests heavily on brain-damaged patients, whereas cognitive neuroscientists also use other techniques, most notably **functional brain imaging in healthy volunteers**. But functional imaging **cannot prove causation** (Cloutman et al., 2011). It is tempting to assume that activity recorded during a cognitive process plays a causal role in it, but correlation does not prove causation. For example, substantial **right-hemisphere activity** is virtually always recorded during language tasks, yet **right-hemisphere lesions only rarely produce lasting language deficits** (Hickok et al., 2008).

### Bavelier's fMRI study of reading

**Bavelier and colleagues (1997)** used fMRI to record brain activity while healthy volunteers **read silently**. The method was notable in two ways. First, they used a **particularly sensitive** fMRI machine that could localise activity more accurately than most earlier studies and **without having to average** across participants. Second, they recorded activity during the reading of **sentences**, a natural task, rather than the simpler, more controllable but unnatural tasks (such as listening to single words) used in most imaging studies. Volunteers read sentences on a screen, alternating with **control periods** in which they saw **strings of consonants**; the difference between reading and control activity identified reading-related areas.

Analysing individual participants on individual trials, **before any averaging**, gave three key findings:

- Activity was **patchy**: tiny areas of activity separated by areas of inactivity (Leech & Saygin, 2011).
- The patches were **variable**: they differed from participant to participant and even from **trial to trial** in the same person.
- Although activity often appeared in parts of the classic Wernicke-Geschwind areas, it was **widespread over the lateral surfaces** of the brain.

The researchers then **averaged** the data in the usual way, deliberately, to show how **misleading** averaging is. Averaging creates the false impression that **large, homogeneous expanses** of tissue are active during reading, whereas on any given trial the patches occupied only **5 to 10 percent** of the illustrated areas. Two points still emerge from the averaged map: there was **far more activity in the left hemisphere** than the right (though right-hemisphere activity was significant), and activity extended **far beyond** the areas the Wernicke-Geschwind model predicts for silent reading (for instance, activity in **Broca's area and primary motor cortex** during *silent* reading would not have been predicted).

> **Trap.** Bavelier et al. (1997): **fMRI**, **silent reading of sentences**, control = **consonant strings**. Unaveraged activity was **patchy, variable and widespread**; the patches covered only **5 to 10 percent** of the averaged areas. The averaged "large active regions" are an **artefact of averaging**, and this is the key point of the study.

### Damasio's PET study of naming

**Damasio and colleagues (1996)** looked selectively at **temporal-lobe** activity during **naming** of objects in particular categories. **PET** activity was recorded from the **left temporal lobes** of healthy volunteers as they named images of three types: **famous faces, animals and tools**. To isolate naming, the researchers **subtracted** the activity recorded while volunteers **judged the orientation** of the same images. They limited the PET images to the left temporal lobe to allow a finer-grained analysis.

Naming activated areas of the left temporal lobe **outside the classic Wernicke's area**, and, remarkably, the exact area depended on the **category**: faces, animals and tools each activated a **slightly different area**. Other imaging studies have confirmed **category-specific encoding** of words in the left temporal lobe (Yi, Leonard, & Chang, 2019). And aphasic patients with naming difficulties **specific to famous faces, animals or tools** have been shown to have damage in **one of the three left temporal areas** Damasio and colleagues identified, which supplies the lesion evidence that imaging alone could not.

> **Trap.** Bavelier = **fMRI**, reading, patchiness. Damasio = **PET**, naming, **category-specific** left temporal areas (faces, animals, tools), outside Wernicke's area, with a subtraction control of **orientation judgement**. Swapping the techniques is a classic distractor.

## 16.8 Cognitive Neuroscience of Dyslexia

### What dyslexia is: developmental versus acquired

**Dyslexia** is a **reading disorder** that does **not** result from general visual, motor or intellectual deficits. There are two fundamentally different types. **Developmental dyslexias** become apparent when a **child is learning to read**. **Acquired dyslexias** are caused by **brain damage** in people who **could already read**. Developmental dyslexia is widespread: estimates among English-speaking children range from **5 to 12 percent** (Norton, Beach, & Gabrieli, 2015), depending on the criteria, and it is **two to three times more common in boys than girls**. Acquired dyslexias are **relatively rare**.

### Developmental dyslexia: causes and neural mechanisms

Because developmental dyslexia is far more common and its causes less obvious, most research has focused on it. Two problems complicate the search for its neural correlates. The disorder occurs in **various forms**, which likely have different neural correlates (O'Brien, Wolf, & Lovett, 2012; Peyrin et al., 2012). And the **large reduction in reading may itself change the brain**, so it is hard to know whether a brain difference is a **cause or a consequence** of dyslexia (Bishop, 2013).

One way around the cause-consequence problem is to compare dyslexic children with **"ability-matched"** children, typically **many years younger**, who read at the same level (Norton, Beach, & Gabrieli, 2015). The two groups are then matched for the **amount of reading** they have done, so any brain difference cannot simply be a result of less reading experience. In one such study, **Hoeft et al. (2007)** found that dyslexic children showed **reduced fMRI activity in left parietal cortex and in the left occipitotemporal junction** (where the occipital lobe borders the temporal lobe) relative to their ability-matched controls.

Many researchers have tried to attribute developmental dyslexia to a single pathology: **attentional, auditory, visual or sensorimotor** deficits (Goswami, 2014; Harrar et al., 2014; Stoodley & Stein, 2013). But although many people with dyslexia have subtle deficits of these kinds, **many do not**, and even where present the deficits **do not account for all aspects** of the disorder. There is now **fairly widespread agreement** that dyslexia results most commonly from a **specific disturbance of phonological processing** (the representation and comprehension of speech sounds) (Ramus et al., 2013; Hahn, Foxe, & Molholm, 2014), though the book flags dissent (**but see Stein, 2018**).

> **Trap.** The "ability-matched" design compares dyslexic children with **younger** children of the **same reading level**, not with same-age controls, precisely to control for **reading experience** as a possible consequence. The current majority view is a **phonological** deficit; visual, auditory, attentional and sensorimotor deficits are present in **some** dyslexics but do not explain the disorder.

### Two ways to read aloud: lexical and phonetic procedures

Cognitive psychologists have long recognised that reading aloud can be done in **two entirely different ways**:

- The **lexical procedure** is based on **specific stored information about written words**: the reader looks at the word, **recognises** it, and says it. It **dominates for familiar words**.
- The **phonetic procedure**: the reader looks at the word, recognises the **letters**, **sounds them out**, and says the word. It **dominates for unfamiliar words**.

This simple analysis explains two kinds of **acquired** dyslexia.

### Surface dyslexia

In **surface dyslexia**, patients have **lost the lexical procedure** (they can no longer pronounce words from specific memories of them) but **retain the phonetic procedure** (they can still apply rules of pronunciation). So they can still read **regular words** whose pronunciation follows common rules (*fish, river, glass*) and can pronounce **nonwords** by the rules (*spleemer, twipple*), but they have great difficulty with **irregular words** (*have, lose, steak*). Their errors are **misapplications of common rules**: *have, lose* and *steak* are pronounced to rhyme with *cave, hose* and *beak*.

### Deep dyslexia

In **deep dyslexia** (also called **phonological dyslexia**), patients have **lost the phonetic procedure** but **retain the lexical procedure**. So they are **completely unable to pronounce nonwords**, and they have difficulty with **uncommon words** and words with **abstract meanings**. They react to words with various **lexical strategies**, responding to the word's **overall look**, its **meaning**, or its **derivation**, which yields a characteristic pattern of errors: "quill" for *quail* (look), "hen" for *chicken* (meaning), "wise" for *wisdom* (derivation).

| | Surface dyslexia | Deep (phonological) dyslexia |
|---|---|---|
| Lost procedure | Lexical | Phonetic |
| Retained procedure | Phonetic | Lexical |
| Nonwords (*spleemer*) | Read correctly | Cannot read at all |
| Irregular words (*steak*) | Regularised errors (rhymes with *beak*) | Read if familiar and concrete |
| Typical error | Rule misapplication | Visual, semantic or derivational substitution ("hen" for *chicken*) |

> **Trap.** The names mislead. **Deep** dyslexia = **phonological** dyslexia = **phonetic** route lost, so **nonwords impossible** and **semantic errors** ("hen" for *chicken*). **Surface** dyslexia = **lexical** route lost, so **irregular words regularised** but nonwords fine. Also, both are described here as **acquired** dyslexias, resulting from brain damage.

### Where are the two procedures in the brain?

Most research on this question has studied deep dyslexia. People with deep dyslexia usually have **extensive damage to the left-hemisphere language areas**, suggesting that the disrupted **phonetic procedure** is **widely distributed in frontal and temporal areas of the left hemisphere**. But what maintains the surviving **lexical** procedure? There are **two theories**, both with some support:

1. The surviving lexical abilities depend on **surviving parts of the left-hemisphere language areas**; evidence is neural activity in those surviving regions during reading (Laine et al., 2000; Price et al., 1998).
2. The surviving lexical abilities depend on the **right hemisphere**. The following case supports this theory.

### The Case of N.I., the Woman Who Read with Her Right Hemisphere

N.I. was a healthy girl until, at **13**, she began to have periods of **aphasia**, followed several weeks later by a **generalised convulsion**. Many convulsions followed, and her speech and motor abilities deteriorated badly. **CT scans** showed **ischaemic damage to the left hemisphere**. Two years after onset she had **continual seizures**, **blindness in her right visual field**, and **no meaningful movement or perception in her right limbs**. To relieve these symptoms she underwent a **total left hemispherectomy**: her **left hemisphere was completely removed**. Her **seizures were totally arrested**.

Her reading is poor, but its pattern is strikingly like that of people with **deep dyslexia** and of **split-brain patients reading with their right hemispheres**. She **recognises letters** but **cannot translate them into sounds**; she can read **concrete, familiar words**; she **cannot pronounce even simple nonsense words** (such as *neg*); and her errors show she reads by **meaning and appearance** rather than by sounding out (shown the word *fruit*, she said, "Juice ... it's apples and pears and ... fruit"). In other words, she has a **severe deep dyslexia** (**Patterson, Vargha-Khadem, & Polkey, 1989**). Because she has **no left hemisphere at all**, her surviving lexical reading must be done by the **right** hemisphere, which is why the case supports the second theory.

The authors note that the case of N.I. **completes the circle**: the chapter began with lateralisation of function, and it ends on the same note.

> **Trap.** N.I. had a **total left hemispherectomy** (removal of the whole left hemisphere), not a commissurotomy. Her reading is **deep dyslexia** (no nonwords, semantic errors such as "juice" for *fruit*), which supports the theory that the **right hemisphere** can mediate the **lexical** procedure.

### Themes revisited

**Clinical implications** dominate the chapter; **creative thinking** counters pop-culture oversimplification; the **evolutionary perspective** and comparative research (Myers and Sperry's cats) were central; **neuroplasticity** appears in the sensitive period for language sounds and in dyslexia; and **consciousness** is the dominant emerging theme, because split-brain patients challenge the unity of conscious experience.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Lateralisation is real but statistical.** Language is the most lateralised ability, yet the right-ear advantage is only 55:45 and the right hemisphere understands many words. Every other difference is a bias, not a monopoly.
2. **Method determines what you can conclude.** Lesions and amytal show necessity; dichotic listening and imaging show bias and correlation; split-brain studies show what each hemisphere can do alone. Imaging cannot prove causation, which is why right-hemisphere activity during language does not mean the right hemisphere is needed for language.
3. **Disconnection reveals function.** The corpus callosum looked useless until Myers and Sperry gave information to one hemisphere and tested the other. The same logic (flash to one visual field, touch under a ledge) exposed the two minds of the human split brain.
4. **Big labels break into small processes.** "Language", "spatial ability" and "memory" are clusters; current research lateralises and localises constituent processes. This is the same move that turns the Wernicke-Geschwind model into the cognitive neuroscience approach.
5. **Box-and-arrow models fail the evidence.** Excisions, large lesion studies, CT/MRI, stimulation mapping and fMRI all show small, scattered, variable language patches, not large dedicated centres; but the anterior-expressive / posterior-receptive tendency survives, and clinicians still use the syndrome names.
6. **Two routes, two dyslexias.** The lexical and phonetic procedures explain surface and deep dyslexia, and N.I. shows that the right hemisphere alone can sustain lexical reading, bringing the chapter back to lateralisation.

## Worked example

*A 24-year-old right-handed engineering student with intractable epilepsy since age 9 is being assessed. Before a planned callosotomy, the team performs a sodium amytal test: left carotid injection produces mutism for about two minutes followed by naming errors; right injection produces no mutism. A year after surgery he reports that he "sometimes can't say what his left hand is holding", that his left hand once switched off a match he was watching, and that he is "fine otherwise". In testing, a picture of a key is flashed for 0.1 second to the left of fixation. He says he saw nothing, but his left hand, under a ledge, picks out a key. When asked to say whether a red or green light was shown to the left, he starts at chance but improves, often changing answers after frowning.*

**Which hemisphere is dominant for speech?** The **left**. Only left-sided injection silenced him, the classic Wada (1949) result; as a right-hander he fits Milner's (1974) 92 percent group.

**Why does he say he saw nothing, yet pick out the key?** A 0.1-second left-visual-field flash reaches only the **right** hemisphere; the speaking left hemisphere truly saw nothing, while the right hemisphere identifies the key non-verbally with the hand it controls (the left). This is hemispheric independence, not blindness or agnosia.

**Why can he not name what his left hand holds, though he knows something is there?** Fine touch from the left hand goes to the right hemisphere; crude ipsilateral somatosensory fibres tell the left hemisphere *that* something is there, but not *what*.

**Is his improvement on the colour task evidence of callosal regrowth?** No. Frowning before changing his answer is **cross-cuing**: the right hemisphere hears a wrong guess and signals through the face, which the left hemisphere perceives. The clinical lesson is the book's: patients solve tasks by unintended routes, which can mask deficits.

**What about the television episode?** Inter-hemispheric conflict, as in the case of Peter (Joseph, 1988), though such conflict is unusual; in most patients the left hemisphere controls everyday behaviour.

**What should you not conclude?** That his hemispheres are completely independent. Emotional tone often crosses (Sperry, Zaidel, & Zaidel, 1979), and harder tasks recruit both hemispheres (Weissman & Banich, 2000).

## MCQ traps

1. **Dax (1836)** noticed the left-hemisphere link first (about 40 patients); **Broca (1861, two patients; 1864, seven more)** gets the credit.
2. **Liepmann's apraxia** has **bilateral** symptoms but follows **left**-hemisphere damage.
3. **Sodium amytal** goes into the **carotid** artery and anaesthetises the **same-side** hemisphere; speech-side injection gives **mutism for a minute or two**.
4. **Dichotic listening** is non-invasive; the right-ear advantage reflects **stronger contralateral** connections winning under competition, not exclusively crossed pathways.
5. The right-ear advantage is only **55:45**.
6. **Handedness lesion data** (Russell & Espir, 1961; Penfield & Roberts, 1959): dextrals 60% vs 2%; sinestrals 30% vs 24%. Left-handers are **more variable**, not mirror-reversed.
7. **Milner (1974):** 92 / 69 / 30 percent left-hemisphere speech.
8. **Levy (1972)** proposed, **McGlone (1977, 1980)** supported, the male-more-lateralised hypothesis; the **17-study meta-analysis** (Hirnstein, Hugdahl & Hausmann, 2019) found **no sex effect**: no longer tenable.
9. **Myers & Sperry (1953):** experimental cats learned at a **normal** rate; after the patch switch they fell to **50 percent** with **no savings**.
10. Human testing used a **0.1-second** flash to one **visual field**, not an eye patch, and a **ledge** to hide the hands.
11. Vogel and Bogen **operated**; Sperry and Gazzaniga **tested**; Sperry's **Nobel 1981**.
12. Failure to name a left-visual-field object is a failure of the **speaking hemisphere's access**, not of perception.
13. **Cross-cuing** is an **external** route (frown, head shake), distinct from indirect brainstem pathways.
14. "**Two oranges**" = hidden-hands two-bag test; **helping hand** = full-view table test, left hand redirects right hand.
15. **Chimeric figures** (Levy, Trevarthen & Sperry, 1972): verbal report describes the **right**-field half-face, completed into a whole face; nothing odd noticed.
16. **Emotion** crosses readily in most split-brain patients; **harder tasks** involve both hemispheres.
17. **Braille** and **geometry** are right-hemisphere entries; **arithmetic** and **ipsilateral movement** are left.
18. Right-hemisphere emotion dominance is supported by **lesions**, not unambiguously by **imaging**.
19. **Kimura (1964):** digits right ear, melodies left ear, in 20 right-handers.
20. Heschl's gyrus lies **anterior** to the planum temporale; Kong et al. (2018): Heschl's larger on the left, frontal operculum **anterior right / posterior left**.
21. Anatomical asymmetries have **not** been correlated with language lateralisation within individuals.
22. **W.L.** (sign-language aphasia, intact pantomime) supports the **linguistic** theory, not the motor theory.
23. Do not confuse Kimura's **motor theory of cerebral asymmetry** with the **motor theory of speech perception**; the latter is contradicted by motor-cortex lesion cases.
24. **Conduction aphasia** = poor **repetition** with good comprehension and spontaneous speech (arcuate fasciculus).
25. **Dejerine (1892):** pure alexia and agraphia; damage to pathways from visual cortex to the **left angular gyrus**.
26. Excision of Broca's area gives **no lasting** aphasia; delayed, transient problems reflect **oedema**.
27. **Hécaen & Angelergues (1964):** 214 patients, **none** with purely expressive or purely receptive aphasia.
28. **Ojemann:** mosaic of scattered columns; **Penfield & Roberts (1959):** right-hemisphere stimulation almost never disrupts speech.
29. Bavelier = **fMRI**, silent reading, patches 5 to 10 percent; Damasio = **PET**, category-specific naming.
30. **Deep dyslexia** = phonetic route lost, nonwords impossible, semantic errors; **surface dyslexia** = lexical route lost, irregular words regularised.
