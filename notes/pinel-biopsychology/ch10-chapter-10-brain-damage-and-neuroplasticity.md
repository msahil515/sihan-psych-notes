# Chapter 10: Brain Damage and Neuroplasticity

**What this chapter is really about.** The 11th edition gives this chapter a question for a subtitle: can the brain recover from damage? Pinel and Barnes cannot answer it straight away, because an answer only makes sense once you know how brains get damaged, which diseases do the damaging, and what the nervous system does on its own when part of it is destroyed. So the chapter is built like a clinical case history written at the scale of a whole field: causes first, then diseases, then the tools used to study them (animal models), then the brain's own responses, and finally the treatments that try to harness those responses. Two motives run through all of it. Damaged brains teach us how healthy brains work, and they are the starting point for every treatment we have.

The answer the chapter reaches is double-edged, and you should be able to state both edges. On the discouraging side, the adult mammalian central nervous system barely regrows its axons, recovery after CNS damage is usually modest, and many apparent "recoveries" turn out to be something else (swelling going down, or the patient finding a new way round the problem). On the encouraging side, the adult brain is plastic: its maps reorganise after damage, healthy neurons sprout into vacated territory, new neurons are born near injuries, and training, enriched experience and exercise measurably improve outcomes in animals and in some human trials. Neuroplasticity is the bridge between the two halves of the chapter's title.

**How to read this chapter.** The book has five modules, numbered here 10.1 to 10.5. 10.1 covers six causes of brain damage (tumours, strokes, traumatic brain injuries, infections, neurotoxins, genetic factors) and the process that mediates most of them, programmed cell death. 10.2 covers five neurological diseases: epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease. 10.3 covers two animal models (kindling for epilepsy, MPTP for Parkinson's disease) and the warning that goes with all animal models. 10.4 covers the four responses of the nervous system to damage: degeneration, regeneration, reorganisation and recovery. 10.5 covers treatments: neurotransplantation, rehabilitative training, and the treatment of phantom limbs. The chapter is bracketed by the case of Professor P., whose brain tumour opens it and whose recovery closes it. Almost every examinable item here is a contrast (encapsulated versus infiltrating, haemorrhage versus ischaemia, thrombus versus embolus, apoptosis versus necrosis, focal versus generalised, tonic-clonic versus absence, anterograde versus retrograde, PNS versus CNS regeneration), so read with a two-column table in your head.

## The Ironic Case of Professor P.

You first met Professor P. in Chapter 5, undergoing a day of pre-surgical tests that he recognised from his own research. Chapter 10 tells the story from the beginning and adds the outcome.

His first symptom was trivial: an occasional loss of balance. He blamed it on getting older, and his doctor did not think it worth pursuing, though Professor P. kept an eye on it. Three years later the balance problem had not gone away, and something new had appeared: on the telephone he could hear well only when he switched the receiver to his left ear. He was losing the hearing in his right ear. A specialist ran a quick, poorly controlled hearing test and reassured him that a little hearing loss is normal in middle age. He did not push for a second opinion, and he still regrets it.

About a year after that the picture became impossible to explain away. The right side of his mouth was numb, swallowing had become difficult, and his right eye was not producing enough tears. Sitting with a diagram of the cranial nerves, he worked out the localisation himself. The auditory and vestibular nerves join to form **cranial nerve VIII (the auditory-vestibular nerve)**; hearing loss and imbalance on the right pointed to the right nerve VIII, and the numbness, swallowing and tearing problems meant that whatever was sitting there had grown large enough to press on neighbouring **cranial nerves V through X**. Something slow-growing at that site was most likely a tumour.

Rather than go straight back to his doctor, he volunteered as a control participant in a friend's MRI study, knowing the scan would show the lesion. It did: a large tumour on the right cranial nerve VIII, exactly where he had predicted. From there he was referred from his doctor to a neurologist and from the neurologist to a neurosurgeon. During the 6-hour operation he stopped breathing, and he woke on life support in intensive care. Confused and hallucinating under morphine, he became convinced that his wife was calling for help and tried to get out of bed to reach her; the nursing staff increased his medication, and when he next came round he had been restrained.

The surgery cut his right auditory-vestibular nerve, so he is permanently deaf on the right and has no vestibular function on that side. He was also left with **partial hemifacial paralysis**, including serious problems with blinking and tearing. He survived and improved a great deal, and the case ends with a reveal: Professor P. is **John Pinel (JP)**, the book's first author.

### Why the case opens this chapter

The case is chosen to carry several of the chapter's lessons at once. The tumour was of the "good" kind, encapsulated and benign, yet it nearly killed him, because a mass in a confined space damages whatever it presses against. The symptoms were localisable by anyone who knew the cranial nerves, but the diagnosis was still delayed for years, partly because early neurological signs (unsteadiness, hearing loss) look like ordinary ageing. And because Professor P. is a biopsychologist, the book can return to him at the end of 10.5 to show someone deliberately applying the research on neuroplasticity to his own recovery.

> **Trap.** Professor P.'s tumour was benign and encapsulated, but "benign" in tumour terminology means surgically removable with little risk of regrowth, not harmless. An encapsulated tumour damages the brain by **pressure**. Also keep the nerve numbers straight: the tumour sat on **cranial nerve VIII** (auditory-vestibular, also called vestibulocochlear); the spread of symptoms implicated **V to X**.

## 10.1 Causes of Brain Damage

The module surveys six causes of brain damage: **brain tumours, cerebrovascular disorders (strokes), closed-head traumatic brain injuries, infections of the brain, neurotoxins, and genetic factors**. It ends with **programmed cell death**, which is not a seventh cause so much as the common pathway through which most of the other six actually kill neurons.

### Brain tumours

A **tumour**, or **neoplasm** (the word literally means "new growth"), is a mass of cells that grows independently of the rest of the body. The first thing to know about any brain tumour is whether it grows inside its own boundary or spreads through the tissue around it, because that one property largely decides how dangerous it is and whether surgery can cure it.

**Meningiomas** make up about **20 percent** of the tumours found in the human brain. They grow between the **meninges**, the three membranes that cover the central nervous system. Every meningioma is an **encapsulated tumour**, meaning it grows within its own membrane. Three consequences follow from encapsulation, and they are worth learning as a chain:

1. Because the tumour has a clear boundary, it is particularly easy to see on a **CT scan**.
2. Because it does not invade the neural tissue, it can affect brain function **only by the pressure** it exerts on surrounding tissue.
3. Because it can be removed whole, it is almost always a **benign tumour**: one that can be removed surgically with little risk of further growth in the body.

Encapsulation is the exception, not the rule. Apart from meningiomas, most brain tumours are **infiltrating tumours**, which grow diffusely through the surrounding tissue. Because there is no clean edge to cut along, they are usually **malignant tumours**: hard to remove or destroy completely, and any cancerous tissue left behind after surgery generally keeps growing. The most common malignant brain tumours are **gliomas**, tumours that develop from **glial cells**; they are infiltrating and they grow rapidly.

About **10 percent** of brain tumours do not start in the brain at all. They grow from infiltrating cells carried to the brain in the bloodstream from somewhere else in the body, and they are called **metastatic tumours** (**metastasis** is the transmission of disease from one organ to another). Many metastatic brain tumours begin as **lung cancers**. The prognosis is poor: once a cancer has established itself at two or more separate sites, the chance of recovery is currently slim.

| | **Encapsulated** | **Infiltrating** | **Metastatic** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth | Within its own membrane | Diffusely through surrounding tissue | Seeded from a cancer elsewhere, via the blood |
| How it harms the brain | Pressure on neighbouring tissue | Invades and replaces tissue | Invades tissue, often at several sites |
| Usual nature | **Benign** | **Malignant** | Malignant |
| Book's examples | Meningiomas (~20% of brain tumours), acoustic neuromas | Gliomas (most common malignant type) | Often from lung cancer (~10% of brain tumours) |

### Acoustic neuromas

Professor P. was lucky in one respect: his tumour was encapsulated. An encapsulated tumour growing on cranial nerve VIII is called an **acoustic neuroma** (a **neuroma** is any tumour that grows on a nerve or tract). The MRI in Figure 10.3 is the actual scan he took to his doctor.

> **Trap.** Two confusions to avoid. First, "most brain tumours are meningiomas" is false: meningiomas are about **20 percent**, and most brain tumours are **infiltrating**; the commonest malignant type is the **glioma**. Second, metastatic tumours are about **10 percent**, and they are defined by where they **came from** (another organ), not by how fast they grow. Outside this book you will see acoustic neuromas called **vestibular schwannomas**, because they usually arise from the Schwann cells of the vestibular part of nerve VIII; the two names refer to the same tumour.

### Strokes

**Strokes** are sudden-onset **cerebrovascular disorders** that damage the brain. In the United States, stroke ranks **fifth among causes of death**; it is also the single biggest source of neurological dysfunction and one of the leading reasons adults become disabled. What a stroke does depends on where it happens, but the common consequences the book lists are **amnesia, aphasia (language difficulties), psychiatric disorders, dementia, paralysis and coma**.

Two terms describe the geography of the damage. The **infarct** is the area of dead or dying tissue. Around it lies a dysfunctional zone called the **penumbra**, whose tissue may either recover or die over the following days depending on a range of factors. This is the single most important idea for understanding stroke treatment: the infarct is already lost, so **the primary goal of treatment is to save the penumbra**. Every acute treatment in this section, and the rehabilitative training in 10.5, is ultimately aimed at that rim of threatened tissue.

There are two major types of stroke, defined by what goes wrong with the blood supply: either a vessel bursts (**cerebral haemorrhage**) or a vessel is blocked (**cerebral ischaemia**, pronounced "iss-KEEM-ee-a").

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel, and Indian answer keys built on them, call stroke the **third** leading cause of death in the United States. The 11th edition says **fifth**. If an option says "third", it is the old figure.

### Cerebral haemorrhage and aneurysms

In a **cerebral haemorrhage** (bleeding in the brain), a cerebral blood vessel ruptures and blood seeps into the neural tissue around it, damaging it. A common cause of bleeding inside the brain is a bursting **aneurysm**: a pathological, balloon-like dilation that forms in an artery wall at a point where the wall has lost its normal elasticity. Think of a weak patch in a bicycle inner tube bulging out under pressure until it bursts.

Aneurysms can form in any part of the body, but those in the brain are particularly dangerous. They may be **congenital** (present at birth) or may result from exposure to vascular poisons or from infection. Because rising pressure in the vessel is what eventually ruptures a weak wall, people at risk are advised to avoid cigarette smoking, alcohol and **hypertension**.

### Cerebral ischaemia: thrombosis, embolism and arteriosclerosis

**Cerebral ischaemia** is a disruption of the blood supply to an area of the brain. It has three main causes, and examiners like to test whether you can tell them apart by **where the plug forms and where it ends up**.

- **Thrombosis.** A plug called a **thrombus** forms in a vessel and blocks blood flow **at the site where it formed**. A thrombus need not be a blood clot: it can be made of a clot, fat, oil, an air bubble, tumour cells, or any mixture of these.
- **Embolism.** The plug, here called an **embolus**, forms in a larger vessel and is **carried by the blood** to a smaller one, where it lodges. Pinel's one-line summary is that an embolus is just a thrombus that has travelled.
- **Arteriosclerosis.** The walls of blood vessels thicken and their channels narrow, usually because of fat deposits, and the narrowing can progress to complete blockage. The angiogram in Figure 10.4 shows a partly blocked **carotid artery**, one of the major routes of blood to the brain.

| Cause | What blocks the vessel | Where the block forms | Where it obstructs flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thrombosis** | Thrombus (clot, fat, oil, air, tumour cells) | In the vessel | The same place |
| **Embolism** | Embolus (a thrombus that moved) | A larger vessel upstream | A smaller vessel downstream |
| **Arteriosclerosis** | The vessel wall itself, thickened by deposits | Gradually, along the wall | Narrowing, eventually complete |

> **Trap.** **Haemorrhage = a vessel bursts and blood enters the tissue; ischaemia = a vessel is blocked and the tissue is starved of blood.** An aneurysm causes haemorrhage, not ischaemia. Thrombosis and embolism differ only in whether the plug **stays where it formed** (thrombus) or **travels** (embolus); a question describing a clot that "breaks loose from the carotid and lodges in a cerebral artery" is describing an **embolism**. The book's description of arteriosclerosis (narrowing from fat deposits) is strictly the form clinicians call atherosclerosis; for the exam, use the book's term.

### Two properties of ischaemic damage

Ischaemia-induced brain damage has two properties that matter both for research and for treatment.

**It takes time to develop.** Shortly after a temporary episode of cerebral ischaemia there is usually little or no sign of brain damage, yet a day or two later substantial neuron loss can often be found. The delay is the clue that the damage is not simply neurons suffocating on the spot; an active process is unfolding after the blood supply has returned. It also defines a window in which treatment could, in principle, intervene.

**It is not uniform across the brain.** Some neurons are far more vulnerable than others, and neurons in certain areas of the **hippocampus** are particularly susceptible. (This is why the ischaemia experiment of Xu and colleagues in 10.5 measures hippocampal damage and spatial memory.)

### Glutamate and the excitotoxic cascade

The delayed process turned out to involve, of all things, **glutamate**, the brain's most prevalent excitatory neurotransmitter. The book describes the mechanism as a cascade, and it is worth learning step by step because each step is a possible point for treatment:

1. A blood vessel becomes blocked.
2. Many of the neurons deprived of blood become overactive and release **excessive glutamate**.
3. The glutamate overactivates glutamate receptors on postsynaptic neurons, above all the **NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors**.
4. Large numbers of **Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ ions** flood into the postsynaptic neurons.
5. The excess internal Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ does two things. It makes these postsynaptic neurons themselves release excessive glutamate, which **spreads the toxic cascade** to further neurons; and it sets off a sequence of internal reactions that ultimately **kill the postsynaptic neurons**.

The process is usually called **excitotoxicity** (the book describes it without using that word). Notice what step 5 explains. Because each dying neuron passes the glutamate surge on to its neighbours, damage spreads outward from the blocked vessel over hours, which fits both the delay in ischaemic damage and the idea of a penumbra that can still be rescued.

> **Trap.** The paradox is the point: the transmitter that kills neurons in ischaemia is the brain's main **excitatory** transmitter, glutamate, acting mainly through **NMDA** receptors. The ions that flood in are **sodium and calcium**, not potassium or chloride. Distractors often offer GABA (inhibitory) or dopamine.

### Treating ischaemic stroke

If excessive glutamate causes much of the damage, blocking the glutaminergic cascade should prevent it. Some clinical trials have found **NMDA-receptor antagonists** effective after acute ischaemic stroke, but only if they are given almost immediately after the stroke begins, which makes them impractical in most real clinical situations: by the time most patients reach hospital, the cascade is well under way.

Two other treatments have been shown to work:

- **Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)**, a drug that breaks down blood clots.
- **Endovascular therapy**, the surgical removal of a thrombus or embolus from an artery.

Given within a few hours of the onset of an ischaemic stroke, either can improve recovery. Both act by restoring blood flow, which is exactly what saving the penumbra requires.

> **Trap.** tPA dissolves clots, so it is a treatment for **ischaemic** stroke. Giving a clot-dissolving drug to someone whose stroke is a **haemorrhage** would make the bleeding worse, which is why the type of stroke has to be established before treatment. The book's three acute options are NMDA antagonists (effective only if given almost at once), tPA and endovascular removal of the plug (effective within a few hours).

### Traumatic brain injuries

A **traumatic brain injury (TBI)** is brain damage caused by physical force. The numbers are larger than most people expect: about **50 to 60 million** people sustain some form of TBI each year, and about **50 percent** of us will have at least one in our lifetime.

The common intuition is that serious brain injury requires the skull to be broken through (a **penetrating TBI**). It does not. Any blow to the head deserves to be taken very seriously, especially if it is followed by confusion, sensory or motor disturbances, or loss of consciousness. Injuries produced by blows that do **not** penetrate the skull are called **closed-head TBIs**, and they are the subject of this section.

### Contusions, subdural haematomas and contrecoup injuries

**Contusions** are closed-head TBIs that damage the brain's **circulatory system**. The broken vessels bleed internally, and the blood collects in a localised pool: in plain terms, a bruised brain.

The paradox worth understanding is that the skull's hardness, the very property that protects the brain from penetrating injury, is the main reason contusions happen. The brain floats in cerebrospinal fluid inside a rigid case. A blow accelerates the head, the brain lags behind and then slams into the inside of the skull, and vessels at the point of impact tear. Blood from such an injury can collect in the **subdural space**, the space between the **dura mater** and the **arachnoid membrane**, where it can badly distort the neural tissue around it. That pool of blood is a **subdural haematoma**; in the CT scan of Figure 10.6 the haematoma has pushed the left lateral ventricle out of position.

The same mechanics explain a fact that surprises most students. Contusions often occur on the side of the brain **opposite** the point of the blow. These are **contrecoup injuries** (French for "counter-blow"): the blow drives the brain across the cranial cavity so that it strikes the inside of the skull on the far side of the head.

> **Trap.** Three easily confused terms. A **contusion** is the closed-head injury itself (damage to cerebral blood vessels, internal bleeding, a bruised brain). A **haematoma** is the localised collection of blood it produces; a **subdural** haematoma sits between the **dura mater and the arachnoid**. The book's own end-of-module quiz uses "hematoma" as the answer for a bruise caused by a localised blood clot, so read the wording of the question carefully. A **contrecoup** injury is on the side **opposite** the blow; the injury directly beneath the blow is the coup injury. If a patient is struck on the back of the head and has frontal damage, that is contrecoup.

### Mild TBI and the retirement of concussion

When a blow to the head disturbs consciousness but there is no evidence of a contusion or any other structural damage, the diagnosis is **mild TBI (mTBI)**. Most TBIs are mTBIs.

mTBIs used to be called **concussions**, and the change of name is itself examinable. "Concussion" was dropped because the word carried a false assumption: that the effects were temporary and left no lasting damage. The evidence now says otherwise. The effects of an mTBI can last for many years, and the effects of **repeated** mTBIs can **accumulate**. Renaming the condition as a (mild) brain *injury* is a way of building that evidence into the diagnosis.

> **Trap.** Older editions of Pinel define **concussion** as disturbed consciousness after a blow with no evidence of structural damage, and call the dementia of boxers **punch-drunk syndrome** (dementia pugilistica). The 11th edition replaces these with **mild TBI (mTBI)** and **chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)**, and says the term "concussion" is no longer appropriate because it implied no long-term damage. Indian papers may use either set of terms; they refer to the same things.

### Chronic traumatic encephalopathy

**Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)** is the **dementia** (general intellectual deterioration) and **cerebral scarring** often seen in boxers, rugby players, American football players and others who have had **repeated mTBIs**. Mez and colleagues (2017) reported that most former American football players, approximately **90 percent**, met the diagnostic criteria for CTE, as did many former recreational players. Treat that figure with some caution: it comes from brains examined after death, and brains donated for this kind of study often come from players whose families had already noticed problems, so the percentage describes that sample rather than every player. The direction of the finding, though, is not in doubt, and the National Football League has acknowledged a connection between playing football and CTE.

### The Case of Junior Seau

Junior Seau played as an all-star linebacker in the National Football League for **20 years** and was known for a hard-hitting, aggressive style. He never reported head injuries to coaches or medical staff, but his family said he had sustained many mTBIs: after games he often had severe headaches and would retreat to a dark bedroom, yet he always returned to play.

After he retired in **2010**, the people close to him saw a marked change in behaviour: heavy drinking, reckless business and financial decisions, gambling, and, most disturbing of all, violent outbursts that were completely out of character and were often aimed at the friends and family trying to help him. In **May 2012**, at the age of **43**, he died by suicide, leaving no note. His family donated his brain to the National Institutes of Health, and a detailed autopsy showed that he met the criteria for a diagnosis of **CTE**.

Read the case as a clinical picture of CTE rather than as a sports story. The injuries were repeated and individually "mild"; the changes emerged years later and after the injuries had stopped; and they were mainly changes in **judgement, impulse control and aggression** (reckless decisions, gambling, heavy drinking, violent outbursts) rather than an obvious memory disorder. The diagnosis could only be confirmed after death, at autopsy.

### Infections of the brain

An invasion of the brain by microorganisms is a **brain infection**, and the inflammation it produces is called **encephalitis**. The two common types are **bacterial** and **viral** infections.

### Bacterial infections: abscesses, meningitis and general paresis

Bacterial infection of the brain often leads to **cerebral abscesses**, pockets of pus within the brain tissue. Bacteria are also the main culprits in **meningitis**, an inflammation of the meninges that kills **30 percent** of the adults who develop it. Antibiotics such as penicillin sometimes clear bacterial brain infections, but they cannot reverse damage that has already been done, which is why speed of treatment matters.

The bacterial infection most students have heard of is **syphilis**. The bacteria pass from an infected to an uninfected person through contact with genital sores. They then lie **dormant for several years** before becoming virulent and attacking many parts of the body, the brain included. The syndrome of mental illness and dementia that results from a syphilitic brain infection is called **general paresis**.

> **Trap.** **Encephalitis** is inflammation of the brain caused by infection; **meningitis** is inflammation of the **meninges**, mostly bacterial, fatal in **30 percent** of adults in the 11th edition (older editions give **25 percent**). **General paresis** is the dementia and mental illness of late **syphilis**, a bacterial infection, not a viral one, and not a form of paralysis despite the name.

### Viral infections: rabies, mumps and herpes

Viral infections of the nervous system come in two kinds: viruses with a **particular affinity for neural tissue**, and viruses that can attack neural tissue but have **no greater affinity for it** than for other tissues.

**Rabies** is the standard example of the first kind. It is usually transmitted by the bite of a rabid animal, and its effect on the brain is cruelly self-serving: the fits of rage it produces make infected animals that normally attack by biting (dogs, cats, raccoons, bats, mice) more likely to bite and so spread the virus. Rabies infection of the brain is almost always fatal, but the virus has one redeeming feature: it usually does not reach the brain for **at least a month** after infection, which leaves time for preventive vaccination.

**Mumps** and **herpes** viruses are examples of the second kind. They sometimes spread into the brain, but typically they attack other tissues.

Viruses may contribute far more to neuropsychological disorders than is currently recognised. Their role in the **aetiology** (cause) of a disorder is hard to spot because a virus can lie **dormant for many years** before producing symptoms, by which time the link to the original infection is invisible.

### Neurotoxins

The nervous system can be damaged by many toxic chemicals, which can reach the general circulation through the **gastrointestinal tract, the lungs, or the skin**. The book groups neurotoxins into three kinds: environmental poisons such as heavy metals, therapeutic drugs that turn out to be toxic, and toxins the body makes itself.

**Heavy metals.** Mercury and lead can accumulate in the brain and permanently damage it, producing a **toxic psychosis**, a chronic mental illness produced by a neurotoxin. Two pieces of English history make the point memorable. The "mad hatter" of *Alice in Wonderland* reflects a real occupational disease: in 18th- and 19th-century England hat makers commonly developed toxic psychosis from the **mercury** used in preparing felt. And the word **crackpot** originally referred to the toxic psychosis seen in some English people, mostly the poor, who brewed their tea in cracked ceramic pots with **lead** cores.

**Toxic therapeutic drugs: tardive dyskinesia.** Sometimes the drugs used to treat neurological or psychiatric disorders are themselves toxic. Some antipsychotic drugs introduced in the early **1950s** were being taken by millions of patients with schizophrenia by the late 1950s. After several years of treatment, about **5 percent** of these patients developed a motor disorder called **tardive dyskinesia (TD)**. Its main symptoms are involuntary movements of the mouth and face: smacking and sucking movements of the lips, thrusting and rolling of the tongue, side-to-side jaw movements, and puffing of the cheeks. ("Tardive" means late-appearing, which is the clue that it emerges only after prolonged treatment.)

**Endogenous neurotoxins.** Some neurotoxins are made by the patient's own body. The body can produce **antibodies** that attack particular components of the nervous system; **stress hormones such as cortisol** are believed to have neurotoxic effects; and, as the stroke section showed, excessive release of a neurotransmitter such as **glutamate** can damage the brain.

> **Trap.** **Toxic psychosis** is a chronic mental illness caused by a neurotoxin (mercury for the hatters, lead for the "crackpots"). **Tardive dyskinesia** is a **motor** disorder of the face and mouth caused by long-term **antipsychotic** treatment, affecting about **5 percent** of treated patients in the book's figure. Do not confuse it with the parkinsonian side effects of the same drugs, and remember that glutamate counts as an **endogenous** neurotoxin.

### Genetic factors

Many neuropsychological disorders of genetic origin are caused by **abnormal recessive genes** passed from parents to offspring; **phenylketonuria (PKU)** from Chapter 2 is the example. The book explains why the inherited ones are rarely caused by **dominant** genes, and the argument is evolutionary:

- A dominant gene that disrupts neuropsychological function shows itself in every carrier, so carriers usually suffer serious survival and reproductive disadvantages, and the gene tends to be **eliminated from the gene pool**.
- A recessive gene can hide. A person with one abnormal copy does not develop the disorder, survives and reproduces normally, and passes the gene on to future generations.

Hold on to that argument; it sets up one of the chapter's best exam questions. **Huntington's disease** (10.2) *is* caused by a dominant gene, and the reason it has not been weeded out is that its symptoms usually appear only after the peak reproductive years.

**Genetic accident** is the other major genetic route to neuropsychological disorder. **Down syndrome**, which occurs in about **0.15 percent** of births, is the book's example. The accident happens in the **mother during ovulation**: an extra **chromosome 21** is created in the egg, so the fertilised egg (the zygote) has **three chromosome 21s** instead of two. The usual consequences are a characteristic physical appearance, **intellectual disability**, **early-onset Alzheimer's disease**, and other medical complications. The Alzheimer's link becomes a research clue in 10.2.

### Why the human genome did not deliver quick cures

When the human genome was documented at the start of this century, there was great optimism among people who study and treat neuropsychological disorders. Inherited factors play a major part in virtually all of them, so it seemed that the offending genes would soon be found and treatments aimed at them would follow. That has not happened, for two reasons (Maurano et al., 2012):

1. Each disorder turned out to be associated with **numerous loci** on the chromosomes, not one or two.
2. About **90 percent** of the chromosomal loci involved in neuropsychological disorders were **not protein-coding genes**; they lay in poorly understood stretches of DNA.

> **Trap.** Down syndrome is a **genetic accident** (an extra chromosome 21, **trisomy 21**, arising in the **mother's** egg at ovulation), not an inherited recessive or dominant gene. Its frequency is about **0.15 percent of births** (roughly 1 in 670), not 1.5 percent. And the general rule "inherited neuropsychological disorders are rarely dominant" has a famous exception: Huntington's disease.

### Programmed cell death

Neurons, like other cells, carry genetic programs for destroying themselves, a process called **apoptosis** (pronounced "A-poe-toe-sis"). You met apoptosis in development, where it removes the surplus neurons produced early in life. It also matters in brain damage: **all six causes** covered in this module (tumours, cerebrovascular disorders, closed-head TBIs, infections, toxins and genetic factors) damage neurons partly by **switching on apoptotic programs**.

It was once assumed that neurons died after brain damage purely by **necrosis**, passive cell death resulting from injury. The current view is that a cell that is not too severely damaged will try to gather enough resources to kill itself by apoptosis instead. Cell death is not strictly one or the other: some dying cells show signs of both.

Why would a cell evolve the machinery to kill itself? Because the way it dies affects its neighbours. Compare the two processes:

| | **Necrosis** | **Apoptosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Passive death caused by injury | Active, genetically programmed self-destruction |
| Sequence | Neuron swells and breaks apart, starting in the **axons and dendrites** and ending in the **cell body** | Starts with **shrinkage of the cell body**; as parts die, the debris is packaged in vesicles (**blebbing**) |
| Inflammation | Fragmentation causes **inflammation** that can damage nearby cells | **No inflammation** |
| Speed | Quick, usually complete in **a few hours** | Slow, usually **a day or two** |
| Effect on neighbours | Collateral damage | Damage to nearby cells kept to a minimum |

The adaptive logic is in the last row. A necrotic neuron bursts and spills its contents, and the resulting inflammation injures healthy cells around it. An apoptotic neuron dismantles itself in tidy packages that can be cleared away quietly. Apoptosis is therefore the more adaptive way for a damaged cell to die, even though it is slower.

> **Trap.** **Apoptosis is slow (a day or two), necrosis is fast (a few hours).** Students often reverse this because "programmed" sounds quick and efficient. Apoptosis begins with **shrinkage** and **blebbing** with **no inflammation**; necrosis begins with **swelling** and fragmentation of the neurites and causes **inflammation**. Apoptosis is not limited to development; it mediates much of the damage from every cause in this module.

## 10.2 Neurological Diseases

10.1 dealt with causes; this module deals with five diseases associated with brain damage: **epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease**. For each one, aim to hold five things: the defining symptoms, the neuropathology (what is damaged and where), the cause or risk factors, the numbers the book gives, and the state of treatment. These five diseases supply a large share of the biological-psychology questions on clinical psychology entrance papers.

### Epilepsy

#### What counts as epilepsy

The primary symptom of epilepsy is the **epileptic seizure**, but having a seizure does not make someone epileptic. An otherwise healthy person can have a single seizure, triggered for example by a convulsive toxin or a high fever, and never have another. The diagnosis of **epilepsy** is reserved for patients whose seizures are **repeatedly generated by their own chronic brain dysfunction**; in other words, epilepsy is defined by **spontaneously recurring** seizures. About **0.8 percent** of the population are diagnosed with epilepsy at some point in their lives.

Defined that way, diagnosis sounds easy. It is not, because seizures are extraordinarily varied. The kind everybody pictures is the **convulsion** (a motor seizure), which typically involves **clonus** (tremors), **tonus** (rigidity), and loss of both balance and consciousness. But most seizures are not like that. Most consist of subtle changes in thought, mood or behaviour that are hard to tell apart from normal ongoing activity.

> **Trap.** A single seizure (from a fever or a toxin) is **not** epilepsy; epilepsy requires **recurrent, spontaneous** seizures arising from the patient's own chronic brain dysfunction. **Clonus = tremor; tonus = rigidity.** Students swap these; remember that "tonic" goes with muscle *tone* and stiffness. Prevalence in the 11th edition is **0.8 percent** (older editions say about 1 percent).

#### Causes of epilepsy

Epilepsy has many causes. **Viruses, neurotoxins, tumours and blows to the head** can all produce it, more than **30 different faulty genes** have been linked to it, and so have many **epigenetic** mechanisms. Three cellular stories recur:

- **Faulty inhibition.** Many cases are associated with faults at **inhibitory synapses** (for example **GABAergic** synapses). These synapses normally stop excitatory activity from running away; when they fail, neurons begin to fire **synchronously**, and synchronous firing is what produces seizures.
- **Astrocyte dysfunction** is also implicated in the development of seizures.
- **Inflammatory processes** seem to be responsible for seizure activity in other cases.

The common denominator is a loss of the balance that keeps neurons firing independently. A seizure is, at bottom, too many neurons firing in lockstep.

#### Diagnosis: the EEG

Diagnosis of epilepsy leans heavily on **electroencephalography (EEG)**. The scalp EEG is useful for two reasons: during a seizure it usually shows bursts of **high-amplitude EEG spikes**, and between seizures individual spikes often punctuate the record. The second fact is clinically valuable, because a patient rarely has a seizure on cue in the clinic, but inter-seizure spikes can still give the diagnosis away. Figure 10.8 shows a cortical EEG at the onset of a tonic-clonic seizure, dominated by **epileptic spikes** (sudden, high-amplitude signals).

#### Epileptic auras

Some people with epilepsy experience peculiar psychological changes just before a seizure, called **epileptic auras**. They take many forms: a bad smell, a particular thought, a vague sense of familiarity, a hallucination, a tightness in the chest. Auras matter for two reasons:

1. **Localisation.** The content of the aura is a clue to where the **epileptic focus** lies, because the aura is the first symptom produced by the abnormal discharge at its site of origin. A smell suggests olfactory-related cortex; a feeling of familiarity suggests the temporal lobe.
2. **Warning.** An aura can warn the patient that a convulsion is coming, giving time to get to a safe position.

#### Focal and generalised seizures

Once someone has been diagnosed with epilepsy, their seizures are usually assigned to one of two general categories, **focal seizures** or **generalised seizures**, and then to a subcategory within it. The types differ so much from one another that epilepsy is best seen not as one disease but as **a number of different, but related, diseases**. The book describes four common types, two of each:

| Category | Type | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Focal** | **Simple** (Jacksonian) | Mainly sensory and/or motor symptoms, one sort only; consciousness rarely lost |
| **Focal** | **Complex** | Often begins in and stays within the **temporal lobes**; automatisms and near-normal complex behaviour; about **half of adult epilepsy** |
| **Generalised** | **Tonic-clonic** | Loss of consciousness and equilibrium; violent tonic-clonic convulsion; tongue biting, incontinence, cyanosis; hypoxia can damage the brain |
| **Generalised** | **Absence** | No convulsion; brief loss of consciousness, stopped behaviour, vacant look, sometimes fluttering eyelids; **3-per-second spike-and-wave** EEG; mostly children, often ceases at puberty |

#### Focal seizures

A **focal seizure** is one that does **not involve the whole brain**. The epileptic neurons at a **focus** begin to discharge together in bursts, and it is this **synchronous firing** that produces the spikes in the EEG. Figure 10.9 shows what a single epileptic neuron does, recorded by extracellular unit recording: instead of firing at irregular intervals it fires in bursts. The synchronised activity tends to spread to other brain areas, but in a focal seizure it never takes over the entire brain.

Two things follow. First, the **behavioural symptoms depend on where the discharge starts and where it spreads**: a focus in motor cortex produces movement, a focus in sensory cortex produces sensation, a temporal-lobe focus produces altered experience and behaviour. Second, because the whole brain is not involved, focal seizures are often **not accompanied by a total loss of consciousness or equilibrium**.

There are many categories of focal seizure, depending on where they start and spread; the book focuses on two, simple and complex.

#### Simple seizures

**Simple seizures** are focal seizures with mainly **sensory symptoms, motor symptoms, or both**. Another name for them is **Jacksonian seizures** after the 19th-century neurologist **Hughlings Jackson**. A simple seizure involves **only one sort** of sensory or motor symptom, and it is **rarely accompanied by loss of consciousness**. (The classic picture outside this book is the "Jacksonian march", in which twitching starts in one body part and spreads along adjacent parts as the discharge travels across the motor cortex.)

#### Complex seizures

**Complex seizures** often begin in the **temporal lobes** and usually stay there, which is why people who have them are often described as having **temporal lobe epilepsy**. Complex seizures account for about **half of adult epilepsy cases**.

During a complex seizure the patient engages in two kinds of behaviour. The first is **automatisms**: compulsive, repetitive, simple actions, such as doing and undoing a button over and over. The second is more complex behaviour that looks almost **normal**, which is exactly what makes these seizures hard to recognise: the person seems to be acting purposefully, but is not fully conscious and will usually not remember the episode.

#### The Subtlety of Complex Seizures: Two Cases

Pinel illustrates how subtle complex seizures can be with two cases reported by **Lennox (1960)**.

- **The surgeon.** A doctor was called to the hospital for an emergency operation. He carried out the operation, a difficult one, with his usual skill, yet afterwards colleagues noticed that some of what he said and did was odd, and he came home dazed and confused. The next day he could remember nothing about the operation or anything connected with it.
- **The music teacher.** A young music teacher at a concert suddenly left his seat, walked up onto the stage, went round the piano twice, jumped down, and hopped out of the hall through the exit. Full awareness returned only on his way home. Episodes like this were not new to him; he had often come to on a bus with no idea where he was going or how he had boarded it.

What the two cases share is the signature of a complex seizure: behaviour that is organised, sometimes highly skilled, carried out without full consciousness and followed by **amnesia** for the episode. The surgeon's case is the striking one, because it shows that a well-practised complex skill can run during a seizure.

#### The 2017 ILAE reclassification

In **2017** the **International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE)**, the body that sets the diagnostic criteria for seizures and epilepsy, published new guidelines. (The book prints the abbreviation as "ILEA"; the organisation's own abbreviation is ILAE.) Among many changes, the guidelines **discourage the categories "simple" and "complex"**. The reasoning is that focal seizures do not fall into two neat boxes; they lie on a **continuum of complexity**. Instead of rating a focal seizure by how complex its behavioural symptoms are, the ILAE recommends classifying it by the **level of disruption of consciousness** during the seizure, from none (as in many seizures formerly called simple) to disrupted consciousness (as in many formerly called complex).

Indian papers mix three generations of names for the same seizures, so learn the translation table:

| Older Pinel editions / older keys | 11th edition (this chapter) | ILAE 2017 wording |
|---|---|---|
| Partial seizure | **Focal seizure** | Focal onset seizure |
| Simple partial (Jacksonian) | **Simple seizure** | Focal aware seizure |
| Complex partial (temporal lobe) | **Complex seizure** | Focal impaired awareness seizure |
| Grand mal | **Tonic-clonic seizure** | (Generalised) tonic-clonic seizure |
| Petit mal | **Absence seizure** | Absence seizure |

> **Trap.** This is the biggest terminology trap in the chapter. "Petit mal" is the **absence** seizure (not a small tonic-clonic seizure), and "grand mal" is the **tonic-clonic** seizure. "Partial" (older editions) and "focal" (11th edition) mean the same thing: a seizure that does not involve the whole brain. The 11th edition still uses "simple" and "complex" while reporting that the ILAE now discourages them, and its own end-of-chapter summary even refers to "complex partial epilepsy". The ILAE criterion that replaces simple/complex is **disruption of consciousness**, not the number or kind of behavioural symptoms.

> **Trap.** **Simple versus complex** is decided by **consciousness**, not by how elaborate the behaviour looks. Simple seizures: one kind of sensory or motor symptom, consciousness **rarely** lost. Complex seizures: usually temporal lobe, automatisms and near-normal behaviour, consciousness **impaired**, amnesia afterwards. A patient who performs elaborate, purposeful-looking actions without awareness (the Lennox surgeon) is having a **complex** seizure, even though the movements are not convulsive.

#### Generalised seizures

**Generalised seizures** involve the **entire brain**. Some begin as focal discharges that gradually spread until the whole brain is involved. Others seem to start almost **simultaneously everywhere**; these sudden-onset generalised seizures may come from diffuse pathology, or they may begin focally in a structure, such as the **thalamus**, that projects to many parts of the brain and so can recruit the whole cortex almost at once.

#### Tonic-clonic seizures

The **tonic-clonic seizure** is the classic convulsion. Its primary symptoms are **loss of consciousness, loss of equilibrium, and a violent tonic-clonic convulsion**, that is, a convulsion with both tonus (rigidity) and clonus (tremor). **Tongue biting, urinary incontinence and cyanosis** (turning blue from lack of oxygen during the convulsion) are common. The lack of oxygen matters beyond appearance: the **hypoxia** (shortage of oxygen supply to a tissue) that accompanies a tonic-clonic seizure **can itself cause brain damage**, so repeated severe seizures are harmful in their own right.

#### Absence seizures

The **absence seizure** is the other generalised type, and it looks almost nothing like the first. There is **no convulsion**. The primary behavioural symptom is a **loss of consciousness** together with a **cessation of ongoing behaviour**, a **vacant look**, and sometimes **fluttering eyelids**. To an onlooker the person seems to have briefly "switched off".

The EEG is distinctive and is the most examinable fact in the epilepsy section: a **bilaterally symmetrical, 3-per-second spike-and-wave discharge**, appearing at the same moment over left and right, frontal and temporal regions alike (Figure 10.10). Absence seizures are most common in **children** and frequently **cease at puberty**.

> **Trap.** **3-per-second (3 Hz) spike-and-wave, bilaterally symmetrical = absence (petit mal).** It is a **generalised** seizure even though it is brief and non-convulsive, so an option calling absence seizures "focal" because they are mild is wrong. Tonic-clonic seizures are the ones with cyanosis, tongue biting, incontinence and potentially damaging **hypoxia**.

#### Treating epilepsy

There is **no cure** for epilepsy, but **anticonvulsant medication** can often reduce the frequency and severity of seizures. The drugs frequently have adverse side effects (memory impairment, for example), and they do not work for everyone. Other options include:

- **Vagus nerve stimulation**.
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation** (Chapter 5).
- The **ketogenic diet**: high fat, moderate protein, low carbohydrate.
- **Brain surgery**, usually only in serious cases after other options have been exhausted.

The ketogenic diet links back to Chapter 12, where caloric restriction was noted to reduce seizure susceptibility in humans with epilepsy: in both cases a change in the brain's fuel supply alters its excitability.

### Parkinson's disease

#### Parkinson's symptoms and course

**Parkinson's disease** is a movement disorder of middle and old age. It affects **1 to 2 percent** of people over **65**, and it is slightly more common in **males** than in females.

It begins quietly, perhaps with no more than a slight stiffness or tremor of the fingers, and the symptoms then inevitably worsen over the years. The most common symptoms of the full disorder are:

- a **tremor that is pronounced during inactivity** but absent during voluntary movement and during sleep (a resting tremor);
- **muscular rigidity**;
- **difficulty initiating movement**;
- **slowness of movement** (bradykinesia);
- a **masklike face**.

Some problems often appear well before the motor symptoms, in some cases **up to a decade** before: **sleep disturbances, loss of the sense of smell, and depression**. These early non-motor signs matter because, as you will see, by the time the motor symptoms appear much of the damage is already done.

> **Trap.** The Parkinsonian tremor is a tremor **at rest**: it is marked when the limb is inactive and disappears during voluntary movement and sleep. A tremor that appears or worsens during purposeful movement points elsewhere (cerebellar damage, from Chapter 8). The early non-motor triad the book lists is **sleep disturbance, loss of smell, depression**.

#### Cognition in Parkinson's disease

Many Parkinson's patients have only **mild cognitive deficits** that do not interfere with daily life: in effect, thinking people trapped inside bodies they cannot control. Roberto Garcia d'Orta, "the Lizard" of Chapter 4, is the book's example. But this is not the whole picture. Some patients have more severe cognitive problems; in general, cognitive decline with age is **faster** in Parkinson's patients than in the general population; and **a majority** of patients will show symptoms of **dementia** if they live **more than 10 years** after diagnosis.

#### Causes of Parkinson's disease

Like epilepsy, Parkinson's disease has no single cause. Faulty DNA, brain infections, strokes, tumours, TBI and neurotoxins have all been implicated in particular cases. In the **majority of cases, though, there is no obvious cause and no family history**. Numerous genes have been linked to the disease, but most cases probably arise from **interactions among multiple genetic and environmental factors**.

#### Neuropathology: the substantia nigra and Lewy bodies

Degeneration in Parkinson's disease is widespread, but it is particularly severe in the **substantia nigra**, the midbrain nucleus whose neurons project through the **nigrostriatal pathway** to the **striatum** of the basal ganglia. The main neurotransmitter released by most substantia nigra neurons is **dopamine**, and long-term patients have very little dopamine left in either the substantia nigra or the striatum.

At autopsy, the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra that have survived often contain clumps of a protein called **alpha-synuclein**. These clumps are **Lewy bodies**, named after the German pathologist who first described them in **1912**. Alpha-synuclein is now the focus of intense research because it is believed to play an important part in how the pathology develops and spreads through the brain, though not everyone accepts that role (the book cites a dissenting view).

#### L-dopa and its limits

Because the missing ingredient is dopamine, the obvious treatment is to restore it. Dopamine itself does not cross the blood-brain barrier, so patients are given **L-dopa**, the chemical from which the body synthesises dopamine. L-dopa alleviates the symptoms, as it did for d'Orta in Chapter 4.

It is not a permanent solution. L-dopa typically becomes less and less effective with continued use until its **side effects (for example involuntary movements) outweigh its benefits**. That is what happened to d'Orta: L-dopa gave him a **3-year respite**, then stopped working; he was switched to another **dopamine agonist**, improved again, and again the improvement was temporary.

At present no drug permanently halts the progression of Parkinson's disease or permanently reduces the severity of its symptoms, though attempts to develop one continue. A key obstacle is timing: by the time the motor symptoms are obvious and the diagnosis is made, **irreversible damage has already occurred**.

#### Deep brain stimulation

When medication fails, **deep brain stimulation (DBS)** is an option. Low-intensity electrical stimulation is applied **continuously** to a particular brain area through a **stereotaxically implanted** electrode, powered by a pacemaker-like unit under the skin (Figure 10.11). For Parkinson's disease the usual target is the **subthalamic nucleus**, which lies just beneath the thalamus and is richly connected to the basal ganglia, stimulated chronically and **bilaterally**.

The stimulation is delivered at **high frequency**, and its effect is to **block** the function of the target structure, much as a lesion would. That explains the time course: switch the current on and symptoms can ease **within seconds**; switch it off and the improvement disappears very quickly. DBS is, in effect, a reversible lesion. It has drawbacks: it can cause cognitive, speech and gait problems, and it **does not slow the progression** of the disease.

> **Trap.** Three DBS details are tested. The current is **low-intensity** but **high-frequency**; it works by **blocking** the target (like a reversible lesion), not by exciting it; and the standard Parkinson's target is the **subthalamic nucleus**, not the substantia nigra. DBS relieves symptoms only while it is on and **does not halt** the disease. Lewy bodies are clumps of **alpha-synuclein**, first described in **1912**; do not attach them to Alzheimer's disease.

### Huntington's disease

#### Huntington's symptoms and course

**Huntington's disease** is, like Parkinson's, a **progressive motor disorder**, but the book sets out three ways in which the two differ. Huntington's is **rare (1 in 7,500)**; its genetics are **simple** (one gene); and **severe dementia always accompanies it**.

Often the earliest clinical sign is that the person becomes **more fidgety**. As the disease develops, **rapid, complex, jerky movements of entire limbs** (rather than of individual muscles) come to dominate, together with prominent **psychiatric symptoms and cognitive deficits**. Eventually motor and cognitive deterioration are so severe that sufferers cannot feed themselves, control their bowels, or recognise their friends and relatives. There is no cure, and death typically occurs about **20 years** after the first symptoms.

#### The huntingtin gene

Huntington's disease is transmitted by a **single mutated dominant gene** called **huntingtin**; the protein it codes for is the **huntingtin protein**. Because the gene is dominant, **everyone who carries it develops the disease**, and so do about **half of their offspring**.

This is where the evolutionary argument from 10.1 pays off. Dominant genes that damage neuropsychological function are normally eliminated, because carriers are at a reproductive disadvantage. The huntingtin gene has survived because its first symptoms usually appear only after the **peak reproductive years, at about age 40**. By the time a carrier knows, they may already have passed the gene to their children.

(Beyond this book: the mutation is an expanded **CAG trinucleotide repeat** in the huntingtin gene on **chromosome 4**, and older texts call the disease **Huntington's chorea**, from the Greek for dance, after its jerky movements. Both facts appear on entrance papers.)

#### Huntington's neuropathology

The mutated huntingtin protein seems to be **"stickier"** than the normal form, so it accumulates in **clumps inside cells**, and these clumps are thought to be toxic. Early in the disease the accumulation is most pronounced in the **striatum**, where cells begin to die. The striatal cell death destroys the connections between the **striatum and the cortex**, and this disconnection is believed to produce the early symptoms. As the disease progresses, cells **throughout the brain** die from the clumps.

#### Genetic testing and treatment

If one of your parents developed Huntington's disease, your chance of developing it would be **50/50**. Medical geneticists have developed a test that tells the relatives of patients whether they carry the gene. Some relatives choose to take it and some choose not to know. The test has two advantages: relatives who turn out **not** to carry the gene can have children without fear of passing it on, and in the future it may allow **preventive therapies** for carriers.

No established treatment currently slows the progression of Huntington's disease, although a **2016** clinical trial of a novel medication produced promising results and many other trials are under way.

| | **Parkinson's disease** | **Huntington's disease** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Progressive motor disorder | Progressive motor disorder |
| Frequency | 1 to 2% of people over 65 | Rare, **1 in 7,500** |
| Genetics | Usually no family history; many genes plus environment | **Single dominant gene** (huntingtin); 50% of offspring |
| Onset | Middle and old age | Symptoms at about **40** |
| Movements | Resting tremor, rigidity, slowness, difficulty starting movement, masklike face | Fidgetiness, then rapid jerky movements of **whole limbs** |
| Cognition | Often mildly affected; dementia in a majority after 10+ years | **Always** severe dementia |
| Key structure | **Substantia nigra** (nigrostriatal dopamine) | **Striatum** first, then whole brain |
| Protein clumps | **Lewy bodies** (alpha-synuclein) | Clumps of mutated **huntingtin protein** |
| Course | Slow progression; L-dopa, DBS relieve symptoms | Death about **20 years** after onset; no treatment slows it |

> **Trap.** Huntington's disease is caused by a **dominant** gene, so **all** carriers develop it and **50 percent** of offspring of a carrier inherit it. A question built on the rule that inherited neuropsychological disorders are usually recessive wants you to spot Huntington's as the exception and to explain it by **late onset** (about 40, after peak reproduction). Older editions give the frequency as **1 in 10,000** and survival as about **15 years**; the 11th edition says **1 in 7,500** and about **20 years**.

### Multiple sclerosis

#### MS pathology: demyelination and sclerosis

**Multiple sclerosis (MS)** is a progressive disease that attacks the **myelin of axons in the CNS**. Part of what makes it so distressing is its timing: the first symptoms usually appear in **early adulthood**.

The damage proceeds in stages. First, microscopic areas of degeneration appear on the myelin sheaths, which in the CNS are made by **oligodendroglia** (Chapter 3). Eventually the damage to the myelin is so severe that the axons it covers stop working properly and themselves degenerate. Finally, many areas of **hard scar tissue** form in the CNS, and that is the origin of the name: **sclerosis means "hardening"**, and there are multiple patches of it. Figure 10.12 shows such areas in a patient's white matter.

#### MS as an autoimmune disease

MS is often considered an **autoimmune disorder**: one in which the immune system turns on the body's own tissue, treating it as though it were foreign. In MS the faulty immune reaction is directed at the **myelin sheath**. Supporting this, an animal model of MS called **experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis** can be produced by injecting laboratory animals with **myelin plus a preparation that stimulates the immune system**: teach the immune system to treat myelin as foreign, and an MS-like disease follows.

The story is not purely one of demyelination, however. In MS, **neurons are damaged even where there has been no demyelination**, and the general consensus is that the disease is driven by an **interaction between immune-system reactivity and neural degeneration**.

#### The failure of remyelination

MS also involves a failure of repair. **Remyelination**, the generation of new myelin sheaths on axons, is the job of oligodendroglia in the CNS. In healthy people, both remyelination and the production of new oligodendroglia continue throughout life. In MS, both processes appear to be disrupted, in two ways:

1. Many **newly born oligodendroglia fail to mature** into cells capable of remyelinating axons.
2. The oligodendroglia that are functional **do not remyelinate the affected areas**.

That is why a major focus of current MS research is developing treatments that **encourage remyelination**.

#### Diagnosis and course of MS

MS is usually diagnosed with **MRI**, focusing on the **time course of development of white-matter lesions**. Diagnosis is often complicated, because the nature and severity of MS lesions depend on their number, size and location.

In most cases MS has periods of **remission of up to 2 years** in which the patient has no symptoms. These are usually only pauses: the disease eventually becomes continuous and severe. Common symptoms of advanced MS are **visual disturbances, muscular weakness, numbness, tremor and ataxia** (loss of motor coordination). Some patients also develop cognitive deficits and emotional changes.

#### Epidemiology and risk factors of MS

**Epidemiology** is the study of factors, such as diet, geographic location and age, that influence how a disease is distributed in the general population. Epidemiological studies have revealed several puzzling features of MS:

- **Genes matter less than in many other neurological disorders.** The concordance rate is only **35 percent in monozygotic twins**, against **6 percent in dizygotic twins**. If MS were mainly genetic, identical twins would be concordant far more often than 35 percent of the time; the gap between 35 and 6 shows that genes contribute, while the fact that 65 percent of identical co-twins stay healthy shows that something else must decide who gets ill.
- **Sex.** Incidence is substantially **higher in females** than in males.
- **Ethnicity.** It is higher in **Caucasians** than in other groups.
- **Climate.** It is higher in people who have lived in **cold climates**, particularly during **childhood**.

The best-established **risk factors** are **vitamin D deficiency**, exposure to the **Epstein-Barr virus** (the most common cause of mononucleosis), and **cigarette smoking**. Notice how neatly the vitamin D factor fits the climate finding: less sunlight in cold, high-latitude regions means less vitamin D synthesis in the skin, especially in childhood.

#### Treating MS

**Immunomodulatory drugs** were approved for MS in the **1990s**, and many are now available. They are still widely prescribed, but their benefits are **only marginal**, and they help only **some** patients. The modest success has driven a search for better drugs, such as those that promote remyelination.

> **Trap.** MS is a **demyelinating** disease of the **CNS** (oligodendroglial myelin), usually regarded as **autoimmune**; its animal model is **experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis**. It is not a disease of the PNS (Schwann cell myelin) and not a disease of grey matter. Twin concordance in the 11th edition is **35 percent MZ versus 6 percent DZ** (older editions: 25 versus 5). Risk factors: **vitamin D deficiency, Epstein-Barr virus, smoking**; higher in **females, Caucasians, cold climates in childhood**. Drug treatment is **immunomodulatory** and only marginally effective.

### Alzheimer's disease

#### Prevalence and stages of Alzheimer's disease

**Alzheimer's disease** is the most common cause of **dementia** in the elderly, currently affecting about **50 million** people worldwide. It sometimes appears in people as young as **40**, but its likelihood rises with age: about **10 percent of people over 65** have it.

The disease is considered to progress through **three stages**:

1. **Preclinical stage.** Pathological changes are under way in the brain, but there are **no behavioural or cognitive symptoms**.
2. **Prodromal stage.** The patient shows **mild cognitive impairment**, far less severe than full Alzheimer's but a sign that the disease is advancing. Mild cognitive impairment together with certain biological changes allows a fairly reliable diagnosis at this stage.
3. **Dementia stage.** At first there is a progressive decline in memory, attention deficits and personality change; later, marked confusion, irritability, anxiety and deterioration of speech; eventually even simple functions such as **swallowing and bladder control** become difficult. Alzheimer's disease is **terminal**.

#### Neuropathology: tangles, plaques and neuron loss

The disease has **three defining neuropathological characteristics**:

- **Neurofibrillary tangles**: threadlike tangles of **tau protein** in the **neural cytoplasm**. Tau normally helps maintain the overall structure of neurons, so tangles are a breakdown of the cell's internal scaffolding.
- **Amyloid plaques**: clumps of scar tissue made of **degenerating neurons** and aggregates of **beta-amyloid**, a protein present in healthy brains only in small amounts (Figure 10.13 shows plaques stained blue).
- **Neuron loss**, which in the later stages leaves the brain visibly **shrunken** (Figure 10.14).

All three occur throughout the brain, but they are especially prevalent in **medial temporal lobe structures**: the **entorhinal cortex, amygdala and hippocampus**, all involved in aspects of memory. That distribution explains why memory loss is the leading symptom.

> **Trap.** **Tangles = tau, inside neurons (cytoplasm). Plaques = beta-amyloid plus degenerating neurons, outside cells.** The pairing is reversed in many distractors. The three medial temporal structures named are **entorhinal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus**. Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein) belong to Parkinson's disease, not to Alzheimer's.

#### Genetics: early-onset genes and APOE4

Early attempts to find the genes behind Alzheimer's disease concentrated on a **rare, early-onset form** that runs in a few families. Mutations in **four different genes** have been shown to contribute to this familial form, but together they contribute very little, only about **1 percent**, to the far more common **late-onset** form.

Research on the late-onset form has implicated other genes, and attention has fastened on one: the gene on **chromosome 19** that codes for the protein **apolipoprotein E (APOE)**. A particular allele, **APOE4**, increases susceptibility to late-onset Alzheimer's disease by approximately **50 percent**. Exactly what APOE does in cells is not yet known, but in Alzheimer's disease it appears to **bind to beta-amyloid**, which both **reduces the clearance** of beta-amyloid from the brain and **increases its clumping**, leading to plaques.

> **Trap.** Keep the two genetic stories apart. **Early-onset familial** Alzheimer's: mutations in **four** genes (older editions say three), rare, all affecting beta-amyloid synthesis, contributing only about **1 percent** to late-onset cases. **Late-onset** Alzheimer's: the **APOE** gene on **chromosome 19**, whose **APOE4** allele raises susceptibility by about **50 percent**; it is a risk factor, not a guarantee. The beta-amyloid gene itself is on **chromosome 21**, which is the Down syndrome connection.

#### The amyloid hypothesis

There is currently **no cure**. One reason the search is hard is that it is not entirely clear which of the pathological features is **primary**, that is, which one causes the others. The dominant view is the **amyloid hypothesis**: amyloid plaques are the primary symptom, and they cause all the other symptoms.

The evidence divides as follows.

**For the hypothesis:**

- The main support comes from the genetics of families with **early-onset** Alzheimer's: **all four** gene mutations that cause it influence the **synthesis of beta-amyloid**.
- The production of neurofibrillary tangles has been found to be **downstream** of changes to beta-amyloid (Amar et al., 2017), which is the order the hypothesis predicts.

**Against the hypothesis:**

- Many people **without observable dementia** carry heavy loads of amyloid plaques; they are called **high-plaque normals** (Herrup, 2015). If plaques caused the disease, these people should be demented.
- But the high-plaque normals also turned out to **lack the inflammation** associated with Alzheimer's disease, which suggests plaques may need an inflammatory response to do their damage. So this objection is not decisive either, and whether beta-amyloid starts the cascade is **still debated**.
- In the past decade, researchers trying to cure the disease by reducing amyloid deposits have been on a roller-coaster: treatment after treatment looked promising in **animal models** and then proved **ineffective in human patients**. Those failures have encouraged further challenges to the hypothesis. (They also illustrate the warning about animal models in 10.3.)

#### Timing of treatment

It is widely believed that treatments will work only if they are given during the **preclinical or prodromal** stages. The problem is that most patients first seek help when they are already in the **dementia stage**, with extensive brain pathology. That is why recent advances in **early diagnosis** may prove critical for developing effective treatments.

#### The Down syndrome link

Down syndrome has attracted attention as a possible window onto the mechanisms of Alzheimer's disease, for good reason:

- By age **40**, almost all individuals with Down syndrome have developed numerous **amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles**, the core pathology of Alzheimer's.
- By age **60**, **two-thirds** of individuals with Down syndrome have developed **dementia**.
- People with Down syndrome have **three copies of chromosome 21**, and the gene that codes for **beta-amyloid** is on chromosome 21. The hypothesis is that the extra chromosome leads to **greater production of beta-amyloid**.
- Strikingly, about **15 percent** of the neurons in the brains of Alzheimer's patients **without** Down syndrome also contain an **extra copy of chromosome 21**.

Put the first three points together and Down syndrome looks like a natural experiment supporting the amyloid hypothesis: a lifelong surplus of the beta-amyloid gene produces Alzheimer's pathology decades early.

#### The pathogenic spread hypothesis

A newer idea, the **pathogenic spread hypothesis**, proposes that many common neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's and Parkinson's among them) result from **misfolded proteins** that start a chain reaction, causing other proteins to misfold. In Alzheimer's disease, a misfolded tau or beta-amyloid protein would "seed" the misfolding of other tau or beta-amyloid molecules; the misfolded proteins would then **spread to other neurons** and aggregate into the tangles and plaques characteristic of the disease. The idea connects with the suggested role of alpha-synuclein in the spread of Parkinson's pathology.

## 10.3 Animal Models of Human Neurological Diseases

The first two modules described diseases and their causes, and along the way they showed some of the ways researchers try to work out what goes wrong. This module looks at one of those ways in detail: experimental work on **animal models**.

### Why animal models need careful handling

Research on patients themselves can seldom identify the neuropathological basis of a neuropsychological disease: you cannot lesion, stimulate or dissect a living patient's brain to test a hypothesis. So animal models play a large part. The difficulty is that **even the best animal models display only some of the features of the disease they model**, yet researchers often treat them as though they reproduce the human condition in every respect. Pinel and Barnes say plainly that this module exists to stop you making the same mistake. As you read about the two models below, keep asking two questions: **in what ways does it match the human disease, and in what ways does it not?** That habit is also the best defence against MCQ options that overstate what a model shows.

### Kindling model of epilepsy

#### The discovery of kindling

In the late 1960s, **Goddard, McIntyre and Leech (1969)** delivered **one mild electrical stimulation per day** to rats through an electrode implanted in the **amygdala**. The first few stimulations produced no behavioural response at all. Soon, however, each stimulation began to trigger a convulsion. The first convulsions were mild, just a slight tremor of the face, but with every further stimulation they became more generalised, until each one involved the **entire body**.

This progressive development and intensification of convulsions produced by a series of periodic stimulations became known as the **kindling phenomenon**, and it was one of the first **neuroplastic** phenomena to be studied widely. The name is apt: like kindling a fire, a stimulus that is too weak to do anything on its own gradually builds up an effect that eventually catches.

Kindling is most often studied in rats given repeated amygdala stimulation, but it is a remarkably general phenomenon:

- It occurs in **mice, rabbits, cats, dogs and various primates**.
- It can be produced by repeated stimulation of **many brain sites** other than the amygdala.
- It can be produced by repeated doses of **convulsive chemicals** given at initially subconvulsive levels.

#### Two key features of kindling

Of kindling's many interesting features, two deserve emphasis.

**The changes are permanent.** Kindle an animal, leave it alone for **several months**, and a single weak stimulation will still throw it into a **generalised convulsion**. Whatever has changed in the brain does not decay.

**Kindling requires distributed, not massed, stimulation.** If the intervals between stimulations are shorter than **an hour or two**, many more stimulations are usually needed to kindle an animal, and under normal circumstances **no kindling at all** occurs with intervals shorter than about **20 minutes**. The brain is being changed by the stimulations in a way that needs time between episodes to consolidate, exactly the property that makes spaced practice more effective than massed practice in learning. That parallel is why kindling counts as neuroplasticity rather than mere damage.

#### How kindling models epilepsy

Much of the interest in kindling comes from the fact that it models epilepsy in **two** ways:

1. **The convulsions themselves.** Convulsions in kindled animals resemble in many respects those of some types of human epilepsy.
2. **The way they develop.** The kindling phenomenon is comparable to the **epileptogenesis** (the development, or genesis, of epilepsy) that can follow a **head injury**. Some people who appear to have escaped serious harm after a blow to the head begin to have convulsions a few weeks later, and the convulsions then recur more and more often and more and more severely. That escalating course is what kindling produces in the laboratory.

#### Where the kindling model falls short

As most laboratories use it, the kindling model fails to capture one defining property of epilepsy. Recall from 10.2 that epilepsy is a disease of **spontaneously recurring** seizures. Kindled convulsions are **elicited**: they happen because the experimenter delivered a stimulation.

A version that overcomes this shortcoming has been developed in several species. If animals are kindled for a very long time, about **300 stimulations** in rats, a syndrome emerges that is much more like epilepsy: the animals begin to have **spontaneous seizures** and go on having them even after the stimulation regime has stopped.

> **Trap.** Kindling: **Goddard, McIntyre and Leech (1969)**, one mild stimulation per day, usually **amygdala**, rats. The changes are **permanent**; the stimulations must be **distributed** (intervals under about **20 minutes** produce no kindling). It models epilepsy in two ways (similar convulsions; epileptogenesis after head injury) but standard kindled seizures are **elicited**, not spontaneous; spontaneous seizures appear only after about **300 stimulations**. An option saying kindling is "best produced by massed stimulation" or "fades after a few weeks" is wrong on both counts.

### MPTP model of Parkinson's disease

#### The Case of the Frozen Drug Users

The leading animal model of Parkinson's disease grew out of an accident, reported by **Langston (1985)**. Parkinson's disease rarely appears before the age of **50**, so doctors were surprised when a group of **young drug users** developed severe parkinsonism. The one thing they had in common was recent use of a new **"synthetic heroin"**. They showed all the typical Parkinsonian symptoms, including **bradykinesia** (slowness of movement), **tremor** and **muscle rigidity**, and even subtle ones such as **seborrhoea** (oily skin) and **micrographia** (small handwriting). Samples of the drug were analysed and the culprit identified as **MPTP (1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine)**, a contaminant produced in its illicit manufacture. **None of the patients recovered.**

#### From accident to animal model

Researchers quickly turned these few patients' misfortune into a benefit for many, by developing a much-needed animal model. **Non-human primates** turned out to react to MPTP much as humans do, developing the **motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease**, losing **substantia nigra neurons**, and showing a **large fall in brain dopamine**. Rodents are less useful: **rats are relatively resistant** to the toxin for reasons nobody yet understands, and the response of **mice differs widely between strains**.

The MPTP model does not reproduce every aspect of Parkinson's disease, but it has proved extremely useful. It has been central to developing many of the treatments in current use, mainly **dopamine agonists**, and in 10.5 it is the model on which fetal transplants were first tested.

> **Trap.** The MPTP model comes from human cases (**Langston, 1985**) of young users of contaminated synthetic heroin, and the best animal model is the **monkey**, not the rat: **rats are relatively resistant**, and mouse responses vary by strain. MPTP destroys **substantia nigra** dopamine neurons. Older editions also discuss **deprenyl** in this context; the 11th edition does not.

## 10.4 Responses to Nervous System Damage: Degeneration, Regeneration, Reorganisation, and Recovery

The first three modules covered causes of brain damage, diseases associated with it, and animal models of those diseases. This module turns to what the nervous system itself does after damage. It describes four neuroplastic responses: **degeneration, regeneration, reorganisation, and recovery of function**.

### Neural degeneration

**Neural degeneration** (neural deterioration and death) is part of both normal brain development and disease. As it usually happens in the body, it is a complex process, influenced by nearby **glial cells**, by the **activity** of the degenerating neurons, and by the particular **cause** of the degeneration. In the laboratory, though, it is often produced in a simple, controlled way: by cutting axons, which is called **axotomy**.

#### Anterograde and retrograde degeneration

Cutting an axon divides it into two segments, and each degenerates in its own way.

- The **distal segment** runs from the cut **to the synaptic terminals**. Its degeneration is **anterograde degeneration**.
- The **proximal segment** runs from the cut **back to the cell body**. Its degeneration is **retrograde degeneration**.

**Anterograde degeneration is fast.** The cut separates the distal segment from the **cell body, the metabolic centre of the neuron**, so it has no supply of proteins and energy. Within **a few hours** the whole distal segment is badly swollen, and within **a few days** it has broken into fragments.

**Retrograde degeneration is slow and its outcome is uncertain.** It progresses gradually back from the cut towards the cell body. After about **2 or 3 days**, major changes are visible in the cell bodies of most axotomised neurons, and they go one of two ways:

- **Degenerative changes** (for example, the cell body **shrinks**) indicate that the neuron will eventually die, usually by **apoptosis**, sometimes by necrosis or a combination of the two.
- **Regenerative changes** (for example, the cell body **enlarges**) indicate that it is engaged in **massive protein synthesis** to build a replacement axon.

Even regenerative changes do not guarantee survival. If the regrowing axon fails to make synaptic contact with an **appropriate target**, the neuron eventually dies. A neuron, in other words, needs its connections to stay alive, which links back to the neurotrophin-dependent cell death of development (Chapter 9).

#### Transneuronal degeneration

Sometimes the damage does not stop at the injured neuron but spreads across synapses to neurons connected to it. This is **transneuronal degeneration**, and it too has two directions:

- **Anterograde transneuronal degeneration** spreads from the damaged neurons to the neurons **they synapse on** (downstream).
- **Retrograde transneuronal degeneration** spreads from the damaged neurons to the neurons **that synapse on them** (upstream).

> **Trap.** Direction is everything in this section, and the anchor is the **cell body**. **Anterograde = away from the cell body, towards the terminals**: the **distal** segment degenerates, quickly (swollen in hours, fragmented in days), because it has lost its metabolic centre. **Retrograde = back towards the cell body**: the **proximal** segment degenerates, slowly, with cell-body changes after **2 or 3 days** that are either degenerative (shrinkage) or regenerative (enlargement). The same logic applies across synapses: anterograde transneuronal goes to the **postsynaptic** neurons, retrograde transneuronal to the **presynaptic** ones. Anterograde degeneration is also called Wallerian degeneration outside this book.

### Neural regeneration

**Neural regeneration**, the regrowth of damaged neurons, works much less well in mammals and other higher vertebrates than in most invertebrates and lower vertebrates. Chapter 9's example was Sperry's eye-rotation experiments, which showed accurate regeneration in the frog visual system. Higher vertebrates have a capacity for accurate axonal growth while they are developing, but it is mostly lost once they mature. In the **CNS of adult mammals, axonal regeneration is virtually nonexistent**, and where it does occur it usually needs very specific conditions.

#### Regeneration in the mammalian PNS: three patterns

The mammalian **PNS** is a different story. Regrowth from the **proximal stump** of a damaged nerve usually starts **2 or 3 days** after the injury, once new **growth cones** have formed. What happens next depends on the kind of injury, and there are three possibilities:

1. **Sheaths intact** (for example, the nerve is **crushed** rather than cut). The regenerating axons grow along their original **Schwann cell myelin sheaths** and reach their **original targets**, at a rate of **a few millimetres per day**.
2. **Nerve severed, ends a few millimetres apart.** Regenerating axon tips often enter the **wrong sheaths** and are guided to the **wrong destinations**. This is why coordinated use of a limb can be hard to regain after nerve damage even when a great deal of regeneration has occurred: the axons have regrown, but to the wrong muscles and skin.
3. **Ends widely separated, or a long section damaged.** There may be **no meaningful regeneration** at all; the regenerating tips grow into a **tangled mass** around the proximal stump.

#### Why the PNS regenerates and the CNS does not

The obvious explanation is that adult PNS neurons are inherently able to regenerate and adult CNS neurons are not. That explanation is only partly right, and the evidence that undermines it comes from a neat transplant logic:

- Some **CNS neurons can regenerate** if they are transplanted into the **PNS**.
- Some **PNS neurons cannot regenerate** if they are transplanted into the **CNS**.

If regeneration depended only on the neuron, moving it should make no difference. Since it does, **something about the environment** must matter: the PNS environment promotes regeneration and the CNS environment does not. The glial cells are a major part of the explanation:

| | **Schwann cells (PNS)** | **Oligodendroglia (CNS)** | **Astrocytes (CNS)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debris | **Clear** debris and scar tissue | Do **not** clear debris | Form a **glial scar** |
| Proliferation | Multiply to produce more Schwann cells | | |
| Growth signals | Produce **neurotrophic factors** | Release factors that **actively block** regeneration | Release **inhibitors** of axonal growth |
| Guidance | Form **physical tracks** that guide regrowing axons | Do not guide | Scar is a **physical barrier** |

The book notes that the astrocyte part of this account is not settled (some evidence suggests the scar may also help). But the overall conclusion stands: the adult CNS fails to regenerate largely because of its **environment**, and that is precisely what the Schwann-cell transplants in 10.5 try to change.

> **Trap.** "CNS neurons cannot regenerate because they are intrinsically incapable" is the half-truth to reject. The transplant evidence (CNS neurons regrow in the PNS; PNS neurons fail in the CNS) points to the **environment**: **Schwann cells promote** regeneration (clear debris, make neurotrophic factors, form tracks), **oligodendroglia block** it, and **astrocytes** form an inhibitory **glial scar**. PNS regrowth starts after **2 or 3 days** and proceeds at **a few millimetres per day**.

#### Collateral sprouting

Regeneration is not the only kind of growth after damage. When an axon degenerates, **branches grow out from neighbouring healthy axons** and form synapses at the sites the degenerating axon has vacated. This is **collateral sprouting**. The sprouts can arise either from the **axon terminal branches** or from the **nodes of Ranvier** of adjacent neurons. Collateral sprouting is not repair of the damaged neuron; it is a takeover of its territory by its neighbours, and it becomes one of the two proposed mechanisms of reorganisation below.

### Neural reorganisation

Chapter 9 showed that the adult mammalian brain reorganises in response to **experience**. This section shows that it also reorganises in response to **damage**.

#### Cortical reorganisation in laboratory animals

Most studies of reorganisation after damage have used the **sensory and motor cortex** of laboratory animals. These areas are ideal because of their **topographic layout**: each cortical point represents a known part of the body or visual field, so any shift in the map can be measured precisely. Reorganisation of these cortices has been studied after damage to **peripheral nerves** and after damage to the **cortical areas themselves**. Reports began to appear in large numbers in the early **1990s**, and three studies were particularly influential.

> **Key study, Kaas and colleagues (1990).** A small lesion was made in **one retina** and the **other eye was removed**. Several months later, neurons in **primary visual cortex** that had originally had receptive fields in the lesioned part of the retina now had receptive fields in the area of retina **next to the lesion**. Remarkably, the change began **within minutes** of the lesion (Gilbert & Wiesel, 1992).

> **Key study, Pons and colleagues (1991).** The primary somatosensory cortex was mapped in monkeys whose sensory neurons from the contralateral arm had been cut **10 years** earlier. The cortical **face representation** had expanded systematically into the original **arm area**. The study caused a stir because of its **scale**: the face area's border had moved by **well over a centimetre**, far more reorganisation than had been thought possible.

> **Key study, Sanes, Suner and Donoghue (1990).** The motor neurons controlling the muscles of rats' **vibrissae** (whiskers) were transected. A few weeks later, stimulating the area of motor cortex that had previously moved the vibrissae now activated **other muscles of the face** (Figure 10.18).

Notice that the three studies cover the visual, somatosensory and motor systems, and that all three show the same rule: cortex deprived of its usual input or output does not fall silent but is **taken over by neighbouring representations**.

#### Cortical reorganisation in humans: the blind

The animal findings encouraged researchers to look for similar effects in human clinical populations. One line of work has used brain imaging to study the cortex of **blind** people. The findings fit the idea that functional circuits are in **continuous competition for cortical space**:

- Without visual input, **auditory and somatosensory cortex expand** (Elbert et al., 2002).
- Auditory and somatosensory input is **processed in formerly visual areas** (Amedi et al., 2005).
- The reorganisation seems to have a functional payoff: blind volunteers are **better than sighted controls** on a variety of auditory and somatosensory tasks (Merabet & Pascual-Leone, 2010).

#### Mechanisms of neural reorganisation

Two kinds of mechanism have been proposed:

1. **Strengthening of existing connections**, possibly through **release from inhibition**. Connections that were always present but held weak by inhibition become effective once the competing input disappears.
2. **Establishment of new connections** by **collateral sprouting**.

Each has indirect support, and the support comes from **timing and scale**:

- For release from inhibition: reorganisation often happens **too quickly** to be explained by growth (recall the minutes-long onset in the Kaas study), and rapid reorganisation **never involves changes of more than 2 millimetres** of cortical surface, which is about as far as existing connections could plausibly reach.
- For collateral sprouting: the **magnitude of long-term reorganisation** can be **too great** to be explained by existing connections (recall the more-than-a-centimetre shift in Pons's monkeys after 10 years).

Figure 10.19 shows how the two could work together after damage to a peripheral somatosensory nerve: two days after nerve B is damaged, part of B's old cortical area responds to touches at A because it has been released from inhibition; six months later, a further area responds to A because A's axons have sprouted into it.

These are the likely mechanisms, but not the only possible ones: **neural degeneration, adjustment of dendritic trees and adult neurogenesis** may also be involved. And reorganisation after damage is **not necessarily mediated by changes in the damaged area itself**; the changes can occur elsewhere in the circuit.

> **Trap.** Match the mechanism to the evidence. **Fast** reorganisation (minutes to days) that never exceeds **2 mm** = strengthening of existing connections by **release from inhibition**. **Large, slow** reorganisation (Pons: over a centimetre after **10 years**) = new connections by **collateral sprouting**. Pons and colleagues (1991) studied **somatosensory** cortex in **monkeys** (face into arm area); Sanes and colleagues (1990) studied **motor** cortex in **rats** (vibrissae); Kaas and colleagues (1990) studied **visual** cortex after **retinal** lesions.

### Recovery of function after CNS damage

#### Why recovery is hard to study

Recovery of function after CNS damage in adult humans is **poorly understood**, largely because improvements tend to be **modest or nonexistent** (Teuber, 1975). This is very different from the picture in films and television, where enough effort guarantees a good outcome.

A second difficulty is that other changes are easily mistaken for recovery:

- Improvement in the **first week or two** after damage may reflect a decline in **cerebral oedema** (brain swelling) rather than recovery from the neural damage itself.
- Gradual improvement over the following **months** may reflect the learning of new cognitive and behavioural strategies, called **substitution of functions**, rather than the return of the lost function.

Despite these problems, one generalisation is clear: recovery is most likely when **lesions are small and patients are young**.

#### Cognitive reserve

**Cognitive reserve**, roughly equivalent to **education and intelligence**, is thought to explain improvements after brain damage that do **not** reflect true recovery of brain function. **Kapur (1997)** carried out a biographical study of **doctors and neuroscientists** with brain damage and found what looked like a surprising degree of cognitive recovery. He suggested that these patients had not actually regained the lost brain function; rather, their cognitive reserve allowed them to accomplish tasks **in alternative ways**. That is substitution of function, made possible by a large store of knowledge and strategies.

#### Adult neurogenesis and recovery

Does the birth of new neurons in the adult brain contribute to recovery? The evidence is suggestive but incomplete:

- In adult laboratory animals, damage leads to **increased migration of stem cells into nearby damaged areas**, and some of these cells develop into neurons that survive for **a few months**. Figure 10.20 shows the effect in the **dentate gyrus**: an electrolytic lesion is followed by a marked increase in new cells, many of which become mature neurons, compared with the unlesioned hemisphere.
- The finding has been replicated in **one human patient** who died **1 week** after a stroke (Minger et al., 2007).
- But stem cells migrate only **short distances**. There is no evidence that, in the adult human brain, they travel from their usual birthplaces in the **hippocampus and subventricular zone** to distant sites of damage.
- And even where new cells survive, there is **no direct evidence that they contribute to recovery**.

> **Trap.** Three things masquerade as recovery: early improvement from reduced **oedema**, later improvement from **substitution of functions**, and the extra capacity of **cognitive reserve** (Kapur, 1997, doctors and neuroscientists). Recovery is most likely with **small lesions in young patients**. Damage-induced neurogenesis is real in animals (and one human stroke patient), but there is **no direct evidence** that the new neurons aid recovery.

## 10.5 Neuroplasticity and the Treatment of CNS Damage

This module explains much of the excitement about neuroplasticity: the hope that what has been learned about the brain's capacity to change can be turned into treatments for CNS damage in human patients. It describes three approaches that the discovery of neuroplasticity has inspired: **neurotransplantation** (early and modern), **rehabilitative training**, and the treatment of **phantom limbs**. As you read, map each treatment onto a response from 10.4: transplants try to replace lost neurons or to block degeneration and encourage regeneration; training tries to steer reorganisation; the mirror box tries to correct a reorganised map.

### Neurotransplantation: early research

#### Fetal substantia nigra transplants in MPTP monkeys

In the late **1980s**, inspired by research on neurodevelopment, researchers began to explore whether damaged CNS tissue could be repaired by **implanting embryonic tissue** near the damage. The hope was that the embryonic tissue would mature, integrate into the host brain, replace the damaged cells and relieve the symptoms. The obvious first target was **Parkinson's disease**, because the deficit is so specific: patients have lost the **dopamine-releasing neurons of the nigrostriatal pathway**. Could transplanting the right fetal tissue put them back?

The early signs were good. **Bilateral transplantation of fetal substantia nigra cells** successfully treated the **MPTP monkey model** of Parkinson's disease (Sladek et al., 1987; Bankiewicz et al., 1990). The transplants **survived** in the MPTP-treated monkeys, the transplanted cells **innervated the adjacent striatum**, they **released dopamine**, and, most importantly, they relieved the severe poverty of movement, the tremor and the rigidity that MPTP had produced.

#### The rush to the clinic

Soon after the monkey results were published, neurotransplantation was offered, **prematurely**, as a treatment for Parkinson's disease at major research hospitals. The first case studies looked promising: the fetal substantia nigra implants survived and released dopamine into the host striatum (Sawle & Myers, 1993), and some patients improved.

These results led to **two double-blind evaluation studies** of neurotransplants in patients with **advanced** Parkinson's disease. At first the results were encouraging: the implants survived and symptoms were modestly reduced. Then, **about a year after surgery**, some patients began to show a range of **uncontrollable writhing and chewing movements** (Greene et al., 1999).

The negative outcome had two positive effects:

1. It all but ended the **premature clinical use** of neurotransplantation for Parkinson's disease. Many had warned that it was wrong to use such an invasive treatment without first establishing its mechanisms, its hazards and how to maximise its effectiveness (Dunnett, Björklund, & Lindvall, 2001).
2. It pushed researchers to take a more careful, systematic look at the effects of different kinds of neurotransplantation in **animal models**, to answer the basic questions first.

> **Trap.** The sequence matters. Fetal substantia nigra transplants worked in **MPTP monkeys** (Sladek et al., 1987; Bankiewicz et al., 1990); case studies in humans looked promising; then **two double-blind trials** showed survival and **modest** benefit, followed **about a year later** by **uncontrollable writhing and chewing movements** in some patients (Greene et al., 1999). The lesson the book draws is about the danger of moving invasive treatments into the clinic before their mechanisms and hazards are understood.

#### The Case of Roberto Garcia d'Orta: The Lizard Gets an Autotransplant

Roberto Garcia d'Orta, "the Lizard" of Chapter 4, had Parkinson's disease. L-dopa helped him at first, but after **3 years** his condition worsened again. He was desperate when he heard about **adrenal medulla autotransplantation**: transplanting the patient's **own adrenal medulla cells** into the **striatum**, usually for Parkinson's disease. The rationale was that adrenal medulla cells release **small amounts of dopamine**, and there had been some early hints that the procedure might relieve Parkinsonian symptoms.

D'Orta asked his doctor for the operation and was refused, on the grounds that its effectiveness and safety had not been established. So he found a neurosurgeon who was less scrupulous (Klawans, 1990). He flew to **Juarez**, where the surgeon was willing to operate as long as he was paid and took offence when d'Orta asked about the risks. D'Orta flew home **2 weeks** after the operation with no improvement, and was told to be patient while the cells grew. A few weeks later he died of a **stroke**, and it is more than possible that the stroke was a complication of the surgery.

The case is the human face of the "rush to the clinic" argument. An unproven, invasive procedure, offered on the strength of early hints rather than controlled evidence, gave no benefit and quite possibly killed the patient.

#### Neuroprotection: implants that block degeneration

In the late **1990s** a different approach was developed. Instead of implanting developing neurons to **replace** lost ones, researchers implanted **non-neural cells** or engineered agents to **block neural degeneration**, or to stimulate and guide **regeneration**. Protecting neurons that are still alive is called **neuroprotection**.

> **Key study, Xu and colleagues (1999).** Cerebral ischaemia was induced in rats by **limiting blood flow to the brain**. In control rats this produced two effects: damage to the **hippocampus** (a structure especially vulnerable to ischaemia, as 10.1 noted) and deficits in **spatial memory**. In the experimental group, the hippocampi were treated with **viruses genetically engineered to release apoptosis inhibitor protein**. The apoptosis inhibitor protein reduced **both** the loss of hippocampal neurons **and** the deficits in **Morris water maze** performance.

The design is worth admiring. It tests the claim from 10.1 that ischaemic damage works partly through **apoptosis**: if blocking apoptosis saves neurons and saves the memory that depends on them, apoptosis must be part of the damage. Many **neurotrophic factors** have since been shown to reduce degeneration; among the most widely studied are **brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)** and **glial-cell-line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF)**.

#### Bridging the gap: Schwann cell implants

A related technique implants **Schwann cell sheaths**, which promote regeneration and guide axon growth, into the CNS, so that the regeneration-friendly environment of the PNS (10.4) is brought to the CNS.

> **Key study, Cheng, Cao and Olson (1996).** The **spinal cords** of rats were transected, leaving them **paraplegic** (paralysed in the posterior part of the body). The researchers then transplanted sections of **myelinated peripheral nerve** across the transection. **Spinal cord neurons regenerated through the implanted Schwann cell myelin sheaths**, and the regeneration allowed the rats to **regain use of their hindquarters**.

This is the experimental confirmation of the environmental explanation for CNS regeneration failure. CNS neurons, given a PNS environment, regrew, and regrew well enough to restore function.

### Modern research on neurotransplantation

There was a fear that the bad outcomes of rushing transplantation into Parkinson's care would choke off further research. The opposite happened. Since its failure to help human Parkinson's patients, neurotransplantation has become **one of the most active areas of clinical neuroscience research**, because the need for a way of repairing brain damage is simply too great to be deterred. **Hundreds** of studies have been conducted since the turn of the century, using animal models of virtually every common neurological disorder, and several of the protocols developed have entered preliminary tests in human patients.

#### Stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells

At the turn of the century, **stem cells** were successfully isolated from the human embryo and maintained in **tissue culture**. Stem cells (Chapter 9) **divide indefinitely** and are **pluripotent**: capable of developing into many, but not all, classes of adult cell. It seemed that stem-cell cultures would provide a **lasting supply** of cells for transplantation, getting round the ethical and technical difficulties of using tissue taken from human embryos.

In practice it did not work out that way. Stem-cell cultures **deteriorate or produce tumours**, because **chromosomal abnormalities** gradually accumulate with repeated cell division.

That is why the report in **2007** that **human skin cells could be induced to return to a pluripotent stem-cell state** generated great excitement (Takahashi et al., 2007). These **induced** stem cells have since been the focus of a massive research effort. The difficulty is that making them requires genetic manipulation (the **insertion of four genes**), which makes it hard to predict how they would behave in a patient. Still, the list of successful transplantation technologies in non-human animals keeps growing.

#### How do transplants actually help?

It is often assumed that transplants help by **replacing** dead or dying neurons with healthy ones. In many cases that does not appear to be the mechanism. In animal models, implants have been shown to have **neuroprotective** properties and to **stimulate regeneration and remyelination** of injured axons, and any of these effects could be the therapeutic one. This is an important qualification: a transplant that works may be working as a source of protective and growth-promoting signals rather than as new wiring.

> **Trap.** Stem cells are **pluripotent** (many, **not all**, cell types) and divide indefinitely, but cultured stem cells **deteriorate or form tumours** as chromosomal abnormalities accumulate. **Induced pluripotent stem cells** from adult **skin cells** were reported in **2007** (Takahashi et al.) and require insertion of **four genes**. And transplants often help by **neuroprotection, regeneration and remyelination**, not by cell replacement.

### Promoting recovery by rehabilitative training

Several demonstrations that **experience shapes the organisation** of both the developing and the adult brain renewed interest in **rehabilitative training** as a way of promoting recovery from CNS damage. The programmes below were derived from those findings. The book adds a speculative thought: perhaps neurotransplants would work better if combined with the right training.

#### Treating strokes

A small stroke leaves a **core** of damaged tissue, and over time function is often lost progressively in the **penumbra** around it (10.1), so the damage effectively grows outward.

> **Key study, Nudo and colleagues (1996).** Small **ischaemic lesions** were made in the **hand area of the motor cortex** of monkeys. **Five days** later a programme of hand training and practice began. Over the following **3 or 4 weeks**, the monkeys plucked **hundreds of tiny food pellets** from food wells of different sizes. The practice **substantially reduced the spread of cortical damage into the penumbra**, and the trained monkeys showed **greater recovery in the use of the affected hand**.

The striking result is the first one: training did not merely help the animals compensate, it changed how much tissue was lost. Rehabilitative training of this kind has produced comparable effects in **humans**.

#### Constraint-induced therapy

A principle that emerged from the study of neurodevelopment is that **neurons compete**: they compete with other neurons for **synaptic sites and neurotrophins**, and the losers die. **Weiller and Rijntjes (1999)** designed a rehabilitation programme based on that principle, called **constraint-induced therapy**. For **2 weeks** the patient's **functioning arm was tied down**, and during that time the **affected arm was given intensive training**. By the end of the fortnight the affected arm was performing much better, and the **motor cortex area controlling it had grown**.

The logic follows from competition. A stroke patient naturally uses the good arm, which means the circuits for the affected arm lose the competition through disuse. Restraining the good arm forces use of the affected one, giving its circuits the activity they need to hold and expand their cortical territory.

> **Trap.** In constraint-induced therapy the **unaffected (functioning) arm is restrained**, for **2 weeks**, and the **affected** arm is trained intensively; the result is better use of the affected arm and an **enlarged motor-cortex representation** of it (Weiller & Rijntjes, 1999). Restraining the affected arm would be the wrong way round. Nudo and colleagues (1996) worked with **monkeys**, began training **5 days** after the lesion, and showed that training **reduced the spread of damage into the penumbra**.

#### Treating spinal injury

In one approach to spinal injury (Wolpaw & Tennissen, 2001), patients who could not walk were supported in a **harness over a moving treadmill**. With most of their weight carried and the treadmill providing feedback, they gradually learned to make walking movements, and as they improved the support was reduced step by step. In one study using this technique, **more than 90 percent** of trained patients eventually walked independently, compared with only **50 percent** of patients given **conventional physiotherapy**. The effectiveness of the method has been confirmed and extended in human patients (e.g., Herman et al., 2002) and in non-human subjects (Frigon & Rossignol, 2008).

#### Benefits of cognitive and physical exercise

People who are cognitively and physically active are **less likely to develop neurological disorders**, and if they do, their symptoms tend to be **less severe** and their **recovery better**. But these are **correlational** findings, and they have the usual problem of causal interpretation: do active people have better neurological outcomes **because** they are active, or are they more active **because** they are healthier to begin with? Because of that problem, research in this area has relied heavily on **controlled experiments with animal models**, where activity can be assigned at random.

One experimental approach houses animals in **enriched environments**, environments designed to promote cognitive and physical activity, typically with **group housing, toys, activity wheels and changing stimulation**. Health-promoting effects of enriched environments have been demonstrated in animal models of **epilepsy, Huntington's disease, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Down syndrome, brain tumours, and various forms of stroke and traumatic brain injury**. Nobody yet knows which mechanism matters, but there is no shortage of candidates. Animals housed in enriched environments show:

- more branching of dendrites;
- larger and more numerous dendritic spines;
- larger synapses;
- a faster rate of adult neurogenesis;
- higher levels of several neurotrophic factors.

Physical exercise alone, in the form of daily **wheel running**, also benefits the rodent brain in several ways (Cotman, Berchtold, & Christie, 2007): increased adult **neurogenesis in the hippocampus**, reduced age-related loss of hippocampal neurons, and better performance on tests of **memory and navigation**, two abilities linked to the hippocampus. And **Adlard and colleagues (2005)** found that wheel running **reduced the development of amyloid plaques** in mice genetically predisposed to develop a model of Alzheimer's disease.

> **Trap.** Human studies linking activity to better neurological outcomes are **correlational**; healthier people may simply be more active. That is why the causal evidence comes from **animal experiments**: enriched environments and wheel running. Adlard and colleagues (2005) showed that wheel running **reduced amyloid plaques** in Alzheimer-model mice; Cotman and colleagues (2007) summarised increased **hippocampal neurogenesis** and better memory and navigation.

#### Treating phantom limbs

Most amputees continue to experience the amputated limb, a condition called **phantom limb**. Even some people born without a limb, about **20 percent**, report a phantom (Melzack et al., 1997), which shows that the experience cannot depend only on memories of a limb that once existed.

The most striking feature of phantom limbs is how **real** they feel. A patient may try to step out of bed onto a leg that is not there or reach for a cup with a hand that is gone. Usually the phantom behaves like a normal limb; a phantom arm, for example, seems to swing in time with the intact arm as the person walks. Sometimes, though, the phantom feels **stuck in an odd position**.

About **50 percent** of amputees suffer **severe chronic pain** in the phantom. A typical complaint is that the missing hand is clenched so tightly that the nails are digging into the palm. Phantom pain can occasionally be eased by having the patient **concentrate on opening the hand**, but surgical treatments are often attempted instead. These were based on the premise that the pain comes from **irritation at the stump**, so surgeons cut off the stump or cut parts of the neural pathway between the stump and the cortex. These operations **have not worked** (Melzack, 1992), which is itself evidence that the premise was wrong.

#### Cases of Carlos and Philip: Phantom Limbs and Ramachandran

Carlos and Philip were both amputees with phantom limbs, and their neuropsychologist was **V. S. Ramachandran**. Ramachandran had read the study of **Pons and colleagues (1991)**: after the arm's sensory neurons were cut in monkeys, the arm area of somatosensory cortex came to receive input from body parts normally mapped next to it, especially the face. His insight was that phantom limbs might **not be in the stump at all, but in the brain**: perhaps the perception of a phantom arm arises from body parts that now feed into the **old arm area of somatosensory cortex** (Ramachandran & Blakeslee, 1998).

He tested the idea simply. He touched various parts of **Carlos's** body and asked what Carlos felt. When he touched the side of the **face on the same side as the amputated arm**, Carlos felt sensations in specific parts of his **phantom hand** as well as on his face. A **second map of the hand** was found on his **shoulder** (Figure 10.22).

The result fits the somatosensory map perfectly. In the somatosensory homunculus the **hand representation lies between the face and the upper arm/shoulder** regions, so when the hand's input is lost, its cortical territory is invaded from both neighbours, and touches to the face and shoulder are now also "read" by the old hand area as touches to the hand. Carlos's phantom was a perceptual consequence of cortical reorganisation of exactly the kind Pons had measured in monkeys.

#### Philip and the mirror box

**Philip**, another of Ramachandran's patients, had **severe chronic pain** in his phantom arm. For a **decade** the phantom had felt frozen in an awkward position (Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran, 2000), and he had great pain in the phantom **elbow**.

Ramachandran reasoned that if the phantom could be made to move, the pain might be relieved, and since movement depends heavily on **feedback** (Chapter 8), he built a feedback device: a **box divided in two by a vertical mirror**. Philip put his intact **right** hand into the box through a hole in the front and looked down through a hole in the top, so that he saw his real hand and its **mirror image**, which appeared to occupy the place of his missing left hand. He was asked to put his phantom into the box and position it, as well as he could, to match the mirror image of his good hand. Then he made **synchronous, bilaterally symmetrical movements** with his real right arm and his phantom left arm while watching the good arm and its reflection. In effect, the mirror gave his brain visual evidence that the phantom was moving.

Ramachandran sent Philip home with the box and told him to use it often. **Three weeks** later, Philip's pain had subsided.

> **Trap.** Ramachandran's starting point was **Pons and colleagues (1991)**, a monkey study of somatosensory reorganisation; his conclusion was that phantoms are generated in the **brain**, not the **stump**, which is also why surgery on the stump fails. **Carlos**: touches on the **face** (same side as the amputation) and **shoulder** produced sensations in the phantom hand. **Philip**: phantom frozen for **ten years**, pain in the **elbow**, treated with the **mirror box** (synchronous symmetrical movements while viewing the mirror image), pain gone after **three weeks**. Phantom limb is reported by about **20 percent** of people born without a limb; severe phantom pain affects about **50 percent** of amputees.

### The Ironic Case of Professor P.: Recovery

The chapter closes where it opened, with Professor P., who says that writing this chapter played a part in his recovery.

When he left hospital he faced two kinds of problem. Some he knew were permanent: without a right auditory-vestibular nerve he would always have hearing and balance problems. The others worried him more. The right side of his face sagged and facial expressions were difficult; his right eye was often painful, probably from inadequate tearing; he had trouble talking; and he had debilitating attacks of fatigue. Neither his neurosurgeon nor his general practitioner seemed to know how to help, so he was largely left to manage on his own.

He built his own programme out of the research he had been writing about. Little about recovery from brain damage had been proven in humans, but the animal research pointed in a consistent direction: **cognitive and physical exercise promote recovery and other forms of neuroplasticity**. Because there was also evidence that the benefits of exercise are greatest **soon after** brain trauma, he went back to work **2 weeks** after leaving hospital, and his job became the cognitive part of the programme. It was harder than he expected: a few conversations were enough to make his face and throat ache and to exhaust him, and he would retreat to his office until he was ready for more "treatment".

For physical exercise he needed something that his balance problems would allow, and he chose **African hand drumming**, which he found both cognitively and physically demanding. He practised, took lessons and drummed with friends whenever he could. Gradually he worked, talked, smiled and drummed his way back. Today his face is reasonably symmetrical, his speech is good, he is fit, and his balance has improved.

Note how carefully he frames the outcome. He likes to think the programme helped, but he says there is **no way of knowing for sure**: he is a single case with no control condition, and some of his improvement might have happened anyway. That is the same caution the chapter asks you to apply to every claim of recovery, and it is the right note on which to end a chapter about what damaged brains can and cannot do.

## How the chapter fits together

Seven threads run through the five modules. If you can follow each one from start to finish, the chapter becomes an argument rather than a catalogue.

1. **Damage spreads after the event.** Ischaemic damage appears a day or two later because of the glutamate cascade; the penumbra may live or die over days; retrograde and transneuronal degeneration spread damage to connected neurons; Huntington's and Alzheimer's pathology spreads from one region to the whole brain, possibly by protein seeding (the pathogenic spread hypothesis). The time it takes is what makes intervention possible, which is why stroke treatment aims at the penumbra and why Alzheimer's treatment may need to start in the preclinical stage.
2. **Cells die in two ways, and the way matters.** Necrosis is fast, inflammatory and harms neighbours; apoptosis is slow, tidy and contained. All six causes in 10.1 work partly through apoptosis, which is why blocking apoptosis (Xu and colleagues, 1999) protects both neurons and memory.
3. **Protein clumps are the signature of neurodegeneration.** Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein) in Parkinson's; huntingtin aggregates in Huntington's; tau tangles and beta-amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's. Learn which protein goes with which disease; it is one of the most reliable sources of MCQs.
4. **Excitation without restraint is destructive.** Glutamate excitotoxicity in stroke, failed GABAergic inhibition and synchronous firing in epilepsy, kindling's progressive escalation of seizures, and the hypoxia of tonic-clonic seizures all come back to the same balance between excitation and inhibition.
5. **The environment, not just the neuron, decides regeneration.** CNS neurons regrow in the PNS and PNS neurons fail in the CNS; Schwann cells help, oligodendroglia and astrocytes hinder; Cheng and colleagues (1996) restored function by giving the spinal cord a PNS environment.
6. **Cortical maps are owned by whoever uses them.** Kaas, Pons and Sanes showed deprived cortex taken over by neighbouring representations; blind people's visual cortex processes sound and touch; Carlos's face and shoulder now feed his old hand area; constraint-induced therapy wins cortical territory back for an affected arm; Nudo's training limits the spread of damage into the penumbra.
7. **Be sceptical of recovery and of models.** Apparent recovery can be reduced oedema, substitution of function, or cognitive reserve; animal models reproduce only some features of the disease (kindled seizures are elicited, rats resist MPTP, amyloid treatments that work in mice fail in people); premature transplantation produced dyskinesias and, for d'Orta, probably death; and even Professor P. admits he cannot know whether his programme helped.

These threads correspond to the book's own "themes revisited": the **clinical implications** theme dominates (Professor P., Junior Seau, the complex seizure cases, the frozen drug users, Carlos and Philip); **thinking creatively** appears in the debate over the primary symptom of Alzheimer's disease, the critique of animal models, the correlation problem in exercise research and Ramachandran's mirror box; the **evolutionary perspective** appears in the comparative basis of animal models and in research on species that regenerate accurately; and the emerging themes of **consciousness** (absence seizures as brief losses of consciousness, auras as alterations of it) and **epigenetics** (epigenetic mechanisms in some epilepsies) appear in the epilepsy section.

## Worked example

*A 58-year-old right-handed schoolteacher with long-standing high blood pressure suddenly loses strength in her right hand and arm and has trouble finding words. She reaches hospital 90 minutes after onset. A CT scan shows no bleeding; an angiogram shows a narrowed left carotid artery with a small clot that appears to have broken off and lodged in a cerebral artery. Over the next two days her arm becomes weaker, then over two weeks it improves somewhat. Six months later her husband reports episodes in which she stares, repeatedly fiddles with her cardigan buttons, does not answer, and afterwards remembers nothing.*

Work through it with the chapter's concepts rather than by pattern-matching.

**What kind of stroke is this?** Ischaemic, not haemorrhagic: the CT shows no bleeding, and the angiogram shows a blocked vessel. The plug formed in a larger vessel (the narrowed carotid) and travelled to a smaller one, so the mechanism is an **embolism**, superimposed on **arteriosclerosis** (the narrowed carotid). Had the clot formed and stayed in the cerebral artery, it would be thrombosis.

**Why does the CT result matter for treatment?** Because the two acute treatments that work, **tissue plasminogen activator** (dissolves clots) and **endovascular removal** of the embolus, restore blood flow, and they are given within a few hours of an ischaemic stroke. She has arrived in time. A clot-dissolving drug would be dangerous if the stroke were a haemorrhage, which is why ruling out bleeding comes first. NMDA-receptor antagonists are not realistic here: they help only if given almost immediately.

**Why did she get worse over the first two days?** Two reasons from 10.1. Ischaemic damage takes **time to develop**, and the **glutamate cascade** spreads it: blood-starved neurons release excess glutamate, NMDA receptors let in Na⁺ and Ca²⁺, and each dying neuron passes the surge on. That is the **penumbra** being lost around the **infarct**. Every treatment decision in the first days is about saving the penumbra.

**Is her improvement over two weeks "recovery"?** Not necessarily. Early improvement may reflect falling **cerebral oedema** rather than repair. Later gains may reflect **substitution of functions** (new strategies), and as a teacher she may have substantial **cognitive reserve**, which, on Kapur's (1997) argument, lets people accomplish tasks in alternative ways without true recovery of the lost function. Her prognosis is helped by a small lesion and hurt somewhat by her age.

**What rehabilitation does the chapter support?** **Constraint-induced therapy**: restrain her **left (unaffected)** arm for two weeks while the right arm is trained intensively, so that the right arm's circuits win back cortical territory in the competition Weiller and Rijntjes (1999) exploited. Nudo and colleagues (1996) suggest that starting training within days of the stroke can also limit the spread of damage into the penumbra. An enriched, active routine and exercise are supported by animal experiments, but the human evidence is correlational and should be described that way.

**What are the new episodes?** Staring, unresponsiveness, a repetitive simple action (buttoning), and amnesia afterwards: these are **automatisms** with impaired consciousness, the picture of a **complex seizure** (focal; in older keys "complex partial"; in ILAE 2017 terms a "focal impaired awareness seizure"). The damaged area has most likely become an **epileptic focus**. It is **not** an absence seizure: absence seizures are **generalised**, are mainly a disorder of **children** that often stops at puberty, and show a bilaterally symmetrical 3-per-second spike-and-wave EEG, whereas new seizures at 58 after a focal brain lesion point to a **focal** origin. It is not a tonic-clonic seizure either, since there is no convulsion.

**What does the delayed onset of seizures resemble?** **Epileptogenesis**: seizures emerging weeks or months after a brain injury and then often becoming more frequent, exactly the course the **kindling** model was taken to mimic. Treatment would start with **anticonvulsants**, noting their side effects (memory impairment, for instance) and that they do not work for everyone.

**What should you not tell her family?** That the new neurons her brain may produce near the damage will repair it. Damage-induced neurogenesis is real in animals and was seen in one human stroke patient, but there is **no direct evidence** that new neurons contribute to recovery, and stem cells are not known to migrate far in the adult human brain.

## MCQ traps

1. **Meningiomas** are about **20 percent** of brain tumours, **encapsulated** and almost always **benign**; most other brain tumours are **infiltrating** and usually **malignant**; the commonest malignant type is the **glioma**.
2. **Metastatic** tumours are about **10 percent** of brain tumours, carried in the blood from elsewhere, often from **lung** cancer.
3. **Acoustic neuroma** = encapsulated tumour on **cranial nerve VIII**; Professor P. (John Pinel) had one on the **right**; surgery left him deaf and without vestibular function on that side.
4. Stroke is the **fifth** leading cause of death in the US in the 11th edition (older editions: **third**).
5. **Infarct** = dead tissue; **penumbra** = threatened rim that may recover; treatment aims to **save the penumbra**.
6. **Haemorrhage** = burst vessel (often an **aneurysm**); **ischaemia** = blocked supply (**thrombosis, embolism, arteriosclerosis**).
7. **Thrombus** blocks where it forms; **embolus** travels from a larger to a smaller vessel ("a thrombus that has taken a trip").
8. Ischaemic damage **takes a day or two** to appear and hits certain **hippocampal** neurons hardest.
9. Excitotoxic cascade: excess **glutamate** → **NMDA** receptors → influx of **Na⁺ and Ca²⁺** → more glutamate release and cell death.
10. NMDA antagonists must be given **almost immediately**; **tPA** and **endovascular therapy** work **within a few hours** of an **ischaemic** stroke.
11. **Contrecoup** contusions occur on the side **opposite** the blow.
12. **Subdural haematoma** lies between the **dura mater and the arachnoid**.
13. **mTBI** replaced "**concussion**" because the old term implied no long-term damage; **CTE** replaced "punch-drunk syndrome"; about **90 percent** of former American football players met CTE criteria (Mez et al., 2017).
14. **Junior Seau**: NFL linebacker **20 years**, retired **2010**, died by suicide **May 2012 aged 43**, CTE at autopsy.
15. **Encephalitis** = infection-caused brain inflammation; **meningitis** = meningeal inflammation, fatal in **30 percent** of adults (older editions: 25 percent).
16. **General paresis** = dementia and mental illness of **syphilis** (bacterial), after years of dormancy.
17. **Rabies** has a particular affinity for neural tissue and reaches the brain only after **at least a month**; **mumps and herpes** have no special affinity.
18. **Toxic psychosis**: **mercury** (mad hatters) and **lead** (crackpots).
19. **Tardive dyskinesia** affected about **5 percent** of patients after several years of 1950s antipsychotics; involuntary lip, tongue, jaw and cheek movements.
20. **Down syndrome**: about **0.15 percent** of births, extra **chromosome 21** from the **mother** at ovulation; leads to early-onset Alzheimer's.
21. About **90 percent** of loci linked to neuropsychological disorders are **not protein-coding** (Maurano et al., 2012).
22. **Apoptosis** is **slow** (a day or two), begins with **shrinkage**, uses **blebbing**, causes **no inflammation**; **necrosis** is **fast** (hours), begins with **swelling** of axons and dendrites, causes inflammation.
23. Epilepsy requires **recurrent, spontaneous** seizures; prevalence **0.8 percent** (older: about 1 percent); **30+** genes implicated.
24. **Grand mal = tonic-clonic; petit mal = absence; partial = focal**; simple partial = **simple (Jacksonian)**; complex partial = **complex (temporal lobe)**.
25. **Absence** seizure: generalised, no convulsion, **bilaterally symmetrical 3-per-second spike-and-wave**, children, often stops at puberty.
26. **Complex** seizures: about **half of adult epilepsy**, temporal lobe, **automatisms**, impaired consciousness, amnesia (Lennox, 1960, surgeon and music teacher).
27. **ILAE 2017** discourages "simple" and "complex"; focal seizures are classified by **disruption of consciousness** (the book prints "ILEA").
28. **Tonic-clonic** hypoxia can itself **damage the brain**; cyanosis, tongue biting, incontinence.
29. **Parkinson's**: **1 to 2 percent** of people over **65**, slightly more males; **resting** tremor; **Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein, 1912)**; L-dopa gave d'Orta **3 years**.
30. **DBS** for Parkinson's targets the **subthalamic nucleus**, is **high-frequency**, acts like a **reversible lesion**, and does **not** slow progression.
31. **Huntington's**: **1 in 7,500**, single **dominant** gene (**huntingtin**), all carriers affected, **50 percent** of offspring, onset about **40**, death about **20 years** later, always severe dementia, **striatum** first (older editions: 1 in 10,000, about 15 years).
32. **MS**: CNS demyelination, autoimmune, model = **experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis**; MZ **35 percent** vs DZ **6 percent** (older: 25 vs 5); remissions **up to 2 years**; vitamin D, Epstein-Barr virus, smoking.
33. **Alzheimer's**: **50 million** worldwide, **10 percent** over 65; **tangles = tau** (inside neurons), **plaques = beta-amyloid**; entorhinal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus.
34. **Four** early-onset genes contribute only **1 percent** to late-onset cases; **APOE4** (chromosome **19**) raises late-onset risk about **50 percent**; beta-amyloid gene on chromosome **21**.
35. Down syndrome: plaques and tangles in almost all by **40**, dementia in **two-thirds** by **60**; about **15 percent** of neurons in non-Down Alzheimer's brains carry an extra chromosome 21.
36. **High-plaque normals** argue against the amyloid hypothesis but **lack the inflammation** of Alzheimer's disease.
37. **Kindling** (Goddard, McIntyre & Leech, **1969**): permanent, needs **distributed** stimulation (none under about **20 minutes**), spontaneous seizures after about **300** stimulations.
38. **MPTP** (Langston, **1985**): "synthetic heroin", primates are good models, **rats resistant**, mice vary by strain.
39. **Anterograde** = **distal** segment, fast; **retrograde** = **proximal** segment, slow, cell-body changes after **2 or 3 days**.
40. PNS axons regrow from about **2 or 3 days** at **a few millimetres per day**; **Schwann cells promote**, **oligodendroglia block** regeneration.
41. **Rapid** reorganisation never exceeds **2 mm** (release from inhibition); **large** long-term reorganisation implies **collateral sprouting**.
42. **Pons et al. (1991)**: monkey face area expanded **over a centimetre** into the arm area after **10 years**.
43. **Constraint-induced therapy**: restrain the **good** arm for **2 weeks** (Weiller & Rijntjes, 1999).
44. Treadmill-harness training: **more than 90 percent** walked independently versus **50 percent** with conventional physiotherapy.
45. Phantom limb in about **20 percent** of people born without a limb; severe phantom pain in about **50 percent** of amputees; mirror box relieved Philip's pain in **3 weeks**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Tumour (neoplasm)** | A mass of cells growing independently of the rest of the body ("new growth") |
| **Meningiomas** | Tumours growing between the meninges; about 20% of brain tumours; all encapsulated |
| **Meninges** | The three membranes covering the CNS |
| **Encapsulated tumours** | Tumours that grow within their own membrane; affect the brain by pressure |
| **Benign tumours** | Surgically removable with little risk of further growth |
| **Infiltrating tumours** | Tumours that grow diffusely through surrounding tissue |
| **Malignant tumours** | Hard to remove completely; remaining tissue keeps growing |
| **Gliomas** | Infiltrating, rapidly growing tumours of glial cells; the most common malignant brain tumour |
| **Metastatic tumours** | Tumours seeded by cancer cells carried in the blood from another organ; about 10% of brain tumours |
| **Metastasis** | Transmission of disease from one organ to another |
| **Neuroma / acoustic neuroma** | Tumour on a nerve or tract / encapsulated tumour on cranial nerve VIII |
| **Strokes** | Sudden-onset cerebrovascular disorders that damage the brain |
| **Infarct** | The area of dead or dying tissue produced by a stroke |
| **Penumbra** | Dysfunctional tissue around an infarct that may recover or die |
| **Cerebral haemorrhage** | Rupture of a cerebral blood vessel with bleeding into neural tissue |
| **Aneurysm** | Balloon-like dilation of an artery wall at a point of defective elasticity |
| **Congenital** | Present at birth |
| **Cerebral ischaemia** | Disruption of the blood supply to an area of the brain |
| **Thrombosis / thrombus** | Blockage by a plug that stays where it formed / the plug itself |
| **Embolism / embolus** | Blockage by a plug carried from a larger vessel into a smaller one / the travelling plug |
| **Arteriosclerosis** | Thickening of vessel walls and narrowing of channels, usually from fat deposits |
| **Glutamate** | The brain's most prevalent excitatory neurotransmitter; drives ischaemic damage when released in excess |
| **NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors** | Glutamate receptors most involved in ischaemic cell death; admit Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ |
| **Tissue plasminogen activator** | Clot-dissolving drug used soon after ischaemic stroke |
| **Endovascular therapy** | Surgical removal of a thrombus or embolus from an artery |
| **Traumatic brain injury (TBI)** | Brain injury produced by physical force |
| **Closed-head TBIs** | Brain injuries from blows that do not penetrate the skull |
| **Contusions** | Closed-head TBIs that damage cerebral blood vessels and cause internal bleeding (a bruised brain) |
| **Subdural haematoma** | A collection of blood in the space between the dura mater and the arachnoid |
| **Contrecoup injuries** | Contusions on the side of the brain opposite the blow |
| **Mild TBI (mTBI)** | Disturbed consciousness after a blow with no contusion or structural damage; formerly "concussion" |
| **Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)** | Dementia and cerebral scarring after repeated mTBIs |
| **Dementia** | General intellectual deterioration |
| **Encephalitis** | Inflammation of the brain resulting from infection |
| **Cerebral abscesses** | Pockets of pus in the brain, usually from bacterial infection |
| **Meningitis** | Inflammation of the meninges, usually bacterial; fatal in 30% of adults |
| **General paresis** | Mental illness and dementia resulting from syphilitic infection |
| **Aetiology** | The cause of a disorder |
| **Toxic psychosis** | Chronic mental illness produced by a neurotoxin |
| **Tardive dyskinesia (TD)** | Motor disorder of the lips, tongue, jaw and cheeks after prolonged antipsychotic treatment |
| **Endogenous** | Produced by the patient's own body |
| **Down syndrome** | Disorder caused by an extra chromosome 21; intellectual disability and early Alzheimer's |
| **Apoptosis** | Active, genetically programmed cell death; slow, no inflammation |
| **Necrosis** | Passive cell death from injury; fast, with inflammation |
| **Blebbing** | Packaging of a dying cell's debris into vesicles during apoptosis |
| **Epilepsy** | Disorder of spontaneously recurring seizures generated by chronic brain dysfunction |
| **Convulsions** | Motor seizures, typically with clonus and tonus |
| **Clonus / tonus** | Tremor / rigidity |
| **Epileptic auras** | Psychological changes just before a seizure; clue to the focus and a warning |
| **Focal seizure** | A seizure that does not involve the whole brain (older term: partial) |
| **Simple seizures** | Focal seizures with one kind of sensory or motor symptom; consciousness rarely lost; Jacksonian |
| **Complex seizures** | Focal seizures, often temporal lobe, with automatisms and impaired consciousness |
| **Automatisms** | Compulsive, repetitive simple behaviours during complex seizures |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Epilepsy with complex seizures arising in the temporal lobes |
| **Generalised seizures** | Seizures that involve the entire brain |
| **Tonic-clonic seizure** | Generalised seizure with loss of consciousness and equilibrium and a tonic-clonic convulsion (older term: grand mal) |
| **Cyanosis** | Turning blue from lack of oxygen during a convulsion |
| **Hypoxia** | Shortage of oxygen supply to a tissue; can itself damage the brain |
| **Absence seizure** | Generalised, non-convulsive loss of consciousness with a vacant look (older term: petit mal) |
| **3-per-second spike-and-wave discharge** | Bilaterally symmetrical EEG pattern of absence seizures |
| **Ketogenic diet** | High-fat, moderate-protein, low-carbohydrate diet used to treat epilepsy |
| **Parkinson's disease** | Progressive movement disorder of middle and old age with loss of nigrostriatal dopamine neurons |
| **Substantia nigra** | Midbrain nucleus whose dopamine neurons degenerate in Parkinson's disease |
| **Nigrostriatal pathway** | Projection from the substantia nigra to the striatum |
| **Striatum** | Basal ganglia structure receiving nigrostriatal input; first damaged in Huntington's disease |
| **Alpha-synuclein** | Protein that clumps in surviving substantia nigra neurons in Parkinson's disease |
| **Lewy bodies** | Clumps of alpha-synuclein, first reported in 1912 |
| **L-dopa** | Dopamine precursor used to treat Parkinson's disease; loses effectiveness over time |
| **Deep brain stimulation** | Continuous low-intensity, high-frequency stimulation through an implanted electrode; acts like a reversible lesion |
| **Subthalamic nucleus** | Nucleus just below the thalamus, connected to the basal ganglia; usual DBS target in Parkinson's |
| **Huntington's disease** | Rare, dominant-gene progressive motor disorder with severe dementia |
| **Huntingtin / huntingtin protein** | The mutated dominant gene causing Huntington's disease / the protein it codes for |
| **Multiple sclerosis (MS)** | Progressive autoimmune disease attacking CNS myelin, leaving patches of hard scar tissue |
| **Autoimmune disorder** | A disorder in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissue as if it were foreign |
| **Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis** | Animal model of MS produced by injecting myelin with an immune stimulant |
| **Remyelination** | Generation of new myelin sheaths; by oligodendroglia in the CNS; fails in MS |
| **Ataxia** | Loss of motor coordination |
| **Epidemiology** | Study of factors influencing the distribution of a disease in the population |
| **Alzheimer's disease** | Most common cause of dementia in the elderly; tangles, plaques and neuron loss |
| **Preclinical / prodromal / dementia stage** | Pathology without symptoms / mild cognitive impairment / full dementia |
| **Neurofibrillary tangles** | Threadlike tangles of tau protein in the neural cytoplasm |
| **Tau protein** | Protein that normally helps maintain neuronal structure |
| **Amyloid plaques** | Clumps of scar tissue made of degenerating neurons and beta-amyloid |
| **Beta-amyloid** | Protein present in small amounts normally; aggregates into plaques |
| **Apolipoprotein E (APOE)** | Protein coded on chromosome 19; the APOE4 allele raises late-onset Alzheimer's risk |
| **Amyloid hypothesis** | The view that amyloid plaques are the primary symptom and cause the others |
| **High-plaque normals** | People with heavy plaque loads but no observable dementia |
| **Pathogenic spread hypothesis** | Misfolded proteins seed misfolding in others and spread between neurons |
| **Animal model** | An animal preparation that reproduces some features of a human disease |
| **Kindling phenomenon** | Progressive intensification of convulsions elicited by periodic brain stimulation |
| **Epileptogenesis** | The development of epilepsy, for example after a head injury |
| **MPTP** | Neurotoxin in a "synthetic heroin" that destroys substantia nigra neurons |
| **Bradykinesia / seborrhoea / micrographia** | Slowness of movement / oily skin / small handwriting |
| **Neural degeneration** | Neural deterioration and death |
| **Axotomy** | Cutting of an axon |
| **Anterograde degeneration** | Degeneration of the distal segment of a cut axon |
| **Distal segment** | The part of a cut axon between the cut and the synaptic terminals |
| **Retrograde degeneration** | Degeneration of the proximal segment of a cut axon |
| **Proximal segment** | The part of a cut axon between the cut and the cell body |
| **Transneuronal degeneration** | Spread of degeneration to synaptically connected neurons (anterograde or retrograde) |
| **Neural regeneration** | Regrowth of damaged neurons |
| **Schwann cells** | PNS myelinating cells that clear debris, make neurotrophic factors and guide regrowth |
| **Oligodendroglia** | CNS myelinating cells that release factors blocking regeneration |
| **Glial scar** | Astrocytic scar that physically and chemically impedes CNS axon regrowth |
| **Collateral sprouting** | Growth of branches from healthy axons into synaptic sites vacated by degenerating axons |
| **Release from inhibition** | Strengthening of existing connections when competing input is lost; fast, under 2 mm |
| **Cerebral oedema** | Brain swelling; its decline can mimic early recovery |
| **Substitution of functions** | Learning new strategies that compensate for, rather than restore, a lost function |
| **Cognitive reserve** | Capacity, roughly education and intelligence, that allows tasks to be done in alternative ways |
| **Neuroprotection** | Treatment that prevents the degeneration of neurons that are still alive |
| **Adrenal medulla autotransplantation** | Transplanting a patient's own adrenal medulla cells into the striatum to supply dopamine |
| **Pluripotent** | Able to develop into many, but not all, classes of adult cell |
| **Induced pluripotent stem cells** | Adult cells (e.g., skin) returned to a stem-cell state by inserting four genes (2007) |
| **Constraint-induced therapy** | Restraining the functioning limb while the affected limb is trained intensively |
| **Enriched environments** | Housing designed to promote cognitive and physical activity |
| **Phantom limb** | Continued experience of an amputated (or congenitally absent) limb |

## Likely exam points

1. **Six causes of brain damage** (tumours, strokes, closed-head TBIs, infections, neurotoxins, genetic factors) and **programmed cell death** as the common mediator.
2. **Encapsulated vs infiltrating vs metastatic tumours**, with the 20% (meningioma) and 10% (metastatic) figures and gliomas as the commonest malignant type.
3. **Professor P.**: acoustic neuroma on the right cranial nerve VIII, symptoms spreading to nerves V to X, delayed diagnosis, surgery leaving him deaf and without vestibular function on the right; recovery by work and African hand drumming.
4. **Infarct vs penumbra**; saving the penumbra as the goal of treatment.
5. **Haemorrhage (aneurysm) vs ischaemia (thrombosis, embolism, arteriosclerosis)**.
6. The **glutamate (NMDA, Na⁺ and Ca²⁺) cascade**; the two properties of ischaemic damage; NMDA antagonists vs tPA and endovascular therapy.
7. **Closed-head TBI**: contusion, subdural haematoma, contrecoup; 50 to 60 million TBIs a year, 50% lifetime risk.
8. **mTBI replacing concussion; CTE**; about 90% of former American football players meeting criteria (Mez et al., 2017); the **Junior Seau** case.
9. **Infections**: encephalitis, abscesses, meningitis (30% fatal), syphilis and **general paresis**; rabies vs mumps and herpes.
10. **Neurotoxins**: toxic psychosis (mercury, lead), **tardive dyskinesia** (5%), endogenous toxins (antibodies, cortisol, glutamate).
11. **Genetics**: why inherited disorders are rarely dominant; **Down syndrome** (0.15%, maternal, trisomy 21); why the genome did not yield quick cures (many loci, 90% non-coding).
12. **Apoptosis vs necrosis**, with speed, sequence, inflammation and blebbing.
13. **Epilepsy definition and diagnosis**: recurrent spontaneous seizures, 0.8% prevalence, EEG spikes during and between seizures, **auras** (localisation and warning).
14. **Four seizure types**: simple and complex (focal), tonic-clonic and absence (generalised), with the **old names** (partial, Jacksonian, grand mal, petit mal) and **ILAE 2017** reclassification by disruption of consciousness.
15. **Absence seizure EEG**: bilaterally symmetrical 3-per-second spike-and-wave; children; stops at puberty.
16. **Complex seizures**: temporal lobe, automatisms, half of adult epilepsy, Lennox's (1960) two cases.
17. **Epilepsy treatment**: anticonvulsants, vagus nerve stimulation, TMS, ketogenic diet, surgery.
18. **Parkinson's disease**: symptoms (resting tremor, rigidity, difficulty initiating, slowness, masklike face), prodromal signs, substantia nigra and nigrostriatal pathway, Lewy bodies, L-dopa and its decline, DBS of the subthalamic nucleus.
19. **Huntington's disease**: dominant huntingtin gene, 50% transmission, onset about 40, striatal then global degeneration, 1 in 7,500, death about 20 years, genetic testing.
20. **Parkinson's vs Huntington's** comparison.
21. **Multiple sclerosis**: CNS demyelination and sclerosis, autoimmunity and EAE, failed remyelination by oligodendroglia, MRI diagnosis, remissions, epidemiology (35% vs 6% concordance, females, Caucasians, cold climates) and risk factors (vitamin D, Epstein-Barr virus, smoking).
22. **Alzheimer's disease**: three stages, tangles (tau) and plaques (beta-amyloid), medial temporal distribution, early-onset genes vs APOE4, amyloid hypothesis with evidence for and against, Down syndrome link, pathogenic spread hypothesis, need for early treatment.
23. **Limits of animal models**: they reproduce only some features of a disease.
24. **Kindling**: Goddard, McIntyre and Leech (1969), permanence, distributed stimulation, two ways it models epilepsy, elicited vs spontaneous seizures (about 300 stimulations).
25. **MPTP model**: Langston (1985), the frozen drug users, primate vs rodent responses, role in developing dopamine agonists.
26. **Anterograde vs retrograde degeneration**, cell-body changes at 2 or 3 days, transneuronal degeneration in both directions.
27. **PNS regeneration patterns** (intact sheaths, slight separation, wide separation) and **why the CNS fails** (environment: Schwann cells vs oligodendroglia and astrocytes); collateral sprouting.
28. **Cortical reorganisation**: Kaas et al. (1990), Pons et al. (1991), Sanes et al. (1990); blind individuals; release from inhibition vs collateral sprouting.
29. **Recovery of function**: Teuber (1975), oedema and substitution of function as confounds, small lesions and young patients, cognitive reserve (Kapur, 1997), damage-induced neurogenesis and its limits.
30. **Neurotransplantation**: fetal substantia nigra grafts in MPTP monkeys, premature clinical use and the dyskinesia outcome, **d'Orta's** adrenal medulla autotransplant, Xu et al. (1999) apoptosis inhibitor, BDNF and GDNF, Cheng et al. (1996) Schwann cell bridges, stem cells and induced pluripotent cells (2007), neuroprotection rather than replacement.
31. **Rehabilitative training**: Nudo et al. (1996), constraint-induced therapy (Weiller & Rijntjes, 1999), treadmill-harness training (90% vs 50%), enriched environments, wheel running (Cotman et al., 2007; Adlard et al., 2005), and the correlational problem in human exercise research.
32. **Phantom limbs**: reality, pain, failure of stump surgery, Ramachandran's Carlos (face and shoulder maps) and Philip (mirror box, three weeks).
