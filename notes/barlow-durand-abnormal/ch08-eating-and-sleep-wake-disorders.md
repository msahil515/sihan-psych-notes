# Ch 8: Eating and Sleep–Wake Disorders

This chapter covers the two great "activities of survival," eating and sleeping, and how psychological and social factors disrupt them. The eating-disorders half (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, plus obesity) is the most **socioculturally driven** group of disorders in the book and contains anorexia, which has the **highest mortality rate of any psychological disorder**. The sleep half splits into **dyssomnias** (amount/timing/quality of sleep) and **parasomnias** (abnormal events during sleep). High-yield for the exam: exact DSM-5(-TR) criteria, the integrative causal model, CBT-E/transdiagnostic treatment, and the dyssomnia-vs-parasomnia distinction.

> **Note on nosology:** The book is written to **DSM-5 (2013)**. Diagnostic criteria below are presented as **DSM-5-TR** (the eating and sleep–wake chapters were essentially carried forward unchanged from DSM-5 to DSM-5-TR; specific TR updates are flagged inline). **ICD-11 notes** are added wherever the current WHO classification differs from the 2013 book text; they are an overlay, not the book's own text.

## Major Types of Eating Disorders — Overview
- Three related disorders share **"an overwhelming, all-encompassing drive to be thin."**
  - **Bulimia nervosa:** out-of-control eating episodes (**binges**) followed by compensatory **purging** (self-induced vomiting, laxatives, diuretics) or other compensation.
  - **Anorexia nervosa:** eats almost nothing; body weight drops dangerously.
  - **Binge-eating disorder (BED):** binges repeatedly, distressing, but **no** compensatory purging.
- **Mortality (anorexia):** up to **20% die** over long follow-up; slightly more than **5% within 10 years**. **Highest mortality rate of any psychological disorder** in the book (higher than depression). **20–30% of anorexia deaths are suicides** = ~**50× the general-population suicide risk**. Overall eating-disorder death rates are **6×** the normal population.
- **Epidemiology trends:** rose dramatically in the West ~1960–1995, then leveled off; bulimia referrals at one Canadian center went from "virtually none to >140/year" (1975–1986). Now **going global** (China, Japan approaching Western rates).
- **Culturally specific:** **>90% of severe cases are young females** in socially competitive environments. "Pro-ana" / "pro-mia" websites give mutual reinforcement.
- Etiology here is **sociocultural > psychological/biological** (unlike most disorders).
- **History:** Eating disorders first included as a separate group in **DSM-IV** (2000).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** All three eating disorders retained. **DSM-5-TR** clarified BED **severity** wording and updated **ICD-10-CM codes**. The big nosological change from DSM-IV was the **elevation of BED to a full disorder** (it was "needing further study" in DSM-IV) and dropping mutually-exclusive eating-disorder diagnoses.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 groups these under **Feeding and Eating Disorders** and adds categories not in DSM, e.g. **Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)** and a dimensional approach; ICD-11 anorexia uses a **BMI < 18.5** anchor for "significantly low weight" and includes a **"with dangerously low body weight"** specifier. ICD-11 retains bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder.

## Bulimia Nervosa
### Clinical Description
- Hallmark: eating an amount of food (typically junk food) **larger than most people would eat** in similar circumstances, experienced as **out of control** (both criteria essential to the definition of a binge).
- Caloric intake of binges varies widely person to person.
- **Compensation** almost always by purging: **self-induced vomiting**, **laxatives** (relieve constipation), **diuretics** (fluid loss via urination). Some fast between binges; some over-exercise (excessive exercise more typical of anorexia: **57% of bulimia** vs **81% of anorexia** patients exercised excessively).
- **Subtypes dropped:** DSM-IV-TR had **purging** vs **nonpurging** types; nonpurging was rare (6–8%) with no meaningful differences, so the distinction was **dropped in DSM-5**.
- Purging is **inefficient**: vomiting removes ~**50%** of calories just consumed (less if delayed); laxatives/diuretics have **little effect** (act too late).
- Key psychological criterion (added in DSM-IV 1994): **self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight** (only 3% of 107 treatment-seeking women did *not* share this attitude).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Bulimia Nervosa (Table 8.1):**
> A. **Recurrent binge eating**, defined by BOTH: (1) eating in a **discrete period (e.g., within any 2-hour period)** an amount **definitely larger than most people would eat**; (2) **a sense of lack of control** during the episode.
> B. **Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behavior** to prevent weight gain (self-induced vomiting; misuse of laxatives, diuretics, other meds; fasting; excessive exercise).
> C. Binge eating AND compensatory behaviors both occur, on average, **≥ once a week for 3 months**.
> D. **Self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight.**
> E. Disturbance does **not** occur exclusively during anorexia nervosa.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Frequency threshold is **once/week × 3 months** (DSM-IV-TR required **twice/week**). Severity is specified by average compensatory episodes/week (mild 1–3, moderate 4–7, severe 8–13, extreme ≥14).

### Medical Consequences
- **Salivary gland enlargement** → "chubby" face.
- Erosion of **dental enamel** on inner front teeth; **esophageal tears**.
- **Electrolyte imbalance** (sodium, potassium) from continued vomiting → **cardiac arrhythmia, seizures, renal (kidney) failure** (can be fatal).
- Paradox: young women with bulimia develop **more body fat** than weight-matched controls (the very thing they fear). Reversible with normalized eating.
- Laxative abuse → severe constipation / permanent colon damage.
- **Russell's sign:** calluses on fingers/back of hand from inducing the gag reflex.

### Associated Psychological Disorders
- **Anxiety disorders** strongly co-occur: **80.6% lifetime** (Hudson et al., 2007); **66% of adolescents** with bulimia had a co-occurring anxiety disorder. But anxiety-disorder patients do NOT show elevated eating-disorder rates.
- **Mood disorders / depression:** ~20% met criteria when interviewed; **50–70%** at some point. Evidence: **depression follows bulimia** (likely a reaction), not the cause.
- **Substance abuse:** **36.8%** of bulimia, **27%** of anorexia patients. Twin study: shared risk factors of **novelty seeking + emotional instability** drive comorbidity.

## Anorexia Nervosa
### Clinical Description
- Literally "nervous loss of appetite" — a **misnomer** (appetite usually remains; cravings are controlled).
- Core is NOT weight loss itself but an **intense fear of obesity and relentless pursuit of thinness**.
- People with anorexia are **proud** of their control; people with bulimia are **ashamed** of their lack of control. Both share a **morbid fear of gaining weight and losing control**; the difference is **success at losing weight**.
- Usually begins in an adolescent who is (or perceives herself) overweight; diet escalates into obsessive thinness. Severe/punishing **exercise** common.
- **Two DSM-5 subtypes** (subtyping now refers only to the **last 3 months**):
  - **Restricting type:** dieting/fasting/excessive exercise to limit intake.
  - **Binge-eating/purging type:** binges on relatively **small** amounts and purges more **consistently** (sometimes every time they eat). ~half of anorexia patients binge/purge.
- Subtyping poorly predicts course (**62% of restricting subtype** began bingeing/purging within 8 years) — reflects a **phase/stage**, not a stable type.
- **Body image disturbance:** sees a "fat" person in the mirror despite emaciation; rarely seeks treatment alone (family pressure usual). Some become expert chefs / hoard food.
- DSM-5 specifies only **"significantly low" body weight** (~15% below expected as a guide); average is **25–30% below normal** by the time treatment is sought.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Anorexia Nervosa (Table 8.2):**
> A. **Restriction of energy intake** → **significantly low body weight** (less than minimally normal for age, sex, developmental trajectory, physical health).
> B. **Intense fear of gaining weight / becoming fat**, OR persistent behavior interfering with weight gain, even at significantly low weight.
> C. **Disturbance in experience of body weight/shape**, undue influence on self-evaluation, OR persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of low weight.
> **Specify type:** Restricting type / Binge-eating–purging type (last 3 months).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** **Amenorrhea was DROPPED** as a criterion in DSM-5 (inconsistent — doesn't occur in all cases, can't apply to males/premenarchal/postmenopausal). Severity is by **BMI**: mild ≥17, moderate 16–16.99, severe 15–15.99, extreme <15. DSM-5-TR clarified the **fear/behavior** wording in Criterion B.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 anchors "significantly low weight" at **BMI < 18.5** in adults and includes a separate **"anorexia nervosa with dangerously low body weight"** qualifier; it also added **ARFID** (restriction without weight/shape concern), which DSM-5 placed in feeding-disorders.

### Medical Consequences
- **Amenorrhea** (cessation of menstruation) — common but now NOT a diagnostic criterion.
- **Dry skin, brittle hair/nails, cold intolerance, lanugo** (downy hair on limbs/cheeks).
- **Cardiovascular:** chronically low blood pressure and heart rate.
- If vomiting present: electrolyte imbalance → cardiac/kidney problems (as in bulimia).
- Starvation induces **loss of gray matter + hormonal dysregulation** (reversible on weight restoration).

### Associated Psychological Disorders
- Anxiety + mood disorders common; **depression in up to 71%** at some point.
- **OCD** notably co-occurs (rituals to rid weight-gain thoughts).
- **Substance abuse** common; with anorexia, a **strong predictor of mortality, particularly suicide**.

## Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)
- Identified in 1990s: marked distress from binge eating **without extreme compensatory behaviors** (so cannot be diagnosed as bulimia).
- DSM-IV: "needing further study"; **DSM-5: full-fledged disorder.**
- Distinct from other eating disorders: **different heritability**, **more males**, **later age of onset**, **greater remission**, **better treatment response**.
- Found in weight-control programs: **18.8%** of mildly obese; up to ~30% in others; ~**20% of obese in weight-loss programs**, rising to ~**50% of bariatric-surgery candidates**.
- BED is a **separate disorder from obesity** and associated with **more severe obesity**.
- ~**33%** of BED patients binge to alleviate "bad moods" / negative affect → more psychologically disturbed than the **67%** who do not.
- Prognosis relatively good: only **18%** still met full BED criteria at 5-year follow-up (but % obese rose 21% → 39%).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Binge-Eating Disorder (Table 8.3):**
> A. **Recurrent binge eating** (discrete period; larger amount than most would eat; sense of lack of control).
> B. Binges associated with **≥3 of:** (1) eating much more rapidly than normal; (2) eating until uncomfortably full; (3) eating large amounts when not physically hungry; (4) eating alone due to embarrassment; (5) feeling disgusted/depressed/very guilty afterward.
> C. **Marked distress** regarding binge eating.
> D. Binge eating, on average, **≥ once a week for 3 months**.
> E. NOT associated with recurrent inappropriate compensatory behavior; NOT exclusively during bulimia or anorexia.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Frequency/duration is **once/week × 3 months** (DSM-IV's proposed research criteria used **twice/week × 6 months**). Severity by binges/week (mild 1–3 … extreme ≥14). BED is also recognized in **ICD-11**.

## Statistics (Eating Disorders)
- Bulimia recognized as a distinct disorder only in the **1970s** (Russell, 1979).
- **90–95%** of treatment-presenting bulimia cases are women. Among male cases, **42%** were gay or bisexual (far higher than in heterosexual males); male athletes in weight-regulated sports (e.g., wrestling) another large group. (Historically, recorded cases were mostly male.)
- **Adolescent girls** most at risk: in one prospective study **>12%** had some eating disorder by age 20.
- **Lifetime prevalence (Hudson et al., 2007, National Comorbidity Survey):** anorexia **0.6%** (M 0.3 / F 0.9), bulimia **1.0%** (M 0.5 / F 1.5), BED **2.8%** (M 2.0 / F 3.5), subthreshold BED 1.2%, any binge eating 4.5%. Lifetime prevalence **2–3× greater for females** (except subthreshold BED).
- Finland telephone survey: lifetime anorexia **2.2%** (half undetected) → anorexia may be **underrepresented** in some surveys.
- **Median age of onset: 18–21 years** for all eating disorders. Anorexia tends to begin younger (~15); bulimia can begin as early as **age 10**.
- **Course:** bulimia tends to be **chronic** if untreated (drive for thinness present 10 years later); poor prognosis (50–67% with serious symptoms each year of a 5-year study). Strongest predictors of persistent bulimia: **childhood obesity** + **continued overemphasis on thinness**. Anorexia chronic but somewhat less so; more **resistant to treatment**. 7-year remission: **33% anorexia vs 66% bulimia**.

### Cross-Cultural & Developmental Considerations
- Disorders develop in **immigrants** to Western countries (Egyptian women: 0% in Cairo vs **12%** in London).
- African American adolescent girls historically had less body dissatisfaction; Curaçao study: anorexia incidence **zero** in majority black population but at Western levels in white/mixed minority. Differences are **diminishing** (Marques et al., 2011). Native Americans generally higher than other groups.
- **Major risk factors across groups:** being overweight, higher social class, acculturation to the majority.
- **Developmental:** after puberty girls gain **fat** (away from thin ideal); boys gain **muscle** (toward muscular ideal). Negative attitudes toward overweight emerge by **age 3**; >half of girls 6–8 want to be thinner; 20% of 9-year-olds trying to lose weight; 40% by age 14. Can also onset after age 55.

## Causes of Eating Disorders
> Biological, psychological, social all contribute, but **the most dramatic factors are social and cultural.**

### Social Dimensions
- For young females in competitive environments, **self-worth = body measurements/body fat**. The **cultural imperative for thinness → dieting → the "slippery slope."**
- **"Glorification of slenderness"** (Levine & Smolak): media females thinner than average; overweight men 2–5× more common as TV characters than overweight women. Meta-analysis (77 studies) → strong link between **thin-ideal media exposure and body-image concerns**.
- Risk tied to **internalizing/"buying in" to** thin-ideal media messages.
- Standards increasingly **unachievable** (average woman has gotten larger with better nutrition).
- **Sex differences in body image (Fallon & Rozin, 1985):** men rate current = ideal = attractive; women rate **current >> attractive > ideal**. Men generally want to be **heavier/more muscular** (chose ideal ~28 lb more muscular; thought women prefer ~30 lb more muscular — but women actually preferred ordinary build). **"Reverse anorexia" / muscle dysmorphia** in male weightlifters → anabolic-androgenic steroid abuse.
- **Friendship cliques** share body-image attitudes — but newer evidence: girls **choose** friends who already share these attitudes (cliques don't necessarily *cause* them).

### Dietary Restraint
- **Keys semistarvation study (1950, WWII conscientious objectors):** 6 months at half rations → lost **25%** body weight; became **preoccupied with food** (recipes, hoarding) — showing starvation itself produces "anorexia-like" psychology.
- **Ballet dancers:** ≥25% developed eating disorders over 2 years (extreme thinness pressure). Same for gymnasts/athletes.
- Dieting adolescents = **8× more likely** to develop an eating disorder 1 year later (Patton et al.). Stice et al.: dieting girls had **>300% greater risk of obesity** over 4 years.
- Why dieting → weight gain? Cottone rat study: withdrawing junk food produced **stress/anxiety** and overeating of pellets — repeated "dieting" causes **addiction-like withdrawal**.
- Risk markers among dieters who developed eating disorders: already binge/purging, eating in secret, desire for empty stomach, food preoccupation, fear of losing control.

### Family Influences
- "Typical" anorexia family described as **successful, hard-driving, appearance-focused, harmony-seeking** (denies conflict). Mothers as "society's messengers" (often dieting/perfectionistic).
- **But** the **Academy for Eating Disorders** position: family factors can play a role but are **NOT the exclusive or even primary mechanism** of risk. Post-onset, family relationships deteriorate (educated parents resorting to physical violence in frustration).

### Biological Dimensions
- Run in families: relatives **4–5× more likely** to develop eating disorders.
- **Twin studies (Kendler):** bulimia concordance **23% MZ vs 9% DZ**. Bulik et al. heritability **0.56** → **genes ~half the equation.**
- What's inherited is unclear: candidates = **emotional instability, poor impulse control, perfectionism, negative affect, need for order.**
- **Hypothalamus** central to eating regulation. Neurotransmitters: norepinephrine, dopamine, and especially **serotonin** (most associated; **low serotonergic activity ↔ impulsivity + binge eating**). Most drug treatments target **serotonin**.
- Cause vs effect: neurobiological abnormalities likely **result from** semistarvation/binge-purge cycles but may **maintain** the disorder.

### Psychological Dimensions
- **Diminished sense of personal control**, low self-esteem, **perfectionism** (attempt to control). Perfectionism alone weakly linked — needs to combine with **feeling overweight + low self-esteem + distorted body image** to drive an eating disorder.
- Intensely **preoccupied with how they appear to others**; feel like "impostors/frauds" → heightened **social anxiety**.
- Body-image perception **fluctuates daily** (bulimic women judged themselves "larger" after a candy bar + soft drink; controls unaffected).
- **Anxiety relief model (Rosen & Leitenberg):** purging relieves anxiety → reinforces purging. But treatment evidence says reducing **dietary restraint** matters more than reducing eating-related anxiety.
- **Mood intolerance:** a subgroup cannot tolerate negative emotion → binge/vomit/exercise to regulate mood; hungry bulimic women had stronger negative reactions to food cues and ate more.

### An Integrative Model
- **No single factor is sufficient.** Shared biological vulnerability with **anxiety disorders** (high stress responsiveness); same drug/psychological treatments work → "**eating disorders could be conceptualized as anxiety disorders focused on fear of becoming overweight.**"
- Pathway: **sociocultural thin pressure → severe dieting**; high-achieving, perfectionistic family + peer reinforcement → exaggerated focus on shape/weight. Whether one becomes anorexic (successful restriction) vs bulimic (failed restriction → binge-purge) may be set by **biology/physiology** or **personality (overcontrol)**. Most anorexics eventually binge/purge.

## Treatment of Eating Disorders
### Drug Treatments
- **Anorexia:** drugs **NOT effective** — fluoxetine (Prozac) gave **no benefit** in relapse prevention after weight restoration (Walsh et al., 2006).
- **Bulimia:** **antidepressants** (same ones used for mood/anxiety) help somewhat, especially during binge-purge cycle. **FDA approved Prozac for eating disorders in 1996.** Binge/purge reduction ~**47% (tricyclics)** and **65% (Prozac)**. But antidepressants alone have **no substantial long-lasting effect**; better than placebo short-term, may slightly enhance psychotherapy.

### Psychological Treatments — the transdiagnostic shift
- Pre-1980s treatments (self-esteem, identity, family communication) were **not effective enough**.
- **Short-term CBT** targeting problem eating + body-shape attitudes became the **treatment of choice for bulimia.**
- **CBT-E (cognitive-behavioral therapy–enhanced; Fairburn 2008):** **transdiagnostic** — applies (with minor variation) to **all** eating disorders, since they share causal factors and DSM-IV's mutually-exclusive categories failed (≥50% of severe cases were "**eating disorder NOS**"; many now classified as BED).
  - **Stage 1:** teach physical consequences of binge/purge, ineffectiveness of vomiting/laxatives, adverse effects of dieting; schedule **5–6 small meals/day, ≤3-hour intervals** (eliminates overeat–restrict cycle).
  - **Later stages:** alter dysfunctional thoughts about shape/weight/eating; coping strategies; **don't be alone after eating** early on.
  - Very-low-weight anorexia (**BMI ≤17.5**) excluded until weight restored.

#### Bulimia Nervosa — outcomes
- **Agras et al. (2000):** CBT vs IPT (220 patients). End of treatment: **CBT 45% recovered vs IPT 8%** (remission 67% vs 40%). At 1 year, **IPT "caught up"** (CBT 40% vs IPT 27% recovered). CBT **preferred because it works faster.** Best predictor of recovery = **substantial improvement in the first six sessions.**
- CBT-E vs 2-yr psychoanalytic psychotherapy (Poulsen): at 5 months **42% vs 6% recovered**; at 2 years **44% vs 15%.**
- **Family therapy** helps adolescents; **guided self-help (CBT principles)** effective for less severe cases. Combining drugs + CBT may modestly boost short-term outcome. CBT > medication alone; nonresponders may benefit from **IPT** or **antidepressants.**

#### Binge-Eating Disorder — outcomes
- CBT adapted from bulimia works well: Agras et al. — **41% abstinent** post-treatment, **33% at 1 year**; **stopping binge eating is critical to sustaining weight loss** (abstainers kept ~9 lb off; continued bingers gained ~8 lb).
- **IPT is as effective as CBT** for BED (~60% binge-free at 1 year). **Prozac ineffective** for BED and adds nothing to CBT.
- Behavioral weight-loss programs (e.g., Weight Watchers) help binging less than CBT. **Self-help should be the first-line** treatment for BED; severe/comorbid cases need therapist-led care.

#### Anorexia Nervosa — treatment
- **First goal: restore weight** to at least low-normal. **Inpatient** if **<85% of average healthy weight** or rapid loss + food refusal (risk of **acute cardiac failure**). Weight gain achievable in **≥85%** (~½–1 lb/day) but is the **easy part** and a **poor predictor of long-term outcome.**
- Must then address **dysfunctional shape attitudes + interpersonal disruptions** or relapse is near-certain.
- **Extended (1-year) CBT** beat nutritional counseling for relapse prevention (**22% vs 73% failing**); nutritional counseling alone is ineffective.
- **Include the family**; **Family-Based Treatment (FBT / Maudsley)** especially effective for **girls <19 with short history.** Lock et al.: FBT remission **42% (49% at 1 yr) vs 23%** for individual therapy. CBT-E promising for adolescents.

### Preventing Eating Disorders
- Best chance: **selective approach** — target girls **age 15+** at risk, focus on reducing exaggerated shape/weight focus and **promoting body acceptance** (mirrors selective prevention of depression).
- **"Healthy Weight"** program (Stice) reduced risk factors durably at 6 months.
- **Internet-delivered** prevention works: "**Student Bodies**" program improved body image / reduced drive for thinness; "**The Body Project**" / **eBody Project** (no clinician) as good as group delivery. Sorority women especially high-risk.

## Obesity
> **NOT a DSM disorder** (anxiety/mood only somewhat elevated; substance abuse actually lower). Included because it's a major public-health epidemic. Based on **BMI** (correlated with body fat). Underweight = **BMI < 18.5**; obesity (chapter focus) = **BMI > 29/30**.

### Statistics
- US adult obesity (BMI ≥30): **30.5% (2000) → 35.7% (2010)**; ~tripled from 12% in 1991. ~**70% of US adults overweight, >35% obese.** Medical costs ~**$147 billion** (9.1% of US health expenditures).
- Mortality: at **BMI 30 risk +30%**; at **BMI ≥40 risk +100% or more**. **6.3%** of adults have BMI >40. In 2000, for the first time in human evolution, **overweight adults surpassed underweight** worldwide.
- **Children/adolescents:** overweight youth tripled in 25 years; obesity (≥95th percentile) ~**17%**, maybe leveling off; **30.4%** overweight-or-obese. Stigma → prejudice; teasing → obesity via depression/binge eating.
- **Global:** Eastern/Southern Europe up to 50%; doubling in Japan; rising in China. Obesity is main driver of **type 2 diabetes**.
- **Ethnicity (US women):** **58% African American, 41% Hispanic, 32% Caucasian** obese.

### Disordered Eating Patterns in Obesity
- **(1) Binge eating** (BED) — only **7–19%** of obese; integrate BED treatment into weight loss.
- **(2) Night eating syndrome:** **6–16%** of obese seeking weight loss, up to **55%** of bariatric candidates. Consume **≥⅓ of daily intake after the evening meal**, get up at night for high-calorie snacks, skip breakfast — **awake throughout** (≠ nocturnal eating syndrome, which is a sleep disorder where they never wake).

### Causes
- Largest contributor = **modernization / "toxic environment"** (Brownell): sedentary lifestyle + cheap, advertised, energy-dense food. **Pima Indians:** Arizona Pima women ate 41% calories as fat, weighed **44 lb more** than Mexico Pima (23% fat) — same genes, different environment. Immigrants doubled obesity (8%→19%) after 15 years in US.
- **Genetics ~30%** of causation (fat cell number, fat storage, activity levels) but a "**toxic environment turns the genes on**." Addictive obese eating shares **reward neurocircuitry** with substance abuse (Gearhardt). Emotional regulation, impulse control, attitudes matter.
- **Social networks:** chance of becoming obese rose **37% → 57%** if a spouse/sibling/close friend was obese — "obesity spreads through social networks."

### Treatment (stepped, least → most intrusive)
- **Self-directed diet books:** short-term loss, almost always regained.
- **Popular diets** (Atkins / Ornish / Zone / Weight Watchers): few differences, **~4–7 lb after 1 year**, only 50–65% adhered.
- **Commercial self-help** (Weight Watchers, Jenny Craig): better than self-directed. Long-term, ~19–37% within 5 lb of goal at 5 years → **up to 80% not successful long-term.** Free program (food provided) **doubled** results at 2 years (incentive effect).
- **Professionally directed behavior modification = most successful**, especially with **periodic group maintenance sessions** (monthly counselor contact > website > self-directed; 71% stayed below entry weight).
- **Very-low-calorie diets** (4–6 liquid "shakes"/day): lose up to 20%, but regain up to 50%.
- **Drugs:** modest effect; cardiovascular side effects a problem. FDA-approved: **lorcaserin (Belviq), phentermine/topiramate (Qsymia)**; **sibutramine (Meridia) withdrawn 2010** (cardiovascular).
- **Bariatric surgery** (BMI ≥40): gastric stapling / gastric bypass. **20–30% weight loss** maintained for years; usually more successful than diets. **~15% fail/regain**; **0.1–0.5% die** intraoperatively (~1% 30-day, ~6% at 5 yr); reduces obesity-related death (e.g., diabetes) up to ~90%. Requires **psychological assessment**.
- **Children/adolescents:** **better outcomes than adults**, especially parent-involved behavior modification (~20% reduction in overweight), brief/intensive/eating-and-exercise-focused programs; pressuring overweight kids not to eat **backfires**.
- **Policy:** food/soda taxes ("Twinkie tax"), removing soda machines from schools, proposed **1¢/oz excise tax** on sweetened beverages.

## Sleep–Wake Disorders: The Major Dyssomnias
- We spend ~⅓ of life asleep (~3,000 hr/year). **28%** of US adults report excessive daytime sleepiness. Even 24h of mild deprivation impairs clear thinking and immune function.
- Sleep problems **interact** with psychological disorders (autism, schizophrenia, depression, bipolar, anxiety). **REM sleep** linked to anxiety (limbic circuit) and depression; sleep abnormalities can **precede** clinical depression; sleep deprivation has **temporary antidepressant** effects (but can induce depressed mood in nondepressed people). Insufficient sleep can stimulate **overeating/obesity.**

### Two major categories
- **Dyssomnias:** problems with **amount, timing, or quality** of sleep (can't get enough, can't sleep when you want, unrefreshing sleep).
- **Parasomnias:** abnormal **behavioral/physiological events** during sleep (nightmares, sleepwalking).

### Assessment
- **Polysomnography (PSG):** overnight lab study measuring respiration/O₂ desaturation (airflow), leg movements, **EEG** (brain waves), **EOG** (eye movements), **EMG** (muscle), **ECG** (heart).
- **Actigraph:** wristwatch-size movement recorder (used even for astronauts).
- **Sleep efficiency (SE):** % of time in bed actually asleep = (time asleep / time in bed). 100% = instant sleep, no waking; 50% = awake half the time in bed.
- A sleep problem is partly **subjective** — judged by **daytime sequelae** (90-min sleep onset is fine if you feel rested, a problem if it causes distress/fatigue).

> **Summary of DSM-5 Sleep–Wake Disorders (Table 8.4):**
> **Dyssomnias:** Insomnia Disorder; Hypersomnolence Disorders; Narcolepsy; Breathing-Related Sleep Disorders (Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea, Central Sleep Apnea, Sleep-Related Hypoventilation); Circadian Rhythm Sleep–Wake Disorder.
> **Parasomnias:** Disorder of Arousal (NREM — confusional arousals, sleepwalking, sleep terrors); Nightmare Disorder; REM Sleep Behavior Disorder; Restless Legs Syndrome (Willis-Ekbom disease); Substance-Induced Sleep Disorder.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5 made big terminology changes from DSM-IV — dropped "primary insomnia," replaced with **Insomnia Disorder** (regardless of comorbidity, reflecting that sleep problems deserve independent treatment), and added **Restless Legs Syndrome** and **REM Sleep Behavior Disorder** as independent disorders. DSM-5-TR carried these forward with updated ICD-10-CM codes.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 places sleep–wake disorders in a **separate dedicated chapter (Chapter 7, "Sleep–Wake Disorders")**, NOT within mental disorders, recognizing their cross-cutting medical-psychiatric nature.

## Insomnia Disorder
- One of the most common sleep–wake disorders. You **can't** go fully without sleep — after 1–2 nights awake, **microsleeps** (seconds) occur. **Fatal familial insomnia** (degenerative brain disorder) is the rare exception where total sleeplessness leads to death.
- Three complaint types: **difficulty initiating sleep**, **difficulty maintaining sleep** (frequent/early awakenings), **nonrestorative sleep**.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Insomnia Disorder (Table 8.4):**
> A. Dissatisfaction with sleep quantity/quality with ≥1 of: (1) **difficulty initiating sleep**; (2) **difficulty maintaining sleep** (frequent awakenings/trouble returning); (3) **early-morning awakening** with inability to return to sleep.
> B. Causes clinically significant distress/impairment.
> C. Occurs **≥3 nights/week**.
> D. Present **≥3 months**.
> E. Occurs **despite adequate opportunity** for sleep.
> F–H. Not better explained by another sleep–wake disorder, substance, or coexisting condition.
> **Specify:** Episodic (1–<3 mo) / Persistent (≥3 mo) / Recurrent (≥2 episodes in 1 year).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Term "**primary insomnia**" (DSM-IV) replaced by **Insomnia Disorder**; the old "Insomnia related to another mental disorder" was eliminated so that insomnia is treated as a disorder in its own right even when comorbid.

### Statistics
- ~⅓ of population reports some insomnia symptoms yearly; **~35% of older adults** report excessive daytime sleepiness (older black men most).
- Decreases total sleep in depression, substance use, anxiety, Alzheimer's. **Alcohol** vicious cycle: induces drowsiness but interrupts sleep → anxiety → more alcohol.
- **Women report insomnia ~2× as often as men** (hormonal differences / reporting). Protective factors in women: moderate alcohol/caffeine, **Mediterranean diet**.
- Children: 20–40% prevalence. Adolescents: biological shift to **later bedtime** clashes with early school start. Older adults complain more (sleep duration decreases with age; >65 often <6 hr, multiple awakenings). Ethnocultural differences (Chinese American youth fewest problems; Mexican American most).

### Causes
- Medical/psychological conditions, pain, inactivity, respiratory problems. **Biological clock / temperature:** people with insomnia have **higher, less-variable body temperatures**; delayed temperature rhythm.
- Drugs, environmental changes (light/noise/temperature), other sleep disorders (apnea, periodic limb movement) can mimic insomnia. Psychological stress (e.g., cadaver dissection study).
- **Cognition matters:** unrealistic expectations ("I need a full 8 hours") disrupt sleep.
- **Learned insomnia:** bed/bedroom becomes associated with frustration/anxiety; bedtime itself triggers anxiety. Children may learn to fall asleep only with a parent present.
- **Cross-cultural:** US norm = infant sleeps alone in separate room; many cultures (Guatemala, Korea, Japan) = co-sleeping; cultural norms shape sleep, and unmet demands cause stress.

### An Integrative Model (Spielman; Durand)
- Both biological + psychological factors, **reciprocally related.**
- **Biological vulnerability** (predisposing conditions — light sleeper, family history) × **sleep stress** (poor sleep hygiene, cultural demands) × **maladaptive reactions** (naps, schedule changes, parental reactions, self-medication) → maintained sleep disturbance.
- **Rebound insomnia:** stopping sleep meds → worse sleep → readminister → cycle. Naps relieve daytime fatigue but **disrupt nighttime sleep.** Anxiety extends the problem.

## Hypersomnolence Disorders
- **Sleeping too much** (hyper- = excess). Sleep through the night, appear rested on waking, but still excessively tired all day (≠ insomnia, where the person is undersleeping; ≠ apnea).
- Diagnosis depends on personal impact (driving, attention, academics).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Hypersomnolence Disorder (Table 8.5):**
> A. Self-reported excessive sleepiness despite a main sleep period **≥7 hours**, with ≥1 of: (1) **recurrent lapses into sleep within the same day**; (2) **prolonged main sleep >9 hours** that is non-restorative; (3) **difficulty being fully awake after abrupt awakening** (sleep inertia).
> B. **≥3 times/week for ≥3 months.**
> C. Significant distress/impairment.
> D–F. Not better explained by another sleep disorder, substance, or coexisting condition.
> **Specify:** Acute (<1 mo) / Subacute (1–3 mo) / Persistent (>3 mo); severity by days/week of daytime alertness difficulty (Mild 1–2, Moderate 3–4, Severe 5–7).

- **Causes:** under-researched. Genetic factors (**HLA-Cw2, HLA-DR11**); subgroup had prior **viral infection** (mononucleosis, hepatitis, viral pneumonia) → likely multiple causes.

## Narcolepsy
- Relatively rare: **0.03–0.16%** of population, ~equal males/females; problems usually first appear in **teenage years.** Excessive sleepiness comes first; cataplexy can appear simultaneously or with a delay **up to 30 years.**
- **Cataplexy:** sudden **loss of muscle tone** while awake (facial weakness → full collapse), seconds to minutes, **precipitated by strong emotion (laughter, anger).** Results from a **sudden onset of REM sleep** — narcoleptics go **directly from waking to REM**, skipping the four NREM stages; REM's muscle-input inhibition causes the collapse.
- **Sleep paralysis:** brief inability to move/speak after awakening (frightening).
- **Hypnagogic hallucinations:** vivid, terrifying multisensory experiences at sleep onset. May help explain some **UFO/"alien abduction" reports** (60% of intense accounts associated with sleep episodes); sleep paralysis with anxiety disorders = **isolated sleep paralysis.**
- **Genetics:** cluster of genes on **chromosome 6**, possibly autosomal recessive (Dobermans, Labradors inherit it). Loss of **hypocretin (orexin) neurons** — peptides important for wakefulness.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Narcolepsy (Table 8.6):**
> A. Recurrent **irrepressible need to sleep / lapsing into sleep / napping**, ≥3×/week over the past **3 months.**
> B. ≥1 of: (1) **cataplexy** (bilateral loss of muscle tone with maintained consciousness, triggered by laughter/joking; in children/early onset, spontaneous grimaces/jaw-opening with tongue thrusting), a few times/month; (2) **hypocretin (CSF hypocretin-1) deficiency** (≤⅓ of healthy values or ≤110 pg/mL); (3) **PSG: REM sleep latency ≤15 min**, or MSLT mean sleep latency ≤8 min with ≥2 sleep-onset REM periods.
> **Specify severity:** Mild / Moderate / Severe (by cataplexy frequency, nap need, nocturnal sleep disruption).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5 incorporated the **biological marker (CSF hypocretin-1)** into the criteria — a notable shift toward objective diagnosis.

## Breathing-Related Sleep Disorders
- Sleepiness/disrupted sleep with a **physical (breathing) origin**; brief arousals all night, not rested after 8–9 hr. Bed partner notices **loud snoring** / interrupted breathing; signs include night sweating, morning headaches, daytime sleep attacks.
- **Three types** (different causes/treatment):
  - **Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea Syndrome:** airflow stops **despite continued respiratory effort** (narrow/blocked airway). Everyone in one OSA group snored; linked to **obesity, age, MDMA/ecstasy use.** Most common in **males, 10–20%** of population.
  - **Central Sleep Apnea:** **complete cessation of respiratory activity** (no effort), often with CNS disorders (cerebrovascular disease, head trauma, degenerative). Wake frequently but **little daytime sleepiness** → underdiagnosed.
  - **Sleep-Related Hypoventilation:** decreased airflow **without** complete pause → rising **CO₂.**

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea (Table 8.7):**
> Either (1) PSG ≥**5 obstructive apneas/hypopneas per hour** PLUS nocturnal breathing disturbance (snoring/gasping/pauses) OR daytime sleepiness/fatigue; OR (2) PSG ≥**15 obstructive apneas/hypopneas per hour** regardless of symptoms.
> **Severity** by apnea-hypopnea index (AHI): Mild <15, Moderate 15–30, Severe >30.
>
> **Central Sleep Apnea (Table 8.8):** PSG ≥**5 central apneas/hour**; not better explained by another disorder.
>
> **Sleep-Related Hypoventilation (Table 8.9):** PSG shows decreased respiration with **elevated CO₂.**

## Circadian Rhythm Sleep–Wake Disorder
- Disturbed sleep from the brain's inability to **synchronize sleep patterns with the day/night** (daylight-saving / jet-lag-like). **Circadian** = "about a day"; rhythms self-regulate but don't exactly match 24h.
- **Biological clock = suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** in the hypothalamus, reset daily by **light** signals from the retina.
- **Melatonin** ("Dracula hormone") from the **pineal gland** — production stimulated by **darkness**, suppressed by light; both light and melatonin set the clock.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Circadian Rhythm Sleep–Wake Disorders (Table 8.10):**
> A. Persistent/recurrent sleep disruption due to **circadian system alteration** or **misalignment** between endogenous rhythm and required schedule.
> B. Leads to **excessive sleepiness or insomnia (or both).**
> C. Clinically significant distress/impairment.
> **Specify:** Episodic / Persistent / Recurrent.

- **Subtypes:**
  - **Jet lag type:** crossing multiple time zones; **westward travel** worse; eastward/<3 zones tolerated better.
  - **Shift work type:** night/irregular schedules; also linked to cardiovascular disease, ulcers, breast cancer; ~⅔ of rotating-shift workers complain of poor sleep.
  - **Delayed sleep phase type:** "night owls" (sleep delayed/later).
  - **Advanced sleep phase type:** "early to bed, early to rise."
  - **Irregular sleep–wake type:** highly varied cycles.
  - **Non-24-hour sleep–wake type:** 25–26 hr cycle, ever-later bedtimes.

## Treatment of Sleep Disorders
### Medical Treatments
- **Insomnia:** **benzodiazepines** and related drugs. **Short-acting preferred** (triazolam/Halcion, zaleplon/Sonata, zolpidem/Ambien) over long-acting (flurazepam/Dalmane) to avoid morning grogginess. **Melatonin-system drug: ramelteon (Rozerem).**
  - **Drawbacks:** excessive sleepiness, **dependence/misuse**, only for **short-term (≤4 weeks)**, **rebound insomnia** on withdrawal; Ambien may increase **sleep-related eating disorder.** Not for chronic problems.
- **Hypersomnolence/narcolepsy:** stimulants — **methylphenidate (Ritalin), modafinil.**
- **Cataplexy:** **antidepressants** (suppress REM, not because patients are depressed) and **sodium oxybate.**
- **Breathing-related disorders:** weight loss (rarely successful long-term). **Gold standard for OSA = CPAP** (continuous positive airway pressure) mask; adherence limited by discomfort/claustrophobia (treated with desensitization, motivational interviewing). Severe cases → **surgery.** Novel: **didgeridoo** practice reduced mild apnea.

### Environmental Treatments
- **Phase delays (later bedtime) are easier than phase advances (earlier).** Stay up several hours later each night until bedtime reaches target; clockwise shift rotation (day→evening) easier.
- **Bright light / phototherapy:** **>2,000 lux** (vs ~250 indoor) resets circadian rhythms for jet lag / shift work.

### Psychological Treatments (for insomnia)
- **Stimulus control:** use the bedroom **only for sleep and sex** (often more effective than relaxation/sleep hygiene alone for adults).
- **Cognitive:** correct unrealistic sleep beliefs ("I must have 8 hours").
- **Guided imagery / progressive relaxation:** reduce physical tension.
- **Paradoxical intention:** instruct the person to **try to stay awake** (removes performance anxiety).
- **Graduated extinction (children):** check on the child after progressively longer intervals.
- **Sleep restriction:** limit time in bed to actual sleep time, lengthen as sleep improves.
- **Internet-delivered** treatment effective (gains held 6 months). **CBT may beat drugs** for insomnia in older adults. **Sonja's case:** sleep restriction + stimulus control + cognitive therapy → from 4–5 to 6–7 hr/night in ~3 weeks.

### Preventing Sleep Disorders — Sleep Hygiene
- Regular bedtime/wake time; **no caffeine 6 hr before bed**; limit alcohol/tobacco; go to bed only when sleepy; **get out of bed after 15 min** if not asleep; no vigorous exercise near bedtime but daytime exercise weekly; reduce noise/light; increase daytime bright light; avoid temperature extremes.
- Parent education about infant sleep (10-min guidance + booklet) reduced later sleep problems.

## Parasomnias and Their Treatment
> Not problems with sleep itself but **abnormal events during sleep / sleep-wake transition.** Key exam distinction is **which sleep stage** each occurs in.

### Nightmare Disorder (REM sleep)
- Disturbing dreams that **awaken** the sleeper (vs "bad dreams" that don't). College students average **30 bad dreams + 10 nightmares/year.** Childhood prevalence high (10–50% of children; 9–30% of adults regularly).
- Influenced by **genetics, trauma, medication**; linked to substance abuse, anxiety, borderline PD, schizophrenia-spectrum.
- **Treatment:** CBT (e.g., imagery rehearsal) and **prazosin** (especially trauma-related).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Nightmare Disorder (Table 8.12):**
> A. Repeated, extended, **extremely dysphoric, well-remembered dreams** (threats to survival/security), generally in the **second half** of the sleep period.
> B. On awakening, **rapidly becomes oriented and alert.**
> C. Significant distress/impairment.
> D–E. Not due to substance; coexisting disorders don't adequately explain it.
> **Severity:** Mild (<1/wk) / Moderate (≥1/wk, not nightly) / Severe (nightly).

### Disorder of Arousal — NREM sleep (sleep terrors, sleepwalking)
- **Sleep terrors:** begin with a **piercing scream**, intense fear, autonomic arousal (mydriasis, tachycardia, rapid breathing, sweating); occur in **NREM** (so **not from frightening dreams**). Child **cannot be easily comforted/awakened** and has **amnesia** for the episode (unlike a nightmare). ~**6% of children** (more boys), ~**2% of adults**; familial → possible genetic component.
  - **Treatment:** wait-and-see; **scheduled awakenings** (wake the child ~30 min before the typical episode time, then fade) nearly eliminated nightly terrors (Durand & Mindell).
- **Sleepwalking (somnambulism):** occurs in **NREM** (deep sleep, first few hours) → **not acting out a dream.** DSM-5 requires leaving the bed (less active = sitting up, picking at blanket). Hard to wake; if woken, no memory. Waking a sleepwalker is **not dangerous** (myth). Mainly **childhood** (15–30% have ≥1 episode; ~2% multiple); few continue past age 15. Factors: extreme fatigue, prior sleep deprivation, sedatives/hypnotics, stress. Occasionally linked to **violence (homicide/suicide)** used as a legal defense; genetic component (twin/family concordance).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — NREM Sleep Arousal Disorders (Table 8.11):**
> A. Recurrent **incomplete awakening**, usually in the **first third** of the major sleep episode, with either:
> (1) **Sleepwalking** (rising and walking; blank staring face; unresponsive; hard to awaken); or
> (2) **Sleep terrors** (abrupt terror arousal with panicky scream, autonomic arousal, unresponsive to comfort).
> C. Little/no dream imagery recalled.
> D. **Amnesia** for the episodes.
> E. Significant distress/impairment.
> F–G. Not due to substance; coexisting disorders don't explain it.

### REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD)
- **Acting out dreams** during REM (vocalization + complex/violent motor behavior) → can injure self or bed partner. Occurs **>90 min after sleep onset**, more in later sleep.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (Table 8.13):**
> A. Repeated arousals during sleep with **vocalization and/or complex motor behaviors.**
> B. Arise during **REM** (>90 min after onset, later in the night).
> C. On awakening, **completely awake, alert, not confused.**
> D. Either **REM sleep without atonia on PSG**, OR history of RBD + an established **synucleinopathy** (e.g., **Parkinson's disease, multiple system atrophy**).
> E–G. Distress/impairment (incl. injury); not due to substance; coexisting disorders don't explain it.

> **Clinical pearl:** RBD is a strong **prodromal marker for synucleinopathies** (Parkinson's, MSA, Lewy body dementia) — high-yield association.

### Other Parasomnias
- **Restless Legs Syndrome (Willis-Ekbom disease):** irresistible urge to move the legs from unpleasant ("creeping/tugging/pulling") sensations — a **new independent DSM-5 dyssomnia.**
- **Nocturnal eating syndrome (sleep-related eating disorder):** rise and eat **while still asleep** (different from waking **night eating syndrome** in the obesity section); ~6% of insomnia-referred patients; can be triggered by Ambien.
- **Sexsomnia:** acting out sexual behavior during sleep with **no memory**; rare; legal/relationship consequences.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** **Restless Legs Syndrome** and **REM Sleep Behavior Disorder** are independent disorders new to DSM-5 (carried into DSM-5-TR).

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11's separate sleep–wake chapter classifies parasomnias as **NREM-related** (confusional arousals, sleepwalking, sleep terrors, sleep-related eating disorder), **REM-related** (RBD, nightmare disorder, recurrent isolated sleep paralysis), and **other** — broadly parallel to DSM-5-TR's arousal/REM split.

## Quick Exam Recall
- **Anorexia = highest mortality** of any psych disorder; ~20% die, 20–30% of those by suicide (~50× general population).
- **Binge = (1) discrete period, large amount + (2) loss of control.** Bulimia compensates (purge), BED does not.
- **DSM-5 changes:** frequency now **1×/week × 3 months** (bulimia/BED); **amenorrhea dropped** (anorexia); **BED elevated to full disorder**; **subtypes dropped** in bulimia; **"primary insomnia" → Insomnia Disorder**; RLS and RBD added.
- **NREM parasomnias** (first third of night, amnesia): sleep terrors, sleepwalking. **REM parasomnias** (later, well-remembered/alert): nightmares, RBD.
- **Cataplexy → sudden REM intrusion;** **SCN + melatonin (pineal)** = circadian clock; **CPAP** = OSA gold standard.
- **CBT-E = transdiagnostic** treatment of choice for bulimia/BED; **FBT (Maudsley)** for adolescent anorexia; **weight restoration first** in anorexia but a poor long-term predictor alone.
