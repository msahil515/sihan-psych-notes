# Ch 11: Substance-Related, Addictive, and Impulse-Control Disorders

This chapter covers **substance-related and addictive disorders** (abuse of and dependence on drugs people take to alter how they think, feel, and behave), the newly added **gambling disorder**, and the **impulse-control disorders** (intermittent explosive disorder, kleptomania, pyromania). It moves from core terminology (use, intoxication, abuse, dependence, tolerance, withdrawal) through every drug class and its clinical picture, into an **integrative biopsychosocial etiology** centered on the brain's dopamine "pleasure pathway," and finishes with biological, psychosocial, and preventive treatments. It is one of the most disorder-dense and treatment-dense chapters in the book, costs U.S. citizens hundreds of billions of dollars per year, kills ~500,000 Americans annually, and is a perennial exam favourite.

> **DSM-5-TR note (chapter-wide):** This text is written to DSM-5 (2013). The major structural change it describes (collapsing DSM-IV's separate "abuse" and "dependence" diagnoses into a single dimensional **substance use disorder** rated mild/moderate/severe, dropping the legal-problems criterion, adding **craving**) was retained in DSM-5-TR (2022). DSM-5-TR made no substantive changes to the substance-use criteria sets below, but updated ICD-10-CM codes, refined remission/severity wording, and continues to list **caffeine use disorder** and **internet gaming disorder** in Section III (conditions for further study). Where a criterion below has no DSM-5-TR change, it is unchanged.

> **ICD-11 note (chapter-wide):** ICD-11 (effective 2022) keeps abuse and dependence conceptually distinct: it codes a milder **"harmful pattern of substance use"** separately from a stricter **"substance dependence"** (requiring ~2 of 3: impaired control, increasing priority of use, physiological features = tolerance/withdrawal). This is a key contrast with DSM-5's single merged spectrum. ICD-11 also formally recognizes **Gaming Disorder** as a diagnosable disorder (not merely "for further study" as in DSM-5), under "disorders due to addictive behaviours" alongside Gambling Disorder.

## Perspectives on Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders
- Almost **9%** of the general U.S. population use illegal drugs. So-called "safe" legal drugs (alcohol, nicotine, caffeine) cause more health problems and a greater mortality rate than **all illegal drugs combined**.
- **Substance** = chemical compounds ingested to alter mood or behavior. **Psychoactive substances** alter mood, behavior, or both (includes alcohol, nicotine, caffeine).
- **Polysubstance use** = using multiple substances (illustrated by the case of **Danny**, the chapter's anchor case: lifelong pattern, multiple dependencies, DUI vehicular manslaughter).

### Levels of Involvement (core terminology)
- **Substance use** = ingestion of psychoactive substances in **moderate amounts** that does NOT significantly interfere with social, educational, or occupational functioning.
- **Substance intoxication** = our physiological reaction to ingested substances (drunkenness / getting high); experienced as impaired judgment, mood changes, lowered motor ability. Depends on the drug, the dose, and the person's biological reaction.
- **Substance abuse** (DSM-5 defines by how significantly it interferes with the user's life): disrupts education, job, relationships, or puts you in physically dangerous situations (e.g., driving). Defining abuse by **amount** ingested is problematic.
- **Substance dependence** = usually called **addiction**; severe form of the disorder. Two definitions:
  - **Physiological dependence**: requires increasing amounts for the same effect (**tolerance**) and responds negatively physically when the substance is stopped (**withdrawal**). Tolerance and withdrawal are physiological reactions to the chemicals.
  - **Psychological dependence**: defined by **drug-seeking behaviors** themselves (repeated use, desperate need to ingest more, likelihood of resuming use after abstinence).
- **Tolerance** = needing markedly increased amounts to achieve intoxication / desired effect.
- **Withdrawal** = negative physical response when the substance is stopped. Can include chills, fever, diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, aches; alcohol withdrawal can produce **alcohol withdrawal delirium** (hallucinations, tremors). Not all substances are physiologically addicting (e.g., no severe physical withdrawal from LSD).
- **Dependence can exist WITHOUT abuse**: e.g., cancer patients on morphine build tolerance and go through withdrawal without abusing it.
- You CAN use some drugs (even heroin, cocaine, crack) occasionally without abusing them; we cannot predict ahead of time who will lose control.

### Diagnostic Issues
- Historically (early DSM) alcoholism/drug abuse were not separate disorders; classed as **"sociopathic personality disturbances"** (forerunner of antisocial PD), seen as moral weakness. **DSM-III (1980)** created a separate substance-abuse category.
- **DSM-5 substance-related disorders** = **11 symptoms** ranging from mild to severe. DSM-5 **removed** the legal-problems symptom and **added craving** (strong desire to use).
- **Severity (applies to all substance use disorders):**

> **DSM-5-TR severity specifier:** **Mild** = 2–3 of the 11 criteria; **Moderate** = 4–5 criteria; **Severe** = 6 or more criteria. (Threshold for diagnosis = at least 2 criteria within a 12-month period.)

- **Comorbidity:** ~three-quarters of people in addiction treatment have an additional psychiatric disorder — mood disorders (e.g., major depression) in **>40%**, anxiety disorders and PTSD in **>25%**.
- **6-week rule:** if schizophrenia-like or extreme anxiety symptoms appear during intoxication or **within 6 weeks** after withdrawal, they are NOT counted as a separate psychiatric disorder; symptoms persisting beyond 6 weeks (or present before drug use) may signal a separate disorder.

### Six Categories of Substances
- **Depressants** — behavioral sedation/relaxation: alcohol (ethyl alcohol), barbiturates (e.g., Seconal), benzodiazepines (Valium, Xanax).
- **Stimulants** — more active/alert, elevate mood: amphetamines, cocaine, nicotine, caffeine.
- **Opiates** — analgesia (reduce pain) + euphoria: heroin, opium, codeine, morphine.
- **Hallucinogens** — alter sensory perception, can produce delusions/paranoia/hallucinations: cannabis, LSD.
- **Other drugs of abuse** — inhalants (airplane glue), anabolic steroids, nitrous oxide.
- **Gambling disorder** — inability to resist the urge to gamble.

## Depressants
Decrease central nervous system activity; reduce physiological arousal and help us relax. **Most likely to produce physical dependence, tolerance, and withdrawal.**

### Alcohol-Related Disorders
**Clinical description / effects:**
- **Apparent stimulation is the initial effect**, although alcohol is a depressant — inhibitory brain centers are depressed first (well-being, reduced inhibitions, more outgoing). With continued drinking, more brain areas are depressed → impaired motor coordination, slowed reaction time, confusion, impaired judgment, impaired vision/hearing.
- **Neuroreceptor systems affected (alcohol is complex, hits many):**
  - **GABA** (inhibitory): alcohol reinforces movement of **chloride ions** into the cell → neurons fire less easily; antianxiety (anxiolytic) effect comes from this GABA interaction.
  - **Glutamate** (excitatory, learning/memory): alcohol's interaction here may cause **blackouts** (loss of memory for events during intoxication).
  - **Serotonin** (mood, sleep, eating): thought responsible for **alcohol cravings**.
- **Long-term/organic effects** (depend on genetic vulnerability, frequency, binge length, blood alcohol level, recovery time): liver disease (cirrhosis), pancreatitis, cardiovascular disorders, brain damage.
  - **Withdrawal:** hand tremors → within hours nausea/vomiting, anxiety, transient hallucinations, agitation, insomnia → at most extreme, **withdrawal delirium / delirium tremens (DTs)** = frightening hallucinations + body tremors (reducible with medical treatment).
  - Two organic brain syndromes from long-term heavy use: **(1) Dementia** (neurocognitive disorder; general loss of intellectual abilities from neurotoxicity / "poisoning of the brain," does NOT reverse); **(2) Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome** = confusion, loss of muscle coordination, unintelligible speech, caused by **thiamine deficiency** (thiamine metabolized poorly by heavy drinkers).
  - **Protective note:** mild–moderate intake (especially wine) may protect against age-related cognitive decline.
- **Fetal alcohol syndrome (FAS):** combination of problems in a child whose mother drank during pregnancy — fetal growth retardation, cognitive deficits, behavior problems, learning difficulties, characteristic facial features. Metabolized via **alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH)** (3 forms: beta-1, -2, -3); **beta-3 ADH** may be prevalent in FAS children and is found most often in African Americans → racial susceptibility may interact with maternal drinking.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Alcohol Use Disorder (Table 11.1):** A problematic pattern of alcohol use causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the following in 12 months:
> 1. Taken in larger amounts/longer than intended
> 2. Persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down/control use
> 3. Great deal of time spent obtaining, using, or recovering from alcohol
> 4. **Craving** / strong desire or urge to use
> 5. Recurrent use → failure to fulfill major role obligations (work/school/home)
> 6. Continued use despite persistent social/interpersonal problems caused by it
> 7. Important social/occupational/recreational activities given up or reduced
> 8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations
> 9. Continued use despite knowing it causes a persistent physical/psychological problem
> 10. **Tolerance** (need more for effect, or diminished effect at same amount)
> 11. **Withdrawal** (characteristic syndrome, or drinking/benzodiazepine taken to relieve it)
> Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

**Statistics on use and abuse:**
- ~Half of all Americans over age 12 are current drinkers. Whites highest frequency (**56.8%**); Asians lowest (**40.0%**).
- **Binge drinking** = 5+ drinks on same occasion; ~58 million Americans (**22.6%**) report it in past month. Asians lowest (11.6%), American Indians highest (24.3%). **Heavy use** = binge drinking 5+ days/month.
- College survey: A-students ≤3 drinks/week; D/F students averaged **11 drinks/week**.
- >3 million U.S. adults alcohol dependent. International prevalence varies hugely: Peru ~35%, South Korea ~22%, Taipei ~3.5%, Shanghai ~0.45%.

**Progression:**
- ~**20%** of severe alcohol dependence cases show **spontaneous remission** (stop on their own).
- **Jellinek's four-stage model** (prealcoholic → prodromal → crucial → chronic) was based on a **faulty study** (only 98 of ~1,600 AA surveys returned, retrospective); attempts to confirm the staged progression failed.
- Course of **dependence** may be progressive for most; course of **abuse** is more variable. Early drinking (ages 11–14) predicts later alcohol-related disorders.
- **Low sensitivity to sedative effects** (less slurred speech/staggering) predicts later abuse → concern about mixing **caffeinated energy drinks with alcohol** (masks sedation).
- Alcohol does NOT directly cause aggression, but increases likelihood of impulsive acts and impairs ability to weigh consequences (laboratory studies show increased aggression).

### Sedative-, Hypnotic-, or Anxiolytic-Related Disorders
- **Sedative** (calming), **hypnotic** (sleep-inducing), **anxiolytic** (anxiety-reducing).
- **Barbiturates** (Amytal, Seconal, Nembutal): family of sedatives first synthesized in Germany **1882**; widely prescribed 1930s–40s; among most abused drugs by 1950s. Low doses relax muscles / mild well-being; high doses → slurred speech, walking/concentration problems; **extremely high doses relax diaphragm → death by suffocation** (common suicide means).
- **Benzodiazepines** (Valium, Xanax, Ativan): since 1960s, primarily to reduce anxiety; also muscle relaxants and anticonvulsants. Considered **much safer than barbiturates** (less abuse/dependence risk). ~74 million prescriptions/year in U.S. **Rohypnol** ("roofies," "forget-me-pill") used in **date rape** (alcohol-like effect, no telltale odor).
- Affect the **GABA** system (slightly different mechanism than alcohol) → **synergistic effects** when combined with alcohol or each other (can reach dangerous/fatal levels; theory behind Marilyn Monroe's and Heath Ledger's deaths).
- Withdrawal resembles alcohol withdrawal (anxiety, insomnia, tremors, delirium).
- Statistics: barbiturate use declined, benzodiazepine use increased since 1960. <1% of treatment-seekers present benzodiazepine problems; those who do tend to be **female, Caucasian, over age 35**.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Sedative-, Hypnotic-, or Anxiolytic Use Disorder (Table 11.2):** Same 11-criterion structure as alcohol. **Important caveat:** the **tolerance** and **withdrawal** criteria are **NOT counted** for individuals taking these drugs **under appropriate medical supervision**. Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

## Stimulants
Most commonly consumed psychoactive drugs in the U.S. Make you more alert and energetic. Include caffeine, nicotine, amphetamines, cocaine.

### Stimulant-Related Disorders: Amphetamines
- Low doses → elation, vigor, reduced fatigue ("up"); then **"crash"** (depressed/tired). First synthesized **1887**; used for asthma and as nasal decongestant; reduce appetite (weight loss). Prescribed for **narcolepsy** and ADHD (**Ritalin, Adderall**) — also abused for psychostimulant effects (31% of fourth-year college students had used illegal prescription stimulants, usually to study).
- **Intoxication symptoms:** euphoria or **affective blunting**, changes in sociability, interpersonal sensitivity, anxiety, tension, anger, stereotyped behaviors, impaired judgment; physiological — heart rate/BP changes, perspiration/chills, nausea/vomiting, weight loss, muscular weakness, respiratory depression, chest pain, seizures, coma. Severe intoxication/overdose → hallucinations, panic, agitation, **paranoid delusions**.
- **Tolerance builds quickly** (doubly dangerous). Withdrawal: apathy, prolonged sleep, irritability, depression.
- **Designer/club amphetamines:** **MDMA (Ecstasy)** — first synthesized 1912 as appetite suppressant; recreational use rose late 1980s; #1 club drug to ERs after methamphetamine; passed LSD in frequency; repeated use can cause **lasting memory problems**. **Methamphetamine** ("crystal meth," "ice") — purified/crystallized, smoked, marked aggressive tendencies, stays in system longer than cocaine.
- **Mechanism:** enhance activity of **norepinephrine and dopamine** (promote release + block reuptake). Too much dopamine/norepinephrine → hallucinations and delusions (links to schizophrenia theories, Ch 13).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Stimulant Use Disorder (Table 11.3):** A pattern of amphetamine-type substance, cocaine, or other stimulant use causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months. **Caveat:** tolerance and withdrawal are **NOT counted** for those taking stimulant medication appropriately under medical supervision (e.g., ADHD, narcolepsy). Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

### Cocaine
- Derived from leaves of the **coca plant** (South American). Replaced amphetamines as stimulant of choice in the **1970s**. Freud's essay "On Coca" (1885) praised it. Coca-Cola contained ~60 mg cocaine per 8-oz serving until **1903**.
- **Clinical description:** small amounts → alertness, euphoria, increased BP/pulse, insomnia, loss of appetite. Effects short-lived (<1 hour). **Cocaine-induced paranoia** common (two-thirds or more of users). Makes heart beat rapidly/irregularly → can be fatal.
- **Crack babies:** more irritable, high-pitched crying; originally feared permanent brain damage but later research suggests effects less dramatic; possible decreased birth weight, decreased head circumference, later behavior problems (confounded by polydrug use + disrupted homes).
- **Statistics:** ~5% of adults worldwide have used cocaine; >1.9 million Americans/year; ~17% of cocaine users have also used **crack** (smoked crystallized form). White males ~third of cocaine-related ER admissions.
- **Mechanism:** the "up" comes primarily from cocaine **blocking reuptake of dopamine** → dopamine accumulates in the synapse → repeated stimulation of the next neuron in the **"pleasure pathway."**
- **The "cocaine fooled us" story:** as late as 1980 the *Comprehensive Textbook of Psychiatry* called it non-addictive. Dependence doesn't resemble other drugs early on — just a growing inability to resist more. **Withdrawal differs:** not tremors/nausea but pronounced **apathy and boredom** → vicious cycle (abuse → apathy → resume). Atypical withdrawal misled people into thinking cocaine was non-addictive; we now know tolerance/withdrawal patterns are comparable to other drugs.

### Tobacco-Related Disorders
- **Nicotine** (named after Jean Nicot) is a psychoactive substance producing dependence, tolerance, withdrawal comparable to other drugs. ~**20%** of Americans smoke (down from **42.4%** in 1965).
- **DSM-5 does NOT describe an intoxication pattern** for tobacco; lists **withdrawal** instead: depressed mood, insomnia, irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, restlessness, increased appetite/weight gain.
- Small doses stimulate CNS, relieve stress, improve mood; can also cause high BP, heart disease, cancer. High doses → blurred vision, confusion, convulsions, death.
- Reaches the brain only **7–19 seconds** after inhaling; stimulates **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs)** in midbrain reticular formation and limbic system (the dopamine pleasure pathway). Smokers dose to keep nicotine ~**35 ng/ml**.
- **Relapse rates** for nicotine are equivalent to those for alcohol and heroin (Figure 11.5).
- **Bi-directional relationship with negative affect:** depression increases risk of nicotine dependence, and nicotine dependence increases risk of depression; shared genetic vulnerability + life stress.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Tobacco Use Disorder (Table 11.4):** Problematic pattern of tobacco use causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months (including craving, tolerance, withdrawal). Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

### Caffeine-Related Disorders
- Most common psychoactive substance: used by ~**90%** of Americans. Called the **"gentle stimulant."** Found in tea, coffee, colas, cocoa; high levels in "energy drinks" (banned in some European countries — France, Denmark, Norway).
- Small doses elevate mood, decrease fatigue; large doses → jittery, insomnia. Takes ~**6 hours** to leave the body.
- Regular use → **tolerance and dependence**; **withdrawal** = headaches, drowsiness, unpleasant mood.
- **Mechanism:** involves the neuromodulator **adenosine** (caffeine blocks adenosine reuptake) and, to a lesser extent, dopamine.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** **Caffeine Use Disorder** remains a **condition for further study** (Section III), NOT an official diagnosis. **Caffeine Intoxication** and **Caffeine Withdrawal** ARE official diagnoses.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Caffeine Intoxication (Table 11.5):** **A.** Recent consumption (typically a high dose >250 mg). **B.** ≥5 of: restlessness, nervousness, excitement, insomnia, flushed face, diuresis, GI disturbance, muscle twitching, rambling flow of thought/speech, tachycardia/arrhythmia, periods of inexhaustibility, psychomotor agitation. **C.** Causes clinically significant distress/impairment. **D.** Not attributable to another medical/mental disorder.

## Opioids
- **Opiate** = the natural chemicals in the opium poppy with a narcotic effect (relieve pain, induce sleep). **Opioids** = broader family: natural opiates + synthetic variations (**heroin, methadone, hydrocodone, oxycodone**) + naturally-occurring brain substances (**enkephalins, beta-endorphins, dynorphins**). Use of opium as medicine dates back >3,500 years.
- Effects: euphoria, drowsiness, slowed breathing; high doses → **death if respiration completely depressed**. Opiates are **analgesics** (relieve pain; morphine given around surgery).
- **Withdrawal** (unpleasant but less distressing than barbiturate/alcohol withdrawal): begins **6–12 hours** after reducing intake — excessive yawning, nausea/vomiting, chills, muscle aches, diarrhea, insomnia; persists 1–3 days; complete in ~1 week.
- **Heroin** = most commonly abused opiate; ~half a million Americans (double the 2007 estimate). Illicit prescription-opioid use rising (12.3% of high school seniors used opioids nonmedically). IV injection → increased risk of **HIV/AIDS**.
- Bleak prognosis: 33-year follow-up of >80 addicts — **22% had died** (~twice the national rate); >half from overdose. Good news: 80% of survivors no longer using; remaining 20% on methadone.
- **Mechanism:** the "rush" comes from activating the body's natural opioid system (enkephalins, endorphins).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Opioid Use Disorder (Table 11.6):** Problematic pattern causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months. **Caveat:** tolerance and withdrawal are **NOT counted** for those taking opioids appropriately under medical supervision. Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

## Cannabis-Related Disorders
- **Cannabis (marijuana)** = drug of choice in the 1960s–early 70s; still the most routinely used illegal substance (**5–15%** of westerners report regular use). **Marijuana** = dried parts of the *Cannabis sativa* (hemp) plant; **hashish** = dried resin of the female plant's leaves.
- **Reactions:** mood swings, things seem funny, dreamlike state, heightened sensory experiences (vivid colors, music). Produces highly variable reactions — some report no reaction to first use; users can "turn off" the high if motivated. Small doses → well-being; larger doses → paranoia, hallucinations, dizziness.
- High-school marijuana smokers get lower grades / less likely to graduate (causation unclear). Long-term use may impair memory, concentration, relationships, employment.
- **Synthetic marijuana** ("K2," "Spice," sold as "herbal incense") can be very harmful (hallucinations, seizures, heart rhythm problems) and is sometimes legally purchasable.
- **Tolerance evidence is contradictory:** chronic users report tolerance to the euphoric high, but **"reverse tolerance"** is also reported (more pleasure after repeated use). **Major withdrawal usually does NOT occur** — chronic users report irritability, restlessness, appetite loss, nausea, sleep difficulty.
- **Medicinal use:** in Canada, cannabis products available medically — herbal extract **Sativex** (nasal spray), **dronabinol (Marinol)**, **nabilone (Cesamet)**, smoked herbal form. Prescribed for chemo-induced nausea/vomiting, HIV-associated anorexia, MS neuropathic pain, cancer pain. Smoke may contain as many carcinogens as tobacco (though occasional use may not harm lung function long-term).
- **Active chemicals:** >80 **cannabinoids**; the most common is **THC (tetrahydrocannabinol)**. The brain makes its own version of THC — **anandamide** (from Sanskrit *ananda* = "bliss"); other endogenous cannabinoids include **2-AG**, noladin ether, virodhamine, N-arachidonoyldopamine.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Cannabis Use Disorder (Table 11.7):** Problematic pattern causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months (including craving, tolerance, withdrawal — cannabis withdrawal IS recognized). Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

## Hallucinogen-Related Disorders
- Change how the user perceives the world (distort sight, sound, feelings, smell).
- **LSD (d-lysergic acid diethylamide)**, "acid" = most common hallucinogen. Synthetic; derived from **ergot** (a grain fungus). Albert **Hoffmann** accidentally discovered LSD's effects on his bicycle ride home in **April 1943** (the first recorded "trip"). Medieval **ergotism** outbreaks (gangrene; convulsions/delirium/hallucinations) came from infected grain. Popularized recreationally in the 1960s; Timothy **Leary** ("turn on, tune in, drop out").
- **Other hallucinogens (many naturally occurring):** **psilocybin** (mushrooms), **lysergic acid amide** (morning glory seeds), **DMT** (Virola tree bark), **mescaline** (peyote cactus). **Phencyclidine (PCP)** — snorted/smoked/injected, causes impulsivity and aggressiveness.
- **Intoxication criteria** (similar to cannabis): perceptual changes — subjective intensification of perceptions, depersonalization, hallucinations; physical — pupillary dilation, rapid heartbeat, sweating, blurred vision.
- Johns Hopkins **placebo-controlled psilocybin study** (vs Ritalin control): perceptual + mood changes, increased reports of **mystical/spiritual experiences** rated as significant 2 months later.
- **Tolerance** develops quickly to LSD, psilocybin, mescaline (lose effectiveness if taken repeatedly over days; sensitivity returns after ~1 week). **No withdrawal symptoms** reported for most hallucinogens. Concerns: possible psychotic reactions, **"bad trips"** (frightening episodes, paranoia; usually "talked down" by supportive people).
- **Mechanism:** most resemble neurotransmitters — LSD/psilocybin/lysergic acid amide/DMT chemically similar to **serotonin**; **mescaline** resembles **norepinephrine**; some resemble acetylcholine. Exact mechanism of hallucinations remains unknown.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Other Hallucinogen Use Disorder (Table 11.8):** Problematic pattern of hallucinogen (other than phencyclidine) use causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months. **Important:** the **withdrawal** criterion does NOT apply — withdrawal symptoms are not established for hallucinogens. (PCP has its own separate Phencyclidine Use Disorder.) Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

## Other Drugs of Abuse
- **Inhalants** — found in volatile solvents breathed directly into the lungs: spray paint, hair spray, paint thinner, gasoline, amyl nitrate, **nitrous oxide ("laughing gas")**, nail polish remover, markers, airplane glue, dry-cleaning fluid. Typical user: male, Caucasian, rural/small town, higher anxiety/depression, impulsive/fearless temperament. Rapidly absorbed through lungs; high resembles alcohol intoxication (dizziness, slurred speech, incoordination, euphoria, lethargy). Builds tolerance; withdrawal (2–5 days) = sleep disturbance, tremors, irritability, nausea. Long-term use damages bone marrow, kidneys, liver, brain; startling a user can cause **"sudden sniffing death"** (cardiac event).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Inhalant Use Disorder (Table 11.9):** Problematic pattern of hydrocarbon-based inhalant use causing clinically significant impairment/distress, ≥2 of the standard 11 criteria in 12 months. Severity: Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.

- **Anabolic–androgenic steroids** ("roids," "juice") — derived from/synthesized from **testosterone**; legitimate uses (asthma, anemia, breast cancer, inadequate sexual development) but illicitly used to **increase muscle bulk**. ~2–6% of males use illegally. Taken orally/injection; "**cycling**" (on/off schedules) and "**stacking**" (combining types). **Differs from other drugs: no desirable high** — used to enhance performance/body size; dependence = desire to maintain gains, not re-experience a state. Long-term: mood disturbances common (depression, anxiety, panic attacks).
- **Dissociative anesthetics / designer drugs** — drowsiness, pain relief, feeling out of one's body; originally developed by pharmaceutical companies. Includes MDMA/Ecstasy (a stimulant), **MDEA ("Eve"), BDMPEA ("Nexus")**; **ketamine** ("K," "Special K," "Cat Valium") = dissociative anesthetic, detachment + reduced pain awareness; **GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyrate, "liquid Ecstasy")** = CNS depressant, low doses relaxation, high doses/with alcohol → seizures, severe respiratory depression, coma. All can cause tolerance/dependence.

## Causes of Substance-Related Disorders

### Biological Dimensions
- **Familial/genetic:** twin, family, adoption studies show certain people are **genetically vulnerable**. Twin studies of smoking show moderate genetic influence. Most genetic data come from **alcoholism** research. Genetic risk factors appear to **cut across all mood-altering drugs** (common genetic influence on use of cannabis, cocaine, hallucinogens, sedatives, stimulants, opiates). **No single reliable "addiction gene"** yet identified → the field of **functional genomics** asks how genes function in addiction.
  - Genes affect how people experience drugs and which treatments work: e.g., **naltrexone** may be most effective in people with a particular variant of the **OPRM1** opioid-receptor gene.
- **Neurobiological — the "pleasure pathway":** the brain has a natural **reward center**; all abused substances activate it (same as food/sex). Discovered >50 years ago by **James Olds** (rat brain electrical-stimulation studies). Believed to include the **dopaminergic system** beginning in the **midbrain ventral tegmental area (VTA)** → **nucleus accumbens** → frontal cortex.
  - Amphetamines/cocaine act **directly** on dopamine. **Opiates inhibit GABA** (the "brain police"/superego that normally restrains dopamine neurons) → less inhibition of dopamine → more dopamine in the pathway. Nicotine and alcohol also stimulate the reward center.
  - Drugs work via both **positive reinforcement** (pleasure) and **negative reinforcement** (removing pain/illness/anxiety; like aspirin). Anxiolytic effect involves the **septal–hippocampal system** (GABA-rich).
- **Individual response differences:** sons of alcoholics may be **more sensitive to alcohol when first ingested**, then **less sensitive later** → appreciate the highs, miss the lows → ideal candidates for continued drinking. Less-sensitive men drank more heavily over a 10-year follow-up.
- **P300 amplitude:** brain-wave spike ~300 ms after a tone; **lower** among those with a family history of alcoholism — but also lower in opioid users, schizophrenia, depression, so it may be a nonspecific marker rather than alcoholism-specific.

### Psychological Dimensions
- **Positive reinforcement:** drugs are pleasurable (animals will self-inject cocaine, amphetamines, opiates, sedatives, alcohol). Social contexts also reinforce use (pairing money with pill-taking made people switch from placebo to Valium).
- **Negative reinforcement:** drugs escape unpleasantness — pain (opiates), stress (alcohol), panic/anxiety (benzodiazepines). Explored as **tension reduction, negative affect, self-medication**. Iraq combat veterans exposed to violence/trauma had increased risk-taking and alcohol use; sexual-abuse survivors more likely to abuse alcohol.
- **Opponent-process theory (Solomon & Corbit):** an increase in positive feelings is followed by an increase in negative feelings (and vice versa); strengthened by use, weakened by disuse. Explains why tolerance grows AND why the crash worsens → motivation shifts from chasing the high to relieving the crash ("hair of the dog").
- **Self-medication:** e.g., cocaine addicts with ADHD reduced cocaine use when treated with methylphenidate (Ritalin) — they had been using cocaine to focus.

### Cognitive Dimensions
- **Expectancy effect:** what you expect to experience shapes your reaction — someone expecting disinhibition acts disinhibited whether given alcohol or a placebo. **Expectancies develop before use** (from parents, peers, advertising, media). Positive expectancies predicted later increased alcohol/marijuana use in adolescents.
- **Cravings:** powerful urges after stopping; DSM-5 includes craving as a criterion. Triggered by drug availability, drug-associated cues (sitting in a bar), specific moods (depression), or a small dose. **Virtual reality** cue studies significantly increased alcohol cravings.

### Social Dimensions
- Exposure is a necessary prerequisite. **Media exposure (e.g., cigarette advertising) may be more influential than peer pressure.** Predictors of early drinking: when best friends started, family alcohol-dependence risk, child behavior problems.
- **Drug-addicted parents monitor children less** → kids develop friendships with drug-using peers → self-perpetuating pattern beyond genetics.
- **Two societal views of dependence:**
  - **Moral weakness model** = drug use is a failure of self-control (psychosocial perspective); leads to **punishment**.
  - **Disease model** = dependence is an underlying physiological disorder (biological perspective; AA's view — an incurable disease over which the addict has no control); leads to **treatment**.
  - Neither alone does justice; a comprehensive biopsychosocial view is needed. Telling people the disorder is "out of their control" can be counterproductive.

### Cultural Dimensions
- **Acculturation** can be a source of strength or stress. Cultural factors: **machismo** (male dominance, Latin), **marianismo** (Latin motherly nurturance/Virgin Mary identification), spirituality, **tiu lien** ("loss of face" among Asians → shame).
- Each culture has its own acceptable/prohibited substances and norms affecting rates.
- **Gene–environment natural experiment:** people of Asian descent more likely to carry the **ALDH2 gene** → severe **"flushing"** (face reddening/burning) after alcohol, historically protective (low drinking). When Korean cultural norms shifted toward expected heavy social drinking (1979–1992), alcohol abuse rose — the **protective value of ALDH2 was diminished** by cultural change.
- Same consumption → different outcomes across cultures (Poland/Finland high conflict/arrests vs Netherlands at similar consumption) partly via differing **expectancies**.

### An Integrative Model
- **Access/exposure** (media, parents, peers, lack of supervision) is necessary but not sufficient; social/cultural expectations gate **use**.
- Path from **use → abuse/dependence** is more complex: psychosocial **stressors** increase risk; **genetic** influences (greater sensitivity, faster metabolism → tolerate higher/more dangerous levels); other psychiatric conditions (**antisocial personality disorder** with lowered arousal; mood/anxiety disorders → self-medication).
- **Neuroplasticity:** continued use reorganizes the brain → increases the drive to obtain the drug and decreases desire for nondrug experiences → contributes to continued use and relapse.
- Abuse/dependence cannot be predicted from any single factor; many genetically/environmentally at-risk people never become abusers. Conditioning (drug-associated settings cue urges) plus tolerance create dependence.

## Treatment of Substance-Related Disorders
- First step often requires the user to **admit the problem**; personal motivation is important but not necessarily essential. Goal is usually **abstinence** (sometimes controlled use, sometimes preventing exposure). Fewer than **25%** of people with significant substance problems seek treatment → push for **routine screening** in doctor's offices, ERs, college clinics.
- **NIDA's 13 principles of effective treatment** (Table 11.1) include: no single treatment fits all; readily available; address multiple needs; continually reassessed; **adequate duration (≥3 months)**; counseling/behavioral therapies are critical; medications important especially with counseling; treat coexisting mental disorders in an integrated way; **detox alone does little**; treatment need NOT be voluntary to be effective; monitor for use during treatment; assess for HIV/hepatitis/TB; recovery is long-term, often needs multiple episodes.

### Biological Treatments
- **Agonist substitution:** give a safe drug with similar chemical makeup. **Methadone** = opiate agonist used as heroin substitute (synthetic narcotic from WWII Germany; no quick high but same analgesic/sedative effects initially). Heroin and methadone are **cross-tolerant** (same receptors) → addict may become **dependent on methadone instead** (a "best case" trade of addictions). **Buprenorphine (Subutex)** = newer agonist, blocks opiate effects, better compliance. **Nicotine replacement** (gum, patch, inhaler, nasal spray) without carcinogens, tapered off; ~**20–25% 6-month abstinence**. **Bupropion (Zyban/Wellbutrin)** curbs cravings without being a nicotine agonist.
- **Antagonist treatments:** block/counteract drug effects. **Naltrexone** (most prescribed opiate antagonist) — limited success unless in a structured program; causes immediate withdrawal in opioid-dependent users (must be withdrawal-free first; user must be highly motivated). **Acamprosate (Campral)** decreases alcohol cravings (works best with motivated people in psychosocial treatment).
- **Aversive treatment:** make ingestion unpleasant. **Disulfiram (Antabuse)** blocks breakdown of **acetaldehyde** → buildup → nausea, vomiting, elevated heart rate/respiration if alcohol is consumed. Major problem: **noncompliance** (skip a few days → resume drinking). **Silver nitrate** lozenges/gum (bad taste with smoking) — not very effective.
- **Other:** **Clonidine** (a hypertension drug) for opiate withdrawal; sedatives tapered off to avoid cardiac arrest/seizures; benzodiazepines to ease alcohol withdrawal.

### Psychosocial Treatments
- No biological treatment alone works for most people; need social support / therapeutic intervention.
- **Inpatient facilities:** first established 1935 (federal narcotic "farm," Lexington, KY). Expensive; research suggests **no difference** in outcomes between intensive residential and quality outpatient care (outpatient can cost ~90% less).
- **Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) & variations:** most popular model; the **Twelve Steps** program founded **1935** by **Bill W. (Wilson)** and **Dr. Bob (Smith)**. Built on the **disease model** + reliance on a **higher power** + group social support; independent from medical community; reduces stigma. ~106,000 groups in 100+ countries; 9% of U.S. adults have attended a meeting. Hard to study (anonymous); regular committed participants more likely to have positive outcomes — effective for **highly motivated** people. Alternatives for those wanting non-abstinence/non-spiritual support: **Rational Recovery, Moderation Management, Women for Sobriety, SMART Recovery**.
- **Controlled use:** controversial; challenges AA's total-abstinence tenet. **Sobell & Sobell (1978)** classic study taught 40 male alcoholics moderation vs abstinence; controlled-drinking group functioned well 85% of time vs 42% for abstinence at 2 years. **Pendery et al. (1982, *Science*)** 10-year reanalysis claimed only 1 of 20 maintained controlled drinking and made headlines (60 Minutes), but was flawed (**no abstinence-group 10-year data**). Controversy still chills controlled drinking in the U.S.; widely accepted in the UK; neither approach succeeds for **70–80%** long-term.
- **Component treatments:**
  - **Aversion therapy** (conditioning): pair use with shock or nausea.
  - **Covert sensitization (Cautela, 1966):** imagine unpleasant scenes (snorting cocaine → becoming violently ill).
  - **Contingency management:** clinician + client pick target behaviors and reinforcers (money, retail items, **cash vouchers up to ~$2,000** for cocaine-negative urine) → greater abstinence than traditional counseling.
  - **Community reinforcement approach:** addresses multiple life facets — recruit a non-using spouse/friend/relative for relationship therapy; teach identifying antecedents/consequences; provide employment/education/finance help; new recreational options. Strong empirical support for alcohol and cocaine.
  - **Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET) (Miller & Rollnick, 2002):** empathetic, optimistic counseling tied to the client's **core values** to address lack of awareness and unwillingness to change. *(Note for Sihan: this is the same MET concept your exam acronym shares — relevant cross-reference.)*
  - **Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT):** addresses reactions to cues, thoughts/behaviors to resist use, and **relapse**. **Marlatt & Gordon's relapse prevention model** sees relapse as a failure of coping skills; identify high-risk situations, develop coping strategies, treat lapses as recoverable (not inevitable spirals). Particularly effective for alcohol.

### Prevention
- Shift from education-based approaches to wide-ranging ones (law changes, community interventions). **DARE** (fear-based "no drug use") — evaluations suggest it may **not** have intended effects. More comprehensive skills-training programs (resisting social/environmental pressures) can work; community-based interventions reduced binge drinking and alcohol-related injuries (responsible beverage service, limiting underage access, drink-driving enforcement).
- **Two new paths:** "**vice vaccines**" using the immune system to fight drugs (heroin, nicotine) so the drug loses pleasurable effects; the **Montana Meth Project** (shocking ads) changed attitudes in 12–17-year-olds. Most powerful long-term lever may be **cultural change** (shift from "if it feels good, do it" to "just say no"; declining social acceptability of smoking).

## Gambling Disorder
- Long history (dice in Egyptian tombs); growing, often legal entertainment. Lifetime prevalence ~**1.9%** of adult Americans. Among pathological gamblers: 14% lost ≥1 job, 19% declared bankruptcy, 32% arrested, 21% incarcerated.
- **Why it is now an "Addictive Disorder" in DSM-5:** same pattern of urges, **need to gamble increasing amounts** (like tolerance), and **"withdrawal" symptoms** (restlessness, irritability) when trying to stop. In **DSM-IV-TR** it was an **impulse-control disorder**; **DSM-5 moved it to "Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders."**
- **Etiology:** decreased activity in impulse-regulation brain regions when watching gambling (fMRI); abnormalities in **dopamine** (pleasure) and **serotonin** (impulsivity) systems.
- **Treatment:** difficult — denial, impulsivity, continuing optimism ("one big win will cover my losses"). Cravings resemble substance dependence. **Gamblers Anonymous** (parallel 12-step) — but **70–90% drop out** and the desire to quit must be present. CBT (financial limits, alternative activities, relapse prevention, imaginal desensitization) shows more optimistic preliminary outcomes.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Gambling Disorder (Table 11.10):** Persistent/recurrent problematic gambling causing clinically significant impairment/distress, **≥4 (not 2)** of the following in 12 months:
> 1. Needs to gamble with increasing amounts of money for desired excitement
> 2. Restless/irritable when attempting to cut down or stop
> 3. Repeated unsuccessful efforts to control/cut back/stop
> 4. Preoccupied with gambling (reliving past wins, planning next venture, getting money to gamble)
> 5. Often gambles when distressed (helpless, guilty, anxious, depressed)
> 6. After losing, often returns to get even — **"chasing" losses**
> 7. Lies to conceal extent of involvement
> 8. Jeopardized/lost a significant relationship, job, or opportunity
> 9. Relies on others for money to relieve desperate financial situations
> **B.** Not better explained by a manic episode.
> Severity: **Mild 4–5 / Moderate 6–7 / Severe 8–9** criteria.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** **Internet Gaming Disorder** remains a **condition for further study** in DSM-5/DSM-5-TR (some develop tolerance/withdrawal-like patterns with online games).
> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 elevates **Gaming Disorder** to a **full, diagnosable disorder** under "disorders due to addictive behaviours" (online or offline), distinct from DSM's "for further study" status — a current-classification difference Sihan should flag.

## Impulse-Control Disorders
- Start with an **irresistible impulse** that is ultimately harmful: increasing **tension** before the act, sometimes **pleasurable anticipation**, then **gratification/relief** during the act.
- **DSM-5 includes three** here: **intermittent explosive disorder, kleptomania, pyromania.**
- **Reclassifications:** **gambling disorder** moved OUT (now an addictive disorder); **trichotillomania (hair-pulling)** moved OUT to the **obsessive-compulsive and related disorders** (Ch 5).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR houses these in the chapter **"Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorders"** (alongside oppositional defiant disorder and conduct disorder). The criteria for the three disorders below are unchanged from DSM-5.

### Intermittent Explosive Disorder
- Episodes of acting on **aggressive impulses** → serious assaults or destruction of property. Diagnosed only after ruling out other disorders (antisocial PD, borderline PD, psychotic disorder, Alzheimer's) and substance use. Lifetime prevalence ~**5.4%**.
- **Controversial** — fear it could be used as an **insanity legal defense** for violent crimes.
- **Etiology:** serotonin, norepinephrine, testosterone × psychosocial influences (stress, disrupted family, parenting). **Treatment:** CBT (identify/avoid aggression "triggers") and drug-treatment-modeled approaches; few controlled studies.

### Kleptomania
- Recurrent failure to resist urges to **steal** things not needed for personal use or monetary value (illustrated by **Winona Ryder**, 2002, ~$5,500 from Saks). Rare, poorly studied (stigma).
- **Pattern:** tension before stealing → pleasure/relief during the theft. High impulsivity scores; some report **amnesia** for the act. Brain imaging shows damage in **inferior frontal regions** (poor decision making).
- High **comorbidity with mood disorders** (and to a lesser extent substance abuse). Some call it an **"antidepressant" behavior** (relieving unpleasant feelings through stealing). Treatment: behavioral interventions or antidepressants; **naltrexone** (opioid antagonist) somewhat reduced the urge to steal.

### Pyromania
- Irresistible urge to **set fires**; same pattern (tension/arousal before, gratification/relief while the fire burns) + **preoccupation with fire and fire-related equipment**. Rare — diagnosed in only ~**3%** of arsonists (most arsonists set fires for money or revenge, not urge satisfaction). Research limited; examines family history of fire-setting and comorbid impulse disorders (antisocial PD, alcoholism). **Treatment:** CBT — identify signals that initiate urges, teach coping strategies to resist starting fires.

## DSM Controversies & Exam-Ready Summary
- **Are dependency and abuse the same?** DSM-5's collapse of abuse/dependence into one **substance use disorder** concerned some experts: scientifically they are different processes, but **clinically they tend to co-occur** (routine abuse → likely dependence), so separate diagnoses were judged unnecessarily complicated. *(Contrast with ICD-11, which keeps them separate — see chapter ICD-11 note.)*
- **Adding "addictive disorder":** gambling disorder added because it shows the same dependence, cravings, and brain pathways — but this opens the door to many "addictions" (internet gaming, even "tanning addiction"); the line for what counts as a **disorder** comes back to whether it causes **harmful distress/dysfunction**.
- **High-yield recall:**
  - Substance use disorder threshold = **≥2 of 11** criteria; severity Mild 2–3 / Moderate 4–5 / Severe 6+.
  - Gambling disorder threshold = **≥4 of 9**; Mild 4–5 / Moderate 6–7 / Severe 8–9.
  - **Tolerance + withdrawal criteria don't count** under appropriate medical supervision (sedatives, stimulants, opioids); **no withdrawal criterion** for hallucinogens; tobacco has **no intoxication** pattern.
  - Pleasure pathway = dopaminergic **VTA → nucleus accumbens → frontal cortex**; opiates act by **inhibiting GABA**.
  - Alcohol: **GABA** (chloride ions, anxiolytic), **glutamate** (blackouts), **serotonin** (cravings); **Wernicke–Korsakoff = thiamine deficiency**; **DTs** = withdrawal delirium; **FAS** + **ADH** enzyme.
  - Treatments to distinguish: **agonist substitution** (methadone/buprenorphine/nicotine), **antagonist** (naltrexone, acamprosate), **aversive** (disulfiram/Antabuse → acetaldehyde buildup), **contingency management**, **community reinforcement**, **MET**, **CBT/relapse prevention**, **controlled drinking** (Sobell vs Pendery controversy).
