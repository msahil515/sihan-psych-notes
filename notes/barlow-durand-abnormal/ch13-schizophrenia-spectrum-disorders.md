# Ch 13: Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders

This chapter covers schizophrenia and the broader spectrum of psychotic disorders: their history, positive/negative/disorganized symptoms, DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, the related disorders (schizophreniform, schizoaffective, delusional, brief psychotic, substance/medication-induced, psychotic disorder due to another medical condition, attenuated psychosis syndrome), and the multifactorial causes (genetic, neurobiological, psychosocial) plus biological and psychosocial treatment. Schizophrenia is a high-yield exam topic for clinical psychology: know the criteria, the symptom triad, the dopamine hypothesis, twin/adoption concordance figures, expressed emotion, and the antipsychotic classes/side effects cold.

---

## Perspectives on Schizophrenia
- **Schizophrenia**: a disorder characterized by a broad spectrum of **cognitive and emotional dysfunctions** including **delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech and behavior, and inappropriate emotions**. Disrupts perception, thought, speech, and movement: almost every aspect of daily functioning.
- Affects approximately **1 of every 100 people** (~1%) at some point in their lives. Complete recovery is rare. Annual US cost exceeds **$60 billion** (caregiving, lost wages, treatment).
- People with schizophrenia are heavily **stigmatized**. Despite media portrayals (>70% of prime-time TV characters with schizophrenia depicted as violent, >1/5 as murderers), violence is **more associated with substance abuse and personality disorders (antisocial/borderline)** than with schizophrenia.
- The "split mind" etymology wrongly inspired the popular (incorrect) belief that schizophrenia = split/multiple personality.

### Early Figures in Diagnosing Schizophrenia (Table 13.1)
- **John Haslam (1809)** – British hospital superintendent; *Observations on Madness and Melancholy*; outlined symptoms of "a form of insanity."
- **Philippe Pinel (1801/1809)** – French physician; described cases of schizophrenia.
- **Benedict Morel (1852)** – French; coined **démence (loss of mind) précoce (early/premature)** → Latin **dementia praecox**; onset often in adolescence.
- **Emil Kraepelin (1898/1899)** – German psychiatrist. Two key contributions:
  - (1) **Unified** three previously separate disorders under **dementia praecox**: **catatonia** (alternating immobility + excited agitation), **hebephrenia** (silly/immature emotionality), and **paranoia** (delusions of grandeur or persecution); believed an **early onset** developing into "mental weakness" was at the heart of each.
  - (2) **Distinguished dementia praecox from manic-depressive illness** (now bipolar disorder); dementia praecox = early onset + poor outcome.
- **Eugen Bleuler (1908)** – Swiss psychiatrist; introduced the term **schizophrenia** (Greek *skhizein* = "split" + *phren* = "mind"). Believed the core problem was **associative splitting** ("breaking of associative threads") — destruction of the forces connecting one function to the next. Emphasized the universal underlying problem (vs. Kraepelin's focus on onset/outcome). Title of 1911 book: *Dementia Praecox or the Group of Schizophrenias*.

### Identifying Symptoms
- Unlike depression (always sadness) or panic (always anxiety), **no single symptom defines schizophrenia** — it is a cluster of behaviors/symptoms not necessarily shared by all who have the diagnosis.
- **Psychotic behavior** (strictest sense) = **delusions** (irrational beliefs) and/or **hallucinations** (sensory experiences in the absence of external events).

> **Case – Arthur ("Saving the Children")**: 22-yo, delusion he could save all the world's starving children with a "secret plan," deteriorating handwriting, emotional swings, neglect of basic needs, lasted only a few days then resolved fully → exemplifies **brief psychotic disorder**.

## Clinical Description, Symptoms, and Subtypes
- **Schizophrenia spectrum disorder** = the group of diagnoses in this chapter. Includes: schizophrenia, **schizophreniform, schizoaffective, delusional, brief psychotic** disorders, plus **schizotypal personality disorder** (Ch 12). All share features of **extreme reality distortion**.
- Three symptom dimensions:
  - **Positive symptoms** – distorted reality (delusions, hallucinations).
  - **Negative symptoms** – deficits in normal behavior (blunted affect, avolition, alogia, anhedonia, asociality).
  - **Disorganized symptoms** – rambling speech, erratic behavior, inappropriate affect.
- **DSM-5 dimensional severity assessment**: rates each symptom on a **0–4 scale** (0 = not present, 1 = equivocal, 2 = present/mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = severe).

> **DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Criteria for Schizophrenia (Table 13.1 in book):**
> **A.** Two (or more) of the following, each present for a significant portion of a **1-month** period (or less if successfully treated). **At least one must be (1), (2), or (3):**
>   1. Delusions
>   2. Hallucinations
>   3. Disorganized speech (e.g., frequent derailment or incoherence)
>   4. Grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior
>   5. Negative symptoms (i.e., diminished emotional expression or avolition)
> **B.** Functioning markedly below pre-onset level in work, interpersonal relations, or self-care (or failure to achieve expected level if onset in childhood/adolescence).
> **C.** Continuous signs persist **≥6 months**, including ≥1 month of Criterion A (active-phase) symptoms; may include prodromal/residual periods (attenuated symptoms or negative symptoms only).
> **D.** Schizoaffective and depressive/bipolar disorder with psychotic features ruled out.
> **E.** Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition.
> **F.** If history of autism spectrum disorder/communication disorder, schizophrenia diagnosed only if prominent delusions/hallucinations also present ≥1 month.
> *Specify if:* **With catatonia**

> **DSM-5-TR note:** No substantive criteria changes for schizophrenia from DSM-5 to DSM-5-TR; the criteria above remain current. DSM-5-TR mainly updated text/prevalence wording and ICD-10-CM codes and clarified that the diagnosis should account for **culture/context** in evaluating delusions and disorganization. The historic **subtypes** (paranoid, disorganized/hebephrenic, catatonic) were already dropped in DSM-5 and remain absent in DSM-5-TR.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 codes schizophrenia under **"Schizophrenia and other primary psychotic disorders"** and, like DSM-5/5-TR, **abolished the classic subtypes**. ICD-11 instead uses **six symptom-rating domains** (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive symptoms) plus course specifiers (first episode, multiple episodes, continuous). ICD-11 only requires symptoms for **≥1 month** (it does **not** impose DSM's 6-month total-duration rule) — a key difference Sihan should flag.

### Positive Symptoms
- The more obvious signs of psychosis. **50%–70%** of people with schizophrenia experience hallucinations, delusions, or both.

#### Delusions
- **Delusion** = a **disorder of thought content**; a belief most members of a society would see as a misrepresentation of reality. Called "the basic characteristic of madness" (Jaspers).
- Types/themes:
  - **Delusion of grandeur** – mistaken belief one is famous/powerful (Arthur ending world starvation).
  - **Delusions of persecution** – belief others are "out to get them" (the Olympic cyclist who believed competitors sabotaged her).
  - **Erotomania** – belief a famous/higher-status person is in love with you.
  - **Capgras syndrome** – belief someone known has been **replaced by a double**.
  - **Cotard's syndrome** – belief that **one is dead**.
- Two theoretical themes for delusions:
  - **Motivational view** – delusions are attempts to **relieve anxiety/stress**; "stories" that make sense of uncontrollable anxieties and distract from upsetting realities (e.g., hallucinations).
  - **Deficit view** – delusions result from **brain dysfunction** creating disordered cognitions/perceptions.

#### Hallucinations
- **Hallucination** = the experience of **sensory events without any input from the surrounding environment**. Can involve any sense.
- **Auditory hallucination** (hearing voices) is the **most common** form in schizophrenia.
- Linked to **metacognition** ("thinking about thinking"): people experiencing hallucinations have intrusive thoughts but believe they come from **someone/somewhere else**, then engage in **meta-worry** (worrying about worrying).
- Brain-imaging evidence: London **SPECT** study (McGuire, Shah, & Murray, 1993) found the area most active during auditory hallucinations was **Broca's area (speech production)**, NOT Wernicke's area (language comprehension) — supporting the metacognition theory that patients are "hearing" their **own thoughts/voices** and cannot recognize the difference.
- **Poor emotional prosody comprehension** (prosody = pitch, amplitude, pauses that convey meaning/emotion) is deficient in those with auditory verbal hallucinations, adding to confusion interpreting "inner voices."

> **Case – David ("Missing Uncle Bill")**: 25-yo with mild intellectual disability; began talking to his deceased Uncle Bill after the uncle's death; auditory hallucinations of his uncle's voice; disorganized, accelerating, threatening speech.

### Negative Symptoms
- Indicate the **absence or insufficiency of normal behavior**. Roughly **25%** of people with schizophrenia display these. Include apathy, poverty of thought/speech, emotional and social withdrawal.
- **Avolition** (a- "without" + volition) – inability to **initiate and persist in activities**; little interest even in basic functions like personal hygiene; also called **apathy**.
- **Alogia** (a- + logos "words") – relative **absence of speech**; brief, empty replies; may reflect a **negative thought disorder** (trouble finding words / formulating thoughts) rather than poor communication skills; can include delayed/slow responses.
- **Anhedonia** (a- + hedonic "pleasure") – presumed **lack of pleasure**; indifference to normally pleasurable activities (eating, social interaction, sex).
- **Affective flattening / flat (blunted) affect** – ~one-quarter of patients; do not show emotions when expected; stare vacantly, flat/toneless speech.
  - **Berenbaum & Oltmanns (1992)**: patients with flat affect showed little facial change to comedy/drama clips **but reported feeling the appropriate emotions** → flat affect = **difficulty expressing** emotion, not a lack of feeling.
  - High-risk children study (Schiffman et al., 2004; videotaped eating lunch in 1972, followed ~20 years): those who later developed schizophrenia showed **less positive and more negative affect** as children.

### Disorganized Symptoms
- Least studied/understood. Erratic behaviors affecting speech, motor behavior, and emotional reactions.
- **Disorganized speech** – Bleuler's "associative splitting" / Meehl's **"cognitive slippage."** Patients often **lack insight** (awareness they have a problem). Forms:
  - **Tangentiality** – going off on a tangent instead of answering the question.
  - **Loose association / derailment** – abruptly changing topic to unrelated areas.
- **Inappropriate affect** – laughing/crying at improper times.
- **Disorganized behavior** – bizarre behaviors (hoarding objects, acting unusually in public).
- **Catatonia** – motor dysfunctions ranging from **wild agitation to immobility (catatonic immobility)**; includes **waxy flexibility** (limbs stay in positions placed by others). DSM-5 now includes **catatonia as a separate schizophrenia spectrum disorder** and as a specifier.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria for Catatonia (Table 13.2 – catatonia specifier):** ≥3 of: **stupor, cataplexy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia (mimicking speech), echopraxia (mimicking movements).**

### Historic Schizophrenia Subtypes
- Three historic divisions: **paranoid** (delusions of grandeur/persecution), **disorganized (hebephrenic)** (silly/immature emotionality), **catatonic** (alternating immobility + agitation).
- Used through **DSM-IV-TR** but **dropped from DSM-5** because they were used infrequently in clinical work and symptoms shift over the illness (people moved between categories). The **dimensional severity assessment** replaced them.

## Other Psychotic Disorders

### Schizophreniform Disorder (Table 13.3)
- Symptoms of schizophrenia lasting **≥1 month but <6 months**; patients can usually resume normal lives. **Lifetime prevalence ~0.2%.**
- Same Criterion A symptom list as schizophrenia. When diagnosis is made before recovery, qualify as **"provisional."**
- Good-prognosis features: onset of psychotic symptoms **within 4 weeks** of first noticeable behavior change, confusion at peak, good premorbid functioning, absence of blunted/flat affect.

### Schizoaffective Disorder (Table 13.4)
- Symptoms of schizophrenia **plus** mood disorder characteristics (depression or bipolar). Prognosis similar to schizophrenia (chronic difficulties for years).
- **DSM-5 criteria:** a major mood episode concurrent with Criterion A of schizophrenia; **delusions or hallucinations for ≥2 weeks in the ABSENCE of a major mood episode** (this is the key feature distinguishing it from a mood disorder with psychotic features); mood episode symptoms present for the **majority** of the total active+residual duration.
- *Specify:* **Bipolar type** (manic episode) or **Depressive type**.

### Delusional Disorder (Table 13.5)
- Major feature: a **persistent belief contrary to reality, in the absence of other schizophrenia characteristics**. Patients typically **lack flat affect/anhedonia/other negative symptoms** but may become socially isolated from suspiciousness. Delusions often long-standing (years).
- **DSM-5 criteria:** ≥1 delusion ≥1 month; Criterion A for schizophrenia **never met**; functioning not markedly impaired apart from delusion's impact; behavior not bizarre/odd.
- **Schizophrenia vs. delusional disorder distinction:** in delusional disorder the imagined events **could** happen but aren't (e.g., being followed); in schizophrenia the events are **impossible** (e.g., brainwaves broadcasting thoughts worldwide). **DSM-5 allows one bizarre delusion** in delusional disorder; schizophrenia requires more than one delusion.
- Subtypes: **Erotomanic** (another, usually higher-status, loves you — some celebrity stalkers), **Grandiose** (inflated worth/power/special relationship to deity/famous person), **Jealous** (partner unfaithful), **Persecutory** (being malevolently treated), **Somatic** (physical defect/medical condition), **Mixed**, **Unspecified**.
- **Shared psychotic disorder (folie à deux)** – delusions developed from a close relationship with a delusional individual. **DSM-5 now folds this under delusional disorder with a "shared" specifier** (no longer a separate disorder).
- Epidemiology: rare, **24–60 per 100,000**; among psychotic disorders, **2%–8%** are delusional disorder. Late onset (first admission **age 35–55**). Slightly **more females (55%) than males (45%)**.
- Opjordsmoen (1989, 30-yr follow-up of 53 patients): fared **better than schizophrenia but worse than schizoaffective**; ~80% had been married, half employed.
- Family studies: suspiciousness/jealousy/secretiveness more common in relatives → possible heritable component. Rule out delusions caused by amphetamines/alcohol/cocaine, brain tumors, Huntington's, Alzheimer's.

### Substance/Medication-Induced Psychotic Disorder (Table 13.6)
- **DSM-5 criteria:** delusions and/or hallucinations developing during/soon after **substance intoxication or withdrawal** or medication exposure, where the substance can produce such symptoms; not better explained by an independent psychotic disorder; not exclusively during delirium; causes significant distress/impairment.

### Psychotic Disorder Associated with Another Medical Condition (Table 13.7)
- **DSM-5 criteria:** prominent hallucinations or delusions that are the **direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition**; not better explained by another mental disorder; not exclusively during delirium.

### Brief Psychotic Disorder (Table 13.8)
- One or more positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech/behavior) lasting **≥1 day but <1 month**, with **full return to premorbid functioning** (Arthur's case).
- Often **precipitated by extreme stress**.
- *Specify:* **With marked stressor(s)** (a.k.a. brief reactive psychosis), **Without marked stressor(s)**, or **With postpartum onset** (onset during pregnancy or **within 4 weeks postpartum**).
- Do not count a culturally sanctioned response.

### Attenuated Psychosis Syndrome
- Individuals starting to develop psychotic symptoms (hallucinations/delusions) who are **distressed and seek help**, at **high risk for schizophrenia**, possibly **prodromal**, but **retain insight** (aware the symptoms are unusual; relatively intact reality testing).
- **DSM-5 placed this in "Conditions for Further Study"** (proposed, not an official diagnosis) — "cut the baby in half." Argument for: early intervention may prevent worsening/years of suffering. Argument against: better to screen the **general population** rather than focus only on this select group (van Os).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Attenuated psychosis syndrome **remains a "Condition for Further Study"** in DSM-5-TR (still not an official codable diagnosis).

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 has no equivalent codable "attenuated psychosis syndrome" diagnosis either; sub-threshold/at-risk states are not a formal ICD-11 psychotic category.

- **Schizotypal personality disorder** (Ch 12) is a related, less-severe spectrum disorder, possibly **genetically related** to schizophrenia.

## Prevalence and Causes of Schizophrenia

### Statistics
- Generally **chronic**; most have difficulty functioning, especially in relationships; many never marry/have children. Schizophrenic delusions tend to be **outside the realm of possibility**.
- **Lifetime prevalence ~0.2%–1.5%** (about **1%** at some point); roughly equal in men and women. Life expectancy slightly below average (higher suicide/accident rates).
- **Age of onset differs by sex:** for men, onset likelihood diminishes with age (can still first occur after 75); for women, frequency is lower than men **until age 36**, then risk switches with more women affected later. **Women have more favorable outcomes.**

### Development
- More severe symptoms first appear in **late adolescence / early adulthood**. Early childhood signs: mild physical abnormalities, poor motor coordination, mild cognitive/social problems (not specific enough to predict schizophrenia).
- **Up to 85%** go through a **prodromal stage** — a **1–2 year period** before serious symptoms, with schizotypal-like behaviors: **ideas of reference** (insignificant events relate to self), **magical thinking** (clairvoyance/telepathy), **illusions** (sensing another's presence when alone), plus isolation, impaired functioning, lack of initiative/energy.
- After symptoms develop it typically takes **1–2 years** to be diagnosed/treated (delay partly from hiding symptoms / paranoia).
- Course (Zubin, Steinhauer, & Condray, 1992): **~22% had one episode and improved** without lasting impairment; the remaining **78% had several episodes** with differing impairment. Pattern of **relapse and recovery** is common; poorer prognosis than most disorders in the book; high suicide risk.

### Cultural Factors
- Some (Laing, Szasz, Sarbin & Mancuso) argued schizophrenia is just a derogatory label for behaving outside cultural norms — contradicted by the immense emotional pain it causes. Kraepelin traveled to Asia to confirm the syndrome exists outside Western Europe. **Schizophrenia is universal** (all racial/cultural groups studied).
- Course/outcome **vary by culture**: stressors from political/social/economic problems (Africa, Latin America, Asia) may worsen outcomes.
- In the US, **proportionately more African Americans** receive the diagnosis than Caucasians; devalued ethnic minority groups (Afro-Caribbean in England; African Americans, Puerto Ricans in US) may face **bias and misdiagnosis**; blacks more likely detained against will, brought by police, given emergency injections (Goater et al., 1999). Differing rates may partly reflect **misdiagnosis** rather than real cultural differences, plus stigma/isolation stress and possible group-specific genetic variants.

### Genetic Influences
- Genes make some individuals **vulnerable**; **no single "schizophrenia gene"** — multiple gene variances combine to produce vulnerability.
- **Family studies (Kallmann, 1938, >1,000 patients):** the **more severe the parent's** disorder, the more likely the child develops it; **all forms** appear within families → you inherit a **general predisposition** for a spectrum of psychotic disorders, not a specific subtype.
- **Gottesman (1991) analysis (~40 studies) — risk by shared genes:**
  - **Identical (monozygotic) twin (100% genes): ~48%**
  - **Fraternal (dizygotic) twin (50% genes): ~17%**
  - **Any relative** raises risk; **~1%** with no affected relative (general population).
- **Twin studies:** truth is "somewhere in the middle" of pure-genetic vs. pure-environment expectations.
  - **Genain quadruplets** (identical, all schizophrenic; pseudonyms Nora, Iris, Myra, Hester = **NIMH**): same genes + same dysfunctional household, yet **differed in onset, symptoms, course, outcome** → role of **unshared environments** and possibly **de novo mutations** (new mutations in a germ cell or after conception). Hester (most disturbed) had symptoms at 18; Myra not hospitalized until 6 years later.
- **Adoption studies (Finland, Tienari):** from ~20,000 women with schizophrenia, 190 adopted-away children: child of a biological mother with schizophrenia had **~5%** risk (vs. ~1% general); if the biological mother had schizophrenia **or a related psychotic disorder**, risk rose to **~22%**. A **good/supportive home environment was protective** → **gene–environment interaction.**
- **Offspring of twins (Fischer; Gottesman & Bertelsen):**
  - Child of an MZ twin **with** schizophrenia: **~17%**.
  - Child of an unaffected MZ twin whose **co-twin has** schizophrenia: **~17%** (same!) → you can be a **"carrier."**
  - Child of a DZ twin **with** schizophrenia: **~17%**.
  - Child of an unaffected DZ twin whose co-twin has it: **~2%**.
  - Strong evidence of genetic vulnerability; the 17% (not 100%) shows other factors matter.
- **Linkage & association studies:** regions on chromosomes **1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 20, 22** implicated. Three reliable susceptibility genes: **NRG1 (Neuregulin 1, chromosome 8)**, **DTNBP1 (dystrobrevin-binding protein 1, chromosome 6)**, **COMT (catecholamine O-methyl transferase, chromosome 22)** — COMT is notable because it affects **dopamine metabolism.**
- **Endophenotypes** – strategy of finding basic processes/deficits underlying symptoms, then the genes causing them. Leading candidate: **smooth-pursuit eye movement (eye-tracking) deficit** — impaired in many with schizophrenia and in their relatives; not due to drugs/institutionalization. Other endophenotypes: social/cognitive/emotional deficits (e.g., emotion identification). The Consortium on the Genetics of Schizophrenia studies >1,200 patients and families.

### Neurobiological Influences

#### Dopamine
- **Dopamine hypothesis**: schizophrenia partly involves **excessive dopamine activity**. Neuroleptics are **dopamine antagonists**.
- Four "circumstantial evidence" clues:
  1. **Antipsychotic drugs (neuroleptics)** effective in schizophrenia are **dopamine antagonists** (block dopamine).
  2. Neuroleptics produce **Parkinson's-like side effects** (Parkinson's = insufficient dopamine).
  3. **L-dopa** (dopamine **agonist** for Parkinson's) can produce **schizophrenia-like symptoms.**
  4. **Amphetamines** (activate dopamine) can **worsen** psychotic symptoms.
- Contradictory evidence: many patients are **not** helped by dopamine antagonists; neuroleptics block dopamine quickly but **symptoms subside only after days/weeks**; drugs only partly reduce **negative symptoms**; **olanzapine** and similar drugs are effective yet are **weak dopamine antagonists** (the "double-edged sword").
- Current view = **three simultaneous neurochemical abnormalities**:
  1. **Excessive stimulation of striatal dopamine D2 receptors** (striatum = part of basal ganglia; controls movement/balance/walking). Effective antipsychotics share **D2 receptor antagonism.**
  2. **Deficient stimulation of prefrontal dopamine D1 receptors** (the thinking/reasoning area) → may account for other symptoms; related to **hypofrontality.**
  3. **Altered prefrontal glutamate transmission** — glutamate is an excitatory neurotransmitter; the **NMDA (N-methyl-d-aspartate) receptors** are studied. **PCP and ketamine** are **NMDA antagonists** that produce psychotic-like behavior and worsen symptoms → a glutamate deficit / NMDA blockade may be involved.

#### Brain Structure
- At-risk children show subtle neurological problems (abnormal reflexes, inattentiveness); adults show deficits in task performance and reaction time.
- **Enlarged ventricles** (lateral and third) — one of the most reliable findings (noted as early as **1927**, Jacobi & Winkler); dilation implies adjacent brain has **not developed or has atrophied**. Not in everyone; more in **men**, increases with **age and illness duration**; linked to **prenatal influenza exposure**. MRI study (Staal et al., 2000): patients **and their unaffected same-sex siblings** had third-ventricle enlargement vs. controls → may reflect susceptibility.
- **Frontal lobes / hypofrontality** (hypo = less active): deficient activity especially in the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)** during DLPFC tasks (Weinberger). Some patients show **hyperfrontality** instead → dysfunction is reliable but expressed differently. Implicated sites: prefrontal cortex, related cortical regions, subcortical circuits (**thalamus, striatum**). Brain damage may develop **progressively, beginning before symptoms, perhaps prenatally.**

#### Prenatal and Perinatal Influences
- **Fetal exposure to viral infection** (e.g., **influenza in the second trimester** — Mednick, Helsinki Type A2 epidemic), **pregnancy complications** (bleeding), and **delivery complications** (asphyxia/anoxia) correlate with later schizophrenia. (Genes may themselves contribute to birth complications.)
- **Chronic/early marijuana (cannabis) use** in high doses is associated with increased risk; patients are more likely to have cannabis use disorder. Link not fully understood.

### Psychological and Social Influences

#### Stress
- **Vulnerability–stress model**: stress can worsen symptoms in those predisposed.
- Living in a **large city (urban living)** increases risk. Combat soldiers can show temporary schizophrenia-like symptoms (Dohrenwend & Egri).
- **Brown & Birley (1968):** patients had a high number of **stressful life events in the 3 weeks before** onset (confirmed cross-nationally by WHO, Day et al., 1987). Limitation: **retrospective** reports may be biased.
- 1994 **Northridge earthquake** study (Horan et al., 2007): patients reported **lower self-esteem** and more **avoidance coping** than bipolar patients and controls. Sociocultural stress (poverty, homelessness, immigration) also relevant. Gene–environment interactions predict who relapses under stress; suggests value of **CBT** for coping.

#### Families and Relapse
- Discredited historical theories (no longer supported, but caused parental guilt):
  - **Schizophrenogenic mother** (Fromm-Reichmann, 1948) – cold, dominant, rejecting mother thought to cause schizophrenia.
  - **Double bind communication** (Bateson, 1959) – conflicting messages (mother responds coolly to embrace but says "Don't you love me anymore?" when child withdraws).
- **Expressed Emotion (EE)** – the key modern concept (George Brown, London). EE = family communication high in **criticism (disapproval), hostility (animosity), and emotional overinvolvement (intrusiveness).**
  - Patients with **limited contact** with high-EE relatives did better. **High EE strongly predicts relapse**: living in a high-EE family makes you **3.7× more likely to relapse** than in a low-EE family.
  - High-EE families view symptoms as **controllable** and become hostile thinking the patient "just doesn't want to help themselves."
  - **Cultural variation** in EE (Jenkins & Karno, 1992 — India, Mexico, UK, US): rates differ across cultures, so **EE does not cause** schizophrenia (rate of schizophrenia is ~1% worldwide regardless). Apparent over-involvement in one culture may be **supportive** in another.

## Treatment of Schizophrenia
- Historical/crude treatments: surgery to remove the "stone of madness" (1500s); **prefrontal lobotomies** (severing frontal lobes; calmed patients but caused cognitive/emotional deficits) as late as the 1950s; Kisii tribal scalp-scraping in Kenya.
- Treatment usually **begins with a neuroleptic drug + psychosocial treatments** (reduce relapse, compensate for skill deficits, improve compliance).

### Biological Interventions
- 1930s experimental treatments: **insulin coma therapy** (massive insulin doses to induce comas; high risk of illness/death), **psychosurgery/lobotomies**, and **electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** (late 1930s; found not beneficial for most with schizophrenia; still used in limited cases, sometimes with antipsychotics).
- **Antipsychotic medications / neuroleptics** ("taking hold of the nerves") — breakthrough in the **1950s**. Reduce hallucinations/delusions; **work mainly on positive symptoms**, less on negative/disorganized. They are **dopamine antagonists** (also affect serotonergic and glutamate systems).

> **Commonly Used Antipsychotics (Table 13.2):**
> - **Conventional / first-generation antipsychotics:**
>   - **Phenothiazines** – Chlorpromazine/**Thorazine** (moderate EPS), Fluphenazine/Prolixin (high), Mesoridazine/Serentil (low), Perphenazine/Trilafon (high), Thioridazine/Mellaril (low), Trifluoperazine/Stelazine (high).
>   - **Butyrophenone** – Haloperidol/**Haldol** (high EPS).
>   - **Others** – Loxapine/Loxitane (high), Molindone/Moban (low), Thiothixene/Navane (high).
> - **Second-generation / atypical agents (all LOW extrapyramidal side effects):** Aripiprazole/Abilify, **Clozapine/Clozaril**, **Olanzapine/Zyprexa**, Quetiapine/Seroquel, **Risperidone/Risperdal**, Ziprasidone/Geodon.

- **First-generation** (Haldol, Thorazine) effective for **~60%–70%** who try them.
- **Second-generation / atypical** (risperidone, olanzapine) help some previously unresponsive patients; were thought to have fewer side effects, **but** two large trials — **CATIE** (US, Clinical Antipsychotic Trials of Intervention Effectiveness) and **CUtLASS** (UK, Cost Utility of the Latest Antipsychotic Drugs in Schizophrenia Study) — found them **no more effective or better tolerated** than older drugs.
- **Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)** – serious side effects:
  - **Parkinsonian symptoms** — e.g., **akinesia** (expressionless face, slow motor activity, monotonous speech).
  - **Tardive dyskinesia** — involuntary movements of tongue/face/mouth/jaw (tongue protrusions, cheek puffing, lip puckering, chewing). Results from **long-term, high-dose** use, often **irreversible**; **3%–5%** in first 5 years, risk rising over time.
- **Noncompliance** is a major obstacle: David "cheeked" his Haldol. In the large study, **74% had stopped their medication 18 months** after initial use. Reasons: negative side effects (grogginess, blurred vision, dry mouth), poor doctor–patient relationship, cost, poor social support. **Injectable depot** medications (every few weeks) were hoped to help but noncompliance persists (patients don't return).
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** for hallucinations — wire coils generate magnetic fields (up to **50×/second**) through the skull to interrupt the brain area involved in hallucinations; can **modestly improve auditory hallucinations** but effects last **less than a month** (Hoffman; Slotema et al., 2012).

### Psychosocial Interventions
- **Psychodynamic/psychoanalytic** approaches: research suggests they are **at best not beneficial, at worst harmful.** Few believe psychological factors cause schizophrenia. Best approach combines **both biological and psychosocial** treatment.
- Historical inpatient settings: 19th-century **"moral treatment"** (socialization, routines, work, religion); **"milieu" treatments** (homelike environments) — none clearly helped except one:
- **Token economy (Paul & Lentz, 1977, Illinois):** inpatients **earned tokens** for appropriate behavior (socialization, group participation, self-care like bed-making) to buy meals/luxuries, and were **fined** for disruptive behavior; combined with a full activity schedule. Patients did better than controls on **social, self-care, vocational skills** and more were discharged. Based on Ayllon & Azrin's behavioral work.
- **Deinstitutionalization** (since 1955): driven by court rulings limiting involuntary hospitalization and by antipsychotic success; often ill-conceived → **homelessness** (estimated **150,000–200,000** people with schizophrenia/serious disorders homeless in the US).
- **Social skills training:** reteach basic conversation, assertiveness, relationship building; complex skills broken into components, modeled, role-played, practiced in the real world with feedback. Challenge = maintaining effects long-term.
- **Independent Living Skills Program (UCLA, Liberman) (Table 13.3):** modules for **symptom management** (identifying/managing warning signs of relapse, coping with persistent symptoms, avoiding alcohol/drugs) and **medication management** (info on antipsychotics, correct self-administration, identifying side effects, negotiating with providers).
- **Virtual reality** technology: assesses working memory/perseveration; studies paranoia in a virtual London subway (Freeman et al., King's College — groups differed in anxiety/worry/sensitivity/depression by prior paranoia); helps older patients improve cognitive/motor skills.
- **Behavioral family therapy** (resembles classroom education): family is educated about schizophrenia, **relieved of the myth they caused it**, taught about medications/side effects, communication skills (empathic listening, constructive expression of negative feelings to replace harsh criticism), and problem-solving. Aims to **reduce expressed emotion** and relapse. Effects significant in **first year, less robust at 2 years** → must be ongoing.
- **Vocational rehabilitation / supportive employment:** job coaches give on-the-job training to help patients keep meaningful jobs.
- **Combined multilevel treatment** (drugs + social skills training + family stress management) reduces relapses more than drugs + simple support/education (Falloon, Brooker, & Graham-Hole, 1992).
- **Self-help / consumer-run model:** e.g., **Fountain House** (NYC) psychosocial clubs — person-centered, employment, friendship, empowerment.

> **An Integrative Treatment Approach (Table 13.4) — six effective approaches:**
> 1. **Collaborative psychopharmacology** – antipsychotics for main symptoms + adjuncts (e.g., antidepressants for secondary depression).
> 2. **Assertive Community Treatment (ACT)** – multidisciplinary team, small caseloads, services in the community, 24-hour coverage (developed in Madison, Wisconsin; most extensively studied).
> 3. **Family psychoeducation** – educate family, reduce home stress/tension, social support.
> 4. **Supportive employment** – support before/during employment.
> 5. **Illness management and recovery** – patient as active participant, medication strategies, coping with recurrence.
> 6. **Integrated dual-disorders treatment** – treat coexisting substance use.

### Treatment across Cultures
- **Xhosa (South Africa):** traditional healers use oral treatments to induce vomiting, enemas, cattle slaughter to appease spirits. **Hispanics** less likely to use institutional settings, rely on **family support** — culturally adapting treatment (adding relatives to Latino social-skills training) is essential.
- **British vs. Chinese (Furnham & Wong, 2007):** Native Chinese hold more **religious/supernatural beliefs** about causes/treatment (e.g., "schizophrenia is due to evil done in a previous life," ancestor worship helps); British use more biological/psychological/community treatments, Chinese rely more on alternative medicine. **Supernatural beliefs in Bali** lead to limited antipsychotic use. In many African countries, patients are kept in **prisons** due to lack of alternatives. General trend: away from large institutions toward **community care.**

### Prevention
- Strategy: identify and treat **high-risk children** (~**17%** of children of a parent with schizophrenia develop it).
- **Mednick & Schulsinger (1960s, Danish high-risk study):** 207 children of mothers with severe schizophrenia + 104 controls, followed 10 years. Predictors identified: **pregnancy/delivery complications** and **instability of early family rearing environment** → poor parenting strains an already-vulnerable person (environmental triggers).
- **Prodromal intervention:** treat individuals showing early mild signs (with insight) to stop progression or prevent relapse — receiving increasing attention.

## Key Exam Takeaways (quick recall)
- **Symptom triad:** Positive (delusions, hallucinations) / Negative (avolition, alogia, anhedonia, flat affect, asociality) / Disorganized (disorganized speech [tangentiality, derailment], inappropriate affect, disorganized/catatonic behavior).
- **Schizophrenia duration:** Criterion A ≥1 month; total continuous signs **≥6 months** (DSM); schizophreniform **1–6 months**; brief psychotic **1 day–1 month**; delusional disorder **≥1 month**.
- **Concordance:** MZ twin **48%**, DZ twin **17%**, general population **1%**.
- **Dopamine hypothesis:** excess **striatal D2**, deficient **prefrontal D1**, plus **glutamate/NMDA** abnormality.
- **Expressed Emotion:** criticism + hostility + emotional overinvolvement → **3.7× relapse risk.**
- **EPS:** akinesia (parkinsonian) and **tardive dyskinesia** (often irreversible, 3–5% in 5 yrs).
- **Atypicals = low EPS;** CATIE/CUtLASS found atypicals **not superior** to conventionals.
