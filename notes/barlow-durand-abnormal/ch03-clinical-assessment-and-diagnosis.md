# Ch 3: Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

This chapter covers the two processes at the core of psychopathology: **clinical assessment** (systematic evaluation/measurement of psychological, biological, and social factors) and **diagnosis** (determining whether a problem meets all criteria for a disorder). It surveys every major assessment technique (clinical interview, mental status exam, behavioral assessment, projective tests, personality inventories, intelligence, neuropsychological, neuroimaging, psychophysiological) and the conceptual foundations (reliability, validity, standardization). It then traces the history and structure of the **DSM** classification system through DSM-5, the categorical/dimensional/prototypical debate, and the future of dimensions and spectra. For MET/NIMHANS this is an extremely high-yield, definition-dense chapter: examiners love **reliability vs validity types, the five MSE categories, projective vs objective tests, MMPI scales, neuroimaging techniques, the DSM editions/history, and categorical vs dimensional vs prototypical classification.**

---

## Assessing Psychological Disorders

### Core definitions
- **Clinical assessment** — the **systematic evaluation and measurement** of psychological, biological, and social factors in an individual presenting with a possible psychological disorder.
- **Diagnosis** — the process of determining whether the particular problem meets **all criteria** for a psychological disorder as set forth in the **DSM-5** (APA, 2013).
- Assessment process is likened to a **funnel** (Antony & Barlow, 2010): the clinician first collects a **broad range** of information about overall functioning, then **narrows the focus** by ruling out some areas and concentrating on the most relevant.

### Illustrative cases
- **Frank** — 24-yr-old mechanic, married 9 months; presents with marital distress/anxiety. Closes eyes 2–3 sec + right leg twitch = effort to suppress **intrusive, frightening thoughts about having a seizure ("a fit")**; jerks leg + prays to make thoughts go away. → points toward **obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)**. Shows that the patient's reported problem (marriage) may not be the principal clinical issue.
- **Brian** — 20-yr-old, recently discharged from army; referred for "sexual problems." Insists he is homosexual with **no actual homosexual arousal/fantasy/behavior** (strong heterosexual fantasies/history). His belief is a **delusion**; reads meaning into others' looks and conversations = **ideas of reference**; displays **homophobia**.

## Key Concepts in Assessment

### The three pillars (Figure 3.1)
> **Reliability** — the degree to which a measurement is **consistent**.
> **Validity** — the degree to which a technique **measures what it is designed to measure**.
> **Standardization** — the application of certain **standards/norms** to ensure **consistency across different measurements**.

### Reliability sub-types
- **Interrater reliability** — two or more **raters** get the **same answers**; improved by carefully designing assessment devices and researching them (e.g., four physicians giving four different diagnoses for stomach pain = **unreliable**).
- **Test–retest reliability** — the technique is **stable across time** (IQ of 110 Tuesday should be ~110 Thursday).

### Validity sub-types
- **Concurrent / descriptive validity** — comparing the new measure's results with **better-known measures** (e.g., a brief IQ test that matches a long IQ test).
- **Predictive validity** — how well the assessment tells you what will happen in the **future** (e.g., IQ predicting school success).

### Standardization
- A set of **norms** for procedures of testing, scoring, and evaluating data; scores compared with a **comparable reference group** (matched on age, race, gender, SES, diagnosis). Example: a 19-yr-old middle-class African American male's score compared with similar peers, not with 60-yr-old working-class Asian women.

## The Clinical Interview

- The **core of most clinical work**; gathers current/past behavior, attitudes, emotions, and detailed life history + presenting problem; pinpoints **onset** and concurrent events (life stress, trauma, illness).
- Collects interpersonal/social history, family makeup, upbringing, sexual development, religious attitudes, **relevant cultural concerns** (e.g., discrimination stress), and educational history.

### The Mental Status Exam (MSE) — systematic observation of behavior (FIVE categories — HIGH-YIELD)
1. **Appearance and behavior** — overt physical behaviors (Frank's leg twitch), dress, appearance, posture, facial expression. **Psychomotor retardation** (slow/effortful motor behavior) may indicate **severe depression**.
2. **Thought processes** — **rate/flow** of speech (fast/slow), **continuity** of speech. In schizophrenia, disorganized speech = **loose association / derailment**. Content: **delusions** (distorted views of reality) such as **delusions of persecution** (people are after me) or **delusions of grandeur** (I am all-powerful); **ideas of reference** (everything everyone does relates to me); **hallucinations** (sensing things that aren't there).
3. **Mood and affect** — **Mood** = predominant, pervasive feeling state. **Affect** = momentary feeling state accompanying what we say; normally **appropriate** (laugh at funny, sad at sad). Mismatch (laughing while reporting a death) = **inappropriate affect**; no emotional expression = **blunted / flat affect**.
4. **Intellectual functioning** — rough estimate of intelligence via vocabulary, use of abstractions/metaphors, memory; noted only if above/below average.
5. **Sensorium** — general awareness of surroundings (date, time, place, who he is, who you are). Intact = **"clear"** and **"oriented times three"** (person, place, time). Brain damage/dysfunction or drug/toxic states impair it.
- Confidentiality: information is protected by **privileged communication** laws; the only exception is when **harm/danger to self or others is imminent** (clinician must inform patient at outset).

### Semistructured clinical interviews
- **Unstructured interviews** — no systematic format.
- **Semistructured interviews** — carefully phrased, tested questions ensuring consistent coverage; clinician **may depart** to follow up ("semi"). Advantage: confidence all key areas covered; disadvantage: loses spontaneity and may inhibit volunteering of unasked-for info. **Fully structured, computer-administered interviews have not caught on.**
- Example: **Anxiety Disorders Interview Schedule for DSM-5 (ADIS-5)** (Brown & Barlow) — for OCD it asks about **obsessions** (recurring thoughts/images/impulses) and **compulsions** (driven repetitive behavior/thought), rating obsessions on a **9-point (0–8) scale** for **persistence–distress** and **resistance**, and compulsions for **frequency**.

## Physical Examination
- Recommended if no physical in the past year, because medical/toxic states mimic psychological disorders:
  - **Hyperthyroidism** (overactive thyroid) → symptoms mimicking **generalized anxiety disorder**.
  - **Hypothyroidism** (underactive thyroid) → symptoms consistent with **depression**.
  - **Brain tumor** → psychotic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations).
  - **Cocaine withdrawal** → **panic attacks** (patients often hide the addiction → misdiagnosis).
- Clinician must determine if a medical/drug condition is **coexisting vs causal** by examining **onset** (e.g., depression starting with sedative use and remitting on discontinuation = **substance-induced mood disorder**).

## Behavioral Assessment
- Uses **direct observation** to formally assess thoughts, feelings, and behavior in **specific situations/contexts**; better than interviews for young children, nonverbal individuals, or those with cognitive impairments, or those who withhold info.
- Conducted in the home/workplace/community or via **role-play simulations**.
- **Target behaviors** are identified and observed to determine influencing factors. Example: the violent 7-yr-old whose mother said he "doesn't listen" / "has an attitude" — a home visit revealed he threw a glass with **no consequence** (revealing the true picture).
- **Analogue settings** — similar/simulated settings mimicking real-life symptoms (Haynes et al., 2009). Examples: simulating situations to find why autistic children engage in **self-injurious behavior** (Durand et al., 2013); **hypnosis** to induce delusions in healthy volunteers during brain scans (Blakemore et al., 2003); film-clip study of men's tendency to **sexually harass** (Parrott et al., 2012).

### The ABCs of Observation (Figure 3.3)
- Focused on the **here and now**: **A**ntecedent (what happened just before) → **B**ehavior → **C**onsequence (what happened after).
- Violent boy: (1) mother asks him to put glass in sink (antecedent) → (2) boy throws glass (behavior) → (3) no response from mother (consequence) → behavior **reinforced** (escapes cleanup, no negative consequence) → likely to recur.
- **Informal observation** relies on observer recollection/interpretation. **Formal observation** uses an **operational definition** — specifying observable, measurable behavior (e.g., "any time the boy does not comply with reasonable requests" instead of vague "having an attitude").

### Self-monitoring (self-observation)
- People observe their **own** behavior to find patterns (e.g., smokers logging cigarettes + times/places); **essential for private behaviors** (e.g., purging in bulimia); increasingly via **smartphones**.
- **Checklists and behavior rating scales** — more formal/structured; used before and during treatment. Example: **Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)** assesses **18** general areas, each rated **0 (not present) to 6 (extremely severe)**; screens for moderate-to-severe psychotic disorders (items: **somatic concern, guilt feelings, grandiosity**).
- **Reactivity** — the mere fact of observation **changes** the behavior (e.g., friend says "like" less when counted). Behaviors people want to **increase** tend to increase, behaviors to **decrease** tend to decrease, when self-monitored — clinicians sometimes exploit this therapeutically.

## Psychological Testing
- Specific tools (cognitive/emotional/behavioral responses tied to a disorder) and general tools (long-standing personality features). Must meet strict **reliability** and **validity** standards (unlike pop-magazine quizzes).

### Projective Testing
- Rooted in **psychoanalytic theory** (Freud's unconscious): **ambiguous stimuli** are presented and the person **projects** unconscious personality/fears onto them. Controversial but still common; most doctoral programs train in their use.
- **Rorschach inkblot test** — developed >80 yrs ago by Swiss psychiatrist **Hermann Rorschach** (died at **38** before fully developing systematic interpretation). Current form: **10 inkblot pictures**; person says what he sees. Early use highly controversial due to lack of reliability/validity. **John Exner** created a standardized version, the **Comprehensive System** (Exner, 2003), specifying card presentation, examiner wording, and recording; still controversial as a valid technique.
- **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** — developed **1935** by **Christiana Morgan & Henry Murray** (Harvard). **31 cards** (30 pictures + 1 blank), ~**20** used per administration; person tells a **dramatic story** ("a test of imagination"). Variants: **Children's Apperception Test (CAT)**, **Senior Apperception Technique (SAT)**, racial/ethnic adaptations; formal scoring (e.g., **Social Cognition and Object Relations Scale**, Westen, 1991). Interpretation depends on examiner's frame of reference → questions about validity remain.
- Projective tests are valuable as **icebreakers** but their lack of reliability/validity makes them weak **diagnostic** tools — reinforces the **scientist–practitioner** approach.

### Personality Inventories
- **Personality inventories** = self-report questionnaires assessing personal traits.
- **Face validity** — wording of questions appears to fit the desired info. **Paul Meehl** (1945) argued face validity is **not** what matters; what matters is **what the answers predict** (empirical/criterion-keying), even if content seems irrelevant (e.g., people with schizophrenia answering "true" to "I have never been in love").
- **Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)** — most widely used inventory in the US; developed late 1930s–early 1940s, first published **1943** (Hathaway & McKinley). **Empirical** (data-based), not theory-based. Person answers **true/false**. **Original = 550 items; MMPI-2 (1989) = 567 items; MMPI-A (1992)** for adolescents.
- **Pattern of responses** (not individual items) is compared with groups who have specific disorders; represented on **standard scales** (Table 3.2); computer scoring + interpretation reduces reliability problems.
- **Validity scales** detect faking/distortion: **Lie (L)** scale, **Infrequency (F)** scale, **Subtle Defensiveness / Correction (K)** scale, plus VRIN, TRIN, Back F, FBS, and Superlative Self-Presentation (S).
- **Clinical scales** (Table 3.2): Hypochondriasis, Depression, Hysteria, **Psychopathic deviate** (antisocial), Masculinity–femininity, Paranoia, Psychasthenia, Schizophrenia, Mania, Social introversion.
- Example: **James S.**, 27, evaluated during trial for kidnapping/raping/murdering a woman; high **validity scores** (tried to look good/fake innocence) + very high **Psychopathic Deviation** ("aggressive, unreliable, irresponsible; unable to learn from experience"). MMPI-2 corrected sexist and culturally insensitive (Christianity-centric) items and re-standardized to **1980 US Census** (incl. African Americans, Native Americans). MMPI is **one of the most extensively researched** instruments; excellent reliability/validity, though it may not change treatment or outcomes (Lima et al., 2005).

### Intelligence Testing
- Originally built to **predict school success**. **Alfred Binet & Théodore Simon (1904, France)** commissioned to identify "slow learners"; tested tasks of attention, perception, memory, reasoning, verbal comprehension; kept tasks that separated slow learners. **Lewis Terman (1916, Stanford)** adapted it → **Stanford-Binet** test.
- **Intelligence quotient (IQ)** — originally **mental age ÷ chronological age × 100** (a child passing the 7-yr level = mental age 7). Problem: a year above counts differently at different ages. Modern tests use **deviation IQ** — score compared only with **same-age peers**.
- **Stanford-Binet V** (Roid & Pomplun, 2005). **Wechsler tests** (David Wechsler): **WAIS-III** (adults), **WISC-IV** (children), **WPPSI-III** (young children); each has **verbal scales** (vocabulary, facts, short-term memory, verbal reasoning) and **performance scales** (psychomotor abilities, nonverbal reasoning, learning new relationships).
- **IQ ≠ intelligence**: a high IQ = greater chance of doing well in the educational system; low IQ may reflect non-native language, etc. Broader views of intelligence add adapting to environment, generating new ideas, efficient information processing. IQ tests are used for **delirium** and **intellectual disability**.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5 renamed "mental retardation" to **intellectual disability**; **DSM-5-TR** formalizes the term **Intellectual Developmental Disorder** (parenthetical, aligning with ICD-11) and emphasizes **adaptive functioning** (conceptual, social, practical domains) over IQ score for severity. See Ch 14.

### Neuropsychological Testing
- **Neuropsychological tests** infer brain dysfunction by observing its **effects** on task performance (receptive/expressive language, attention/concentration, memory, motor skills, perception, learning, abstraction): "you don't see damage, but you can see its effects."
- **Bender Visual–Motor Gestalt Test** — simple screening (child copies lines/shapes; excess errors vs same-age norms = suspected dysfunction); cannot localize the problem.
- Advanced batteries that **localize**: **Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery** (Golden et al., 1980) and **Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery** (Reitan & Davison, 1974). Halstead-Reitan includes the **Rhythm Test** (compare rhythmic beats), **Strength of Grip Test**, and **Tactile Performance Test** (blindfolded block placement → learning/memory).
- Validity: the two batteries were **equivalent**, ~**80% correct** at detecting damage (Goldstein & Shelly, 1984).
> **False positive** — test shows a problem when **none exists**.
> **False negative** — test finds **no** problem although difficulty **is** present (particularly dangerous for brain dysfunction tests). Hence neuropsych tests are used as **screening devices paired with other assessments**.

### Neuroimaging: Pictures of the Brain
- **Neuroimaging** = looking inside the nervous system to image structure and function. Two categories: **structure** and **function**.

**Images of brain STRUCTURE:**
- **CT / CAT scan (computerized axial tomography)** — first technique (early 1970s); multiple **X-rays** from different angles, computer reconstructs slices (~**15 min**); good for tumors, injuries, structural/anatomical abnormalities; downside: **x-radiation risk**.
- **MRI (nuclear magnetic resonance imaging)** — head placed in a high-strength **magnetic field**; radio signals excite hydrogen protons; relaxation measured; lesions appear lighter/darker. Higher resolution, no X-ray risk; originally ~**45 min**, now as little as **10 min**; downside: **claustrophobia** (enclosed tube).

**Images of brain FUNCTIONING:**
- **PET scan (positron emission tomography)** — inject **radioactive tracer** bound to isotopes interacting with blood/oxygen/glucose; active regions become **"hot spots."** Must stay motionless ~**40 sec**; can superimpose on MRI. Findings: reduced **parietal-lobe glucose metabolism** in early Alzheimer's-type dementia; findings in OCD and bipolar disorder. **Very expensive** (~$6 million to set up, ~$500,000/yr to run).
- **SPECT (single photon emission computed tomography)** — like PET but a different tracer; **less accurate but less expensive** → used more often.
- **fMRI (functional MRI)** — fast MRI taking **milliseconds**; images the brain **at work** second-to-second; has **largely replaced PET** in leading centers. **BOLD-fMRI** (Blood-Oxygen-Level-Dependent) is the most common technique.
- Newest: **radiolabeled neuroreceptor ligands** in SPECT/PET to study distribution/density of receptors (e.g., **dopamine, serotonin**) down to the **synapse** level.

### Psychophysiological Assessment
- **Psychophysiology** = measurable nervous-system changes reflecting emotional/psychological events; measured from brain or peripherally.
- **EEG (electroencephalogram)** — electrodes on scalp record **brain wave activity** (low-voltage neuron currents); used in waking and sleep.
  - **Event-related potential (ERP) / evoked potential** — brief EEG response to a specific meaningful stimulus.
  - **Alpha waves** — regular pattern of a relaxed, calm, waking adult (stress-reduction treatments aim to increase alpha).
  - **Delta waves** — slow, irregular; deepest sleep stage (~1–2 hrs after falling asleep). Sleep panic attacks occur almost exclusively in the **delta wave stage**; delta during waking may indicate localized brain dysfunction.
- **Peripheral measures**: heart rate, respiration, **electrodermal responding** (formerly **galvanic skin response, GSR**) = sweat-gland activity via the **sympathetic / autonomic nervous system** (responsive to stress/arousal). Used for **PTSD** (trauma cues evoke strong responses), **sexual dysfunctions** (penile circumference / vaginal blood flow to erotic stimuli).
- Basis for **biofeedback** (feeding back blood pressure etc. so patients learn to regulate responses); used for headaches and hypertension. Limitations: needs skill/expertise; can produce inconsistent results.

## Diagnosing Psychological Disorders

### Idiographic vs nomothetic
> **Idiographic strategy** — determines what is **unique** about an individual's personality, culture, circumstances → tailors treatment.
> **Nomothetic strategy** — determines the **general class** to which a problem belongs → enables drawing on accumulated knowledge; making a **diagnosis** is a nomothetic act.
- **Prognosis** — the likely **future course** of a disorder under certain conditions.

### Classification terminology (HIGH-YIELD)
> **Classification** — any effort to construct groups/categories and assign objects/people by **shared attributes** (a nomothetic strategy).
> **Taxonomy** — classification of entities for **scientific purposes**.
> **Nosology** — a taxonomic system applied to **psychological/medical** phenomena (all healthcare diagnostic systems are nosological).
> **Nomenclature** — the **names/labels** of disorders within the nosology (e.g., "anxiety" or "mood disorders").
- Most professionals use the **DSM-5** (APA, 2013) — official system in the US, used worldwide.

## Classification Issues

### Categorical and Dimensional Approaches
- **Classical (pure) categorical approach** — originates with **Emil Kraepelin (1856–1926)** and the biological tradition; assumes each diagnosis has **one clear underlying pathophysiological cause**, each disorder is **unique** with one non-overlapping set of causes, and a single set of criteria everyone in the category must meet. Useful in **medicine** (fever + stomach pain → flu vs appendicitis). But the mental health field has **rejected** it as inappropriate to the complexity of psychological disorders (psychological + social interact with biological).
- **Dimensional approach** — notes cognitions, moods, behaviors and **quantifies them on a scale** (e.g., severely anxious = 10, moderately depressed = 5, mildly manic = 2 → profile 10,5,2). Applied especially to personality disorders but relatively **unsatisfactory**; no agreement on number of dimensions (from **1 to 33**, Millon).
- **Prototypical approach (DSM-5's basis)** — a categorical approach that **combines features of both**: identifies certain **essential** characteristics for classification while allowing **nonessential variations** (analogy: a "dog" has essential doggish traits but varies in color/size/species). Requires a **certain number** of prototypical criteria + **some** of additional criteria. Downsides: **blurred boundaries**, symptoms shared across disorders; advantages: fits current knowledge, **user-friendly**.

> **DSM-5(-TR) — Major Depressive Episode criteria (prototypical example):** **Five (or more)** of the following symptoms present during the **same 2-week period**, a change from previous functioning; **at least one** must be (a) **depressed mood** or (b) **loss of interest/pleasure**. (Exclude symptoms clearly attributable to another medical condition.)
> 1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day.
> 2. Markedly diminished interest/pleasure in all/almost all activities most of the day, nearly every day.
> 3. Significant weight loss (when not dieting) or weight gain.
> 4. Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day.
> 5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation nearly every day.
> 6. Fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day.
> 7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt nearly every day.
> 8. Diminished ability to think/concentrate or indecisiveness nearly every day.
> 9. Recurrent thoughts of death, recurrent suicidal ideation without a specific plan, or a suicide attempt / specific plan.
>
> **DSM-5-TR note:** MDE criteria are **unchanged** in DSM-5-TR. DSM-5-TR added a clarifying note that the bereavement exclusion was removed (it differentiates normal grief from MDE) and introduced new **suicidal behavior** and **nonsuicidal self-injury** as conditions for further study/coding. The headline DSM-5-TR addition in the depression/grief area is **Prolonged Grief Disorder** (covered in Ch 7), not part of MDE itself.

### Reliability (of a classification system)
- Two clinicians interviewing the same patient (condition unchanged) should observe/measure the **same** behaviors → disorder identified **reliably**. Unreliable systems are vulnerable to **clinician bias**.
- Most unreliable category: **personality disorders** (chronic, trait-like inappropriate behaviors/emotional reactions). Morey & Ochoa (1989), **291 clinicians**: substantial bias — **less experienced or female** clinicians over-diagnosed **borderline personality disorder**; more experienced/male clinicians under-diagnosed it; patients who were **white, female, or poor** were over-diagnosed with BPD. → more reliable criteria needed.

### Validity (of a classification system)
> **Construct validity** — the signs/symptoms chosen as criteria consistently **go together** and **differ from other categories** (depression discriminable from social phobia); may predict **familial aggregation** (extent the disorder appears among relatives).
> **Predictive / criterion validity** — the diagnosis predicts **course** and **treatment response** of the prototypical patient (outcome = the criterion).
> **Content validity** — the criteria **reflect how experts think** about that disorder ("getting the label right").
- **Familial aggregation** — the extent to which a disorder is found among the patient's relatives.

## History of the DSM

### Diagnosis before 1980
- Detailed observations of depressed/phobic/psychotic symptoms go back to earliest records. As late as **1959** there were at least **nine** worldwide systems; only **three of nine** listed "phobic disorder" separately (Marks, 1969).
- **Kraepelin** first identified **schizophrenia**, calling it **dementia praecox** (deterioration of the brain occurring "prematurely") — reflecting his belief in **brain pathology**. His **1913** textbook described dementia praecox, **bipolar disorder** (then **manic depressive psychosis**), and organic brain syndromes. **Philippe Pinel** characterized depression (**melancholia**) as a separate entity.
- **1948** — **WHO** added a mental disorders section to **ICD-6** (little influence). **1952** — APA's **DSM-I** (little influence). **1968** — **DSM-II**; **1969** — **ICD-8**. These lacked precision, relied on unproven etiological theories, and had **little reliability**. As late as the 1970s, France and Russia had their own systems.

### DSM-III and DSM-III-R (1980 / 1987)
- **1980 landmark — DSM-III** (APA), under **Robert Spitzer**. Three major changes:
  1. **Atheoretical** approach — precise **descriptions** of disorders, not psychoanalytic/biological etiology (e.g., phobia moved from "neurosis" to the new **"anxiety disorders"** group).
  2. **Specific, detailed criteria** that made it possible to **study reliability and validity**.
  3. **Multiaxial system** (five axes): **Axis I** = clinical disorders; **Axis II** = enduring **personality** disorders; **Axis III** = physical disorders/conditions; **Axis IV** = psychosocial stress (dimensional); **Axis V** = current level of adaptive functioning.
- **DSM-III-R (1987)** revision. Despite low reliability for some disorders and arbitrary criteria, DSM-III became the most popular system internationally (precise descriptive format + neutrality on cause + multiaxial breadth).

### DSM-IV and DSM-IV-TR (1994 / 2000)
- Driven by need for a worldwide system; **ICD-10** published **1993** (US required by treaty to use ICD-10 codes). Work proceeded simultaneously on ICD-10 and **DSM-IV (1994)**.
- DSM-IV relied as little as possible on expert consensus; changes based on **scientific data**, literature reviews, reanalyzed datasets, and **12 field trials** of reliability/validity.
- Most substantial change: **eliminated the distinction between organically based and psychologically based disorders** (all disorders have biological + psychological/social components).
- **Multiaxial changes**: only **personality disorders and intellectual disability** on Axis II (pervasive developmental, learning, motor skills, communication disorders moved to Axis I); **new Axis IV** = psychosocial/environmental problems; Axis V essentially unchanged.
- **DSM-IV-TR (2000)** — text revision updating research text + minor criteria clarifications. (Task force chaired/advised by the textbook authors; **David Kupfer** chaired DSM-5.)

### DSM-5 (2013)
- Published **spring 2013** after >10 yrs; collaborative with **ICD-11** (then due 2014), each workgroup including an international expert. Largely **unchanged from DSM-IV**, with some new/reclassified disorders + structural changes.
- **Three sections**: Section 1 = introduction/how to use; Section 2 = the disorders; **Section 3** = conditions needing **further research** before becoming official diagnoses.
- **Removed the multiaxial system** — former Axes I, II, III combined into disorder descriptions; clinicians make **separate notations** for psychosocial/contextual factors (old Axis IV) and disability (old Axis V).
- **Expanded dimensional axes** for rating severity/intensity/frequency/duration. Example: **NSESSS-PTSD** (National Stressful Events Survey PTSD Short Scale, LeBeau et al.) — 9-item self-report rating PTSD severity over the past 7 days.
- **Cross-cutting dimensional symptom measures** — not specific to one disorder; evaluate symptoms common across disorders (anxiety, depression, sleep), monitored over treatment (e.g., rating anxiety alongside a bipolar diagnosis since anxiety predicts poorer response). Rated on a **0–4 scale** (0 = none, 4 = very severe). These dimensions are **added on to** categorical diagnoses, not replacing categories.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 (officially adopted 2019, in effect Jan 2022 — after this 2015 textbook) is the **current global classification**. Key differences from DSM-5-TR: ICD-11 added **Gaming Disorder** and **Complex PTSD (CPTSD)** as a distinct sibling of PTSD; uses a **fully dimensional, trait-based personality disorder model** (severity + trait domains: negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia) instead of DSM's **categorical clusters A/B/C**; merged categories and simplified criteria; uses **flexible clinical descriptions/diagnostic requirements** rather than rigid criterion counts. The 2015 book's references to ICD-11 reflect its draft/planned status only.

### DSM-5 and Frank
- Frank's diagnosis = **OCD**; if long-standing social-avoidance traits exist, possibly **schizoid personality disorder**; job/marital difficulties noted as psychosocial/environmental problems; severity rated dimensionally to monitor treatment.
- **Impairment is crucial**: criteria require **clinically significant distress or impairment** in social, occupational, or other important functioning. Same symptoms but only mildly annoying → does **not** merit a diagnosis (the **threshold** of impairment). The authors' own clinics use a **0–8 severity scale** (0 = none, 8 = severely disabling/housebound); a disorder must rate **at least 4** to meet criteria; ratings of 2–3 = **subthreshold**. Frank's OCD rated **5**.

### Social and Cultural Considerations in DSM-5
- **Culture** = values, knowledge, and practices derived from ethnic/religious/social-group membership, and how it shapes the experience of disorder.
- **Cultural Formulation** / **Cultural Formulation Interview** — describes the disorder from the patient's perspective and primary social/cultural group. Suggested areas: primary cultural reference group; acculturation/language; whether the patient uses **old-country idioms** (e.g., **ataques de nervios** in Hispanic culture, close to panic disorder); acceptance of Western vs alternative healthcare (e.g., traditional herbal doctors); culturally specific meanings of **"disabled."** As of the book, **no research yet supports** these guidelines (Lewis-Fernandez et al., 2009).

### Criticisms of DSM-5
- DSM-5 and ICD-11 are the most advanced nosologies ever, but still a **work in progress** allowing interim revisions.
- **"Fuzzy" categories** blur at the edges → **comorbidity** (being diagnosed with **more than one disorder at the same time**), complicating conclusions about course/treatment.
- Two further criticisms: (1) systems emphasize **reliability sometimes at the expense of validity** (e.g., accepting "I feel depressed" gives perfect reliability but poor validity); (2) nosologies **perpetuate flawed past definitions** rather than starting fresh (Carson, 1991/1996), which is unlikely due to expense and lost accumulated wisdom.
- **Reification** — literally making a category a "thing," assuming a meaning that doesn't really exist; categories are conveniences and can change with new knowledge; not everything must fit neatly.

### A Caution About Labeling and Stigma
- **Labeling** — using a label to characterize the **totality** of a person ("He's a schizo").
> **Stigma** — a combination of **stereotypic negative beliefs, prejudices, and attitudes** resulting in **reduced life opportunities** for the devalued group (Hinshaw & Stier, 2008); stigmatization is reportedly **increasing**, not decreasing.
- Labels for intellectual disability had to be repeatedly changed as they became pejorative: once-neutral **moron, imbecile, idiot** became insults. Newer approach: classify by **levels of support needed** (intermittent, limited, extensive, pervasive) rather than IQ; DSM-5 dropped **"mental retardation"** for **"intellectual disability."**
- Distinguish **"John is a diabetic" vs "John is a person who has diabetes"** — terms identify patterns of behavior, not people.

## Creating a Diagnosis (how categories are made)

### Mixed Anxiety-Depression (a diagnosis NOT included)
- **Primary care settings** — where a person goes first; many patients present with minor unexplained aches + feeling uptight/down/anxious, classic but not severe/frequent enough for an existing anxiety/mood disorder.
- A **threshold** = the **minimum number of criteria** required to meet a disorder's definition; thresholds must be set **high enough** because of **legal/policy implications** (eligibility for reimbursement/disability paid by taxpayers).
- Klerman & Weissman (1989, Wells et al.) found these patients **more impaired** than many with chronic medical (cardiac/pulmonary) conditions and a heavy burden on the system. ICD-10 had created a **mixed anxiety-depression** category but never defined criteria.
- A 7-site worldwide study (Zinbarg et al., 1994, 1998): such patients were common in primary care, substantially impaired, distinguishable from existing disorders → established **content validity** and some **construct validity**, but lacked data on course, treatment response, familial aggregation, reliability, predictive validity → placed in the **DSM-IV appendix** (for diagnoses under study).
- Later studies (Weisberg et al., 2005): the condition is **rare without a current/prior anxiety or mood disorder**, symptoms **don't last long**, and it was hard to identify reliably → **eliminated** from DSM-5; not even placed in **Section 3**, and unlikely to ever be considered.

### Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (a diagnosis that WAS created)
- Raises the issue of **bias and stigmatization**. Clinicians identified women with severe/incapacitating emotional reactions in the **late luteal phase** of the menstrual cycle.
- **Arguments for**: suffering/impairment deserves attention/care/funding; would promote research. **Arguments against**: little scientific data; risk of **stigmatizing a normal endocrinological stage** as a psychiatric disorder; parallels to discredited **"hysteria"** (diagnosed only in women); questions whether it is endocrinological/gynecological rather than mental; no comparable male disorder.
- **DSM-III-R (1987)** placed it in the appendix, named **late luteal phase dysphoric disorder (LLPDD)** to differentiate it from milder **PMS**. Research surged (~one article/month by 1991).
- Key figures: **PMS** occurs in **20–40%** of women but only ~**4.6%** suffer the severe LLPDD symptoms; biological abnormalities and some effective treatments found.
- **DSM-IV (1994)** retained it in the appendix and renamed it **premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)** (symptoms not exclusively tied to the late luteal endocrine state).
- Continued research: disabling premenstrual symptoms in ~**2–5%** of women, moderate symptoms in another **14–18%**; ACOG published treatment guidelines. PMDD distinguished from **premenstrual exacerbations** of other disorders (PMDD symptoms must be absent/mild postmenstrually; some symptoms must differ from mood disorders). Evidence supports PMDD as a **disorder of mood**, not endocrine → included as a **distinct disorder in DSM-5** in the **mood (depressive) disorders chapter** (Ch 7).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** PMDD remains in the **Depressive Disorders** chapter in DSM-5-TR with criteria essentially unchanged. **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 lists PMDD in the genitourinary chapter ("Diseases of the genitourinary system") with a **cross-reference** to mental/behavioral disorders, i.e., it is dual-classified rather than solely a mental disorder.

## Beyond DSM-5: Dimensions and Spectra
- Exclusive reliance on **discrete categories** has not produced a satisfactory nosology: persistent **comorbidity**, **fuzzy boundaries**, little validating evidence, and **not one biological marker / lab test** clearly distinguishes one disorder from another. Categories also **lack treatment specificity** (e.g., CBT or antidepressants help many dissimilar categories).
- A **dimensional strategy** is expected to grow. **"Spectrum"** = groups of disorders sharing basic biological/psychological qualities or dimensions. Example: **Asperger's syndrome** + autistic disorder merged into **autism spectrum disorder** in DSM-5 (Ch 14). But research isn't advanced enough for a wholesale switch, so DSM-5 categories still resemble DSM-IV; richer dimensional approaches may be ready for the **6th edition in 10–20 years**.
- **Personality disorders** are **not qualitatively distinct** from normal personality — they are **maladaptive, extreme variants of common traits** (Widiger & Edmundson, 2011); broad dispositions (shy/inhibited vs outgoing) have **stronger genetic loading** than categorical PDs.
- For **anxiety and mood disorders**, Brown & Barlow (2009) propose a dimensional system: anxiety and depression share much and may sit on a continuum of **negative affect** / a **spectrum of emotional disorders**. Dimensional/spectrum strategies may even be superior for schizophrenia.
- Neuroscience is expected to find neurobiological processes tied to **specific cognitive/emotional/behavioral traits** (e.g., behavioral inhibition) that do **not** map neatly onto current DSM-5 categories.

## Exam-focus summary
- **MSE = 5 categories**: appearance/behavior, thought processes, mood/affect, intellectual functioning, sensorium ("oriented ×3").
- **Reliability types**: interrater, test–retest. **Validity types**: concurrent/descriptive, predictive; for diagnoses add **construct, criterion/predictive, content**.
- **Projective (Rorschach 10 blots, Exner; TAT 31 cards Morgan & Murray)** = theory-based, weak reliability. **MMPI** = empirical, 567 items (MMPI-2), validity + clinical scales.
- **Neuroimaging**: CT/MRI = structure; PET/SPECT/fMRI(BOLD) = function.
- **Classification**: classical categorical (Kraepelin) vs dimensional vs **prototypical (DSM-5)**.
- **DSM timeline**: ICD-6 1948 → DSM-I 1952 → DSM-II 1968 → **DSM-III 1980 (Spitzer, atheoretical, multiaxial)** → DSM-III-R 1987 → DSM-IV 1994 → DSM-IV-TR 2000 → **DSM-5 2013 (Kupfer, dropped multiaxial system)**. ICD-11 = current global system.
- **Created-diagnosis cases**: Mixed Anxiety-Depression = rejected; **PMDD** = accepted (mood disorders chapter).
