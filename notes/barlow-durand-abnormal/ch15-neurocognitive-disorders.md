# Ch 15: Neurocognitive Disorders

This chapter covers the two broad classes of acquired cognitive impairment that usually develop later in life: **delirium** (an acute, often reversible disturbance of attention and awareness) and **mild/major neurocognitive disorder (NCD)** (a progressive deterioration of multiple cognitive domains, formerly called *dementia*). It details every DSM-5 etiologic subtype of NCD (Alzheimer's, vascular, frontotemporal, TBI, Lewy body, Parkinson's, HIV, substance, Huntington's, prion), their clinical descriptions, statistics, causes (with heavy emphasis on the genetics and neuropathology of Alzheimer's), and the limited treatment/prevention options. High-yield for MET/NIMHANS because it is disorder-dense, criteria-heavy, and overlaps neuropsychology, geriatrics, and biological psychology.

---

## Perspectives on Neurocognitive Disorders
### Terminology shift and history
- **Neurocognitive disorders (NCD)** = the **DSM-5** category name (APA, 2013) for the various forms of **dementia** and **amnestic disorders**, with **"major"** or **"mild"** subtypes; DSM-5 **retains the label "delirium."**
- Two classes reviewed: **delirium** = often temporary, confusion + disorientation; **mild/major NCD** = progressive, gradual deterioration of a range of cognitive abilities.
- Most NCDs develop **much later in life**, whereas intellectual disability and specific learning disorder are present **from birth** (Ch 14).
- Historical labels: early DSM = **"organic mental disorders"** (the word *organic* signaled presumed brain damage/dysfunction; covered so many disorders the distinction became meaningless). The traditional organic triad (**delirium, dementia, amnestic disorders**) was kept together; the others (organic mood/anxiety/personality/hallucinosis/delusional) were moved to disorders sharing their symptoms.
- After dropping *organic* → DSM-IV used **"cognitive disorders"** (predominant feature = impairment of memory, attention, perception, thinking). Problem: intellectual disability / specific learning disorder also have cognitive impairment but appear early.
- DSM-5 created the unified **neurocognitive disorders** category because of the **overlap** among dementia types (e.g., Alzheimer's) and amnestic disorder; one person may suffer multiple types simultaneously (Ganguli et al., 2011).
- Why in an abnormal psychology text (not just medicine): NCDs produce **profound changes in behavior and personality** — intense anxiety, depression, paranoia, extreme agitation, aggression — and devastate families/caregivers.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR keeps the same architecture (delirium + mild vs. major NCD across etiologic subtypes) and retains the term **major/mild neurocognitive disorder** rather than reverting to "dementia." DSM-5-TR primarily refreshed ICD-10-CM codes and clarified text; the core criteria below are unchanged from the DSM-5 the book describes.
> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 places these under **"Neurocognitive disorders"** too, listing **delirium, mild neurocognitive disorder, amnestic disorder, and dementia** — note ICD-11 **retains the word "dementia"** (e.g., *dementia due to Alzheimer disease*) where DSM-5/-TR says "major NCD." ICD-11 also keeps **amnestic disorder** as a separate stand-alone category, whereas DSM-5 folded it into the general NCD group.

## Delirium
### Clinical description and statistics
- **Delirium** = impaired **consciousness and cognition** developing over the course of **several hours or days**. One of the **earliest-recognized** mental disorders — descriptions written **>2,400 years ago**.
- Presentation: confused, disoriented, out of touch with surroundings; **cannot focus or sustain attention** on even the simplest tasks; marked **impairments in memory and language**.
- Onset is **rapid (hours to a few days)**, NOT gradual, and symptoms **fluctuate over the course of a day**.
- **Prevalence: ~20% of older adults** admitted to acute care facilities (ERs). Most prevalent among **older adults, people undergoing medical procedures, cancer patients, and people with AIDS**.
- Often thought temporary, but effects may be **more lasting**; some lapse into **coma and may die**. Push to make delirium a **"vital sign"** (with heartbeat, breathing rate, temperature, blood pressure) checked routinely in older adults.
- **Causes/triggers:** drug/poison intoxication; **withdrawal** (alcohol; sedative, hypnotic, anxiolytic drugs); infections; head injury; brain trauma. **Ecstasy (MDMA)** of particular concern. Improper medication use is a big problem for the elderly — they use more prescriptions AND eliminate drugs less efficiently → **adverse drug reactions causing hospitalization are ~6× higher** in the elderly. Believed responsible for many **falls → hip fractures**.
- In children: high fevers or certain meds can cause delirium, often mistaken for **noncompliance**.
- **Co-occurs with dementia: as many as 50%** of people with dementia have **≥1 episode of delirium**.
- In **~25% of cases** delirium can signal **the end of life**. Other triggers: **age itself**, sleep deprivation, immobility, excessive stress.
- Brain mechanism (S.-H. Choi et al., 2012, fMRI): both **lasting disruption of connectivity** (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex ↔ posterior cingulate cortex) and **reversible disruption** (thalamus ↔ reticular activating system). Ethical issue: a delirious person **cannot give informed consent** — a spouse/relative must agree; fMRI is frightening to someone disoriented.

> **DSM-5-TR — Diagnostic Criteria for Delirium (Table 15.1):**
> - **A.** Disturbance in **attention** (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain, shift) AND **awareness** (reduced orientation to environment).
> - **B.** Develops over a **short period** (hours to a few days), is a **change from baseline**, and **fluctuates in severity** during the day.
> - **C.** Additional disturbance in **cognition** (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability, or perception).
> - **D.** A and C **not better explained** by another preexisting/established/evolving NCD and do **not occur in the context of a severely reduced level of arousal (e.g., coma).**
> - **E.** Evidence (history, exam, labs) that it is a **direct physiological consequence** of another medical condition, **substance intoxication or withdrawal**, toxin exposure, or **multiple etiologies**.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 delirium criteria are essentially equivalent (disturbance of attention/awareness + other cognition, acute onset, fluctuating course, due to an underlying condition/substance). ICD-11 explicitly allows specifying delirium **superimposed on dementia**, mirroring the high comorbidity the book cites.

### Treatment
- Delirium from **alcohol/drug withdrawal** → **haloperidol** or other antipsychotics to calm the person.
- Infections, brain injury, tumors → appropriate **medical intervention**, which often resolves the accompanying delirium.
- When cause is unknown in **acute delirium** → antipsychotics **haloperidol** or **olanzapine**.
- **Recommended first line = psychosocial intervention.** Goal: **reassure** the person, help manage agitation/anxiety/hallucinations. Comfort with **familiar personal belongings** (e.g., family photographs); **include the patient in treatment decisions** to retain a sense of control. May **delay institutionalization** in elderly.

### Prevention
- Most successful in people **susceptible** to delirium. **Proper medical care** and **therapeutic drug monitoring** are key. Managed care + patient **counseling on drug use** → more appropriate prescription use among the elderly.

## Major and Mild Neurocognitive Disorders
### Overview
- **Major neurocognitive disorder** (previously **dementia**) = **gradual deterioration** of brain functioning affecting memory, judgment, language, and other advanced cognitive processes.
- **Mild neurocognitive disorder** = a **new DSM-5 disorder** created to focus attention on **early stages** of cognitive decline; person has **modest** impairments but can, **with accommodations** (lists, elaborate schedules), continue to function **independently**.
- Some causes (infection, depression) are **reversible** with treatment of the primary condition; others (Alzheimer's) are **irreversible**.
- vs. delirium: NCD has **gradual progression** (not acute onset); people with NCD are **not disoriented/confused in early stages**.
- **Causes** include: stroke, syphilis, HIV, severe head injury, toxins, and diseases such as **Parkinson's, Huntington's, and (most common) Alzheimer's**.

> **DSM-5-TR — Major NCD (Table 15.2):**
> - **A.** Evidence of **significant cognitive decline** from a previous level in ≥1 domain (**complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition**) based on (1) concern of individual/informant/clinician AND (2) **substantial impairment** preferably documented by standardized neuropsychological testing.
> - **B.** Deficits **interfere with independence** in everyday activities (at minimum need help with **complex instrumental ADLs** like paying bills, managing meds).
> - **C.** Not exclusively during a **delirium**.
> - **D.** Not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., MDD, schizophrenia).

> **DSM-5-TR — Mild NCD (Table 15.3):**
> - **A.** **Modest** cognitive decline in ≥1 domain (same domains), based on (1) concern + (2) **modest impairment** on testing.
> - **B.** Deficits do **NOT interfere with capacity for independence** (complex IADLs preserved, but **greater effort, compensatory strategies, or accommodation** may be needed).
> - **C.** Not exclusively during delirium. **D.** Not better explained by another mental disorder.
> - **Specify whether due to:** Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, Lewy body disease, vascular disease, traumatic brain injury, substance/medication use, HIV infection, prion disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, another medical condition, multiple etiologies, unspecified.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Criteria for mild vs. major NCD are unchanged in DSM-5-TR. The key distinction tested on exams = **whether deficits compromise independence in everyday/IADL activities** (major) or **not** (mild).
> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 keeps **"dementia"** for the major-severity end and **"mild neurocognitive disorder"** for the milder end, and lists the same etiologic specifiers. ICD-11 mild NCD likewise requires decline beyond normal aging that does **not** significantly interfere with independence.

### Clinical description and statistics (general NCD)
- Initial stages: **memory impairment** = inability to **register ongoing events** (can talk, recall distant past, but cannot recall the past hour). Case **Pat Summitt** (NCAA coach, diagnosed early-onset Alzheimer's at 57; coached 1974–2012, **1,098 wins**): vivid childhood memories but couldn't recall driving directions.
- **Visuospatial skills impaired.** **Agnosia** = inability to recognize and name objects (one of the most familiar symptoms). **Facial agnosia** = inability to recognize even familiar faces — distressing to family.
- General deterioration of intellectual function from impaired **memory, planning, abstract reasoning**.
- **Emotional changes:** delusions (irrational beliefs), depression, agitation, aggression, apathy — hard to separate brain-driven from frustration/discouragement-driven.
- Eventually needs near-total support; **death** often from **inactivity + other illness (e.g., pneumonia).**
- **Globally one new case of major NCD identified every 7 seconds** (Ferri et al., 2005).
- **Prevalence:** a little **>5% in people >65**, rising to **20–40% in those >85**. Among **centenarians, up to 100%** showed signs. NCD due to Alzheimer's **rarely occurs under age 45.**
- **Mild NCD prevalence** (Einstein Aging Study, Yeshiva; n = 1,944, age ≥70): **~10%** had mild NCD; **11.6%** met criteria for **mild amnestic NCD** (the more severe state of which used to be the separate DSM **amnestic disorder**). **Black men and women** at higher risk for mild NCD than white.
- **Incidence** (new cases/year) is the most reliable measure; **rate of new cases doubles every 5 years of age after 75.** Greater increases reported among **women.**
- **Costs:** caring for Alzheimer's-NCD ~**$100 billion/yr** in the US; total worldwide societal cost of major NCD **>$315 billion**; business/insurance/caregiver costs **>$140 billion** in the US alone.
- **DSM-5 lists 11 etiologic classes:** (1) Alzheimer's, (2) vascular injury, (3) frontotemporal degeneration, (4) traumatic brain injury, (5) Lewy body disease, (6) Parkinson's, (7) HIV, (8) substance use, (9) Huntington's, (10) prion disease, (11) another medical condition. **Alzheimer's ≈ half** of all NCD cases.

## Neurocognitive Disorder Due to Alzheimer's Disease
### Description and statistics
- **1907 — Alois Alzheimer** (German psychiatrist) described a **51-yr-old woman** with a "strange disease of the cerebral cortex": progressive memory impairment + behavioral/cognitive problems incl. suspiciousness. Called it an **"atypical form of senile dementia."**
- DSM-5 criteria: **multiple cognitive deficits, gradual and steady**; predominant impairment of **memory, orientation, judgment, reasoning**. Inability to integrate new info → can't learn new associations; forget important events, lose objects, narrowing interests, social isolation; later agitation, confusion, depression, anxiety, combativeness.
- **"Sundowner syndrome"** = difficulties **more pronounced late in the day** (fatigue or disturbed biological clock).
- Other cognitive disturbances: **aphasia** (language difficulty), **apraxia** (impaired motor functioning), **agnosia** (failure to recognize objects), and difficulty planning/organizing/sequencing/abstracting.
- **Definitive diagnosis** historically **only via autopsy** (characteristic brain damage). Now: brain scans + chemical tracers (**ADNI — Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative**) and **beta amyloid in spinal fluid** improve accuracy. Clinical bedside tool = simplified **mental status exam** (Mini-Mental State, **max score 30**) assessing orientation (5+5), registration (3), attention/calculation (count back from 100 by 7s, or spell "world" backward; 5), recall (3), language (9).
- **Nun Study** (Snowdon et al., 1996): writings of Catholic nuns over decades; **low "idea density"** early in life predicted Alzheimer's; among 14 autopsied, **all 5 nuns with Alzheimer's had low idea density.** Elegant (similar lifestyles ruled out confounds) but **small sample — caution against overgeneralizing.**
- **Course:** deterioration **slow early and late, more rapid in the middle stages.** Average survival **~8 years** (many live dependently >10 yrs). **Early onset** = 40s/50s; usually appears in **60s/70s.** **~50% of NCD = Alzheimer's**, >5 million Americans afflicted.
- **Education:** higher education → **delays observation of symptoms**, but those people **decline more rapidly** once symptoms start → education = a **"buffer"/reserve**, not prevention.
- **Cognitive reserve hypothesis** (biological version): the **more synapses** a person develops over life, the **more neuronal death must occur before dementia signs appear**; mental activity from education builds this reserve.
- **Sex:** Alzheimer's may be **more prevalent among women** even after adjusting for women's longer survival. Possible role of **estrogen** (women lose estrogen with age → maybe protective). BUT the **Women's Health Initiative Memory Study** found combined **estrogen + progestin (Prempro)** in women >65 **increased** Alzheimer's risk (Coker et al., 2010).
- **Race/ethnicity:** early studies suggested lower rates in Japanese, Nigerian, some Native American, Amish groups — but later work attributes much to **help-seeking and education differences.** Roughly **equal across ethnic groups** (slightly lower in American Indians in one study).

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild NCD due to Alzheimer's Disease (Table 15.4):**
> - **A.** Criteria met for major or mild NCD. **B. Insidious onset and gradual progression** in ≥1 domain (for **major**, **two** domains must be impaired).
> - **C.** Criteria met for **probable** OR **possible** Alzheimer's:
>   - *Major* — **Probable** if either (1) **causative Alzheimer's genetic mutation** (family history/genetic testing) OR (2) **all three**: (a) clear decline in **memory/learning + ≥1 other domain**, (b) **steadily progressive, gradual** decline without plateaus, (c) **no mixed etiology**. Otherwise diagnose **possible**.
>   - *Mild* — **Probable** only if a **causative genetic mutation** is present; **Possible** if no mutation AND all three: clear decline in memory/learning, steady gradual decline, no mixed etiology.
> - **D.** Not better explained by cerebrovascular/other neurodegenerative disease, substance, or another disorder.

## Vascular Neurocognitive Disorder
### Description and statistics
- Each year **500,000 people die from strokes** (stroke = disease/trauma to brain restricting or stopping blood flow); stroke is the **3rd-leading cause of death** in the US.
- **Vascular NCD** = progressive brain disorder, one of the **more common** causes of NCD. **Vascular** = blood vessels; blocked/damaged vessels deprive brain tissue of oxygen/nutrients → damage at **multiple sites** → **profile of degeneration differs person to person.**
- DSM-5 criteria emphasize **declines in speed of information processing and executive functioning** (complex decision-making) — **in contrast to Alzheimer's, where memory is the initial disturbance.**
- **Prevalence: ~1.5% at ages 70–75**, rising to **15% over age 80.** **Risk slightly higher in men** (vs. higher in women for Alzheimer's), likely due to higher male **cardiovascular disease** rates.
- **Onset more sudden** than Alzheimer's (because it follows **stroke**, which inflicts immediate brain damage). Outcome similar: eventually need nursing care, often succumb to infection (pneumonia) from weakened immunity.

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild Vascular NCD (Table 15.5):**
> - **A.** Criteria met for major/mild NCD. **B.** Features consistent with **vascular etiology**: (1) onset **temporally related** to one or more **cerebrovascular events**, OR (2) decline prominent in **complex attention (incl. processing speed) and frontal-executive function**.
> - **C.** Evidence of **cerebrovascular disease** from history/exam/neuroimaging sufficient to account for deficits. **D.** Not better explained by another brain/systemic disorder.
> - **Probable** = neuroimaging-supported parenchymal injury, OR temporal relation to documented cerebrovascular events, OR clinical + genetic evidence (e.g., **CADASIL** — cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy). **Possible** = clinical criteria met but neuroimaging unavailable / temporal relation unestablished.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 lists **"Dementia due to cerebrovascular disease"** as the parallel category and similarly emphasizes a temporal link to cerebrovascular events.

## Other Medical Conditions That Cause Neurocognitive Disorder
DSM-5 identifies **eight specific causes** besides Alzheimer's and vascular damage: frontotemporal degeneration, traumatic brain injury, Lewy body disease, Parkinson's disease, HIV infection, substance use, Huntington's disease, and prion disease. Plus a residual **"due to another medical condition"** (e.g., **normal pressure hydrocephalus** — excess water in cranium from brain shrinkage; **hypothyroidism**; **brain tumor**; **vitamin B12 deficiency**).

### Frontotemporal Neurocognitive Disorder
- Overarching term for brain disorders damaging the **frontal or temporal regions** — areas affecting **personality, language, behavior.**
- DSM-5 identifies **two variants**: declines in **behavior** (socially inappropriate actions, apathy, poor judgment) OR **language** (speech problems, word finding, naming objects).
- **Pick's disease** = a rare neurological condition (occurs in **~5%** of those with neurocognitive impairment) producing Alzheimer's-like symptoms; usually onset in **40s/50s** → an **early-onset NCD**; course ~**5–10 years**; genetic component.

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild Frontotemporal NCD (Table 15.6):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Insidious onset, gradual progression.
> - **C.** Either **(1) Behavioral variant** — ≥3 of: **behavioral disinhibition; apathy/inertia; loss of sympathy or empathy; perseverative/stereotyped/compulsive-ritualistic behavior; hyperorality and dietary changes** + prominent decline in **social cognition and/or executive abilities**; OR **(2) Language variant** — prominent decline in **language** (speech production, word finding, object naming, grammar, comprehension).
> - **D.** Relative **sparing of learning/memory and perceptual-motor** function. **E.** Not better explained by other disease.
> - **Probable** = causative genetic mutation OR disproportionate frontal/temporal involvement on neuroimaging.

### Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)
- **Severe head trauma** → lasting brain injuries → NCD. **NCD due to TBI** = symptoms persisting **≥1 week** post-trauma, incl. **executive dysfunction** (e.g., difficulty planning complex activities) and **learning/memory problems.**
- Greatest risk: **teens and young adults**, especially with **alcohol abuse** or **lower SES.** Common causes: traffic accidents, assaults, falls, suicide attempts, **bomb blasts in combat.**
- Repeated blows to the head: formerly **dementia pugilistica** (boxers/pugilists) → now **chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)**, caused by **repetitive head trauma** provoking distinctive neurodegeneration. Linked to **NFL players** (e.g., **Junior Seau**, NFL star, suicide 2012; NIH confirmed CTE on brain exam).

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild NCD due to TBI (Table 15.7):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Evidence of **TBI** (impact / rapid brain movement within skull) with ≥1 of: **loss of consciousness; posttraumatic amnesia; disorientation/confusion; neurological signs** (neuroimaging injury, new seizures, worsening seizure disorder, visual field cuts, anosmia, hemiparesis).
> - **C.** NCD presents **immediately after** the TBI or after recovery of consciousness and **persists past the acute post-injury period.**

### Lewy Body Disease
- **Second most common type** of NCD **after Alzheimer's.** **Lewy bodies** = microscopic **protein deposits** that damage brain cells over time.
- Signs come on **gradually**: impaired **alertness and attention**, **vivid visual hallucinations**, **motor impairment** like Parkinson's (overlap with Parkinson's NCD).

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild NCD with Lewy Bodies (Table 15.8):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Insidious onset, gradual progression. **C.** Combination of **core** + **suggestive** features.
> - **Core features:** (a) **fluctuating cognition** with pronounced variations in attention/alertness; (b) **recurrent well-formed, detailed visual hallucinations**; (c) **spontaneous parkinsonism** with onset **subsequent to** the cognitive decline.
> - **Suggestive features:** (a) meets criteria for **REM sleep behavior disorder**; (b) **severe neuroleptic sensitivity.**
> - **Probable** = two core features OR one suggestive + ≥1 core. **Possible** = one core feature OR ≥1 suggestive.

### Parkinson's Disease
- Degenerative brain disorder affecting **~1 in every 1,000 people worldwide** (e.g., **Michael J. Fox**, **Janet Reno**).
- Motor signs: **stooped posture, bradykinesia (slow body movements), tremors, jerky walking**, soft monotone voice. Caused by damage to **dopamine pathways** (dopamine needed for complex movement → its reduction → uncontrolled movement, tremor, weakness). **Lewy bodies** also present.
- **~75%** of those who survive **>10 years** with Parkinson's develop NCD; rate **4–6×** the general population.

> **DSM-5-TR — NCD due to Parkinson's Disease (Table 15.9):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Occurs in the setting of **established Parkinson's disease.** **C.** Insidious onset, gradual progression. **D.** Not attributable to another condition.
> - **Probably due to Parkinson's** if **both**: (1) no mixed etiology AND (2) Parkinson's **clearly precedes** the NCD onset. **Possibly** if only 1 or 2 is met.

### HIV Infection
- **HIV-1** (causes AIDS) → **NCD due to HIV infection**; impairment seems **independent** of opportunistic infections (the virus itself is responsible).
- Early symptoms: **cognitive slowness, impaired attention, forgetfulness**; also clumsiness, **tremors/leg weakness**, apathy, social withdrawal.
- With **HAART (highly active antiretroviral therapy)**, **<10%** of patients now experience NCD (down from highly common). HIV-1 = relatively small % of NCD cases.

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild NCD due to HIV Infection (Table 15.10):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Documented **HIV infection.** **C.** Not better explained by **non-HIV conditions** (e.g., progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, cryptococcal meningitis). **D.** Not attributable to another condition/mental disorder.

### Cortical vs. Subcortical Dementia (high-yield distinction, Table 15.2 / Cummings)
- **Subcortical dementia** = affects **inner brain areas below the cortex**; includes NCD due to **Parkinson's, HIV, Huntington's.** **Cortical** = e.g., **Alzheimer's.**

| Characteristic | Alzheimer's (cortical) | Subcortical dementias |
|---|---|---|
| **Language** | **Aphasia** (difficulty articulating speech) | **No aphasia** |
| **Memory** | Both **recall AND recognition** impaired | **Recall impaired; recognition normal/less impaired** |
| **Visuospatial skills** | Impaired | Impaired |
| **Mood** | **Less** severe depression/anxiety | **More** severe depression/anxiety |
| **Motor speed** | Normal | **Slowed** |
| **Coordination** | Normal until late | **Impaired** |

### Huntington's Disease
- **Genetic** disorder that initially affects **motor movements**, typically **chorea** (involuntary limb movements). Patients can live **~20 years** after first signs.
- Only a **portion (20%–80%)** develop NCD, though some believe **all** would if they lived long enough. Follows **subcortical** pattern.
- **Autosomal dominant**: ~**50% of offspring** of an affected adult develop it. **Nancy Wexler** studied the world's largest affected extended family in **Venezuela** (she lost her mother, three uncles, maternal grandfather). Gene mapped to **chromosome 4** (Gusella et al., 1983) then identified (1993). **Single gene** (unusual; most inherited mental disorders are **polygenic**).

> **DSM-5-TR — Major/Mild NCD due to Huntington's Disease (Table 15.11):** A. Criteria met. B. Insidious onset, gradual progression. C. **Clinically established Huntington's** OR risk based on **family history/genetic testing.** D. Not attributable to another condition.

### Prion Disease
- Rare **progressive neurodegenerative** disorder caused by **prions** — proteins that **reproduce themselves** and damage brain cells. Thought by some to have **no DNA/RNA** (so not destroyable by chemicals/radiation).
- **No known treatment; always fatal.** Not contagious normally — contracted via **cannibalism (causing kuru)** or **accidental inoculation** (e.g., infected blood transfusion).
- **Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD)** = affects **~1 in a million.** New variant (10 cases) possibly linked to **bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease")** → led to UK beef export ban; link not definitive.

> **DSM-5-TR — NCD due to Prion Disease (Table 15.12):** A. Criteria met. B. Insidious onset, **rapid progression common.** C. Motor features (**myoclonus or ataxia**) or biomarker evidence. D. Not attributable to another condition.

### Substance/Medication-Induced Neurocognitive Disorder
- Prolonged drug use (esp. with poor diet) can damage the brain → NCD that **persists beyond intoxication/withdrawal.**
- **As many as 7% of alcohol-dependent** individuals meet NCD criteria. Other culprits: **inhalants** (glue, gasoline), **sedative/hypnotic/anxiolytic** drugs. Brain damage can be **permanent** with Alzheimer's-like symptoms.
- DSM-5 criteria: **memory impairment + ≥1 of**: **aphasia** (language disturbance), **apraxia** (can't carry out motor activities despite intact motor function), **agnosia** (failure to recognize objects despite intact senses), or **executive dysfunction.**

> **DSM-5-TR — Substance/Medication-Induced Major/Mild NCD (Table 15.13):**
> - **A.** Criteria met. **B.** Impairments **not exclusively during delirium** and **persist beyond** usual intoxication/acute withdrawal. **C.** Substance/medication and **duration/extent** capable of producing the impairment. **D.** Temporal course **consistent with timing of use and abstinence** (deficits stable or improve after abstinence). **E.** Not attributable to another condition/mental disorder.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 captures these as **"Dementia due to psychoactive substances including medications"** and likewise distinguishes persisting impairment from the acute effects of intoxication/withdrawal.

## Causes of Neurocognitive Disorder
### Biological influences (focus on Alzheimer's neuropathology)
- A **word of scientific caution:** Alzheimer's findings appear almost daily; like premature "single gene for bipolar/schizophrenia/alcoholism" claims, they must be **replicated.**
- **Smoking–Alzheimer's negative correlation** (Brenner et al., 1993): smokers *appeared* less likely to develop Alzheimer's — likely a **differential survival** artifact (nonsmokers live longer → more likely to reach the age Alzheimer's appears). Smoking may **exacerbate** degeneration, causing affected smokers to die earlier.
- **Two hallmark neuropathologies** (from Alzheimer's own autopsy):
  - **Neurofibrillary tangles** = tangled, strandlike filaments **within brain cells**; occur in **everyone** with Alzheimer's.
  - **Amyloid plaques** (a.k.a. **neuritic/senile plaques**) = gummy protein deposits **between neurons**; core is a waxy **amyloid beta (Aβ)** peptide. Present in healthy older adults too, but **far fewer.** Both accumulate over years.
  - Brains also **atrophy (shrink)** more than normal aging — but shrinkage has many causes, so **only tangles + plaques confirm diagnosis.**
- **Genetics (Table 15.3 — multiple genes):**
  - **Chromosome 21 — APP gene** (amyloid precursor protein), onset **43–59 yrs**; link found first via **Down syndrome** (extra chromosome 21 → extra APP gene → high Alzheimer's rate).
  - **Chromosome 14 — Presenilin 1**, onset **33–60 yrs** (early onset; Pat Summitt's form).
  - **Chromosome 1 — Presenilin 2**, onset **50–90 yrs**.
  - **Chromosome 19 — apo E4**, onset **~60 yrs** (late onset).
  - **Deterministic genes** (APP, Presenilin 1 & 2) → **nearly 100%** chance of Alzheimer's, but **rare** in population.
  - **Susceptibility genes** (**apolipoprotein E4 / apo E4**) → only **slightly increase** risk but are **common** → interfering with apo E4 could help many.
- **Mechanisms of amyloid buildup:** (1) **APP** is broken down into the amyloid protein in plaques (gene on chromosome 21); (2) **apolipoprotein E (apo E)** transports cholesterol incl. amyloid — three forms **apo E2, apo E3, apo E4.** Late-onset (most common) Alzheimer's linked to **apo E4** (chromosome 19). Among Alzheimer's patients **with** family history, **majority** carry ≥1 apo E4 gene; **without** family history **~64%** carry it; only **31%** of nonaffected carry it. **Two apo E4 genes:** up to **90%** developed Alzheimer's, and mean onset age dropped from **84 → 68 years.**
- **Gene–environment interactions:** apo E4 + **stressful environment** → more cognitive decline; in African Americans, **low cholesterol reduced risk only in non-apo E4 carriers**; **physical exercise** reduced risk **only in non-apo E4 carriers** → suggests prevention via lowering cholesterol + exercising.
- **External contributors / stressors:** **head trauma** (repeated blows → CTE; **boxers/fighters with apo E4** at greater risk; NFL links); **diabetes, high blood pressure, herpes simplex virus-1.**

### Psychological and social influences
- Psychosocial factors don't directly cause brain deterioration but **influence onset and course.** Lifestyle determines exposure to causes (substance abuse → NCD; diet/exercise/stress → cardiovascular disease → vascular NCD).
- **Cultural factors:** hypertension/strokes prevalent among **African Americans and certain Asian Americans** → more vascular NCD. **Prion exposure via ritual cannibalism in Papua New Guinea → kuru.** Head trauma + malnutrition common in preindustrial rural societies; deficiency of **vitamins B9 and B12** → NCD.
- **Course:** education delays onset; certain skills help coping. In some cultures (e.g., **Chinese**) dementia symptoms viewed as **normal aging** → NCD goes undetected for years.

## Treatment
- Unlike most disorders, NCD treatment prospects are **poor.** The brain has **plasticity** (billions of neurons compensate), but beyond a limit of neuronal loss, vital functioning is disrupted; **with extensive damage, no known treatment restores lost abilities.**
- **Three treatment goals:** (1) **prevent** conditions that bring on NCD (substance abuse, strokes); (2) **delay onset** of symptoms for better quality of life; (3) **help individuals + caregivers cope.** Most effort = goals 2 and 3.
- **Caregiver toll:** **>23%** of caregivers have symptoms of an anxiety disorder; **10% clinically depressed**; use more psychotropic meds; report **stress symptoms at 3× the normal rate.** Caregiving stress may itself **increase caregiver risk of NCD** (Norton et al., 2010).

### Biological treatments
- Reversible if caught early: NCD from **infectious disease, nutritional deficiency, depression.** No cure for the disorders behind the **vast majority** of cases (stroke, Parkinson's, Huntington's untreatable at primary-disorder level).
- Promising/experimental: **glial cell–derived neurotrophic factor** (may preserve/restore neurons); **stem cell (fetal brain tissue) transplants** (preliminary); new **clot-prevention drugs** for stroke-related NCD.
- **Alzheimer's-targeting drugs:** most show **initial** benefit but **no long-term** improvement in placebo-controlled studies.
  - **Cholinesterase inhibitors** — prevent breakdown of **acetylcholine** (deficient in Alzheimer's) → more acetylcholine available → modest cognitive gains (back to ~6 months earlier), **not permanent**, and lost if drug stopped (≈ 3/4 stop due to side effects like **liver damage, nausea**): **donepezil (Aricept), rivastigmine (Exelon), galantamine (Reminyl)**; **tacrine hydrochloride (Cognex)** rarely used now (**liver damage** risk).
  - Newer drugs target **beta amyloid (plaques).**
- Symptomatic meds: **SSRIs** for depression/anxiety; **antipsychotics** for agitation.
- **Failed/abandoned approaches:** **Ginkgo biloba** (maidenhair) — initial modest memory benefit **not replicated**; **vitamin E** — high doses (**2,000 IU/day**) delayed progression in moderately severe cases but **did not prevent** disease, and high doses may **increase mortality** → **no longer recommended.** **Exercise** = modest slowing.
- **Vaccines** targeting the **Aβ** overproduction process: early human trials abandoned (**severe brain inflammation**); newer animal/human research shows promise. Done first in **transgenic mice** (DNA altered to produce the same small proteins; mice age fast — a **22-month mouse ≈ 65-yr-old human**); promising mouse results → small human studies.
- **Figure 15.2 (Becker et al., 2007):** medical + nonmedical interventions **compress** the period of severe impairment — typical course = **3–5 years** of severe impairment before death; with intervention, **less time (≤1 year)** severely impaired, living more fully longer (still inevitable death). = **compression of morbidity.**

### Psychosocial treatments
- Teach **compensatory skills**: **"memory wallets"** (Michelle Bourgeois, 2007/1992) — index cards with declarative statements ("My husband John and I have 3 children") → improve conversations; tablet computers that "speak" for the person.
- **Cognitive stimulation** — practicing learning/memory skills (word games, famous-face recognition, making change) → **delays onset of more severe effects** and maintains cognitive activity/quality of life.
- **Advanced NCD problems:** can't feed/bathe/dress/communicate, **wander** and get lost, public sexual behavior (e.g., masturbation), agitation, physical violence.
- **Wandering** → danger; physical/medical **restraint or sedation** has its own risks and worsens loss of control. Alternatives: navigation **cues**, **"smart home" surveillance technology** (raises **privacy/ethical** concerns).
- **Aggression** → communication-skills training (like autism interventions) may reduce it; **caregivers given assertiveness training** (Table 15.4 sample assertive responses) to avoid passive acceptance OR angry retaliation. Caregiver risk of **elder abuse** (withholding food/medication, physical abuse) — most common among caregivers of cognitively impaired elders.
- **Supportive counseling** for families (frustration, depression, guilt, loss). **Cultural coping differences** (rural Alabama: white caregivers used acceptance + humor; black caregivers used religion + denial). Early caregiver needs = info on causes/treatment, financial/legal issues; later = managing behavior, communication, hospitalization decisions, bereavement.
- **Overall outlook:** optimistic for **slowing (not stopping)** decline; meds give some recovery but don't halt deterioration; psychological help best in **earlier stages**; major emphasis on **supporting caregivers.**

### Prevention
- Hard to study (need long follow-up). Major **Sweden** study (socialized medicine; **1,810 participants >75**, followed **~13 years**; Fratiglioni et al., 2007) → three modifiable conclusions: **(1) control your blood pressure, (2) do not smoke, (3) lead an active physical and social life.** Genetics can't be changed, so these are the levers to **decrease NCD risk.**

## DSM Controversies: Is Normal Aging a Mental Disorder?
- DSM-5 introduced **mild neurocognitive disorder** to capture decline more pronounced than expected for age, enabling **early intervention** (consistent with brain-scan/biomarker advances).
- **Concern 1:** the **mild vs. major** distinction is hard. DSM-5 ties it to test performance roughly **1–2 standard deviations below age expectation**, but below-average ≠ declining unless **baseline pre-decline testing** exists — and routine cognitive testing isn't done until concern arises (Frances, 2010).
- **Concern 2:** adding disorders (mild NCD, premenstrual dysphoric disorder) + broadening definitions risks **most people qualifying** for some diagnosis → **labeling/medicating normal age-related forgetting.** Consequences: pharma incentive to expand customers; legal use of "mental illness" as mitigation. DSM-5 = a **work in progress.**

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR retains **mild NCD** (and PMDD) despite these critiques. The controversy about over-pathologizing normal aging persists; DSM-5-TR did not alter the mild/major NCD threshold.
