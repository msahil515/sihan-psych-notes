# Ch 14: Neurodevelopmental Disorders

This chapter covers the disorders that reveal themselves in a clinically significant way during a child's developing years but are typically **lifelong**: ADHD, specific learning disorder, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and intellectual disability (ID), plus the communication and motor disorders. Because they are all presumed to be **neurologically based**, DSM-5 groups them as **neurodevelopmental disorders**. For the exam, master each disorder's clinical description, the DSM-5(-TR) criteria, statistics, genetic/neurobiological causes, and the heavy emphasis on **behavioral / educational treatment** (drugs play a limited core role except in ADHD). Note also the major DSM-5 reorganizations (collapsing the pervasive developmental disorders into ASD; renaming "mental retardation" to intellectual disability; merging the separate learning disorders).

---

## Overview of Neurodevelopmental Disorders

### Developmental framework
- Almost all disorders in the book are **developmental** in that they change over time; most **originate in childhood** even if full presentation appears later. Calling them "childhood disorders" is misleading because they often **persist into adulthood**.
- **Developmental psychopathology** (Ch 2): the study of how disorders arise and change with time. Childhood is critical because the **brain changes significantly for years after birth** and key social, emotional, cognitive competencies develop.
- Development follows a **sequential pattern**: the child masters one skill before acquiring the next. Therefore **any disruption in early skills disrupts later skills** (e.g., autism's disruption of early social development may prevent normal relationships and, secondarily, communication).
- **Caution against over-pathologizing normal development:** **echolalia** (repeating others' speech) was once thought a sign of autism, but in typically developing children it is a normal **intermediate step in language development**. In ASD it merely reflects delayed language, not a symptom unique to the disorder (Tager-Flusberg et al., 2009).
- Biological and psychosocial influences **continuously interact**; even biologically based disorders (ADHD, ASD) present differently in each individual, and change at either level can reduce impact.

### Communication and motor disorders (Table 14.1)
- **Childhood-Onset Fluency Disorder (stuttering):** disturbance in speech fluency — repeating syllables/words, prolonging sounds, obvious pauses, word substitutions. Occurs **twice as often in boys**; begins most often by age 6, **98% of cases before age 10**; ~**80%** who stutter before school no longer stutter after a year of school. Etiology: rather than anxiety causing it, stuttering **makes people socially anxious**; multiple brain pathways and genetic influences. Treatment: parent counseling; **regulated-breathing method** (stop speaking at a stutter, take a deep breath); **altered auditory feedback**; self-monitoring.
- **Language Disorder:** limited speech in all situations; **expressive language significantly below receptive language** (receptive usually average). Occurs in **10–15% of children under 3**; nearly **5x more likely in boys**. Etiology: unfounded psychological theory (parents don't speak enough); biological theory of **middle ear infection**. May be **self-correcting**.
- **Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder:** difficulties with **social aspects of verbal and nonverbal communication** (verbosity, prosody, excessive topic-switching, dominating conversations). **Does NOT have the restricted/repetitive behaviors of ASD.** Treatment: individualized **social skills training** (modeling, role-playing, conversation rules). *New to DSM-5.*
- **Tourette's Disorder:** involuntary motor movements (tics, e.g., head twitching) or vocalizations (grunts) occurring in rapid succession, suddenly, in idiosyncratic/stereotyped ways; vocal tics may include involuntary repetition of obscenities (**coprolalia**). Up to **20% of children** show some tics; **1–10 per 1,000** have Tourette's; usually develops **before age 14**; high comorbidity with **ADHD and OCD**. Etiology: likely **multiple vulnerability genes**. Treatment: self-monitoring, relaxation training, **habit reversal**.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 places these under "neurodevelopmental disorders" too. Tic disorders (including Tourette syndrome) in ICD-11 are cross-listed with diseases of the nervous system. **Developmental language disorder** and **developmental speech sound / fluency disorders** are the ICD-11 equivalents of DSM's language and fluency disorders.

## Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

### Clinical description
- One of the **most common reasons children are referred for mental health services** in the US.
- Core features: difficulty **sustaining attention**, **motor hyperactivity** (fidgety, can't sit still), and **impulsivity** (acting without thinking, blurting answers).
- DSM-5 differentiates **two symptom categories**: (1) **Inattention** (not listening, losing items, careless mistakes, not attending to details); (2) **Hyperactivity and impulsivity** (fidgeting, can't sit, "on the go," blurting answers, trouble waiting turns). **Either** the inattention set **or** the hyperactivity/impulsivity set must be present to diagnose.
- Secondary problems: **academic decline**, peer rejection/unpopularity. Genetic research suggests ADHD and learning disabilities may **share a common biological cause**; a **COMT gene polymorphism + low birth weight** predicts later behavior problems.

> **DSM-5-TR — Diagnostic Criteria for ADHD (Table 14.1):**
> **A.** Persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity interfering with functioning/development, characterized by (1) and/or (2):
> **(1) Inattention:** ≥6 symptoms persisting ≥6 months, inconsistent with developmental level (e.g., fails to attend to detail/careless mistakes; difficulty sustaining attention; doesn't seem to listen; doesn't follow through/finish; difficulty organizing; avoids sustained mental effort; loses things; easily distracted; forgetful). **For age 17+, at least 5 symptoms required.**
> **(2) Hyperactivity & impulsivity:** ≥6 symptoms persisting ≥6 months (fidgets/taps/squirms; leaves seat; runs/climbs inappropriately [adults: feels restless]; can't play quietly; "on the go"/"driven by a motor"; talks excessively; blurts answers; difficulty waiting turn; interrupts/intrudes). **For age 17+, at least 5 symptoms required.**
> **B.** Several symptoms present **prior to age 12**.
> **C.** Symptoms present in **two or more settings** (home, school/work, etc.).
> **D.** Clear evidence symptoms interfere with/reduce quality of social, academic, or occupational functioning.
> **E.** Not exclusively during schizophrenia/psychosis and not better explained by another disorder.
> **Specify presentation:** Combined (A1 + A2 met); Predominantly inattentive (A1 only); Predominantly hyperactive/impulsive (A2 only).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** Criteria are essentially unchanged from DSM-5. DSM-5 already moved the age-of-onset from "before 7" (DSM-IV) to **before age 12** and allowed ADHD to be diagnosed alongside **autism spectrum disorder** (a DSM-IV exclusion). DSM-5-TR refined associated-features/prevalence wording and updated ICD-10-CM codes (F90.x).

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 lists ADHD among neurodevelopmental disorders with the same three presentations (predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined). ICD-10 had called it **hyperkinetic disorder** with a more restrictive definition; ICD-11 now aligns much more closely with DSM.

### Statistics
- Worldwide prevalence ~**5.2%** of children across all regions (Polanczyk et al., 2007) — important because it counters claims that ADHD is just a Western cultural construct. Best data: **3–9%** of the worldwide population meet criteria with significant interference.
- US **over-diagnosis** concern: ~**11% of US children aged 4–17** labeled with ADHD (CDC, 2011–2012).
- **Boys 3x more likely** to be diagnosed than girls (gap widens in clinic samples). Girls with ADHD show more **"internalizing"** behaviors (anxiety, depression); boys more aggressive/disruptive → more referral. Concern that criteria, derived mostly from boys, may not capture girls (Nadeau, Crawford).
- Children first identified as different around **age 3–4** (active, mischievous, slow to toilet train, oppositional). About **half** have ongoing difficulties into adulthood. Over time impulsivity declines but **inattention persists**; adolescents at greater risk for **pregnancy, STIs, driving problems (crashes, speeding, license suspension)**.
- **Klein et al. (2012)** 33-year follow-up of 200+ boys: **84% employed** but in lower positions; **2.5 fewer years of education**; more likely divorced, with substance use problems and **antisocial personality disorder**; more risky driving, STDs, head injury, ER admissions.
- **Comorbidity/overlap:** **oppositional defiant disorder (ODD)** (loses temper, argues, deliberately annoys, spiteful/vindictive), **conduct disorder** (precursor to antisocial PD), and **bipolar disorder** all overlap with ADHD and complicate diagnosis.

### Causes
- **Highly genetic**; environmental influence relatively small vs. other disorders. **Multiple genes** responsible. Mutations may create extra gene copies or deletions — **copy number variants (CNVs)**.
- Main neurochemical focus: **dopamine** (also norepinephrine, serotonin, GABA). Strong evidence implicates the **dopamine D4 receptor gene, the dopamine transporter gene (DAT1), and the dopamine D5 receptor gene**. **DAT1** is key because **methylphenidate (Ritalin) inhibits it**, increasing available dopamine.
- **Endophenotype** search: poor **"inhibitory control"** (inability to stop responding when signaled) is common in children with ADHD and their unaffected relatives — a possible genetic marker.
- **Gene–environment interaction:** children with the **DAT1 genotype** were more likely to show ADHD if their **mothers smoked during pregnancy**; maternal smoking is one of the more consistent findings (though SES confounds it). Also implicated: maternal stress, alcohol use, parental marital instability, pregnancy complications, low birth weight.
- **Brain:** overall brain volume **3–4% smaller**; affected areas involve **self-organizational abilities**; changes less pronounced in medicated children. Old labels "minimal brain damage / dysfunction" reflect the long-held brain-damage view.
- **Toxins/diet:** little support for allergens/food additives historically (Feingold diet controversial); some evidence of a **small measurable impact of artificial food colors + sodium benzoate** on hyperactivity in young children (McCann et al., 2007); possible role of **pesticides**.
- **Psychosocial:** negative responses from parents/teachers/peers → low self-esteem, negative self-image, impaired friendships. Integration of biological + psychological needed for treatment.

### Treatment of ADHD
- Two fronts: **psychosocial and biological**. Current guidance: try **parent/teacher-delivered behavioral interventions first** for young children before medication.
- **Psychosocial interventions:** behavioral programs (increase time seated, work completed, appropriate peer play); **reinforcement programs** (reward improvements, lose rewards for misbehavior); **parent education** (respond constructively, structure the day); **social skills training**. For adults: **CBT** to reduce distractibility and improve organization.
- **Biological interventions:** **stimulants** (first used 1937, Bradley). >**4 million US children** treated. **Methylphenidate (Ritalin), amphetamine (Adderall)**; non-stimulants **atomoxetine (Strattera)** (a selective **norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitor**, no "highs"), **guanfacine (Tenex)**, **clonidine**. Stimulants reinforce the brain's ability to focus attention; same effect in people with and without ADHD at low doses (paradoxical calming). Imipramine and clonidine work on the norepinephrine/dopamine systems too.
- **Concerns:** stimulant **abuse potential** (Ritalin/Adderall abused for elation, reduced fatigue), misperception that they're harmless, increased later substance-abuse risk; side effects = **insomnia, drowsiness, irritability**; effects don't last when discontinued; many respond in attention but not in academics/social skills.
- **Psychopharmacogenetics:** study of how genetic makeup influences drug response. Children with the **adrenergic alpha-2A receptor gene (ADRA2A) defect** showed a strong positive response to methylphenidate (esp. inattention). Promises to replace trial-and-error dosing but raises **privacy/confidentiality/discrimination** concerns.
- **Combined approach — MTA study** (NIMH, Jensen et al., 2001): **579 children**, 14 months, 4 groups (community care; medication management [usually methylphenidate]; intensive behavioral treatment; combination). Findings: **combination and medication-alone superior** to behavioral-alone and community care **for core ADHD symptoms**; combination showed **slight advantages** for non-core problems (social skills, academics, parent–child relations, oppositional behavior, anxiety/depression). Controversy over whether combination beats medication alone (behavioral treatment was faded over time).

## Specific Learning Disorder

### Clinical description
- **Performance substantially below expected** given the person's **age, IQ score, and education** — "**unexpected underachievement**." Disability must **not** be caused by sensory difficulty (sight/hearing) or by poor/absent instruction.
- **DSM-5 merged** the previously separate **DSM-IV-TR disorders of reading, mathematics, and written expression** into one **specific learning disorder** (with specifiers), due to significant overlap — encourages a broader view of the individual's learning style. Clinicians rate **severity**.
- Historically defined as a **discrepancy of >2 standard deviations** between achievement and IQ. The discrepancy model is controversial (delay before the gap becomes measurable). Alternative: **response to intervention (RTI)** — diagnose when the child's response to a known-effective intervention (e.g., an early reading program) is significantly inferior to peers; provides an early warning system.

> **DSM-5-TR — Diagnostic Criteria for Specific Learning Disorder (Table 14.2):**
> **A.** Difficulty learning/using academic skills, ≥1 symptom persisting ≥6 months despite targeted interventions: inaccurate/slow word reading; difficulty understanding meaning of what is read; spelling difficulties; written-expression difficulties; difficulties mastering number sense/facts/calculation; difficulties with mathematical reasoning.
> **B.** Affected skills **substantially and quantifiably below** those expected for chronological age, causing significant interference (confirmed by individually administered standardized achievement measures + clinical assessment). For age 17+, a documented history of impairing learning difficulties may substitute.
> **C.** Learning difficulties begin during **school-age years** but may not fully manifest until demands exceed capacity (timed tests, heavy loads).
> **D.** Not better accounted for by intellectual disability, uncorrected vision/hearing, other mental/neurological disorders, psychosocial adversity, lack of language proficiency, or inadequate instruction.
> **Specify:** with impairment in **reading** (word reading accuracy, rate/fluency, comprehension); in **written expression** (spelling, grammar/punctuation, clarity/organization); in **mathematics** (number sense, fact memorization, calculation, math reasoning).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5 was the version that combined the three separate DSM-IV-TR learning disorders into a single specific learning disorder. DSM-5-TR keeps this structure; the terms **dyslexia** and **dyscalculia** are noted as alternative terms for the reading- and mathematics-impairment specifiers. Updated ICD-10-CM codes (F81.x).

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 uses **"developmental learning disorder"** with specifiers for impairment in reading, written expression, and mathematics — closely paralleling the DSM-5 single-category approach.

### Statistics
- ~**6.5 million students aged 3–21** received services (2009–2010, US). Diagnosis frequency higher in **wealthier regions** (better access → more identification; Figure 14.1).
- Racial differences (~1% white vs. 2.6% black children receiving services in 2001) were related to **economic status, not ethnicity**.
- **Reading disorders are the most common**, occurring in **4–10%** of the general population. **Mathematics disorder ~1%**. Limited data on written-expression prevalence. Boys and girls **may be equally affected** by reading disorder (older view that boys predominate is outdated).
- Students with learning disorders: more likely to **drop out**, be **unemployed**, and to have **suicidal thoughts and attempt suicide** — mitigated by supportive caring adults and accommodations.

### Causes
- **Genetic, neurobiological, and environmental** factors. Learning disorders **run in families**; twin/family studies confirm heritability. Many genetic effects are **not specific** — the same genes affect learning across reading, math, and writing domains (Plomin & Kovas).
- Reading disorders broken into: **word recognition** (decoding single words — sometimes called **dyslexia**), **fluency** (smooth/automatic reading), and **comprehension** (getting meaning). Genes on chromosomes **1, 2, 3, 6, 11, 12, 15, 18** repeatedly linked. **Home reading habits** can lessen the genetic impact (reading to at-risk children).
- **Brain:** three left-hemisphere areas implicated in dyslexia/word recognition — **Broca's area** (articulation, word analysis), **left parietotemporal area** (word analysis), **left occipitotemporal area** (recognizing word form). The **intraparietal sulcus** is critical for number sense → implicated in **mathematics disorder**. No specific deficit identified for written-expression disorder.
- Reading disorders diagnosed more in **English-speaking countries**; cross-language research (English/French/Italian; Paulesu et al., 2001) found all had **reduced left-temporal-lobe activity**, but English/French may simply be harder to read.
- **Psychological/motivational** factors (SES, cultural and parental expectations, child management, school support) heavily influence outcome — e.g., Alice succeeded with reading-aloud and note-taking strategies.

### Treatment of learning disorders
- Primarily **educational intervention**; drug (stimulant) treatment only for **comorbid ADHD**.
- Two broad categories: **(1) specific skills instruction** (vocabulary, finding main idea, finding facts) and **(2) strategy instruction** (cognitive skills via decision-making and critical thinking).
- **Direct Instruction** (research-supported): **systematic instruction** (scripted lessons, small groups by progress), **teaching for mastery** (until all concepts understood), constant assessment, plan modification.
- Brain-imaging evidence (Temple et al., 2003): after **8 weeks of intensive computer-based auditory/language training**, children with reading disorders improved reading **and** their brains began functioning like good readers — behavioral intervention can change brain function.

## Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

### Overview & DSM-5 reorganization
- ASD at its core **affects how one perceives and socializes with others**.
- **DSM-5 collapsed** most former **"pervasive developmental disorders"** — **autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, and childhood disintegrative disorder** — into the single category **ASD**.
- **Rett disorder** (a genetic condition affecting mostly females) is now diagnosed as **ASD "associated with Rett syndrome" / "associated with MeCP2 mutation."**
- **"Not otherwise specified"** designation was **deleted**.
- **Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder** is new to DSM-5: the social-communication difficulties of ASD **without** the restricted/repetitive behaviors; some former PDD-NOS cases fall here.

### Clinical description — two symptom clusters
1. **Impairments in social communication and social interaction** (across multiple contexts), defined by three required aspects:
   - **Deficits in social-emotional reciprocity** (failure of back-and-forth interaction, reduced sharing of interests/emotions/affect). In more severe ASD: inability to engage in **joint attention** (e.g., looking from toy to caregiver and back to share interest). In milder ASD: appearing self-focused.
   - **Deficits in nonverbal communicative behaviors** (poor eye contact, body language, gestures; lacking facial expressions; abnormal **prosody** — tone of voice).
   - **Deficits in developing/maintaining/understanding relationships** (difficulty adjusting behavior to context, sharing imaginative play, making friends; absence of interest in peers).
   - **All three** must be present. **~25%** do not develop speech sufficient to communicate needs; some show **echolalia** (repeating words AND intonation). Eye-tracking study (Klin et al., 2002): a man with ASD scanned **nonsocial** parts of a scene (mouth, jacket) while a man without ASD looked **eye-to-eye** — suggesting reduced interest in social situations.
2. **Restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities** (≥2 of):
   - **Stereotyped/repetitive movements, use of objects, or speech** (simple motor stereotypies, lining up toys, echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases).
   - **Insistence on sameness**, inflexible routines, ritualized patterns ("**maintenance of sameness**" — extreme distress at small changes).
   - **Highly restricted, fixated interests** abnormal in intensity/focus (e.g., memorizing airline schedules, ZIP codes).
   - **Hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input** (indifference to pain/temperature, adverse response to sounds/textures, fascination with lights/movement).

> **DSM-5-TR — Diagnostic Criteria for ASD (Table 14.3):**
> **A.** Persistent **deficits in social communication and social interaction** across multiple contexts: (1) social-emotional reciprocity; (2) nonverbal communicative behaviors; (3) developing/maintaining/understanding relationships.
> **B.** **Restricted, repetitive patterns** — ≥2 of: stereotyped/repetitive movements/speech; insistence on sameness; restricted fixated interests; sensory hyper/hyporeactivity.
> **C.** Symptoms present in the **early developmental period** (may be masked later by learned strategies).
> **D.** Symptoms cause **clinically significant impairment**.
> **E.** Not better explained by **intellectual disability or global developmental delay** (the two frequently co-occur; for comorbid diagnosis, social communication should be below the general developmental level).
> **Severity levels** (rated separately for social communication and for restricted/repetitive behaviors): **Level 1 — "Requiring support," Level 2 — "Requiring substantial support," Level 3 — "Requiring very substantial support."** Levels are described **qualitatively** (no quantitative equivalent), making mid-range cases hard to rate.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** ASD criteria are essentially unchanged from DSM-5. DSM-5-TR clarified wording in **Criterion A** to read "as manifested by **all** of the following" (emphasizing all three social-communication deficits are required) and refined associated-features/prevalence text. The collapsing of Asperger's, autistic disorder, etc., into ASD is the major change (introduced in DSM-5). Updated ICD-10-CM code F84.0.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 also uses a single **"autism spectrum disorder"** category (Asperger's eliminated), and adds explicit qualifiers for **with/without disorder of intellectual development** and **with/without impairment of functional language** — a more dimensional descriptor scheme than DSM-5's three support levels.

### Statistics
- Once thought rare (~**1 in 10,000**); 2013 CDC estimate ~**1 in 50** US school-aged children. Rise largely due to **DSM changes** and **increased awareness**, though environmental factors (prenatal toxins) cannot be ruled out.
- **Male-to-female ~4.4 to 1.** Universal phenomenon (identified worldwide — Sweden, Japan, Russia, Hong Kong).
- ~**38%** of individuals with ASD have **intellectual disability** (IQ < 70 + adaptive deficits + onset before 18). **Higher IQ and better language skills = better prognosis.**

### Causes — Psychological and Social Dimensions (presented first, departing from usual format)
- **Historically blamed on failed parenting:** parents described as "perfectionistic, cold, aloof" ("**refrigerator mother**" idea, Kanner), high SES/IQ. **Devastating and false.** Better research shows parents of children with autism don't differ substantially from parents of children without disabilities.
- Theories based on unusual speech (avoiding first-person pronouns; saying "He wants something to drink") suggested a **lack of self-awareness** — also debunked; some people with ASD **do** have self-awareness (follows a developmental progression; self-concept lacking only when cognitive delays co-occur).
- **Echolalia** myth corrected: it is part of typically developing language (Dawson, Mottron, & Gernsbacher).
- **Savant skills** (e.g., *Rain Man*): occur in only ~**one third** of individuals with ASD and **none** with the most severe form; result from **superior working memory + highly focused attention**, not universal.
- **General conclusion:** **social deficiencies are the primary distinguishing characteristic** of ASD.

### Causes — Biological Dimensions
- **Genetic:** ASD has a **significant, highly complex genetic component** with **moderate heritability**; many genes each with small effect (like schizophrenia). Families with one child with ASD have ~**20% chance** of another child with it — **>100x the general-population risk**. Genes for **oxytocin** (bonding, social memory) of interest; preliminary link to an **oxytocin receptor gene**. **Older parental age** raises risk (fathers 40+ over 5x more likely than fathers under 30; holds for maternal age too) → suggests **de novo mutations** in sperm/eggs.
- **Neurobiological:** **amygdala** theory — adults with ASD have amygdalae of normal size but **fewer neurons**, while young children with ASD have a **larger amygdala**; proposed that early enlargement causes excessive **anxiety/fear** (→ social withdrawal), then **chronic cortisol stress damages** the amygdala, leaving fewer neurons in adulthood. **Oxytocin** lower in blood of some children with ASD; giving oxytocin improved emotional memory/processing (e.g., remembering happy faces).
- **Vaccine myth (highly controversial, debunked):** **mercury preservative (thimerosal)** in childhood vaccines was claimed to cause ASD. Large Danish epidemiological studies show **no increased risk** in vaccinated children; number of vaccinations also **does not** increase risk. The temporal coincidence of MMR vaccination (12–15 months) and symptom emergence (before age 3) fuels persistent (false) belief. **Negative consequence:** some parents stop vaccinating → resurgence of measles and mumps.
- No integrative theory yet links biological, psychological, and social factors.

### Treatment of Autism Spectrum Disorder
- **No completely effective treatment exists**; efforts on the core social-communication deficits have not succeeded. Focus instead: **enhancing communication and daily-living skills, reducing problem behaviors** (tantrums, self-injury).
- **Psychosocial:** early **psychodynamic / ego-development** treatments (based on bad-parenting belief) **failed**. **Behavioral approaches** (Charles Ferster; **Ivar Lovaas**) are central — premise: **people with ASD can learn and be taught skills they lack.**
  - **Lovaas (mid-1960s)** taught nonspeaking children to **imitate speech** using **shaping** and **discrimination training**: reinforce any sound while watching teacher → reinforce sound after a request ("Say 'ball'") → shape closer approximations (sound of "b," with physical prompting) → add second word ("mama") → build to labels, plurals, sentences.
  - **Naturalistic teaching strategies:** move instruction from desk to home/school/community, **child-directed** (arrange environment so child initiates — favorite toy out of reach → "I want truck"). Evidence-based packages: **incidental teaching, pivotal response training, milieu teaching.**
  - For non-responders: **alternatives to vocal speech** — sign, **pointing to pictures**, voice-output devices/tablets.
  - **Social skills training** (including using typically developing **peers as trainers**); programs for milder ASD teach self-control, recognizing emotions, broadening interests, understanding **non-literal idioms** ("get off my back").
  - **Early intensive behavioral intervention** (Lovaas, 1987): **40+ hours/week** improved intellectual/educational functioning, with **long-lasting** gains (McEachin et al., 1993). Targeting **joint attention and play skills** in toddlers facilitates later language; may **"normalize" brain functioning** vs. untreated children.
- **Biological:** medication has **little impact on core social/language symptoms**; **major tranquilizers and SSRIs** used to decrease agitation. No single drug works for everyone.
- **Integrating treatments:** **early intervention = most hope** for core changes; older/non-responsive children get combined school education + behavioral supports; **parents need support**; older individuals → community integration, supported living/work.

## Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)

### Clinical description
- **ID** = significantly **below-average intellectual AND adaptive functioning** evident in childhood. DSM-5 identifies deficits in three **domains**: **conceptual** (language, reasoning, knowledge, memory), **social** (social judgment, friendships), and **practical** (personal care, job responsibilities).
- **DSM-5 renamed** DSM-IV-TR's "**mental retardation**" to "**intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder)**."
- ID was previously on **Axis II** of DSM-IV-TR (chronic, less treatable; reminder to consider effect on Axis I). **DSM-5 abolished the multiaxial system** — no separate axis.
- **DSM-5 removed numeric IQ cutoffs from the criteria themselves** (still mentioned in narrative; ~**70**), to emphasize **comprehensive functional assessment** over a single number. The **AAIDD** cutoff is ~**70–75**.

> **DSM-5-TR — Diagnostic Criteria for Intellectual Disability (Table 14.4):** A disorder with onset during the developmental period including both intellectual and adaptive functioning deficits in conceptual, social, and practical domains. All three must be met:
> **A.** **Deficits in intellectual functions** (reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgment, academic learning, learning from experience), confirmed by **clinical assessment AND standardized intelligence testing**.
> **B.** **Deficits in adaptive functioning** → failure to meet developmental/sociocultural standards for personal independence and social responsibility; without ongoing support, deficits limit ≥1 activity of daily life (communication, social participation, independent living) across multiple environments.
> **C.** **Onset during the developmental period** (before age 18).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR formally uses **"intellectual developmental disorder"** as the preferred name (parenthetical), aligning with ICD-11 terminology. Severity is determined by **adaptive functioning**, not IQ score (the key DSM-5 change). Diagnosis still rests on the three criteria; IQ ~70 retained only in the explanatory text.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 uses **"disorders of intellectual development"** and defines severity (mild/moderate/severe/profound) using **standardized behavioral indicators of intellectual + adaptive functioning** rather than IQ cutoffs — very close to DSM-5's functional emphasis.

### Levels and the AAIDD approach
- Traditional **four levels by IQ:** **Mild (50–55 to 70), Moderate (35–40 to 50–55), Severe (20–25 to 35–40), Profound (below 20–25).** Severe/profound: extremely limited communication (no speech or 1–2 words), need great/total assistance with dressing/bathing/eating.
- **Three criteria summary:** (1) significantly subaverage intellectual functioning; (2) concurrent deficits in **adaptive functioning** (communication, self-care, home living, social/interpersonal, community resources, self-direction, academics, work, leisure, health, safety) — excludes people who do poorly on IQ tests but function well (e.g., non-native English speaker); (3) **onset before age 18** (rules out brain trauma/dementia in adults).
- **AAIDD** classifies by **level of needed support: intermittent, limited, extensive, pervasive** — emphasizing needed supports (translatable into training goals) rather than the person's ability alone. E.g., James: DSM-5 = "moderate ID"; AAIDD = "needs **limited supports** in home living, health/safety, academic skills."
- **Society-defined:** the 70/75 cutoff is statistical (2 SD below the mean), not inherent; most diagnosed are in the **mild** range.

### Statistics
- ~**90%** of people with ID are in the **mild** range (IQ 50–70). All levels together ≈ **2% of the general population.**
- **Course is chronic** (no remission). Prognosis varies; mild ID → relatively independent productive lives with support.
- **Flynn effect:** IQ scores have risen over the last century; test-makers re-norm every decade or two to keep the mean ~100. For people near the cutoff this can determine diagnosis (one study: number scoring just below 70 **tripled** on a revised test).

### Causes
- Four broad categories: **Environmental** (deprivation, abuse, neglect), **Prenatal** (disease/drugs in the womb, **fetal alcohol syndrome**, poor nutrition), **Perinatal** (labor/delivery difficulties, **anoxia**), **Postnatal** (infections, head injury, malnutrition).

#### Genetic / Chromosomal Influences
- Multiple types: **chromosomal disorders** (extra 21st chromosome), **single-gene disorders** (dominant, recessive, X-linked), **mitochondrial disorders**, **multiple genetic mutations**, and **de novo mutations** (in sperm/egg or after fertilization). **Most cases of ID have no identified etiology.**
- **Only a few dominant genes** cause ID (natural selection reduces transmission). Example dominant-gene disorder: **tuberous sclerosis** (~1 in 30,000 births; ~60% have ID, most have seizures + acne-like skin bumps).
- **Phenylketonuria (PKU)** — recessive disorder, ~**1 in 10,000** newborns; inability to break down **phenylalanine**. Routine **newborn screening** + special diet **prevents** the ID, seizures, and behavior problems — a **rare example of successful prevention**. Untreated **maternal PKU** can harm the fetus → lifelong dietary restriction recommended (hence "Phenylketonurics: Contains Phenylalanine" warnings).
- **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome** — **X-linked recessive**; ID, signs of cerebral palsy (spasticity), and **self-injurious behavior** (finger/lip biting). **Only males affected** (no second X to balance); women are unaffected carriers.
- **Down syndrome** — **most common chromosomal form** of ID; identified by Langdon Down (1866); caused by an **extra 21st chromosome = trisomy 21** (via **nondisjunction**). Features: upward-slanting eyes with corner folds, flat nose, small mouth/flat palate, protruding tongue, congenital heart malformations. **Greatly increased risk of Alzheimer's-type dementia**, with **earlier onset** (sometimes early 20s) → led to discovery that at least one form of Alzheimer's is tied to a **gene on chromosome 21**.
  - **Maternal age effect:** age 20 → ~1 in 2,000; age 35 → 1 in 500; age 45 → 1 in 18. (But more Down children born to younger mothers, who have more children overall.) Possible reasons: older ova exposed longer to toxins/radiation interfering with meiosis; hormonal changes.
  - **Prenatal detection:** **amniocentesis** (sampling amniotic fluid — invasive), **chorionic villus sampling (CVS)** (placenta tissue — invasive), and newer **maternal blood tests** (first trimester). Detects presence, **not severity**. ~**25%** of prenatal Down diagnoses lead to elective abortion.
- **Fragile X syndrome** — second common chromosomal cause; **mutation on the tip of the X chromosome** (looks like it hangs by a thread). Primarily affects **males** (moderate-to-severe ID, hyperactivity, short attention span, gaze avoidance, **perseverative speech**, large ears/testicles/head circumference). Female **carriers commonly show mild-to-severe learning disabilities** (unlike Lesch-Nyhan carriers). **~1 in 4,000 males, ~1 in 8,000 females.**

#### Psychological and Social Dimensions
- **Cultural–familial intellectual disability:** cognitive impairment from a combination of **psychosocial (abuse, neglect, social deprivation) and biological** influences; specific mechanisms unknown. **Rare today** due to better child-care systems and early identification.

### Treatment of Intellectual Disability
- **Biological treatment not currently a viable option.** Treatment parallels that for severe ASD: **teach skills for productivity and independence.** Mild ID → like learning-disorder intervention (identify and address specific deficits) + community support.
- **Behavioral innovations (early 1960s):** **task analysis** (break a skill into component parts, teach each in succession), reinforce with praise/desired objects; success measured by **independence** attained.
- **Communication training:** central for personal satisfaction/social participation; goals scaled to existing skills (articulation → conversation organization). For severe disabilities: **sign language**, **augmentative communication** (picture books — point to a cup to request a drink), **computer/tablet voice-output devices.**
- **Behavior problems (aggression, self-injury):** debate over **painful punishers**; effective alternative is teaching the person to **communicate the need/desire** (e.g., for attention) that the problem behavior was achieving.
- **Community support:** **"supported employment"** — help find/sustain a competitive job; shown effective and **cost-effective**. Controversy is over **where** teaching occurs — separate special classrooms vs. **inclusion** in neighborhood schools/local businesses (trend toward inclusion).

## Prevention of Neurodevelopmental Disorders

- Prevention efforts are in **early stages**. **Early intervention** (described for ASD) shows promise and can target children at risk for **cultural–familial ID**.
- **Head Start** combines educational, medical, and social supports. One intensive preschool project (Martin, Ramey, & Ramey, 1990): control children (medical/nutritional support only) had IQ < 85 at age 3, but **all experimental-group 3-year-olds tested above 85**.
- Open questions: how to identify children/families who benefit, **how early** to start, **how long** to continue.
- **Genetic/biomedical horizon:** possible future detection/correction of genetic and chromosomal abnormalities. Mouse-model **fragile X** research: a drug blocking certain **glutamate receptors in the amygdala** normalized neuron functioning → potential early medical intervention; raises prospect of **prenatal gene therapy** (with ethical difficulties).
- **Psychologists' role:** support biomedical advances via **safety training** (e.g., lead-based paint), **substance-use treatment/prevention**, and **behavioral medicine ("wellness")** to prevent disorders linked to malnutrition and toxin exposure (lead, alcohol).

## Exam-focused recap of DSM-5 reorganizations in this chapter
- **ASD** absorbed autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder, and PDD-NOS into one spectrum with three **support levels**; **Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder** added.
- **Specific learning disorder** merged the three separate DSM-IV-TR learning disorders.
- **"Mental retardation" → "intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder)"**; IQ cutoffs de-emphasized; multiaxial Axis I/II system abolished.
- **DSM Controversy:** eliminating Asperger's drew concern about **lost services** (initial estimate ~40% might not meet ASD criteria; later ~9%) and **lost identity / neurodiversity** ("Aspies," "neurotypical"). DSM-5-TR retains all these structures.
