# Ch 1: Abnormal Behavior in Historical Context

This chapter builds the conceptual and historical foundation for the whole book: how a **psychological disorder** is defined, who studies and treats it, and the three great historical traditions (supernatural, biological, psychological) that competed to explain abnormal behavior. It ends by arguing that the only currently valid model is **multidimensional and integrative**. High-yield for MET/NIMHANS: the 3-criteria definition, clinical-description vocabulary, humoral theory, syphilis/general paresis, moral therapy reformers, Freud's structural model + defense mechanisms + psychosexual stages, and the conditioning theorists (Pavlov, Watson, Skinner).

> **DSM-5-TR note (whole chapter):** This 2015 edition is written to DSM-5 (2013). Chapter 1 is historical/definitional and contains **no formal diagnostic criteria sets**, so there is essentially nothing here that DSM-5-TR (2022) revised at the criteria level. The few overlay points are flagged inline below (e.g., DSM-5-TR keeps the same definition of mental disorder, refines the *cultural/socioeconomic context* wording, and updated codes to ICD-10-CM).

## Understanding Psychopathology

### Opening case — Judy ("The Girl Who Fainted at the Sight of Blood")
- **Judy**, 16, referred to an anxiety clinic after escalating fainting (5–10× a week, often in class) triggered by anything evoking blood/injury.
- Began ~2 years earlier after a graphic frog-dissection film → lightheadedness → progressive avoidance (gory magazines, raw meat, Band-Aids, even the phrase "Cut it out!").
- Diagnosis: **blood–injection–injury phobia**. A **phobia** = a psychological disorder characterized by **marked and persistent fear of an object or situation**.
- Teaching point: most psychological disorders are **extreme expressions of otherwise normal emotions, behaviors, and cognitive processes** (many people feel queasy at blood without having a disorder).

### What Is a Psychological Disorder? — the three-criteria definition
- **Psychological disorder (= abnormal behavior):** a **psychological dysfunction** within an individual that is associated with **distress or impairment in functioning** and a **response that is not typical or culturally expected** (Figure 1.1).
- **No single criterion alone defines abnormality** — all three are needed and even then it is hard.

> **The 3 criteria (callout):**
> 1. **Psychological dysfunction** — a breakdown in **cognitive, emotional, or behavioral** functioning. Where to draw the line is hard, so disorders are increasingly seen on a **continuum/dimension** rather than as present/absent categories (McNally, 2011; Stein et al., 2010; Widiger & Crego, 2013).
> 2. **Personal distress or impairment** — distress alone is insufficient (grief is normal; some disorders, e.g., a **manic episode**, involve *no* distress and even euphoria, so patients resist treatment — see Ch 7). **Impairment** = inability to function (e.g., shyness so severe one cannot date/interact). Useful but not fully satisfactory.
> 3. **Atypical or not culturally expected** — deviation from the average is insufficient (talented/eccentric people deviate too — Lady Gaga, J. D. Salinger). Violating **social norms** is useful but can be abused (e.g., committing political dissidents to institutions in Saddam-era Iraq / Iran).

- **Cultural relativity:** a trance/possession state may be a disorder in most Western cultures but **accepted and expected** elsewhere.
- **Sapolsky & the Masai (Rhoda):** the woman is "crazy" not because she kills a goat or hears voices (both happen in Masai culture) but because **"she hears voices at the wrong time"** — captures cross-cultural psychiatry: behavior is abnormal when it is out of context.
- **Jerome Wakefield (1999, 2009):** shorthand definition = **harmful dysfunction**. Related useful idea: behavior **out of the individual's control** (Widiger & Crego, 2013).
- **Prototype:** because criteria are prototypes, a patient can have only *some* (a minimum number of) symptoms and still meet criteria if the set is close enough to the prototype.
- **An accepted definition (DSM-5):** behavioral, psychological, or biological **dysfunctions** that are **unexpected in their cultural context** and associated with **present distress and impairment**, or **increased risk** of suffering, death, pain, or impairment.
- **DSM-5 vs DSM-IV difference highlighted:** DSM-5 added **dimensional estimates of severity** (e.g., panic-disorder symptoms rated 0–4: 1 = mild/occasional, 4 = continual/severe).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR retains this same definition of a *mental disorder* (a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior reflecting a dysfunction in underlying processes; usually associated with distress/disability; an expectable response to a stressor or a culturally sanctioned response is *not* a disorder). DSM-5-TR adds explicit guidance on **racism, discrimination, and social determinants** when judging "cultural context," and replaced DSM-5 codes with **ICD-10-CM** codes throughout.
> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 (in force from 2022, WHO) is the parallel current global system. It defines a mental disorder similarly (clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior) and, unlike DSM, is **dimensional in several places** (notably its personality-disorder model — see Ch 11). NIMHANS/MET answers should treat **DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 as the two current nosologies**.

## The Science of Psychopathology

- **Psychopathology** = the **scientific study of psychological disorders**.
- **Mental health professions (training):**
  - **Clinical & counseling psychologists** — Ph.D. (or Ed.D./Psy.D.), ~5 yrs graduate study; research + diagnose/assess/treat. **Counseling** psychologists lean toward adjustment/vocational issues in relatively healthy people; **clinical** lean toward more severe disorders. **Psy.D./professional schools** emphasize clinical training, de-emphasize research; **Ph.D. (university)** integrates both.
  - **Psychiatrists** — M.D. + 3–4 yr psychiatry residency; often biological viewpoint, prescribe drugs but also use psychosocial treatments.
  - **Psychiatric social workers** — master's in social work; focus on social/family situation.
  - **Psychiatric nurses** — advanced degree (master's or Ph.D.); care/treatment, usually hospital teams.
  - **Marriage & family therapists / mental health counselors** — 1–2 yr master's; clinical services under supervision.

### The Scientist-Practitioner
- **Scientist-practitioner** (Barlow, Hayes, & Nelson, 1984): mental health professional who uses a scientific approach in **three ways** (Figure 1.2): (1) **consumer of science** — uses current diagnostic/treatment procedures; (2) **evaluator of science** — tests whether their own treatments work (accountable to patients, government, insurers); (3) **creator of science** — conducts research producing new procedures. Protects against unproven "miracle cures."
- Research has **three basic aims** (Figure 1.3): **describe** disorders, **determine their causes (etiology)**, and **treat** them.

### Clinical Description (key vocabulary — high-yield)
- **Presenting problem / "presents":** why the person came to the clinic.
- **Clinical description:** the unique combination of **behaviors, thoughts, and feelings** that make up a specific disorder; specifies what differs from normal and from other disorders.
- **Prevalence:** how many people in the population *have* the disorder.
- **Incidence:** how many *new* cases occur in a given period (e.g., a year).
- **Sex ratio:** % of males vs females with the disorder.
- **Age of onset:** typical age at which it begins.
- **Course** (pattern over time):
  - **Chronic course** — lasts a long time, sometimes a lifetime (e.g., schizophrenia, Ch 13).
  - **Episodic course** — recover then recur (e.g., mood disorders, Ch 7).
  - **Time-limited course** — improves without treatment in a short period.
- **Onset:** **acute** (sudden) vs **insidious** (gradual).
- **Prognosis:** anticipated course ("prognosis is good" = likely recovery; "guarded" = poor outlook).
- **Developmental psychology** = study of changes in behavior over time; **developmental psychopathology** = study of changes in abnormal behavior; **life-span developmental psychopathology** = abnormal behavior across the entire age span. (Children with panic/anxiety often assume they are physically ill → frequently misdiagnosed/treated medically.)

### Causation, Treatment, and Etiology Outcomes
- **Etiology** = the **study of origins** (why a disorder begins); includes **biological, psychological, and social** dimensions (whole of Ch 2 devoted to it).
- **Effect ≠ cause:** that a treatment relieves a disorder does not prove the absence of that treatment caused it (aspirin/headache analogy). But successful drug/psychosocial treatments give **hints** about underlying mechanisms.
- **Psychosocial treatment** = nonmedical treatments focused on psychological, social, and cultural factors. The book deliberately has **no separate chapters by theoretical orientation** (psychodynamic/CBT/humanistic); instead treatments are described **per disorder** in an integrative, multidimensional way.

## Historical Conceptions of Abnormal Behavior

- Across history, explanations derive from the **models popular at the time**. Three enduring models date to the beginnings of civilization:
  - **Supernatural model** — outside agents (divinities, demons, spirits, magnetic fields, moon, stars).
  - **Biological model** — the body/brain as the cause.
  - **Psychological model** — the mind/psyche (soul), in interpersonal and social context.
- The mind ("soul"/"psyche") was long considered **separate from the body** (mind–body split), seeding the biological vs psychological traditions.

## The Supernatural Tradition
- Deviant behavior seen as the **battle between good and evil**. In the **Great Persian Empire (900–600 BC)**, all physical and mental disorders were the **work of the devil** (Millon, 2004). Barbara Tuchman's *A Distant Mirror* (1978) chronicled the bleak late 14th century.

### Demons and Witches
- Late 14th c.: religious/lay authorities backed superstition; belief in **demons and witches** grew (the Catholic Church had split, a rival pope in southern France). Bizarre behavior = work of the **devil and witches**.
- **Treatments:** **exorcism** (religious rituals to rid the victim of evil spirits); shaving a **cross** into the hair; securing sufferers to a church wall to hear Mass.
- Belief persisted into the 15th c. and beyond — e.g., the **Salem (Massachusetts) witch trials**, late 17th c.

### Stress and Melancholy (the competing enlightened view)
- A parallel **enlightened view**: insanity is a **natural phenomenon caused by mental/emotional stress and is curable** (Alexander & Selesnick, 1966).
- Depression & anxiety recognized as illnesses; despair/lethargy sometimes tied by the church to the sin of **acedia (sloth)**.
- **Treatments:** rest, sleep, healthy/happy environment, baths, ointments, potions. Medieval villages moved sufferers **house to house** as neighbors took turns caring for them (a forerunner of modern community care, Ch 13).
- **Nicholas Oresme** (14th-c. bishop/philosopher, adviser to the King of France): argued **melancholy (depression)**, not demons, caused some bizarre behavior; noted "confessions" of witchcraft came from people who had been **tortured**.

### Case — Charles VI ("The Mad King," 1392)
- King Charles VI of France, under great stress (Church schism), turned on his own army (killing several knights) when an aide dropped a lance. Advisers concluded he was **mad**.
- Episodes: hid believing he was **made of glass**, howled like a wolf, couldn't remember who he was, raged at his own royal coat of arms.
- Illustrates **alternating natural and supernatural explanations** in one case: a 92-year-old physician prescribed clean country air, **rest, relaxation, recreation**, and freedom from royal duties (temporarily worked); after the physician died, the charlatan **Arnaut Guilhem** blamed **sorcery** (treatments failed). Even the king came to blame **evil and sorcery**.

### Treatments for Possession
- Logic of sin → sufferer is responsible/being punished. Parallel modern example: early **AIDS** epidemic (late 1980s–early 1990s) framed as **divine punishment**.
- But possession could also be seen as **involuntary/blameless**; **exorcism** is relatively painless and sometimes works (like other **faith healing**).
- When exorcism failed: confinement, beatings, torture (Kemp, 1990). **Shock-based** "therapies": hanging sufferers over **snake pits** (sometimes produced temporary relief → reinforced therapists → snake pits built in institutions); **dunkings in ice-cold water** (hydrotherapy).

### Mass Hysteria
- Large-scale outbreaks of bizarre group behavior during the Middle Ages (groups running, dancing, shouting, raving) — named **Saint Vitus's Dance** and **tarantism**. Explanations offered: possession, insect bites, or what we now call **mass hysteria**.

### Modern Mass Hysteria
- Case (Rockney & Lemke, 1992): a 14-year-old reported a "funny smell" from a vent and collapsed; **of 86 susceptible people, 21 (17 students + 4 teachers)** developed dizziness/headache/nausea/stomach pain severe enough for the ER. No physical cause found; all recovered quickly.
- **Mechanism — emotion contagion:** an emotion spreads to those around us (Hatfield, Cacioppo, & Rapson, 1994). High emotion → suggestibility; one person's "cause" is adopted by others. Colloquially **mob psychology**.

### The Moon and the Stars
- **Paracelsus** (Swiss physician, **1493–1541**): rejected possession; proposed **movements of the moon and stars** affect psychological functioning; gravitational moon effects on **bodily fluids**. Source of the word **lunatic** (Latin *luna* = "moon"). No scientific support; survives in **astrology**.

### Comments
- The supernatural tradition survives mostly in small religious sects/some cultures. The **Roman Catholic Church requires all healthcare resources be exhausted before exorcism** is considered. Almost no one advocates supernatural treatment for severe disorders except, perhaps, as a last resort.

## The Biological Tradition
- Key elements: a man (**Hippocrates**), a disease (**syphilis**), and the **consequences** of believing disorders are biologically caused.

### Hippocrates and Galen
- **Hippocrates (460–377 BC):** father of modern Western medicine; **Hippocratic Corpus** (written 450–350 BC). Argued psychological disorders should be treated like any other disease and may stem from **brain pathology, head trauma, or heredity (genetics)** — and recognized **psychological/interpersonal contributions** (e.g., family stress; sometimes removed patients from families). Considered the **brain** the seat of wisdom/consciousness/intelligence/emotion.
- **Galen (≈AD 129–198):** Roman physician; extended Hippocrates → the **Hippocratic–Galenic** school, influential into the 19th c.

> **Humoral theory (first "chemical imbalance" model):** normal brain functioning depends on **four humors**:
> - **Blood** (from the heart) → personality **sanguine** (ruddy, cheerful, optimistic; excess in brain → insomnia/delirium).
> - **Black bile** (from the spleen) → **melancholic** (depressive; excess thought to cause **melancholia/depression**; *melancholer* = "black bile").
> - **Yellow bile / choler** (from the liver) → **choleric** (hot-tempered).
> - **Phlegm** (from the brain) → **phlegmatic** (apathetic/sluggish, but calm under stress).
> Disease = **too much or too little** of a humor. Humors mapped to four qualities: **heat, dryness, moisture, cold**.

- **Humoral treatments:** regulate environment (heat/dryness/moisture/cold) + rest, nutrition, exercise; plus **bloodletting/bleeding** (measured blood removal, often with **leeches**) and **induced vomiting** (Robert Burton's 1621 *Anatomy of Melancholy* recommended tobacco + half-boiled cabbage to induce vomiting).
- **China/Asia parallel:** instead of humors, the movement of **"wind"** in the body; disorders caused by blockages or **cold/dark wind (yin)** vs **warm/life-sustaining wind (yang)**; treated by restoring flow, e.g., **acupuncture**.
- **Hysteria:** Hippocrates coined the term (learned from the Egyptians) for what we now call **somatic symptom disorders** — physical symptoms (paralysis, some blindness) with **no findable physical cause**. Wrongly assumed restricted to women: the **"wandering uterus"** theory (Greek *hysteron* = uterus) — an empty uterus wanders seeking conception; cure = marriage or vaginal fumigation. Stigmatizing "hysterical woman" stereotype persisted into the **1970s**. (Somatic symptom disorders affect both sexes — Ch 6.)

### The 19th Century
- Biological tradition reinvigorated by two factors: the **nature/cause of syphilis** and support from American psychiatrist **John P. Grey**.

#### Syphilis & General Paresis
- Advanced **syphilis** (STD, bacterial microorganism entering the brain) produces **delusion of persecution**, **delusion of grandeur**, and bizarre behavior — resembling **psychosis** (= disorders marked by **delusions** [false beliefs] and/or **hallucinations** [false perceptions]).
- A subgroup of "psychotic" patients deteriorated, became paralyzed, and **died within 5 years**. In **1825** this was designated a disease — **general paresis** — for its consistent presentation and course.
- **Louis Pasteur's germ theory (≈1870)** enabled identification of the syphilis bacterium.
- **Malaria cure:** patients with general paresis who caught **malaria** recovered (high fever "burned out" the bacteria); doctors deliberately injected malarial blood (unethical today). Ultimately **penicillin** cured syphilis. First time "madness" was traced to a **curable infection** → optimism that all disorders might have biological causes/cures.

#### John P. Grey
- Most influential American psychiatrist of the era; **1854** superintendent of **Utica State Hospital** (NY, largest in the country); editor of the *American Journal of Insanity* (precursor of the *American Journal of Psychiatry*, APA's flagship).
- Position: causes of insanity are **always physical** → treat the patient as physically ill (rest, diet, room temperature, ventilation; **invented the rotary fan**). Hospitals improved but grew **large and impersonal**.
- ~100 years later: the **community mental health movement** and **deinstitutionalization** (releasing patients into communities) — has **as many negative consequences as positive**, including increased homelessness among the chronically disabled.

### The Development of Biological Treatments
- **1930s:** electric shock and brain surgery; effects of new drugs found largely **by accident**.
- **Insulin shock therapy:** Manfred Sakel (Vienna, 1927) raised insulin doses until patients convulsed/became comatose; some recovered. **Abandoned** (too dangerous — prolonged coma, death).
- **ECT origins:** **Benjamin Franklin** (1750s) found a mild head shock caused brief convulsion + amnesia but little harm (a colleague felt "strangely elated"). **Joseph von Meduna** (1920s, Hungary) wrongly believed schizophrenia rarely co-occurs with epilepsy → induced seizures might cure it. **Ugo Cerletti & Lucio Bini** (Italy, 1938) applied electric shock to the brain; a London surgeon treated a depressed patient with six small shocks → recovery. **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** persists today (Ch 7); mechanism still poorly understood.
- **Drugs (1950s onward):** first systematic effective drugs for severe psychosis. **Rauwolfia serpentina → reserpine** and **neuroleptics (major tranquilizers)** reduced hallucinations/delusions/agitation. **Benzodiazepines (minor tranquilizers; e.g., Valium, Librium)** reduced anxiety — by the 1970s among the most prescribed drugs worldwide, then declined as side effects/limited efficacy emerged. **Bromides** (sedatives, ~1 in 5 US prescriptions by 1928) faded after side effects became known. Pattern (Alexander & Selesnick): **"initial enthusiasm followed by disappointment."**

### Consequences of the Biological Tradition
- Ironically, Grey's belief that disorders stem from **undiscovered, incurable brain pathology** **reduced treatment** — only option was to hospitalize; some nurses' successful treatments were suppressed to avoid "raising hopes." Focus shifted to **diagnosis, legal responsibility, and brain pathology**.
- **Emil Kraepelin (1856–1926):** founding father of modern psychiatry; little involved in treatment but pivotal in **diagnosis and classification** (Ch 3). First to distinguish disorders by **different age of onset, time course, symptom clusters, and probable cause**; his schizophrenia descriptions remain useful.

## The Psychological Tradition
- Long precursor: **Plato** — maladaptive behavior caused by **social/cultural influences** and **learning** in one's environment (e.g., abusive parents); treatment = **reeducation through rational discussion** so reason predominates. **Aristotle** also emphasized social environment and early learning; both wrote on fantasies, dreams, cognitions (anticipating psychoanalysis and cognitive science) and advocated **humane care**.

### Moral Therapy
- First half of the 19th c.: a strong psychosocial approach. **"Moral"** referred to **emotional/psychological** factors, not a code of conduct.
- Tenets: treat institutionalized patients **as normally as possible**, **encourage and reinforce normal social interaction**, nurture relationships, **eliminate restraint and seclusion**, emphasize positive consequences for appropriate behavior.
- Roots: Plato; Greek **Asclepiad Temples** (6th c. BC — care, massage, soothing music); enlightened practices in Middle Eastern Muslim countries.
- **Founders:** French psychiatrist **Philippe Pinel (1745–1826)** and superintendent **Jean-Baptiste Pussin (1746–1811)** at **La Bicêtre** (Paris). When Pinel arrived in **1791**, Pussin had already **removed chains** and instituted humane interventions; Pinel adopted them at La Bicêtre, then **Salpêtrière** (women's hospital).
- Spread: **William Tuke (1732–1822)** in England; **Benjamin Rush (1745–1813)**, often called the **founder of US psychiatry**, introduced moral therapy at **Pennsylvania Hospital**. Asylums (which existed from the 16th c. but were prison-like) became habitable/therapeutic.
- **Outcomes data (Horace Mann, 1833, Worcester State Hospital):** 32 "incurable" patients treated, cured, released. Of 100 assaultive patients, **≤12 still violent** a year later; of 40 who tore off clothes, only **8** continued. (Side feature: **Nantucket asylum/poor farm**, voted 1822, inspired by moral-therapy results at McLean Asylum; burned down Feb 1844, 10 inmates killed.)

### Asylum Reform and the Decline of Moral Therapy
- Moral therapy worked best with **≤200 patients** (allowing individual attention).
- **Decline factors:** (1) post–Civil War **immigration** swelled patient loads to **1,000–2,000+**; immigrant groups often denied moral treatment. (2) **Dorothea Dix (1802–1887)** — schoolteacher, crusader; the **mental hygiene movement**; campaigned for humane care for all including the homeless, inspired many new institutions — but the resulting **flood of patients** outpaced staffing → shift from moral therapy to **custodial care**. (3) Mid-19th-c. belief that mental illness = **incurable brain pathology**.
- The psychological tradition then lay dormant, re-emerging in the 20th c. as **psychoanalysis** (Freud) and **behaviorism** (Watson, Pavlov, Skinner).

### Psychoanalytic Theory
- **Franz Anton Mesmer (1734–1815):** attributed problems to blocked **"animal magnetism"** (a fluid in all organisms); patients sat around a chemical vat in a dark room. **Benjamin Franklin** debunked it with a **double-blind** experiment (magnetized vs nonmagnetized water + suggestion; both groups improved) → it was **strong suggestion**. Mesmer = **father of hypnosis** (a trance-like state of high suggestibility).
- **Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893):** head of **Salpêtrière**; legitimized **hypnosis** for psychological disorders. In **1885** the young **Sigmund Freud** came from Vienna to study with him.
- **Josef Breuer (1842–1925):** asked hypnotized patients to describe problems in detail. Two observations: (1) patients became emotional and felt relieved/improved afterward; (2) they often **could not recall** what they had described → discovery of the **unconscious mind** and its influence on disorders.
  - **Catharsis:** therapeutic release of tension by reliving emotional trauma made unconscious.
  - **Insight:** a fuller understanding of the link between current emotions and earlier events.
- **Anna O. (Bertha Pappenheim, 1859–1936):** Breuer's classic case (1895). Healthy until age 21, then (after caring for her dying father) developed blurred vision, limb weakness, speech difficulty, unpredictable behavior. Breuer treated **one symptom at a time** via hypnosis + "talking through," tracing each to events around her father's death (anticipates single-case scientific method, Ch 4; revisited in Ch 6).

> **The Structure of the Mind (Freud, Figure 1.4):**
> - **Id** — source of strong **sexual and aggressive** energies ("the animal within"); energy = **libido**; secondary **death instinct = thanatos**. Operates on the **pleasure principle**; uses **primary process** thinking (emotional, irrational, illogical, fantasy-laden).
> - **Ego** — the **executive/manager**; acts realistically per the **reality principle**; uses **secondary process** thinking (logical, rational); **mediates** between id and superego.
> - **Superego** — **conscience**; the **moral principles** instilled by parents/culture; counteracts the id.
> - **Intrapsychic conflicts** = clashes among these. Id and superego are **almost entirely unconscious**; we are aware only of the ego's secondary processes (a small part of the mind).

- **Defense mechanisms:** **unconscious protective processes** that keep conflict-linked emotions in check so the ego can function; triggered when conflict produces overwhelming **anxiety** (a signal). Fully developed by **Anna Freud (1895–1982)** (ego psychology; *Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense*, 1946). Adaptive or maladaptive; today often called **coping styles** (Vaillant; Perry & Bond, 2012 — fewer maladaptive + more adaptive defenses like **humor, sublimation** correlate with health).

> **Defense mechanisms (APA, 2000):**
> - **Denial** — refuses to acknowledge an aspect of objective reality/experience obvious to others.
> - **Displacement** — transfers a feeling/response from a threatening object onto a less-threatening one (e.g., yelling at sibling/dog after unfair grading).
> - **Projection** — falsely attributes one's own unacceptable feelings/impulses/thoughts to another.
> - **Rationalization** — conceals true motives via reassuring/self-serving but incorrect explanations.
> - **Reaction formation** — substitutes behavior/thoughts/feelings that are the **direct opposite** of unacceptable ones.
> - **Repression** — blocks disturbing wishes/thoughts/experiences from conscious awareness.
> - **Sublimation** — channels maladaptive impulses into **socially acceptable** behavior (e.g., work).

> **Psychosexual Stages of Development (Freud):** oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital. Each is a distinctive pattern of gratifying basic needs/drive for pleasure. **Fixation** (insufficient or excessive gratification) leaves a lasting personality imprint.
> - **Oral stage** (~birth–2 yrs): focus on the mouth/feeding; oral fixation → thumb-sucking, nail-biting, dependency/passivity or (reaction) rebelliousness/cynicism.
> - **Phallic stage** (~3–5/6 yrs): genital self-stimulation. **Oedipus complex** (boys): lustful impulses toward mother + anger/envy toward father + **castration anxiety** (fear father will remove the penis); resolved by reconciling feelings and channeling libido into heterosexual relationships. **Electra complex** (girls): wish to replace mother/possess father, centered on **penis envy**; "resolved" via healthy heterosexual relationships and desire for a baby. (Explicitly flagged as **theory, not fact — no systematic research support**, and criticized as sexist.)

- **Neuroses (neurotic disorders):** Freud's term for all **nonpsychotic** disorders resulting from underlying unconscious conflict + anxiety + defense mechanisms.

> **DSM-5-TR / ICD note:** The term **"neurosis"** was **dropped from the DSM in 1980 (DSM-III)** and remains absent in DSM-5/DSM-5-TR. **ICD-11** has likewise abandoned the old "neurotic disorders" grouping (ICD-10 still had "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders"), replacing it with discrete chapters (anxiety/fear-related, OCD-related, stress-related, etc.).

#### Later Developments in Psychoanalytic Thought
- **Anna Freud** — **ego psychology**: abnormal behavior arises when the ego is deficient in delaying/controlling impulses or marshaling defenses.
- **Heinz Kohut (1913–1981)** — **self-psychology**: formation of self-concept; healthy self vs neurosis (Kohut, 1977).
- **Object relations** — how children **introject** (incorporate) the images/memories/values of important people ("objects"); these become part of the ego or create conflicting identities.
- **Carl Jung (1875–1961)** — rejected Freud's sexual emphasis; introduced the **collective unconscious** (inherited cultural wisdom passed across generations); stressed spiritual/religious drives and traits of **introversion/extroversion**.
- **Alfred Adler (1870–1937)** — feelings of **inferiority** and striving for superiority; coined **inferiority complex**; emphasized positive human nature and **self-actualization**.
- **Karen Horney (1885–1952)** and **Erich Fromm (1900–1980)** — culture/society and life-span emphases.
- **Erik Erikson (1902–1994)** — development across the life span in **eight stages** with characteristic crises (e.g., the **mature age** [~65+] reviews life with satisfaction vs despair).

#### Psychoanalytic Psychotherapy
- Aim: reveal unconscious processes/conflicts via **catharsis** and **insight**.
- **Free association** — say whatever comes to mind without censoring (reveals repressed material). The **couch** symbol comes from Freud sitting behind reclining patients.
- **Dream analysis** — interpret dream content (reflecting id primary-process) to expose unconscious conflicts.
- **Transference** — patient relates to the **psychoanalyst** as to important childhood figures (often parents); **countertransference** — therapist projects their own issues onto the patient (ethically must be managed).
- **Classical psychoanalysis:** 4–5×/week for 2–5 years to restructure personality and put the ego back in charge. Symptom reduction is "inconsequential" (symptoms are just expressions of conflict; removing one risks **symptom substitution**). Now **seldom used** (cost + lack of efficacy evidence).
- **Psychodynamic psychotherapy** — looser, eclectic, social/interpersonal focus; **briefer** than classical analysis; de-emphasizes personality reconstruction, focuses on relieving suffering. **Seven tactics (Blagys & Hilsenroth, 2000):** (1) focus on affect/emotional expression; (2) explore avoidance/resistance; (3) identify recurring patterns; (4) emphasize past experiences; (5) focus on interpersonal experiences; (6) emphasize the **therapeutic relationship**; (7) explore wishes/dreams/fantasies.
- **Comments:** main criticism — psychoanalysis is **basically unscientific** (relies on retrospective reports, analyst interpretation, no measurement, not falsifiable). But it contributed lasting ideas: unconscious processes, symbolic triggers of emotion, repression, and the **therapeutic alliance** (an important variable across all therapies).

### Humanistic Theory
- Grew from Jung's and Adler's **positive, optimistic** view of human nature (vs Freud's battleground). Adopted mid-century → **humanistic psychology**; watchword = **self-actualizing** (we can reach our highest potential if free to grow; blocks usually originate **outside** the person).
- **Abraham Maslow (1908–1970):** **hierarchy of needs** — from basic physical needs (food, sex) up through social needs (friendship) to **love, self-esteem, and self-actualization**; lower needs must be met before progressing.
- **Carl Rogers (1902–1987):** most influential humanist therapist; originated **client-centered (later person-centered) therapy** — therapist takes a **passive** role, minimal interpretation. Core conditions: **unconditional positive regard** (complete, almost unqualified acceptance of the client's feelings/actions) and **empathy** (sympathetic understanding of the client's worldview). Rogers studied therapist–client relationships scientifically; fueled the **human potential movements** of the 1960s–70s.
- **Comments:** contributed **relatively little new** to psychopathology (proponents largely avoided research; stressed unique, nonquantifiable experience). Greatest application = people **without** disorders; use for severe disorders has declined.

### The Behavioral Model
- Also called the **cognitive-behavioral** or **social learning model**; brought a **scientific** approach to the psychological aspects of psychopathology.

#### Pavlov and Classical Conditioning
- **Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936)** (St. Petersburg): studied why dogs salivate before food → **classical conditioning** (a neutral stimulus paired with a response until it elicits that response). "Conditioning" is a mistranslation of **"conditional"** (response occurs only *on the condition* of a particular stimulus).
- **Terms:**
  - **Unconditioned stimulus (UCS)** — elicits a response with no learning (food; chemotherapy).
  - **Unconditioned response (UCR)** — natural/unlearned response (salivation; nausea).
  - **Conditioned stimulus (CS)** — previously neutral stimulus that acquires the power to elicit the response (metronome; the nurse's uniform).
  - **Conditioned response (CR)** — learned response to the CS.
  - **Stimulus generalization** — response spreads to **similar** stimuli (strength ∝ similarity); e.g., chemotherapy patients feel nausea at the sight of any nurse/the hospital (Morrow & Dobkin, 1988).
  - **Extinction** — repeated CS without UCS eventually eliminates the CR.
- A very strong UCS (chemotherapy) can produce a CR in **one trial**; most learning needs repeated pairing.
- Pavlov's precision (measuring, ruling out alternatives) contrasted with the unmeasurable methods of psychoanalysis and the **introspection** of early experimental psychologists like **Edward Titchener (1867–1927)** ("armchair" psychology, inconsistent results).

#### Watson and the Rise of Behaviorism
- **John B. Watson (1878–1958):** founder of **behaviorism**; rejected introspection; psychology should be an **objective experimental natural science** aiming at the **prediction and control of behavior** (1913 article).
- **Little Albert (Watson & Rosalie Rayner, 1920):** an 11-month-old conditioned to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise; after **five trials** he feared the rat and **generalized** to other white furry objects (e.g., a Santa mask). First lab demonstration of producing a new fear. (Unethical today; Albert may have had neurological impairment — Fridlund et al., 2012.)
- **Mary Cover Jones (1896–1987):** showed fear could be **unlearned**. Worked with **Peter** (2 yrs 10 mo, afraid of furry objects) by gradually bringing a white rabbit closer while fearless children played nearby; fear extinguished and did not return — a forerunner of exposure therapy.

#### The Beginnings of Behavior Therapy
- **Joseph Wolpe (1915–1997)** (South Africa): dissatisfied with psychoanalysis; developed **systematic desensitization** — gradually expose the patient (often via **imagination** of the feared scene) while doing something **incompatible with fear**, namely **deep relaxation**. Example: dog phobia treated by relaxing while imagining progressively closer contact with a dog. Worked with **Hans Eysenck** and **Stanley Rachman** in London; coined **behavior therapy**. (Procedures seldom used as-is today but paved the way for modern fear-reduction methods that can eliminate phobias in as little as **1 day** — Ch 5.)

#### B. F. Skinner and Operant Conditioning
- **B. F. Skinner (1904–1990):** *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938) laid out **operant conditioning** — behavior changes as a function of **what follows it** (its consequences). Behavior **operates on the environment** and changes it. Also wrote *Walden Two* (1948) and *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* (1971).
- Built on **Edward L. Thorndike (1874–1949)** and the **law of effect** — behavior is **strengthened or weakened** by its consequences.
- **Key terms:** **reinforcement** (preferred over "reward"; increases behavior); **punishment** (Skinner viewed it as relatively ineffective long-term); **schedules of reinforcement** (Ferster & Skinner, 1957); **shaping** (reinforcing **successive approximations** to a target behavior — e.g., teaching a pigeon to "play Ping-Pong"). Subjects were mostly **pigeons and rats**. Skinner saw emotion/cognition as side effects of a reinforcement history.
- Pavlov, Watson, and Skinner collectively shaped **behavior therapy** (Wolpe, 1958).
- **Comments:** powerful but **incomplete** — historically left **no room for biology**, ignored **life-span development**, and predated modern understanding of **information processing** (conscious + subconscious). Integration of all these requires a new model.

## The Present: The Scientific Method and an Integrative Approach
- "What's past is prologue." Three traditions reviewed: **supernatural, biological, psychological** (the last subdivided into **psychoanalytic** and **behavioral**).
- Supernatural explanations persist (astrology, moon/stars) but have little scientific influence; biological, psychoanalytic, and behavioral models continue to advance knowledge.
- **Why each tradition failed:** (1) scientific methods were rarely applied (no evidence to confirm/disprove → fads thrived); (2) professionals viewed disorders **too narrowly** (Grey: only brain disease; Watson: only psychological/social).
- **1990s convergence:** (1) increasingly **sophisticated scientific tools/methods**; (2) the realization that **no single influence — biological, behavioral, cognitive, emotional, or social — ever occurs in isolation**. Thoughts/feelings/actions **change the brain's function and structure**, sometimes permanently → behavior is the product of **continual interaction** of psychological, biological, and social influences.
- **Adolf Meyer (1866–1950)** ("dean of American psychiatry"): early advocate that psychopathology is **multiply determined** (equal biological, psychological, sociocultural contributions) — recognized ~100 years later.
- **2010:** the **National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)** instituted a strategic plan to study the interrelationship of these factors and translate findings to front-line care (Insel, 2009).
- **Conclusion:** the only currently valid model of psychopathology is **multidimensional and integrative** (developed in Ch 2).

> **DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 currency note (for MET answers):** The book's DSM timeline ends at **DSM-5 (2013)**. The current systems are **DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022)** and **ICD-11 (WHO, in effect 2022)**. DSM-5-TR is a *text revision* (updated text, **ICD-10-CM codes**, added **Prolonged Grief Disorder**, refined cultural/socioeconomic and suicide/self-injury content) — it did **not** alter the historical or definitional material covered in this chapter.
