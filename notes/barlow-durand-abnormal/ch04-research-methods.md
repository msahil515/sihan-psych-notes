# Ch 4: Research Methods

This chapter is the methodological backbone of abnormal psychology: how scientists use the **scientific method** to answer three core questions of the book (what problems cause distress/impairment, why people behave abnormally [**etiology**], and how to help them). It covers the basic anatomy of a research study (hypothesis, variables, validity), the four families of research methods (case study, correlation, experiment, single-case designs), genetic and developmental designs, cross-cultural research, replication, and research ethics. For the MET/NIMHANS exam this is a high-yield, definition-dense chapter: examiners love **internal vs external validity, correlation vs causation, types of control groups, single-case designs, twin/adoption studies, cross-sectional vs longitudinal, and informed consent**. Note: this is a methods chapter, so it contains **no diagnostic criteria**; DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 overlays are limited (flagged where relevant at the end).

## Examining Abnormal Behavior

### Why abnormal behavior is hard to study
- Behavioral scientists study humans the same way others study volcanoes or cell phones on brain cells: via the **scientific method**.
- Two obstacles: (1) the **interaction of biological and psychological dimensions** (complexity of human nature); (2) **inaccessibility**: we cannot get inside people's minds except indirectly.
- Being a good **consumer of research** lets you tell **fact from fiction**, and a **fad** from an established approach.

### The three guiding questions
- **What** problems cause distress and impair functioning? (nature of the problems)
- **Why** do people behave in unusual ways? (causes / **etiology**)
- **How** do we help them behave more adaptively? (treatment evaluation)

## Important Concepts: Basic Components of a Research Study

### Core elements (Table 4.1)
- **Hypothesis** — an **educated guess** / statement to be supported by data. Humans are "meaning-making" organisms (Kegan, in Lefrancois, 1990); heightened motivation to make sense of the world when order/meaning is threatened (Proulx & Heine, 2009).
- **Research design** — the **plan for testing the hypothesis**; shaped by the question, the hypothesis, and practical considerations.
- **Dependent variable (DV)** — the aspect of the phenomenon that is **measured** and **expected to change/be influenced** by the IV (e.g., overt behaviors, thoughts, feelings, biological symptoms).
- **Independent variable (IV)** — the aspect **manipulated** or thought to **influence** the change in the DV.
- **Testability** — the ability to support (or fail to support) the hypothesis; lets us conclude either a relationship exists or it does not.

> **Worked example (MDMA / "ecstasy"):** Wagner et al. (2012), University of Cologne, followed **109 young adults over 1 year**. Question: "Does MDMA use over 1 year lead to a cognitive performance decrease?" **DV = cognitive performance** (digit span, Stroop, Trail-making tests). **IV = MDMA use** (users = ≥10 pills over the prior year). Finding: strong relationship between MDMA use and poor performance on **visually oriented learning tasks**.

### Internal vs external validity (HIGH-YIELD)
> **Internal validity** — the extent to which results can be **attributed to the IV** (i.e., the IV truly caused the DV to change). Threatened by confounds.
> **External validity (generalizability)** — the extent to which results can be **generalized/applied outside** the immediate study to other people and settings.
- **Confound (confounding variable)** — any factor in a study that makes results **uninterpretable** because a variable other than the IV may also affect the DV.
  - MDMA example: if only some agreed to be followed up a year later and those users had **lower IQ**, a systematic difference (IQ, i.e., type of population) would confound the MDMA→cognition conclusion.
- **Internal and external validity are often inversely related**: controlling everything (e.g., only 25–30-year-old male college graduates) boosts internal validity but makes results irrelevant to the real world. Researchers must **balance** the two; the best fix is often **several related studies** (a program of research).

### Three strategies to ensure internal validity
- **Control group** — people similar to the experimental group in every way **except** they are **not exposed to the IV**; rules out alternative explanations.
- **Randomization** — assigning people to groups so each has an **equal chance** of any group (coin flip, random number table); eliminates **systematic bias in assignment**. Note: it does not necessarily eliminate bias in your final group, and people can **self-select** ("put themselves in groups"). Example of self-selection bias: severely depressed people choosing the **wait-list control** over a demanding twice-weekly treatment.
- **Analogue models** — create in the controlled lab conditions **analogous** to the real phenomenon (e.g., asking volunteers to binge eat in a lab, sampling all ages/genders/races so eating attitudes aren't confounded by only using people with bulimia). Such "artificial" studies **improve internal validity**.

## Statistical versus Clinical Significance (HIGH-YIELD)

- The introduction of statistics marks psychology's shift from a **prescientific to a scientific discipline**.
- **Statistical significance** — the **probability of obtaining the observed effect by chance is small** (a mathematical calculation about the difference between groups).
- **Clinical significance** — whether the difference is **meaningful** for those affected (Thirthalli & Rajkumar, 2009).
> **Worked example (naltrexone, Anton et al., 2006):** With **1,383 volunteers**, medication+psychotherapy → **77 days** sober on average vs **75 days** for placebo. This 2-day difference was **statistically significant** (because of the large sample) but arguably **not clinically significant** (few would take meds + therapy for 2 extra sober days).
- **Effect size** — statistical methods that capture **how large** the differences are, considering how much each individual improves/worsens (Durand & Wang, 2011; Fritz, Morris, & Richler, 2012), not just whether groups differ.
- **Social validity (Montrose Wolf, 1978)** — a **subjective** approach: obtain input from the **person treated** and **significant others** about the importance of the changes. If the effect impresses those directly involved, it is clinically significant.

### The "Average" Client
- **Patient uniformity myth (Kiesler, 1966)** — the tendency to treat all participants as one **homogeneous group**; comparing group means ("Group A improved 50% over Group B") hides important individual differences/reactions.
- Leads to **inaccurate generalizations** about disorders and treatments. Clients are **heterogeneous** (age, cognitive ability, gender, treatment history), so a statistically significant treatment may not work for a given individual.

## Types of Research Methods

### Studying Individual Cases (Case Study Method)
- **Case study method** — intensively investigating **one or a few individuals** who display the behavioral/physical pattern (Yin, 2012). (Textbook's playful illustration: the fictional "Homer Simpson disorder.")
- **Defined by what it is NOT:** it does **not use the scientific method**, makes **few efforts to ensure internal validity**, and typically has **many confounds**.
- Relies on the **clinician's observations** comparing one person/group with a disorder vs people with other disorders vs people with none; clinician collects detailed personal/family/education/health/work history.
- **Historical importance:** Freud built **psychoanalytic theory** on dozens of cases; Freud & Breuer's **Anna O.** → **free association**; **Masters & Johnson (1966)** dispelled sexual myths via many cases; **Joseph Wolpe (1958, _Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition_)** based **systematic desensitization** on 200+ cases. Reliance has **decreased** as knowledge grew.
- **Weaknesses:** irrelevant **coincidences** can lead to mistaken causal/treatment conclusions; no experimental controls → results may be unique to one person or a special combination of factors. **Sensational media cases** (e.g., Virginia Tech shooter, April 16, 2007, 32 killed) drive premature conclusions; people are more swayed by **dramatic accounts than scientific evidence** (Nisbett & Ross, 1980).

### Research by Correlation (HIGH-YIELD)
- **Correlation** — a **statistical relationship between two variables**; studies phenomena **as they occur** (no manipulation), unlike experiments.
- **"Correlation does not imply causation."** Classic example: marital problems correlate (~**+0.50**) with child behavior problems (Yoo & Huang, 2012), but:
  - A→B (marital problems cause child problems), or
  - B→A (child problems cause marital problems; Rutter & Giller, 1984), or
  - C→both (genes influence both marital discord and conduct problems; D'Onofrio et al., 2006; Lynch et al., 2006).
- **Directionality problem** — even a very strong correlation (**+0.90**) tells us **nothing about the direction of causality** (A→B, B→A, or C→both).

> **Correlation coefficient** ranges from **–1.00 to +1.00**:
> - **+1.00** = perfect positive ("Where I go, you will go!"); both increase/decrease together = **positive correlation**.
> - **0.00** = no relationship (strangers; e.g., schizophrenia and height).
> - **–1.00** = perfect negative ("Anywhere you go, I won't be there!"); as one rises the other falls = **negative correlation**.
> - **Strength** depends on the absolute value regardless of sign (e.g., +0.80 is stronger than +0.75; –0.80 is stronger than +0.75).
- Examples: social supports & illness ≈ **–0.40** (more support → less illness, from Ch 2); schizophrenia & height ≈ 0.00.

#### Epidemiological Research
- **Epidemiology** — the study of the **incidence, distribution, and consequences** of a problem in one or more populations (a type of correlational research; "scientists as detectives").
- **Prevalence** — the **number of people with a disorder at any one time** (e.g., **binge drinking ≈ 40%** among U.S. college students; binge drinking = 5+ drinks in a row; Beets et al., 2009).
- **Incidence** — the estimated **number of NEW cases** during a specific period (college binge drinking lowered only slightly from 1980 to present; SAMHSA, 2012).
- Compares groups (e.g., alcohol abuse prevalence among African Americans lower than among whites; SAMHSA, 2012).
- **Classic discovery (Joseph Goldberger):** an early-1900s "strange mental disorder" (catatonia/paranoid-schizophrenia-like symptoms) in poor African Americans — wrongly attributed to racial/class "inferiority" — was found via epidemiology to be a **niacin (B vitamin) deficiency** from poor diets; cured by **niacin therapy + improved diet**; led to **vitamin-enriched bread in the 1940s** (Colp, 2009).
- **Stress/9-11 study (DeLisi et al., 2003):** interviewed **1,009** Manhattan men and women; the most negative reactions occurred in those with **preexisting psychological disorders**, **greatest exposure** (e.g., WTC evacuation), and **women**; common reactions = anxiety and painful memories. Correlational (the attack wasn't an experiment).
- **AIDS example:** tracking incidence across populations (gay men, IV drug users, partners/children of infected) in the 1980s inferred transmission via **bodily fluids** (unprotected sex, nonsterile needles). Like all correlational research, epidemiology **cannot prove causation** but points researchers in the right direction.

### Research by Experiment (HIGH-YIELD)
- **Experiment** — the **manipulation of an IV** and **observation of its effects**; the way to answer **causality**.
- **Manipulating a variable** — introducing or withdrawing a variable in a way that **would not have occurred naturally**.

#### Group Experimental Designs
- Researchers **actively change an IV** to see how group behavior is affected (vs correlational, which only observes associations).
- Example: insomnia treatment in older adults (Epstein et al., 2012) — treatment = IV; follow group 10 years; the **unanswerable question** without a control group is **what would have happened untreated** (their sleep might have been worse).

#### Clinical Trials terminology (KNOW THE HIERARCHY)
- **Clinical trial** — an experiment to determine the **effectiveness and safety** of treatment(s); a **method of evaluation** following accepted rules (participant selection, sample size, group assignment, analysis, **formal protocols** so everyone is treated the same), **not a design by itself** (Durand & Wang, 2011).
- Nested terms:
  - **Randomized clinical trials** — employ **randomization** into each group.
  - **Controlled clinical trials** — rely on **control conditions** for comparison.
  - **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)** — the **preferred method**, uses **both randomization AND one or more control conditions**.

#### Control Groups (types)
- **Control group** — people similar to the experimental group in every way except they are **not exposed to the IV**; ideally **near-identical** in age, gender, SES, presenting problems, with the **same assessments before and after**.
- **Placebo effect** — behavior change due to the **person's expectation of change** rather than the manipulation (Latin _placebo_ = "I shall please"). (Textbook's coined opposite: a "frustro effect," from _frusto_ = "to disappoint," in disappointed controls — e.g., making depressed controls worse, flattering the treatment group by comparison.)
- **Placebo control group** — controls get an **inactive treatment** (e.g., sugar pill) so they **believe** they are being treated (Kendall & Comer, 2011). Easy with medication; harder for psychotherapy (give part of the therapy, e.g., same homework, but **not** the active ingredient). The placebo effect is **one portion of any treatment**; in an experiment it is a **confound** that can dilute validity, so a placebo control distinguishes positive-expectation effects from real treatment effects.
- **Double-blind control** — neither the **participants** (single blind) **nor the researchers/therapists** (double blind) know group assignment; eliminates investigator bias.
  - **Allegiance effect** — a researcher who prefers one treatment may unconsciously "try harder" for it (Munder et al., 2012); blinding reduces this.
  - **Limitation:** double-blind doesn't work perfectly — **side effects** of medication, or the obvious power of a psychological treatment, can tip off participants/researchers and alter expectations.

#### Comparative Treatment Research
- **Comparative treatment research** — give **different treatments** to two or more **comparable** groups with the same disorder and compare outcomes (e.g., insomnia: medication vs cognitive-behavioral intervention).
- **Process research** — focuses on the **mechanisms** of change: **"why does it work?"** (the "active ingredients"). Example: adding **relaxation training** to an insomnia package gave **no additional benefit** (Harvey, Inglis, & Espie, 2002) → clinicians can drop it and focus on CBT. Knowing active ingredients saves clients time/money and enables more powerful treatments.
- **Outcome research** — focuses on the **results** (positive/negative): **"does it work?"** / what changes occur after treatment.
  - Mnemonic: **Process = why/how; Outcome = what/does it work.**

### Single-Case Experimental Designs (HIGH-YIELD)
- Formalized by **B. F. Skinner**; the **systematic study of individuals under a variety of experimental conditions**. Skinner valued knowing a lot about **one** individual over a few observations of an "average" group (Barlow, Nock, & Hersen, 2009).
- **Differ from case studies** by using **strategies to improve internal validity** and reduce confounds.

#### Repeated Measurement
- **Repeated measurement** — measuring a behavior **several times** (not just once before and once after the IV change) to learn its **variability** (day-to-day change) and **trend** (getting better/worse).
- **Wendy example:** anxiety rated 9 before treatment, 6 after — but daily ratings ranged 5–7 (good days) to 8–10 (bad days) before AND after, so the pre/post change may be **normal daily variation**. Three components illustrated (Fig 4.2):
  - **Level** — degree of behavior change with the intervention.
  - **Variability** — degree of change over time (fluctuation).
  - **Trend** — direction of change (e.g., anxiety already dropping **before** treatment → improvement may be spontaneous). Before/after scores alone don't reveal the cause.

#### Withdrawal Designs
- **Withdrawal design** — three parts: (1) **baseline** (pre-treatment evaluation), (2) **introduce the IV** (begin treatment), (3) **withdraw treatment ("return to baseline")** and reassess. If behavior improves with treatment and worsens on withdrawal, the treatment is implicated. (Often written **A-B-A**.)
- **Difference from a case study:** the change is **deliberately designed to show whether treatment caused the change**.
- **Limitations:** ethically hard to **remove an effective treatment**; impossible when treatment can't be removed (e.g., imagining a beach, or **learned skills** that can't be unlearned).
- **Counterarguments (Barlow et al., 2009):** treatment is routinely withdrawn with meds (**drug holidays** test whether a med is responsible and avoid unnecessary side effects); withdrawal sometimes happens naturally; a **brief** withdrawal can suffice.

#### Multiple Baseline
- **Multiple baseline** — instead of stopping treatment, start treatment at **different times** across **settings** (home vs school), **behaviors** (crying then fighting), or **people**. If behavior changes **only when and where treatment is applied**, the treatment (not coincidence) caused the change → **improves internal validity**.
  - Across settings: treat Wendy at home first, then at work; improvement appears in each setting only after treatment there. (If she won the lottery and improved everywhere at once, treatment couldn't be credited.)
  - Across behaviors: reduce crying first, fighting later.
- **Durand (1999) ASD study (Fig 4.3):** multiple baseline across **5 children** using **functional communication training** (teach children to communicate instead of misbehave; Ch 14). DVs = **challenging behavior** and **unprompted communication**; behavior improved and communication began **only when treatment started** for each child → ruled out coincidence.
- **Advantages over withdrawal:** no need to withdraw treatment; **resembles natural treatment implementation** (a clinician can't treat all problems at once, takes repeated measures, observes orderly change).
- **Criticism:** small N → questionable **external validity**; addressed by using several people at once.

## Genetics and Behavior across Time and Cultures

### Studying Genetics
- Goal of **behavioral geneticists**: tease out the role of genetics in the **gene × environment interaction** that determines development (Ch 2).
- **Phenotype** — the **observable characteristics/behavior** of the individual.
- **Genotype** — the **unique genetic makeup** of the individual. (Down syndrome: phenotype = intellectual disability, slanted eyes, thick tongue; genotype = **extra 21st chromosome**.)
- **Endophenotypes** — the **genetic mechanisms** that ultimately contribute to the underlying problems causing symptoms (Grebb & Carlsson, 2009). In schizophrenia (Ch 13), researchers don't seek a "schizophrenia gene" but genes for the **working-memory problems** (the endophenotype).
- **Human genome project** — began **1990**; genome = "all the genes of an organism"; double helix discovered **1953 (Watson & Crick)**; humans have **~25,000 genes** on **46 chromosomes**; identified hundreds of disease genes.

#### Four broad genetic-research approaches (Kendler, 2005; Table 4.2)
- **Basic genetic epidemiology** — family/twin/adoption statistics → **Is the disorder inherited (heritability) and how much?**
- **Advanced genetic epidemiology** — family/twin/adoption statistics → **What factors influence the inherited disorder?** (developmental timing, sex differences, gene–environment interplay)
- **Gene finding** — **linkage and/or association** studies of families/individuals → **Where is the gene(s)?**
- **Molecular genetics** — biological analysis of **individual DNA** → **What biological processes do the genes affect?**

#### Specific techniques
- **Family studies** — examine a trait within a family; the studied member = **proband**. Genetic influence is suggested if the trait occurs more in **first-degree relatives** (parents, siblings, offspring) than in distant relatives or the general population. **Familial aggregation** (trait runs in families) is as high as **60%** for **blood–injury–injection phobia** (Judy, Ch 1) — one of the highest rates. **Weakness:** families share **environment**, so aggregation may be environmental (e.g., contagious emotional reactions to blood).
- **Adoption studies** — locate **first-degree relatives raised in different families**; if separated siblings share the disorder more than chance, **genes** are implicated; separates **genetic from environmental** influences.
- **Twin studies** — **identical (monozygotic)** twins share **100%** of genes (subtle differences from **epigenetic markers** in the womb; Gordon et al., 2012); **fraternal (dizygotic)** twins share **~50%** (like all first-degree relatives). Compare concordance between MZ and DZ twins.
  - **Height heritability ≈ 90%:** correlations 0.45 (first-degree relatives & DZ) vs **0.90 (MZ)**; ~10% variance = environment (caveat: 90% is an average; abuse/deprivation can override it).
  - **Lyons et al. (1995), Vietnam Era Twin Registry (~8,000 twin men, served 1965–1975):** antisocial behavior — MZ resembled each other more than DZ, **more so for adult than juvenile** antisocial behavior → **family environment** more influential for **juvenile** antisocial traits; **genetics** more influential for **adult** antisocial behavior.
  - **Weakness:** MZ twins may share **more similar environments** than DZ (dressed/named alike) and influence each other more (Johnson et al., 2009). **Fix:** combine **adoption + twin** methods (identical twins, one/both adopted) to separate nature vs nurture.
- **Genetic linkage analysis** — when a family disorder is studied, other inherited characteristics with **known chromosomal locations** (**genetic markers**) are measured simultaneously; a **link** between inheritance of the disorder and of a marker implies the disorder gene is **close to the marker on the same chromosome**.
  - **Egeland et al. (1987), Amish bipolar disorder:** two markers on **chromosome 11** (insulin gene + a cancer gene) linked to mood disorder → suggested a bipolar gene on chr 11. **Caution:** this and an X-chromosome linkage study (Biron et al., 1987) **failed to replicate** (Craddock & Jones, 2001) — replication failure is **common** (Zheng et al., 2012) and casts doubt on single-gene explanations for complex disorders. Be skeptical of "a gene for X" media claims.
- **Association studies** — also use genetic markers, but compare people **with vs without** the disorder (linkage compares markers within affected families). If certain markers occur significantly more in the affected group, the markers are assumed close to disorder genes. Better at detecting **weakly associated** genes.

### Studying Behavior over Time
- Why study change over time: decide **whether to treat** (e.g., grief over a grandparent often remits with social support → may not need expensive treatment; **childhood aggression usually does NOT go away** → treat early); gain **insight into how problems develop and worsen**.
- **Prospective studies** — record changes **as they occur** over time (more accurate; e.g., ASD onset is actually **gradual**, contradicting parents' retrospective reports of abrupt change; Rogers, 2009).
- **Retrospective studies** — ask people to **remember the past** (usually **less accurate**).
- **Retrospective information** — looking-back data; typically inaccurate (e.g., adults recalling childhood school anxiety).

#### Prevention Research — four categories (Daniels et al., 2009)
- **Health promotion / positive development strategies** — blanket **entire populations** (even those not at risk); focus on **skill building** to keep problems from developing (e.g., **Seattle Social Development Program** in high-crime elementary schools → long-term gains in achievement, reduced delinquency; Lonczak et al., 2002; Bailey, 2009).
- **Universal prevention** — entire populations, targeting **specific risk factors** (e.g., behavior problems in inner-city classrooms) without focusing on specific individuals.
- **Selective prevention** — targets **whole groups at risk** (e.g., children whose parents have died).
- **Indicated prevention** — targets **individuals already showing early signs** (e.g., depressive symptoms) but **not yet** diagnosable.

#### Designs across time
- **Cross-sectional design** — take a **cross section of the population across age groups** and compare on a characteristic (different people, same time).
  - **Brown & Finn (1982):** "drinking to get drunk" endorsed by **36%** of 12-yr-olds, **64%** of 15-yr-olds, **42%** of 17-yr-olds; drinking with friends: **28% → 80% → 88%**. Hypothesis: teen excessive drinking is a **deliberate attempt to get drunk** (attitudes before drinking drive intake), not poor judgment once drinking.
  - **Cohort** — participants in each age group (Brown & Finn: 12-, 15-, 17-yr-old cohorts); same age at same time → similar experiences.
  - **Cohort effect** — the **confounding of age and experience** (different cohorts had different cultural/historical exposure, e.g., "Just Say No"); a key **limitation** of cross-sectional designs.
  - **Advantages:** easier than longitudinal; some biologically driven phenomena (e.g., **Alzheimer's at 60 vs 70**) are less susceptible to cohort effects. **Limitation:** does not show **how individuals develop**.
- **Longitudinal design** — follow **one group over time** and assess change directly (same people, different ages).
  - **Advantages:** no cohort-effect problem; reveals **individual change**.
  - **Gershoff et al. (2012) spanking study:** **11,044 families** over 3 years; kindergartners → 3rd grade; early **spanking predicted MORE behavior problems** (arguing, fighting, anger) — physical punishment created more, not fewer, problems; supports critics of spanking.
  - **Limitations:** **costly, time-consuming**, attrition (participants move away or die), question may become irrelevant; and the **cross-generational effect** — difficulty generalizing findings to groups with different experiences (e.g., drug histories of 1960s young adults vs those born in the 1990s).
- **Sequential design** — combines longitudinal + cross-sectional: **repeated study of different cohorts over time**.
  - **Chassin et al. (2001) smoking beliefs:** 10 cohorts of middle/high schoolers tracked since the early 1980s into their mid-30s; as middle schoolers (ages 11–14) children saw smoking as **less risky** and even **beneficial** (looking mature); beliefs shifted later → **target smoking prevention in middle school** (Macy, Chassin, & Presson, 2012).

### Studying Behavior across Cultures
- Studying only one culture (or one age) misses important aspects; most literature is **Western** (Lambert et al., 1992) → an **ethnocentric** view that limits understanding and treatment (Gaw, 2008).
- Example: Malaysian **sakit gila** — supernatural-attributed disorder with some schizophrenia features but important differences (Barrett et al., 2005).
- Some treat **culture as the "treatment"** (the IV = effect of different cultures on behavior; López & Guarnaccia, 2012). **Key limitation:** you **cannot randomly assign infants to cultures**, and cultures differ in many ways (e.g., genetic backgrounds) that could explain behavior for non-cultural reasons.
- **Symptom expression varies by culture (depression):**
  - **Nigerians:** heaviness/heat in the head, crawling sensations in head/legs, burning sensations, belly bloated with water (Ebigno, 1982; James et al., 2012).
  - **United States:** worthlessness, inability to start/finish things, loss of interest, suicidal thoughts.
  - **China:** less likely to report feeling depressed or loss of interest, but may report **suicidal thoughts or worthlessness** (Phillips et al., 2007).
  - A standard cross-cultural definition of depression yields vastly different outcomes (Sue et al., 2012).
- **Varying thresholds for abnormality:** different tolerances complicate incidence/prevalence comparisons (e.g., traditional Chinese **talking to deceased relatives/local deities** could look like schizophrenia elsewhere; Lin et al., 2012).
- **Treatment models reflect cultural values:** Japan — **family model** of psychiatric hospitalization (caregivers as parents); 19th-century North America used a family model before today's **medical model** (Colp, 2009); Saudi Arabia — **veiling** complicates the therapeutic relationship (Ali et al., 2004); Islamic perspective — **medicine and religion inseparable**, so medical + religious treatments combined (Hakim-Larson et al., 2007).

## Power of a Program of Research

- No single method is "best"; the right method depends on the **question** and **practical limits**. Big issues are resolved by a **series of studies** (a **program of research**), not one perfect study.
- **Durand's autism program (worked example of method integration):**
  - **Single-subject withdrawal design** (Carr & Durand, 1985): self-injury/aggression are **primitive communication** — some children hit themselves for **attention** ("Please come here"), others to **escape hard tasks** ("This is too hard").
  - **Single-subject designs** (Durand, 1990; Durand & Carr, 1992): teaching appropriate communication (**functional communication training**) significantly **reduced** challenging behavior.
  - **3-year prospective longitudinal study** (>100 children with ASD; Durand, 2001): two predictors of severe behavior problems — parents (1) **pessimistic about their ability to help** and (2) **doubtful their child can change**; such parents "give up" and let the child dictate routines.
  - **Randomized clinical trial** (Durand & Hieneman, 2008; Durand et al., 2013): adding a **cognitive-behavioral intervention** to make pessimistic parents optimistic (replace "I have no control" with "I can help my child") improved **optimism AND child outcomes**, using **written protocols**.
- Takeaway: research proceeds **in stages**; a full picture emerges only from **many perspectives**.

## Replication

- Science's motto: **"Show Me Again."** Scientists are skeptical and never fully convinced something is "true."
- **Replication** — repeating findings (in different ways) builds confidence that results aren't coincidence; the more a process is repeated with expected results, the surer the causal conclusion. Replication is central to **every** strategy above (recall the failed bipolar linkage replications).

## Research Ethics (HIGH-YIELD)

- Core tension: **When does a scientist's interest in internal validity outweigh a client's right to treatment?** (e.g., withdrawal designs remove treatment; placebo control groups withhold it; Fisher & Vacanti-Shova, 2012.)
- **Informed consent** — a participant's **formal agreement to cooperate following full disclosure** of the research nature and their role. Origin: **post-WWII Nazi medical-experiment war trials**. In delay/withdrawal studies, participants are told why, plus risks/benefits; in placebo studies, participants are told they **may not** get active treatment (all blind to group), usually with the **option of treatment after the study ends**.
- **Four components of true informed consent** (Bankert & Amdur, 2006): **competence, voluntarism, full information, comprehension**. Hard to attain with **children**, people with **intellectual disability or schizophrenia**, **institutional** (coercion) settings, and across **cultures** (Lakes et al., 2012).
- **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** — committee of university faculty **and** community (nonacademic) members that reviews procedures to **protect participants' rights, welfare, and dignity**; required in university/medical settings (Fisher & Vacanti-Shova, 2012).
- **APA _Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct_** (Knapp et al., 2012): protect from **physical AND psychological harm**; **participant welfare has priority over experimental design**; maintain **confidentiality** and the participant's **right to concealment of identity**; **deception** requires approval by a **committee of peers**; if deception/concealment is used, participants must be **debriefed** (told the true purpose in language they understand).
- **Society for Research in Child Development (2007):** for children — confidentiality, protection from harm, debriefing, **plus informed consent from caregivers AND from children age 7+** (explained in understandable language). Other concerns: handling research errors, **fraud**, proper credit to others.
- **Participatory action research (Chevalier & Buckles, 2013)** — involving **consumers** (e.g., people with schizophrenia, depression, anxiety) as partners in **designing, running, and interpreting** research to improve relevance and participant treatment ("keep the face" on disorders).

## DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 overlay

> **DSM-5-TR note:** This is a **research-methods** chapter and contains **no diagnostic criteria sets**, so there are **no DSM-5/DSM-5-TR criterion changes** to flag here. Two terminology points to carry forward: (1) the book's older phrase **"intellectual disability"** is termed **intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability)** in DSM-5-TR; (2) DSM-5-TR continues to use **categorical** diagnostic criteria, which is precisely why methods like effect-size and clinical-significance analysis remain important. DSM-5-TR also reaffirms the importance of **culture** in research/assessment via the **Cultural Formulation Interview** — the modern formalization of this chapter's cross-cultural cautions.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 (in effect since 2022) shifts toward a more **dimensional** philosophy in several areas (e.g., the **dimensional personality-disorder model** vs DSM's categorical clusters), which is relevant to this chapter's **statistical vs clinical significance / effect size** theme. For cross-cultural research, ICD-11 retains **culture-related** descriptors but, like DSM-5-TR, moves away from listing discrete "culture-bound syndromes," instead framing them as **cultural concepts of distress** — the current-classification counterpart to the book's **sakit gila** example. These are overlays on the 2015 text, not part of the original chapter.

## Exam-focused recall (quick map)

- **Internal validity = IV caused DV (confounds threaten it); External validity = generalizability (controlling too tightly threatens it); the two are inversely related.**
- **Three internal-validity tools:** control group, randomization, analogue models.
- **Statistical (chance is small) vs clinical (meaningful) significance; effect size; social validity; patient uniformity myth.**
- **Methods:** case study (no scientific method, low validity) → correlation (relationship, not causation; directionality; epidemiology = incidence/prevalence) → experiment (manipulate IV; placebo / double-blind / comparative; clinical trial → RCT) → single-case (repeated measurement: level/variability/trend; withdrawal A-B-A; multiple baseline across settings/behaviors/people).
- **Genetics:** phenotype/genotype/endophenotype; family (proband, familial aggregation 60% BII phobia), adoption, twin (MZ 100% vs DZ 50%; height heritability 90%), linkage vs association.
- **Time:** prospective vs retrospective; cross-sectional (cohort effect) vs longitudinal (cross-generational effect) vs sequential; prevention = health promotion / universal / selective / indicated.
- **Ethics:** informed consent (competence, voluntarism, full information, comprehension; post-WWII origin), IRB, APA code, debriefing, consent from children 7+, participatory action research.
