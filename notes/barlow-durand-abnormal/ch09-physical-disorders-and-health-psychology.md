# Ch 9: Physical Disorders and Health Psychology

This chapter covers how psychological and social factors influence physical health and disease, through two pathways: (1) directly disrupting biological processes (e.g. stress on the immune system) and (2) via long-standing unhealthy lifestyles (smoking, diet, no exercise, unsafe sex). It surveys the physiology of stress (Selye's GAS, the HPA axis), psychoneuroimmunology, and stress-linked physical disorders: AIDS, cancer, hypertension, coronary heart disease, chronic pain, and chronic fatigue syndrome, then their psychosocial treatments. **Important nosology note:** the disorders in this chapter are *true physical disorders* with observable pathology, NOT the DSM somatic symptom disorders of Ch 6. Most of these conditions are diagnosed by medical criteria, not the DSM; DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 overlays below apply only where a psychiatric/diagnostic category is genuinely involved.

> **DSM-5-TR note (chapter-level):** This is a health-psychology chapter, not a DSM-disorder chapter. The vast majority of conditions (AIDS, hypertension, CHD, cancer, chronic pain, CFS) are medical diagnoses, so DSM-5 vs DSM-5-TR changes mostly do NOT apply. The DSM/ICD overlays in this note appear only where they are accurate (e.g. Somatic Symptom Disorder cross-reference, Illness Anxiety Disorder, the DSM-5/DSM-5-TR sleep–wake and depressive categories that overlap with CFS-type presentations).

## Psychological and Social Factors That Influence Health

### Historical / conceptual shift
- **First revolution in public health:** elimination/control of infectious disease. Deaths from infectious diseases (influenza, pneumonia, diphtheria, TB, typhoid, measles, GI infections) fell from **38.9% to 4%** of yearly total deaths.
- **Second revolution in public health:** shift of focus to **psychological and behavioral** contributors to illness and death.
- **1900 leading causes of death:** pneumonia/influenza (11.8%), tuberculosis (11.3%), diarrhea/enteritis (8.3%). **2010 leading causes:** heart disease (24.2%), cancer (23.3%), chronic lower respiratory disease (5.6%), stroke (5.2%).
- Old labels now seen as **misleading**: **psychosomatic medicine** (Alexander, 1950 — psychological factors affect somatic function) and **psychophysiological disorders**. Misleading because they implied mood/anxiety disorders lacked biological components. Reality: **biological, psychological, and social factors are implicated in EVERY disorder, mental and physical.**
- Key contrast: the **physical disorders** here have known/inferred physical causes and observable pathology (genital herpes, damaged heart muscle, malignant tumors, measurable hypertension) — UNLIKE **somatic symptom / conversion disorders** (Ch 6) where patients report damage but show no physical pathology.

### Two new fields
- **Behavioral medicine** (Agras, 1982): **interdisciplinary** field applying behavioral science to the **prevention, diagnosis, and treatment** of medical problems (psychologists + physicians + other health professionals together).
- **Health psychology**: NOT interdisciplinary; usually considered a **subfield of behavioral medicine**. Studies psychological factors in promoting/maintaining health, and analyzes/recommends improvements in health-care systems and policy *within psychology*.

### Two ways psychosocial factors affect health (Figure 9.1)
1. **Affect basic biological processes** that lead to illness/disease (e.g. stress disrupting biology).
2. **Long-standing behavior patterns (lifestyle)** put people at risk for physical disorders.
- **Genital herpes** example: incurable STI; **>50 million Americans (~20% of population)** infected with herpes simplex virus; virus dormant then reactivates; recurs ~**4 times/year**; spreads because people don't change behavior (e.g. condom use). **Stress triggers recurrences**; relaxation/stress-control reduces recurrences and episode duration via positive effects on the immune system.
- **Lifestyle** = enduring habits integral to daily living: smoking, drinking, poor eating, no exercise, insufficient injury control (no seat belts).
- **Smoking** = leading **preventable** cause of death; estimated to cause **70% of lung cancer deaths** and **20% of all deaths from any cause**.
- Fully **50% of deaths from the 10 leading causes** can be traced to lifestyle behaviors.

## The Nature of Stress

### Selye and the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)
- **Hans Selye (1936)**, Montreal: discovered (serendipitously) that daily injections themselves — not the substance — produced ulcers and atrophy of immune tissues in rats. Borrowing from engineering, called the nonspecific reaction **stress**; founded **stress physiology**.
- **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)** — 3 stages of response to sustained stress:
  1. **Alarm** — alarm response to immediate danger/threat.
  2. **Resistance** — mobilize coping mechanisms.
  3. **Exhaustion** — if stress too intense/long, body suffers permanent damage or death.
- Selye not correct in all details, but the core idea (**chronic stress causes permanent bodily damage / contributes to disease**) has been confirmed and elaborated.
- Definitions: in engineering, **stress** = response of a bridge to a truck's weight; the truck is the **stressor** (stimulus). The book focuses on **stress as the physiological response of the individual to a stressor.**

### The Physiology of Stress
- **Sympathetic nervous system** activated → mobilizes resources for **fight or flight**, increases strength and mental activity.
- **Endocrine system** activated via the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical (HPA) axis.**
- **Neuromodulators / neuropeptides** (hormones affecting the nervous system, secreted by glands into bloodstream) act like neurotransmitters.
- **HPA cascade:** **Corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF)** secreted by hypothalamus → stimulates **pituitary gland** → (with autonomic NS) activates the **adrenal gland** → secretes **cortisol** and related **"stress hormones."**
- **HPA–limbic feedback loop:** hypothalamus is next to limbic system (contains **hippocampus**, controls emotional memories). Hippocampus is **responsive to cortisol**; when stimulated, it helps **turn off the stress response** (negative feedback loop).
- **Sapolsky's hippocampus finding:** chronic stress → high cortisol → **kills nerve cells in the hippocampus** → compromised hippocampal activity → even more cortisol → further aging of the hippocampus. Leads to deficient problem-solving in the aged and ultimately **dementia**; affects susceptibility to infection. Hippocampal cell death from chronic stress occurs in humans with **PTSD (Ch 5)** and **depression (Ch 7).**

### Contributions to the stress response — Sapolsky's baboons
- Studied free-ranging baboons in Kenya (their stress is **psychological, not physical**); arranged in a **social dominance hierarchy.**
- **Dominant males:** lower resting cortisol; cortisol rose more quickly during a real emergency.
- **Subordinate males:** chronically high cortisol; **excess CRF** from hypothalamus + **diminished pituitary sensitivity** to CRF → HPA system less sensitive to cortisol, less efficient at turning off the stress response.
- Subordinates also had **fewer circulating lymphocytes (white blood cells)** = immune suppression, and **less high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol** = higher atherosclerosis/CHD risk.
- **Key conclusion:** the most important factor regulating stress physiology is a **sense of control** (predictability + controllability). Dominance combined with **stability** produced optimal hormone profiles; dominance with instability (constant infighting) produced subordinate-like profiles.

### Stress, Anxiety, Depression, and Excitement (Figure 9.3 continuum)
- **Vaillant (1979):** followed >200 healthy Harvard sophomore men (1942–44) for 30+ years; those who developed psychological disorders / were highly stressed became chronically ill or died at significantly higher rates.
- The four states — **excitement → stress → anxiety → depression** — lie on a continuum depending on **sense of control / ability to cope** (more control = excitement; less control = depression).
- **Excitement:** rapid heartbeat, energy, jumpy stomach but pleasurable if well-prepared/confident.
- **Stress:** challenge feels manageable if you had time/help, but lacking resources → pressured, tense, headache, upset stomach.
- **Anxiety:** threat is real and you believe little can be done; less sense of control.
- **Depression:** perceive life as permanently uncontrollable; lose hope, stop trying to cope.
- Underlying physiology is **similar** (sympathetic arousal, neurotransmitters/neurohormones), but they differ in **blood pressure** (rises when challenges overwhelm coping → low control = anxiety/depression; unchanged in excitement/marked stress) and, most markedly, in **self-efficacy** (Bandura, 1986) — confidence one can cope.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** This continuum is a dimensional model, NOT a diagnostic set. It aligns conceptually with DSM-5-TR's emphasis on transdiagnostic factors (negative affectivity), but excitement/stress here are not DSM diagnoses.

## Stress and the Immune Response

### Stress increases infection risk
- **Sheldon Cohen et al.:** exposed volunteers to a controlled dose of cold virus; chance of getting sick was **directly related to stress in the past year**; intensity of stress/negative affect at exposure predicted **cold severity (mucus production).**
- **Sociability** (quantity/quality of social relationships) — most sociable least likely to catch a cold; least sociable most likely (socializing relieves stress). A **positive/optimistic cognitive style** protects against colds.
- **Exam periods** = stressors that increase upper-respiratory infections.
- Stress effects on the immune system are **rapid**: weakened immune response within **2 hours** of stress exposure.
- **Cohen et al. (1999):** infected 55 participants with influenza A; higher stress → more severe flu; stress hormones triggered the cytokine **interleukin-6**, producing tissue **inflammation** — a mechanism by which stress breaks down resistance.
- Stressful events linked to lowered immunity: **marital discord/relationship difficulties, job loss, death of a loved one.**
- **Depression lowers immune functioning** (esp. older adults); the underlying **sense of uncontrollability** may be the crucial mechanism. Depression also → poor self-care + riskier behaviors.
- **Acute vs chronic stress:** most studies use acute stressors, but **chronic stress** is more problematic (lasts longer) — e.g. lowered immunity in caregivers of chronically ill family (Alzheimer's patients).

### How the immune system works (Figure 9.4)
- **Immune system** = identifies and eliminates **antigens** (foreign materials: bacteria, viruses, parasites; also the body's own aberrant/damaged cells, e.g. tumors; and donated organs).
- **Two branches: humoral and cellular.** White blood cells = **leukocytes** do most of the work.
- **Macrophages:** first line of defense — surround and destroy antigens; signal **lymphocytes** (= **B cells** and **T cells**).
- **B cells (humoral branch):** produce highly specific **immunoglobulins** = antibodies that combine with antigens to neutralize them. After neutralization, **memory B cells** speed future responses (basis of **inoculations/vaccinations**).
- **T cells (cellular branch):** do NOT produce antibodies.
  - **Killer T cells:** directly destroy viral infections and cancerous processes; create **memory T cells.**
  - **Helper T cells (T4 cells):** enhance immune response — signal B cells to make antibodies and tell other T cells to destroy antigens.
  - **Suppressor T cells:** suppress antibody production by B cells when no longer needed.
- Should have **twice as many T4 (helper) cells as suppressor T cells.** Too many T4 → overreactive immune system attacks normal cells = **autoimmune disease** (e.g. **rheumatoid arthritis**). Too many suppressor T cells → body open to antigen invasion.
- **HIV directly attacks helper T cells** (crucial to both humoral and cellular immunity) → severely weakens immunity → **AIDS.**

### Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)
- **Robert Ader & Cohen (1974/1975):** classical conditioning paradigm — paired sweet water + immune-suppressing drug in rats; later, **sweet water alone** suppressed immunity = the immune system was **conditioned.**
- Founded **psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)** = study of **psychological influences on the neurological responding implicated in immune response.**
- **Pathways (Figure 9.5):** psychological state → CNS innervation + hormonal response + behavioral change → immune change → disease susceptibility.
- **Molecular pathway (Cole et al., 2010):** stress activates a **transcription factor (GABA-1)** that activates the **interleukin-6 gene** → makes a protein turning on the **inflammatory response.** Good for a cut, but **chronic inflammation exacerbates cancer, heart disease, and diabetes, and shortens life.** The **serotonin transporter gene** also makes one vulnerable to certain stressors.

## Psychosocial Effects on Physical Disorders

### AIDS
- **Highest-priority** disease worldwide. People living with HIV reached an estimated **34.2 million in 2011** (22% higher than 2000). Deaths began to level off only in **2004** with treatment/prevention; still **1.8 million died of AIDS in 2010.** Hardest-hit: southern Africa (**15–28% of adults** in worst regions); **67% of people living with HIV are in sub-Saharan Africa**; ~**18 million children orphaned.**
- **Transmission (Table 9.2):** Worldwide — heterosexual contact **59–69%**, male-to-male contact 5–10%, injection drug use 10%. **US** — male-to-male contact **50%**, injection drug use 17%, heterosexual contact 32%. In most of the world (esp. underdeveloped), **heterosexual activity** is primary.
- **Disease course:** highly variable. After months-to-years symptom-free → minor problems (weight loss, fever, night sweats) = **AIDS-related complex (ARC).** Diagnosis of AIDS made only when a serious disease appears (pneumocystis pneumonia, cancer, dementia, wasting syndrome). **Median time from infection to full AIDS = 7.3 to 10+ years.**
- **Treatment: highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART)** — drug combinations that suppress the virus even in advanced cases; slowed progression, reduced mortality (2-year-plus survival rose to 85% by 2005; US death rate fell ≥50% from 2002–2010). BUT **not a cure** (virus lies dormant, lifetime of meds); high dropout from side effects (nausea, diarrhea — **61% in one study**); **drug-resistant strains** now transmitted. Current recommendation: start early + maximize adherence; HAART can even **prevent acquisition** in high-risk populations.
- **Psychological factors and progression:** people exposed but not infected have robust **cellular immunity** (partly genetic, partly psychological). Strong confidence in providers + strong social support = stronger immunity / non-progression. **High stress, depression, low social support → faster progression** (depression strongly reduces drug adherence).
- **CBSM (cognitive-behavioral stress-management) studies:**
  - **Lutgendorf et al. (1997):** decreased depression/anxiety; reduced antibodies to herpes simplex virus II (better cellular immune control).
  - **Antoni et al. (2000):** 73 symptomatic HIV+ gay/bisexual men; CBSM → lower anxiety/anger/perceived stress and, **1 year later, better immune functioning (higher T cells).**
  - **Goodkin et al. (2001):** 10-week treatment buffered against rise in **HIV viral load** (predictor of progression).
  - **Antoni et al. (2006):** CBSM + medication training → **decreased viral load 15 months later** vs medication training alone; mediated by reduced depression → reduced cortisol.
- **Prevention** is the most effective intervention: reduce risky behavior, promote safe sex, earlier diagnosis, link to treatment, maximize adherence (all behavior change).

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 codes HIV disease medically (1C62); not a mental disorder. No DSM diagnosis for AIDS itself, though associated **neurocognitive disorder due to HIV infection** appears in DSM-5/DSM-5-TR (Major/Mild Neurocognitive Disorder due to HIV, Ch 13–style coding).

### Cancer
- Development and course of cancers are subject to **psychosocial influences**; new field = **psychoncology** (oncology = study of cancer).
- **Spiegel et al. (1989):** 86 women with advanced metastatic breast cancer; 50 received weekly **group psychotherapy.** Therapy group survived ~**3 years** vs control ~**18 months** (twice as long). At 4 years, 1/3 of therapy patients alive while all non-therapy best-care patients had died. Reanalysis found no medical-treatment differences accounting for the effect. NOT a cure — at 10 years only 3 therapy patients survived.
- Mixed replication: some studies confirmed survival/reduced recurrence; others (Coyne, Stefanek, & Palmer, 2007; Goodwin et al., 2001) found reduced depression/pain/increased well-being but **no survival benefit.**
- **Andersen et al. (2008):** 227 surgically treated breast cancer patients → psychological intervention (stress reduction, mood, health behaviors, adherence) vs assessment only. After **median 11-year follow-up**, intervention reduced **risk of dying from breast cancer by 56%** and **recurrence by 45%** (Figure 9.9).
- **Giese-Davis et al. (2011):** reducing depressive symptoms improved survival in metastatic breast cancer.
- **Mechanisms** of psychological treatment benefit: better health habits, closer adherence, improved endocrine functioning/stress response → better immune function. Psychological factors may contribute to **course AND development** of cancer via immune function, regulation of cancer-causing viruses, **DNA repair**, and expression of tumor-growth genes.
- **Telomeres** = DNA-protein complexes on chromosome ends protecting DNA. **Chronic psychological stress → accelerated cellular aging (short telomeres)** — but it's the **negative interpretation/appraisal** of stress, not the absolute level, that drives cellular aging (O'Donovan et al., 2012).
- **"Benefit finding":** some breast cancer patients report enhanced sense of purpose, deepening spirituality, closer ties, changed priorities; reflects coping skills, sense of control, optimism — resilience traits targeted in treatment.
- **Children with cancer:** invasive/painful procedures; psychological prep (breathing exercises, watching films of the procedure, rehearsal with dolls — Melamed & Siegel, 1975) reduces suffering. Parents nearly all develop **PTSD symptoms**; problem-solving interventions (Sahler et al., 2005) reduced parental stress.

### Cardiovascular Problems
- **Cardiovascular system** = heart, blood vessels, control mechanisms.
- **Stroke / cerebral vascular accident (CVA):** blockage or rupture of blood vessels to/in the brain → temporary or permanent brain damage.
- **Raynaud's disease:** loss of circulation to peripheral parts (fingers, toes), pain, sensations of cold.
- **Case of John** ("The Human Volcano"): 55-yo executive, smoker, mildly obese, on BP meds since 42, chest pain, time-urgent, hostile, multitasks (proofreads + phone + lunch simultaneously) — classic Type A + hypertension.

#### Hypertension
- **Hypertension (high blood pressure)** = major risk factor for stroke, heart disease, kidney disease. BP rises when blood vessels constrict → heart works harder → wear and tear.
- ~**90%** have no specific physical cause = **essential hypertension** (small % traced to kidney disease / adrenal tumors).
- **WHO threshold:** high if >**160/95**; but **140/90 or higher** is cause for concern and more commonly used.
- **Systolic** = pressure when heart is pumping (first value); **Diastolic** = pressure between beats at rest (second value). **Elevations in diastolic** are more worrisome.
- **Prevalence:** 27.6% of ages 35–64 in North America; **44.2%** in six European countries; median **38% (men) / 40% (women)** by US county. Called the **"silent killer"** (few/no symptoms).
- **African Americans:** **1.5–2× more likely** to develop hypertension than whites; hypertensive vascular disease **5–10× greater** rate; greater vascular responsiveness even in those without high BP (Saab et al., 1992); risk augmented by **stereotype threat** (Blascovich et al., 2001).
- **Biological causes:** strong **genetic** component (runs in families; offspring of hypertensives at 2× risk; greater BP reactivity if parents hypertensive); two neurobiological factors — **autonomic NS activity** (sympathetic → vessel constriction → higher BP) and **kidney sodium/water regulation** (too much salt → higher blood volume/pressure → hence salt restriction).
- **Psychological factors:** personality, coping style, stress level; strong relationship between **social support and BP**; loneliness, depression, uncontrollability; a **"warm touch"** (affectionate touching) significantly decreased BP in married couples.
- **CARDIA study** (>5,000 adults, 15 yrs): two factors each **nearly double** hypertension risk — **hostility** (esp. interpersonal) and **time urgency/impatience.** Anger and hostility raise BP. Per **Alexander (1939)**, inability to express anger → hypertension; but what matters more is **how often** anger/hostility are experienced and expressed during stress. **Constructive anger expression** = markedly lower BP.

#### Coronary Heart Disease (CHD)
- **CHD** = blockage of arteries supplying blood to the heart muscle (the **myocardium**). **#1 cause of death in Western cultures.**
- Terms: **angina pectoris (angina)** = chest pain from partial artery obstruction; **atherosclerosis** = fatty plaque builds up inside arteries; **ischemia** = deficiency of blood to a body part from narrowed arteries; **myocardial infarction (heart attack)** = death of heart tissue when an artery is clogged.
- **Inherited vulnerability** + diet, exercise, culture.
- **Psychological factors:** stress, anxiety, anger + poor coping + low social support → CHD. **Myocardial stunning** = heart failure from severe stress (e.g. sudden death of family member). Emotional-trigger heart attacks (rare) disproportionate in people high in **negative affectivity + social inhibition.**
- **Stress-reduction value:** meta-analysis of 37 studies → **34% reduction in death from heart attacks, 29% reduction in recurrence**, plus benefits to BP/cholesterol/weight (Dusseldorp et al., 1999).
- **Type A behavior pattern** (Friedman & Rosenman, 1959, 1974): excessive competitive drive, time pressure/urgency, impatience, high energy (accelerated speech/motor activity), angry outbursts. **Type B** = relaxed, less deadline-concerned, no overriding ambition.
- **Western Collaborative Group Study (WCGS):** 3,154 healthy men, age 39–59, 8-yr follow-up — Type A **at least 2× more likely** to develop CHD; in younger men (39–49), CHD **~6× more often** in Type A.
- **Framingham Heart Study** (40+ years; 1,674 healthy men/women, 8 yrs): both sexes with Type A **>2× more likely** to develop CHD (men nearly 3×); for Type A women, highest CHD risk with **low education.**
- **Cultural effects:** Japanese men in Hawaii study did NOT replicate (Type A prevalence 18.7% vs ~50% US; CHD 4% vs 13% US). Japanese Americans who were **"most Japanese"** had lowest CHD (= Japan rates); **"least Japanese"** had **3–5× greater** CHD.
- **Type A construct problems:** hard to measure (inconsistent across interviews/questionnaires); most people are a mixture of A and B; later studies did not consistently support the A–CHD link.

##### The Role of Chronic Negative Emotions
- **Anger** is the key component responsible for much of the Type A–CHD link (Chida & Steptoe, 2009). **Ironson et al. (1992):** anger impaired heart pumping efficiency → risk of **arrhythmias.** **Iribarren et al. (2000):** high hostility/anger → coronary artery calcification (early CHD sign).
- Important components: **chronically high negative affect (anger)** + **time urgency/impatience.**
- **Anxiety and depression** are as important as anger:
  - **Frasure-Smith et al. (1999):** post-heart-attack depressed patients **3× more likely to die** in the following year regardless of initial severity.
  - **Whooley et al. (2008):** depressive symptoms → **31% higher** rate of cardiovascular events.
  - Women with depression history → **29% greater** stroke risk (Pan et al., 2011).
  - Early-onset anxiety predicted later CHD (Swedish 4-decade study, ~50,000 men).
- **Type D personality:** social inhibition + heightened negative emotions; implicated in CHD (Compare et al., 2013), though not all studies agree.
- **Mechanism:** inflammatory processes of the stress response directly contribute to atherosclerosis and heart failure.
- **Reserve capacity model (Gallo & Matthews, 2003; Figure 9.12):** low SES → fewer resources → more stressful events; coping skills + social support form a **reserve capacity** buffering stress; negative emotion/cognition raise risk while **positive emotions / optimism reduce** CHD risk.

> **DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 note:** Depression and anxiety here are risk factors; their formal criteria belong to Ch 5 (anxiety) and Ch 7 (depressive). DSM-5-TR added **Prolonged Grief Disorder** (relevant since bereavement is a cardiovascular stressor). Type A/D are personality *behavior patterns*, not DSM personality disorders; ICD-11 uses a **dimensional personality model** (severity + trait domains) vs DSM-5's categorical clusters, but neither classifies Type A/D.

### Chronic Pain
- Pain itself is **not a disorder** — it's the fundamental signal of injury/illness; low-level pain prevents further injury.
- **NIH:** chronic pain = **costliest medical problem** in the US — affects ≥**116 million** people, costs up to **$635 billion/year**; Americans spend ≥**$125 billion/year** on treatment; pain causes **80% of all physician visits.**
- **Two kinds of clinical pain:**
  - **Acute pain:** follows injury, disappears when injury heals (often within a month).
  - **Chronic pain:** may begin acute but does NOT decrease over time even after healing; typically in muscles, joints, tendons (esp. lower back); also vascular pain, headaches, degenerative/terminal disease pain, cancer-tumor pain.
- **Three components of pain experience:**
  - **Pain** = subjective experience reported by patient.
  - **Pain behaviors** = overt manifestations (changed sitting/walking, complaining, grimacing, avoiding activities/work).
  - **Suffering** = emotional component that may or may not accompany pain.

#### Psychological and Social Aspects of Pain
- Severity of pain does NOT predict reaction; differences are largely **psychological** (same factors as the stress response — anxiety, depression).
- Determining factor = **general sense of control** over the situation; control + optimism = less distress/disability. Treating depression diminishes chronic pain.
- **Philips & Grant (1991):** 117 back/neck pain patients; 40% had chronic pain at 6 months. Subsequent disability related more to **personality, socioeconomic factors, and whether the person planned a lawsuit** than to pain intensity. **Preexisting anxiety/personality problems** predict chronic pain.
- **Negative profile** predicting chronic pain: anxiety/depression, poor coping, low social support, possibility of **compensation via disability claims.**
- **Zautra et al. (2005):** positive affect in women with arthritis/fibromyalgia predicted less subsequent pain.
- **Phantom limb pain:** people who lost a limb feel excruciating pain in the absent limb, describing exact location/type; due to changes in the **sensory cortex** (Flor et al., 1995; Ramachandran, 1993). Catastrophizing → more intense pain. Coping (e.g. **mirror** therapy used by Boston Marathon bombing victims).
- **Social factors:** family becoming caring/sympathetic = **operant control of pain behavior** (behavior under control of social consequences). BUT a **strong general social support network reduces pain** (Jamison & Virts, 1990 — lack of family support → more pain sites, more pain behavior, more distress). Even a **photo of a loved one reduces pain** (Master et al., 2009). Different mechanisms: general support reduces stress/promotes coping, while specifically **reinforcing pain behaviors** increases them.

#### Biological Aspects of Pain
- **Endogenous (natural) opioids — endorphins / enkephalins:** act like neurotransmitters; the brain uses them to **shut down pain** even with marked tissue damage; basis of **"runner's high."** Implicated in tolerance/dependence, eating disorders, stress reactions. **Bandura et al. (1987):** higher self-efficacy/control → higher pain tolerance and increased endogenous opioid production.
- **Gate control theory of pain (Melzack & Wall, 1965, 1982):** integrates psychological + physical factors. Pain impulses travel to spinal column → brain. The **dorsal horns** of the spinal column act as a **"gate."**
  - **Small fibers (A-delta, C fibers):** tend to **OPEN** the gate → increase transmission of painful stimuli.
  - **Large fibers (A-beta fibers):** tend to **CLOSE** the gate.
  - The brain sends signals back down the spinal cord affecting the gate: **fear/anxiety → more intense pain** (vigilance message); **positive emotion / absorption → inhibitory signal closes the gate** (reduces pain).
  - Criticized as overly simplistic, updated periodically (Melzack, 1999, 2005), but basic elements supported.
- **Gender differences in pain:** women suffer more **migraines, arthritis, carpal tunnel, TMJ pain** (+ menstrual cramps, labor pains); men have more **cardiac pain and backache.** Both sexes have endogenous opioid systems (possibly more powerful in males), but women may have an **extra estrogen-dependent pain-regulating pathway** (Mogil et al., 1993) — implication: men and women may benefit from different drugs/interventions.
- **Inseparability of psychological and physical pain (placebo response):** brain-imaging shows placebo pills produce **real** chemical brain changes (activating the **endogenous opioid/endorphin system**) — not just reported relief. **Distraction + placebo** had an **additive** effect (different brain circuits — Buhle et al., 2012). **Acetaminophen (Tylenol)** reduced "hurt feelings" from social rejection and reduced neural responses in the **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex** and **anterior insula** — physical and **social pain share neural mechanisms** (Eisenberger, 2012). Stimulating the **right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** (emotion regulation) reduced pain after social exclusion.

### Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS)
- **Neurasthenia** (George Beard, 1869, "lack of nerve strength"): lack of energy, fatigue, aches/pains, low-grade fever, no physical pathology; attributed to demands of the time, material-success preoccupation, changing role of women. Disappeared in Western cultures early 20th C but remains common in **China.**
- **Chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS)** symptoms ≈ identical to neurasthenia.
- **Definition / criteria (Table 9.4, Fukuda et al., 1994):**
  > **Inclusion criteria:** clinically evaluated, **medically unexplained fatigue ≥ 6 months** that is: of **new onset** (not lifelong), **not from ongoing exertion**, **not substantially relieved by rest**, with **substantial reduction** in previous activity level. PLUS **four or more** of: subjective memory impairment, sore throat, tender lymph nodes, muscle pain, joint pain, headache, unrefreshing sleep, **post-exertional malaise lasting >24 hours.**
- **Proposed causes:** viral infection (initially **Epstein-Barr virus**; later **XMRV**, a retrovirus similar to HIV — but this was **disproven** as based on faulty research), immune dysfunction, toxins, clinical depression. **No physical cause confirmed.**
- **Epidemiology:** ~**0.4%** community prevalence (Jason et al., 1999; higher in Latino/African American); up to **3%** in primary care; **predominantly women**; usually begins early adulthood (can occur in children as young as 7); twin study 2.7%; by age 53, 1.1% lifetime.
- **Course:** chronic — in 100 patients followed 18 months, symptoms did not decrease in **79%.** Better outcomes with better baseline mental health, less sedating medication, and a more **"psychological"** (vs medical) causal attribution. Not associated with increased mortality/suicide.
- **Sharpe's model (1997; Figure 9.14):** achievement-oriented individuals (driven by a sense of inadequacy) undergo extreme stress/acute illness → **misinterpret** lingering fatigue/pain as a continuing disease worsened by activity and improved by rest → behavioral avoidance, helplessness, depression → chronic inactivity → weakness/lack of stamina → episodic bursts of activity → further fatigue. Core beliefs: "I am inadequate," "I must always perform perfectly / always cope / never show weakness."
- **Over-exercise** possibly a cause (Harvey et al., 2008 — high pre-onset exercise + long bursts after onset). Preexisting **stress + emotional instability** are important (Kato et al., 2006); higher early adverse stressful events (Heim et al., 2006).
- **Treatment:** **pharmacological treatment not effective.** **Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT)** works — Sharpe's CBT (increase activity, regulate rest, cognitive therapy, relaxation): **73% improved** vs control. Deale et al. (1997): **70% of CBT completers** achieved substantial improvement at 6-month follow-up vs **19%** of relaxation-only (Table 9.5); gains maintained at 5 years. Emphasis on **preventing bursts of overexercise**; **graded (not intense) exercise** can help; cognitive reappraisal of fatigue's meaning + increased self-efficacy are important.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** CFS is a **medical** diagnosis (no DSM disorder). Its presentation overlaps with **Somatic Symptom Disorder** and **Illness Anxiety Disorder** (Ch 6) when illness beliefs dominate, and with depressive/sleep–wake disorders. DSM-5-TR retains Somatic Symptom Disorder (positive symptom-focus criteria) — relevant because CFS patients with a "medical" attribution fare worse.
>
> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 classifies the entity as **postviral fatigue syndrome / myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME/CFS)** under diseases of the nervous system (8E49), reflecting the ongoing biomedical-vs-psychosocial debate — a contrast with this 2015-era textbook's CBT-centric, stress-attribution framing.

## Psychosocial Treatment of Physical Disorders

### Pain, immunity, and the case for pain relief
- **Liebeskind / Page et al. (1993):** postsurgical pain in rats **doubled** the rate a cancer metastasized to the lungs; rats given morphine had **fewer** metastases than even non-surgery rats. Pain may **reduce natural killer cells** (via stress) — a vicious circle. Applies to humans; yet **fewer than half** of US cancer patients get sufficient pain relief.
- **Pre-emptive pain relief:** patients given pain medication **before** surgery reported less pain after and requested less medication.

### Biofeedback
- **Biofeedback:** making patients aware of physiological functions not normally noticed (heart rate, BP, muscle tension, EEG/brain waves, blood flow), then learning to control them.
- **Neal Miller (1969):** rats learned to control physiological responses via operant conditioning (hard to replicate in animals, but applied successfully to humans).
- Equipment makes the response **visible/audible**; success produces a signal (gauge reading + tone).
- Humans can discriminate ANS changes with high accuracy; **Zillmann (1983)/Shapiro (1974):** we lost this skill through disuse — adaptive to ignore internal stimuli to focus on tasks.
- **Headache application:** biofeedback successful but **no more so than deep muscle relaxation.** **Holroyd et al. (1984):** biofeedback's success depends not on tension reduction but on instilling a **sense of control** over pain (links back to Sapolsky's baboons). **38–63%** of patients on relaxation/biofeedback achieve significant headache reduction vs ~**35%** placebo. Effects are **long-lasting.** Apply both (some benefit more from one than the other).

### Relaxation and Meditation
- **Progressive muscle relaxation (Edmund Jacobson, 1938):** tense muscle groups in sequence, then relax them to learn to recognize/reduce tension (Table 9.6 lists tensing instructions per group).
- **Meditation:** focus attention on a body part / single thought/image with slowed breathing. **Transcendental meditation:** focus on a repeated syllable (**mantra**).
- **Herbert Benson — the relaxation response:** stripped-down TM; silently repeat a mantra (suggested "one"); 10–20 min/day → calmer; reduces neurotransmitter/stress-hormone flow, mediated by increased **sense of control/mastery.** Taught in **60%** of US medical schools.
- Relaxation has positive (sometimes modest) effects on headaches, hypertension, acute/chronic pain; almost always part of comprehensive pain-management programs.

### A Comprehensive Stress- and Pain-Reduction Program (Barlow, Rapee, & Parini)
- Components:
  1. **Self-monitoring** of stress (Daily Stress Record — time, intensity 0–8, triggers, symptoms, thoughts) — reveals patterns, helpful in itself.
  2. **Deep muscle relaxation** — tense then actively let go beyond inactivity.
  3. **Cognitive therapy** — develop realistic appraisals/attitudes (case of **Sally** — reappraising "my mother always calls at the worst time" from 100% to 20%, and reducing catastrophic interpretation).
  4. **Coping strategies** — **time management** (prioritize, ignore nonessentials) and **assertiveness training.**
- **Comprehensive programs are generally MORE effective than individual components alone** for chronic pain, CFS, tension headaches, hypertension, TMJ pain, cancer pain, and chronic lower back pain (meta-analysis of 22 studies).

### Drugs and Stress-Reduction Programs
- Chronic reliance on **OTC analgesics** lessens efficacy of comprehensive programs and may worsen headaches via **rebound headaches.**
- **Michultka et al. (1989):** only **29% of high analgesic users** vs **55% of low users** achieved ≥50% headache reduction.
- **Holroyd et al. (1991):** CBT produced ≥50% headache reduction in **56%** vs only **27%** for the antidepressant **amitriptyline.**
- **Grazzi et al. (2002):** medication-only condition had more relapse than medication + biofeedback/relaxation at 3 years. Psychological treatment consistently **reduces drug consumption.**

### Denial as a Means of Coping
- Generally professionals work to eliminate denial (people who deny pain miss symptom variations, avoid treatment/rehab). **Compas et al. (2006):** adolescents using denial/avoidance/wishful thinking had higher anxiety/somatic complaints.
- BUT **denial isn't always harmful** (Shelley Taylor, 2009): most well-functioning people deny serious-condition implications **initially.** During the acutely stressful **diagnosis** period, denial may help patients endure the shock and then develop coping responses later. **Value of denial depends on timing** — eventually one must face, process emotions, and come to terms.
- **Scheier et al. (1989):** optimistic coronary bypass patients recovered faster, returned to activity sooner, better 6-month quality of life; optimists less likely to use denial.

### Modifying Behaviors to Promote Health
- Two influence pathways: directly affecting biology, and via **unhealthy lifestyle.**
- Top risk behaviors: **unhealthy eating, lack of exercise, smoking**; also unprotected sex, no injury precautions, excessive alcohol, excessive sun.
- **Smoking:** **21%** of US adults are regular smokers; leading preventable cause of death, **20% of all deaths**, ~**443,000 deaths/year.**

#### Injury Prevention
- **Accidents** = leading cause of death for ages **1–45**; 5th leading cause overall. Avg societal cost of one automobile death = **$4.5 million.**
- **Lizette Peterson:** preventing child injuries (injuries kill more children than the next 6 causes combined; nearly half of poisonings occur in children under 6). Parents often see injuries as fated/out of their hands; some believe minor injuries have value.
- **Behavior-change programs work:** children taught to escape fires, report emergencies, cross streets, ride bikes safely, deal with cuts — skills maintained for months. Repeated warnings ineffective; programmatic behavior change needed (but nonexistent in most communities).

#### AIDS Prevention
- No vaccine → **changing high-risk behavior** is the most effective prevention. **Testing alone** and **education alone** do little to change behavior.
- **San Francisco model (Table 9.7):** coordinated community-level program across media, clinics, providers, community organizations, schools, worksites, testing centers — targeting **Information, Motivation, Skills, Norms, Policy/legislation.** Frequent unprotected sex dropped from **37.4%/33.9%** to **1.7%/4.2%** in two gay-men samples (vs no change in comparison groups).
- Must extend to **women and minorities** (women = **50% of new AIDS cases** in 2003; women's highest risk age 15–25 vs men late 20s–early 30s; different circumstances, e.g. prostitution from economic deprivation).
- **SiHLE** (Sistas Informing, Healing, Living, Empowering; DiClemente et al., 2004): for inner-city African American adolescent females; targets relational skills, pride, self-efficacy, peer influences. 522 girls aged 14–18; four 1-hour sessions on ethnic/gender pride, HIV knowledge, communication, condom skills, healthy relationships → more condom use, less unprotected sex, fewer partners, fewer STIs/pregnancies at 1 year.
- **Africa:** focus on **couples** counseling/testing (only 22% of adults 15–49 know their HIV status; 55–93% of new infections occur within cohabiting relationships); successful in Rwanda, Uganda, Kenya.

#### Smoking in China
- ~**320 million** habitual smokers (more than the entire US population); ~90% male; China consumes **33%** of the world's cigarettes; smoking projected to kill **100 million** Chinese in the next 50 years. **47%** of Chinese boys (grades 7–9) had smoked vs 16% of girls.
- **Hangzhou study (1989):** used strong family ties — children of smokers in 23 primary schools took home antismoking literature, wrote letters asking fathers to quit, reported monthly. **~12%** of fathers in the intervention group quit ≥6 months vs **0.2%** in controls.
- **Ma et al. (2008)** myths Chinese smokers hold: smoking as personal freedom; tobacco important socially/culturally; health effects controllable via "measured use"; tobacco's economic importance.

#### Stanford Three Community Study (Meyer et al., 1980)
- Model **community-level** program; three similar central-California towns (1972–75); target = **reduce CHD risk factors** (smoking, high BP, diet, weight).
- **Tracy** = control (information collected only). **Gilroy** + part of **Watsonville** = **media blitz** only. Most of **Watsonville** = media + **face-to-face behavioral counseling** for high-risk townspeople.
- Results (Figure 9.16): interventions markedly reduced CHD risk factors; **Watsonville's intensive-instruction group** had substantially lower risk factors and higher knowledge than media-only or control communities. Saves lives + reduces disability leave (more than covers cost), but not yet widespread.

## Key Terms (chapter glossary)
- **behavioral medicine** — interdisciplinary application of behavioral science to medical problems.
- **health psychology** — subfield studying psychological factors in health promotion/maintenance and health-care policy.
- **general adaptation syndrome (GAS)** — Selye's alarm → resistance → exhaustion sequence.
- **stress** — physiological response of the individual to a stressor.
- **self-efficacy** — Bandura's confidence that one can cope with stress/challenge.
- **immune system / antigens** — system that identifies and eliminates foreign materials (antigens).
- **autoimmune disease** — immune system attacks the body's normal tissue (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis).
- **psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)** — study of psychological influences on neurological/immune responding.
- **AIDS-related complex (ARC)** — pre-AIDS minor symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats).
- **psychoncology** — study of psychosocial factors in the course/treatment of cancer.
- **cardiovascular disease / stroke (CVA) / hypertension / essential hypertension** — see above.
- **coronary heart disease (CHD)** — blockage of arteries supplying the myocardium.
- **type A / type B behavior pattern** — competitive/time-urgent/hostile vs relaxed.
- **acute pain / chronic pain** — heals vs persists past healing.
- **endogenous opioids** — natural endorphins/enkephalins that inhibit pain.
- **chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS)** — ≥6 months medically unexplained fatigue + symptoms.
- **biofeedback** — learning to control physiological responses made visible/audible.
- **relaxation response** — Benson's stripped-down mantra meditation.

## Exam high-yield summary
- Two pathways: psychosocial factors → biology, and lifestyle → disease. **50% of top-10 US deaths = lifestyle.**
- Selye's **GAS** (alarm/resistance/exhaustion); **HPA axis** = CRF → pituitary → adrenal → **cortisol**; chronic cortisol kills hippocampal cells.
- **Sapolsky's baboons:** subordinate = chronic cortisol, immune suppression, low HDL; **sense of control** is protective.
- Continuum: **excitement–stress–anxiety–depression** by sense of control; **self-efficacy** differs most.
- Immune system: macrophages, **B cells → immunoglobulins/memory B cells** (humoral); **T cells → killer/helper(T4)/suppressor** (cellular); HIV attacks **helper T cells.** **PNI** founded by **Ader & Cohen.**
- **CBSM** improves immune markers in HIV; **psychotherapy extended survival** in metastatic breast cancer (Spiegel; Andersen 56%/45%).
- **Hypertension** = silent killer, ~90% essential, 140/90 cutoff; African Americans disproportionately affected; **hostility + time urgency** double risk.
- **CHD** terms (angina, atherosclerosis, ischemia, MI); **Type A** (WCGS, Framingham); **anger** is the toxic core; **Type D**; reserve capacity model.
- **Gate control theory:** dorsal horns gate; small fibers open, large fibers close; brain modulates via emotion. **Endogenous opioids**; placebo = real opioid release; physical/social pain overlap.
- **CFS:** ≥6 months unexplained fatigue, no confirmed physical cause, predominantly women, **CBT works (70% vs 19%)**, drugs don't.
- Treatments: **biofeedback, relaxation/meditation (Benson), comprehensive stress-management** (monitoring + relaxation + cognitive therapy + coping) > single components.
- Behavior change: injury prevention (Peterson), AIDS prevention (San Francisco model, SiHLE, African couples), smoking in China (Hangzhou), **Stanford Three Community Study.**
