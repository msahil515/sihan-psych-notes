# Ch 6: Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders and Dissociative Disorders

This chapter covers two historically linked families of disorders that were once grouped together as **"hysterical neurosis."** The **somatic symptom and related disorders** involve an excessive or maladaptive response to physical symptoms or health concerns ("soma" = body), while the **dissociative disorders** involve disruptions ("detachments") of consciousness, memory, or identity. Both are exam-heavy at the disorder level: you must know clinical descriptions, DSM-5(-TR) criteria, statistics, biopsychosocial causes, and treatment for each. The chapter is integrative (biological + psychological + social), and the DID/false-memory controversy is a frequently tested theme.

> **Historical core (memorize):** "Hysteria" traces to Hippocrates (and the Egyptians before him) and the idea of a "wandering uterus" (thought to occur mainly in women). Freud's **conversion hysteria** = unexplained physical symptoms reflecting the *conversion* of unconscious emotional conflict into a more acceptable form. The term **conversion** survives (stripped of theory); the stigmatizing term **hysterical** is dropped. **Neurosis** (psychoanalytic: unconscious conflict → anxiety → ego defense mechanisms) was eliminated from the diagnostic system in **1980 (DSM-III)** because it was too vague and implied an unproven cause.

> **DSM-5-TR overlay note (chapter-wide):** Barlow & Durand is written to **DSM-5 (2013)**. DSM-5-TR (2022) retained essentially all of these categories and their criteria; the main TR-era changes relevant to this chapter are **updated ICD-10-CM codes** and minor wording, plus the general DSM-5-TR convention of using "another medical condition" phrasing. No new disorder was added to either grouping in DSM-5-TR. Where a specific criterion is unchanged, it is flagged below.

## PART 1 — SOMATIC SYMPTOM AND RELATED DISORDERS

DSM-5 lists **five** somatic symptom and related disorders: **(1) somatic symptom disorder, (2) illness anxiety disorder, (3) psychological factors affecting other medical conditions, (4) conversion disorder, (5) factitious disorder.** In each, the individual is pathologically concerned with bodily functioning. The first three overlap heavily. The shorthand label is **"medically unexplained physical symptoms"** but in many cases the medical cause IS known and it is the emotional distress / impairment that is excessive.

> **Big DSM-IV → DSM-5 shift (tested):** DSM-IV required symptoms to be **medically unexplained**. DSM-5 **dropped** "medically unexplained" as a required criterion and shifted to a **dimensional** focus on (a) number/severity of somatic symptoms and (b) severity of the **psychological** (cognitive/affective/behavioral) response. This reduces the burden on physicians to prove symptoms have no physical cause. DSM-IV **hypochondriasis** was split into **illness anxiety disorder** (mostly) and part of **somatic symptom disorder**. DSM-IV **somatization disorder** ("Briquet's syndrome") and **pain disorder** were folded into **somatic symptom disorder**.

### Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD)
- Historical root: **1859, Pierre Briquet** (French physician) described patients with endless somatic complaints with no medical basis → for years called **Briquet's syndrome**; now subsumed under SSD.
- **Case (Linda, "Full-Time Patient"):** intelligent woman in her 30s; pages of documented health-care contacts and medications; chronic fluctuating incapacitation; saw a family practitioner + **six specialists** + two ministers; her symptoms were her **identity** ("without them she would not know who she was"); classic extreme **"sick role."** Had earlier conversion symptom (leg paralysis, recovered after a year on a psychiatric ward → got disability status).
- **Case (Medical Student, "Temporary Pain"):** 25-yr-old, intermittent abdominal pain, no physical cause; coincided with marital separation; recovered with **relaxation + supportive therapy.** Illustrates that psychological/behavioral factors (anxiety, distress) compound severity whether or not pain has a clear medical cause. **Key point: the pain is real and it hurts** regardless of physical cause.
- DSM-5 emphasis on the **psychological** symptoms (anxiety/distress focused on somatic symptoms) is the most important treatment target.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Somatic Symptom Disorder (Table 6.1):**
> A. One or more **somatic symptoms** that are distressing and/or disrupt daily life.
> B. **Excessive** thoughts, feelings, or behaviors related to the symptoms/health concerns, shown by ≥1 of: (1) disproportionate, persistent thoughts about seriousness of symptoms; (2) high health-related anxiety; (3) excessive time/energy devoted to symptoms/health.
> C. Although any one symptom may not be continuously present, the **state of being symptomatic is persistent (typically >6 months).**
> **Specify if:** With predominant pain (previously *pain disorder*).
> **Specify current severity:** Mild = one Criterion B symptom; Moderate = ≥2 Criterion B symptoms; Severe = ≥2 Criterion B symptoms PLUS multiple somatic complaints (or one very severe symptom).
> **DSM-5-TR note:** criteria unchanged from DSM-5; only ICD-10-CM coding/wording updated.

### Illness Anxiety Disorder (IAD)
- Formerly **"hypochondriasis"** (term still widely used by public).
- Physical symptoms **absent or mild**; the **severe anxiety is about the possibility of having/developing a serious disease.** If ≥1 physical symptom is relatively severe + anxiety/distress → diagnose **SSD** instead. In IAD the concern is with the **idea of being sick**, not the symptom itself.
- Reassurance from physicians does **not** help ("the threat seems so real").
- **Case (Gail, "Invisibly Ill"):** married at 21, low self-esteem, abusive drunk stepbrother, husband's affair; minor sensations catastrophized (headache = brain tumor; breathlessness = heart attack); avoided exercise/drinking/laughing; feared restrooms/phones as infection sources; "disease of the month" from news triggered vigilance. Onset coincided with learning of the affair.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Illness Anxiety Disorder (Table 6.2):**
> A. **Preoccupation** with having/acquiring a serious illness.
> B. Somatic symptoms **not present**, or only **mild**; if another medical condition is present or there is high risk (e.g., strong family history), the preoccupation is clearly **excessive/disproportionate**.
> C. High level of **health anxiety**; easily alarmed about personal health status.
> D. Performs **excessive health-related behaviors** (e.g., repeatedly checks body) OR shows **maladaptive avoidance** (e.g., avoids doctors/hospitals).
> E. Preoccupation present **≥6 months** (the specific feared illness may change).
> F. Not better explained by another mental disorder (SSD, GAD, OCD, etc.).
> **Specify whether:** Care-seeking type / Care-avoidant type.
> **DSM-5-TR note:** unchanged from DSM-5.

#### Clinical Description (SSD + IAD together)
- Share features with **anxiety and mood disorders, especially panic disorder** (similar age of onset, personality, familial aggregation). Anxiety/mood disorders are the most likely **comorbid** diagnoses.
- Core process = **anxiety/fear that one has a serious disease**; the essential problem is anxiety expressed differently from other anxiety disorders.
- **Preoccupation with bodily symptoms → misinterpretation as illness.** Almost any sensation (heart rate, perspiration, cough, aches, fatigue) can become the focus.
- Go **first to family physicians**; reach mental health professionals only after medical causes are ruled out.
- **Disease conviction** = difficult-to-shake belief that one has a disease; a **core feature** of both disorders; reassurance has only short-term effect.
- **vs. Panic disorder:** panic patients fear **immediate** symptom-related catastrophe (during the few minutes of an attack) and learn to **stop** seeking medical help; SSD/IAD patients focus on a **long-term** disease process (cancer, AIDS) and keep seeking more opinions/treatments. Panic fears focus on ~10-15 sympathetic symptoms; SSD/IAD concerns range much wider.
- Minor physical complaints (abdominal aches) are common in young children and usually pass as stress responses.

#### Statistics (SSD + IAD)
- Estimates from DSM-IV-era studies: DSM-IV **hypochondriasis** prevalence ~**1%-5%** (APA 2000). In primary care: median hypochondriasis ~**6.7%**, up to **16.6%** for distressing somatic symptoms (≈ SSD + IAD combined).
- NOT more prevalent in the elderly (myth); spread fairly evenly across adulthood; disorders are **chronic.**
- Typical **onset in adolescence** (as with Linda). Patients tend to be **women, unmarried, lower SES.** (68% female in Kirmayer & Robbins 1991.)
- Overuse/misuse health care: medical bills up to **9× the average** patient; **19%** on disability in one study.
- **Sex ratio** when severe enough to meet criteria ≈ **2:1 female to male** (WHO study). Cross-cultural rates fairly uniform worldwide.
- **Culture-specific syndromes:**
  - **Koro** — belief (with severe anxiety/panic) that the **genitals are retracting into the abdomen**; mostly **Chinese males** (Rubin 1982 links it to centrality of male sexual functioning, guilt over masturbation/intercourse).
  - **Dhat** — India; anxious concern about **losing semen**; vague physical symptoms (dizziness, weakness, fatigue) attributed to semen loss.
  - Other culture-bound: hot/crawling sensations in the head (African patients); burning in hands/feet (Pakistani/Indian patients).
  - "Somatizing" psychological distress is fairly **uniform worldwide** (NOT specific to non-Western/developing countries, contrary to old belief).

#### Causes (SSD + IAD)
- Fundamentally **disorders of cognition/perception** with strong emotional contributions: **faulty interpretation of physical signs/sensations as illness.**
- Self-focus increases arousal → sensations feel more intense → misinterpretation → more anxiety → more symptoms = **vicious cycle** (Warwick & Salkovskis 1990 integrative model, Figure 6.1: trigger → increased focus on body → physiological arousal → misinterpretation/perceived threat → apprehension → checking/reassurance seeking → back to increased focus).
- **Stroop test** studies: enhanced **perceptual sensitivity to illness cues**; tendency to interpret ambiguous stimuli as threatening; **"better safe than sorry"** approach; **restrictive concept of health as symptom-free.**
- Causes parallel anxiety disorders: **modest, nonspecific genetic contribution** (tendency to overrespond to stress) + biological & psychological vulnerabilities; runs in families; learned focus on physical symptoms from family members.
- **Three additional etiological factors:** (1) develops after a **stressful life event** (often involving death/illness); (2) **disproportionate incidence of disease in family during childhood** → strong illness memories; (3) **social/interpersonal influence** — illness gets attention → reinforces the **"sick role"** (increased attention + avoidance of responsibilities).
- **Severe SSD ↔ Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD) link (integrative, frequently tested):** family/genetic studies link severe SSD to ASPD. ASPD = mostly **males**; severe SSD = mostly **females**, yet they share features (early onset, chronic course, lower SES, hard to treat, marital discord, substance abuse, suicide attempts) and run in families. **Lilienfeld** hypothesis: both may share a **neurobiologically based disinhibition syndrome** (impulsivity / short-term gain at long-term expense). **Difference explained by gender socialization:** aggression/independence (male) → ASPD; **dependence**/lack of aggression (female) → severe SSD. Same biological vulnerability, different gendered expression.

#### Treatment (SSD + IAD)
- Relatively little known; psychodynamic uncovering rarely shown effective.
- **Reassurance + education** can help (surprisingly): Mental health professionals can reassure more sensitively/with more time. **Fava et al. (2000) "explanatory therapy"** (20 DSM-IV hypochondriasis pts) → significant reduction in fears/beliefs and health-care use, maintained at 6 months (best for milder forms).
- **CBT** is the most robust treatment: **Barsky & Ahern (2005)** randomized 187 DSM-IV hypochondriasis pts to 6 sessions CBT vs treatment-as-usual → CBT superior at post and all follow-ups on the **Whiteley Index** (CBT mean dropped 3.58→2.82→2.65 vs control 3.51→3.21→3.02). CBT targets: challenge illness-related misinterpretations, show patients they can **create symptoms** by focusing on body areas (demonstrating controllability), reduce reassurance-seeking. Results "modest"; many refuse treatment (believe problem is medical).
- **Allen et al. (2006):** 40% of severe SSD pts improved with CBT vs 7% with standard care; gains lasted ≥1 year.
- **Drugs:** antidepressants (same drugs used for anxiety/depression) help some. **Greeven et al. (2007):** CBT 45% responded, Paxil (paroxetine, an SSRI) 30%, placebo 14%; only CBT was significantly better than placebo.
- Clinic strategy: provide reassurance, reduce stress, reduce **help-seeking** via a **gatekeeper physician** who must authorize specialist visits; reduce supportive consequences of relating to others via symptoms; encourage healthy adjustment / part-time employment (reduce reliance on "sick" role and disability).

### Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions
- **Essential feature:** a **diagnosed medical condition** (asthma, diabetes, cancer pain) is **adversely affected** (increased frequency/severity) by **psychological or behavioral factors.**
- Examples: anxiety severe enough to worsen asthma; a diabetic in **denial** who consistently neglects insulin monitoring.
- Distinguish from **adjustment disorder** (stress/anxiety *in response to* having a medical condition).
- (Health psychology covered in Ch 9.)
- **DSM-5-TR note:** unchanged; this category groups here because it involves physical symptoms/medical concern.

### Conversion Disorder (Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder)
- Term **conversion** popularized by **Freud** (anxiety from unconscious conflict "converted" into physical symptoms → discharges anxiety without experiencing it; like phobic displacement).
- DSM-5 added **"functional neurological symptom disorder"** subtitle (used by neurologists who see most such patients; more acceptable to patients). **"Functional"** = symptom without organic cause. The old term "conversion" is likely to be dropped in future DSM editions.
- **Clinical description:** physical malfunctioning (paralysis, blindness, **aphonia** = difficulty speaking) with **no organic pathology**; usually mimics neurological sensory-motor disease but can mimic any physical malfunction.
- **Case (Eloise, "Unlearning Walking"):** 20-yr-old, borderline intelligence; difficulty walking over 5 yrs (right leg gave way, eventually crawling); no physical problem found → classic conversion disorder; specific symptom **astasia-abasia** (weakness/balance difficulty → frequent falls). Strongly reinforced by mother's attention.
- **Other conversion symptoms:** total mutism, loss of sense of touch, **psychogenic non-epileptic seizures** (psychological seizures with no EEG changes), **globus hystericus** (sensation of a lump in the throat → difficulty swallowing/eating/talking).

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Conversion Disorder / FNSD (Table 6.3):**
> A. ≥1 symptom of **altered voluntary motor or sensory function.**
> B. Clinical findings provide evidence of **incompatibility** between the symptom and recognized neurological/medical conditions. *(This — not "preceding stress" — is the principal diagnostic criterion in DSM-5.)*
> C. Not better explained by another medical/mental disorder.
> D. Causes clinically significant distress/impairment or warrants medical evaluation.
> **DSM-IV → DSM-5 change:** the requirement that conversion be **associated with preceding stress was DROPPED** (stress is common and unreliable). DSM-5-TR: unchanged.

#### Distinguishing conversion from real disease and malingering
- **La belle indifférence** (blasé attitude toward symptoms) was long thought a hallmark but is **NOT useful**: Stone et al. (2006) found it in only **21%** of 356 conversion pts vs **29%** of 157 organic-disease pts.
- Conversion often precipitated by **marked stress / physical injury** (37% prior physical injury in one survey), but stress is unreliable as a sign.
- Conversion patients can usually **function normally but seem genuinely unaware** of the ability (e.g., conversion blindness — they avoid objects but report not seeing them; conversion leg paralysis — may run in an emergency).
- **Misdiagnosis rate** ~**4%** (Stone et al. 2005, improved from earlier decades); Moene et al. (2000): 10/85 (11.8%) later showed neurological disorder ~2.5 yrs later. **Ruling out medical causes is crucial.**
- **fMRI study (Voon et al. 2010):** conversion **tremor** showed **lower activity in the right inferior parietal cortex** vs voluntary "mimicked" tremor — a region that compares **internal predictions with actual events** (may explain why movement is experienced as involuntary).

#### Closely Related Disorders
- **Malingering** = deliberate faking, fully aware, for an obvious external goal (avoid work/legal trouble, gain a settlement).
- **Factitious disorder** = symptoms under **voluntary control** (like malingering) but **no obvious external reward** except to assume the **sick role** / get attention.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Factitious Disorder (Table 6.4):**
> A. **Falsification** of physical/psychological signs/symptoms, or **induction of injury/disease**, associated with identified **deception**.
> B. Individual presents self (or another) as ill/impaired/injured.
> C. Deceptive behavior is evident **even in the absence of obvious external rewards.**
> D. Not better accounted for by another mental disorder (e.g., delusion, psychosis).
> **Specify:** Single episode / Recurrent episodes (≥2). **Subtypes:** Factitious disorder imposed on **self** and **imposed on another.**

- **Factitious disorder imposed on another** (formerly **Munchausen syndrome by proxy**) = an adult (almost always a **mother**) deliberately makes a **child** sick for the attention/pity of being the parent of a sick child = **atypical child abuse.**
  - Extreme tactics (stirring urine with menstrual tampon; mixing feces into vomit; injecting own urine into the child's IV → recurring **E. coli** infection).
  - Mother typically forms a positive relationship with staff and eludes suspicion.
  - **Detection:** trial separation of mother and child; **video surveillance.** Hall et al. (2000): of 41 monitored children, 23 were factitious-disorder-imposed-on-another, video surveillance established >half the diagnoses.
  - **Typical vs atypical (Munchausen by proxy) child abuse (Table 6.2):** typical → direct physical contact, signs on exam, perpetrator hides it, anger is causal, awareness usually present; atypical → misrepresented illness not obvious on exam, perpetrator **presents** the manifestations to health care, child is the **vector** for attention (anger not primary), awareness usually absent.

#### Unconscious Mental Processes (conversion)
- **Anna O.** (real name **Bertha Pappenheim**): nursing dying father, hallucinated a black snake; right arm "asleep"; later right-arm paralysis whenever she recalled it; spread; also deafness and inability to speak German (kept English). **Breuer** used hypnosis; reliving the trauma = **catharsis** (purging/releasing emotionally traumatic events) → paralysis resolved, German returned.
- **Blind sight / unconscious vision** (Weiskrantz 1980): people with localized brain damage could identify objects in their visual field with **no awareness** of seeing.
- **Case (Celia, "Seeing Through Blindness"):** 15-yr-old "blind" but performed **perfectly** on an upright-triangle discrimination test → not faking (a faker would deliberately err).
- **Sackeim, Nordlie & Gur (1979):** hypnotized 2 participants with suggestion of total blindness; the one told to "appear blind at all costs" performed **far below chance** (like a malingerer); the other performed **perfectly** while reporting blindness (like true conversion).
- **Rule:** truly blind → **chance** performance; conversion → **good** performance (sees but dissociated from awareness); malingerer → **below-chance** performance (tries hard to appear blind).

#### Statistics (Conversion)
- Comorbid with SSD (Linda), anxiety, and mood disorders.
- **Rare** in mental health settings; high in **neurological settings** (~**30%**); ~30% of epilepsy-center referrals have **psychogenic non-epileptic seizures.**
- Primarily in **women**, onset in adolescence or slightly after; common in **soldiers exposed to combat**; symptoms can disappear then recur under new stress.
- Outcome: psychogenic non-epileptic seizure study (Ettinger et al. 1999) — only ~half recovered; ~20% had attempted suicide. **Children/adolescents have better outlook**: 85% of 40 children recovered 4 yrs later (Turkey study; early diagnosis best).
- **Conversion shares features with dissociative disorders** (e.g., dissociative symptoms/feelings of unreality more common in conversion pts than controls).
- Cross-cultural: seizures, paralysis, trances common in some **rural fundamentalist religious groups** (seen as contact with God) — not a disorder unless persistent/impairing.

#### Causes (Conversion)
- **Freud's four processes:** (1) traumatic event / unacceptable unconscious conflict; (2) conflict + anxiety **repressed** (made unconscious); (3) anxiety threatens to surface → **converted** into physical symptoms (relieves pressure) = **primary gain** (the reinforcing reduction of anxiety); (4) increased **attention/sympathy** + avoidance of a difficult task = **secondary gain.**
- Authors agree Freud was likely right on counts 1, 2, 4 but **NOT on primary gain** (count 3): formal tests of **la belle indifférence** find little support — conversion pts show **equal or greater** anxiety/arousal than controls (Lader & Sartorius 1968; Stone et al. 2006).
- Trauma frequently includes **sexual abuse, parental divorce/death, physical abuse** (Wyllie et al. 1999: child pseudo-seizure pts had 32% mood disorders, 24% separation anxiety/school refusal; high rates of trauma). Mothers rated as **overinvolved/overprotective** (reinforcement).
- Social/cultural: occurs in **less educated, lower SES** groups; patients **adopt familiar symptoms** (prior family illness); incidence has **decreased over decades** (more medical knowledge → less secondary gain).
- Biological: increasing brain-imaging evidence of connectivity between conversion symptoms and emotion-regulating regions (e.g., **amygdala**).

#### Treatment (Conversion)
- Similar to SSD. Principal strategy: **identify and attend to the traumatic/stressful event**; therapeutic **reliving (catharsis)** as in Anna O.
- **Reduce secondary gain** — Eloise's mother reinforced immobility (attention) and punished mobility. Eloise re-learned to walk with daily staff support/praise but **relapsed** when discharged home (mother did not maintain the program; her **affect** betrayed displeasure at progress).
- **CBT:** Moene et al. (2002, 2003) — 65% of 45 mostly motor-conversion pts responded; **hypnosis added little/no benefit** to CBT.

## PART 2 — DISSOCIATIVE DISORDERS

- **Dissociation / dissociative experiences** = mild alterations or detachments in consciousness/identity ("This isn't really me"); **normal** in mild form. Morton Prince (founder of the *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*) noted this >100 yrs ago.
- More likely after a **stressful event**, when **tired/sleep-deprived**. **~half** the general population has transient dissociation at some point; **31%-66%** experience it during a traumatic event.
- **Stanford execution study (Freinkel, Koopman & Spiegel 1994):** journalists witnessing the execution of **Robert Alton Harris** (sleep-deprived all night) — **40%-60%** had dissociative symptoms (things seemed unreal/dreamlike, time stopped, estrangement, feeling like strangers to themselves).
- **Two key experiences:**
  - **Depersonalization** = altered perception of the **self** — temporarily lose sense of your own reality (as if dreaming / watching yourself).
  - **Derealization** = sense of reality of the **external world** is lost (things change shape/size; people seem dead/mechanical).
- Disintegrated experience involves alterations in relationship to **self, world, or memory.**
- **Strong social/cultural influence** in dissociative disorders — even severe cases stay close to culturally sanctioned forms.

### Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder
- Diagnosed when feelings of unreality are so **severe and frightening** they dominate life and impair functioning (rare).
- **Case (Bonnie, "Dancing Away from Herself"):** late-20s dance teacher; "flipping out" while teaching — feels not really herself, no control of legs, standing behind herself watching, **tunnel vision**, separation from surroundings, then panic/perspire/shake. Began after first smoking marijuana ~10 yrs earlier.
- ~50% of intense **panic attacks** include unreality feelings; also part of **acute stress disorder.** Diagnose this disorder only when depersonalization/derealization are the **primary** problem.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder (Table 6.5):**
> A. Persistent/recurrent **depersonalization, derealization, or both:**
>   • *Depersonalization:* unreality/detachment/being an outside observer of one's thoughts, feelings, sensations, body, or actions (perceptual alterations, distorted time sense, unreal/absent self, emotional/physical numbing).
>   • *Derealization:* unreality/detachment re: surroundings (individuals/objects experienced as unreal, dreamlike, foggy, lifeless, visually distorted).
> B. During the experience, **reality testing remains intact.** *(Key — distinguishes from psychosis.)*
> C. Clinically significant distress/impairment.
> D. Not due to a substance or another medical condition (e.g., seizures).
> E. Not better explained by another mental disorder (schizophrenia, panic disorder).
> **DSM-5-TR note:** unchanged; DSM-5/TR very similar to DSM-IV criteria.

- **Statistics:** prevalence ~**0.8%-2.8%**; Simeon et al. (2003) — 117 cases ~equally split men/women; **mean onset age ~16**, chronic course, all substantially impaired; comorbidity **73% mood**, **64% anxiety** disorders (lifetime).
- **Cognitive profile** (Guralnik et al. 2000, 2007): equal intelligence but specific deficits in **attention, information processing, short-term memory, spatial reasoning** → easily distracted, slow to process new info (corresponds to "tunnel vision" and "mind emptiness").
- **Biology:** reduced emotional responding — **lower skin conductance** (Sierra et al. 2002); brain-imaging deficits in **perception and emotion regulation**; **HPA axis dysregulation.** Prozac showed **no** treatment effect vs placebo (Simeon et al. 2004). Psychological treatments not systematically studied.

### Dissociative Amnesia
- Easiest severe dissociative disorder to understand; several patterns:
  - **Generalized amnesia** = unable to remember anything, including **who you are**; may be lifelong or extend from a recent period (e.g., past 6-12 months). Rarer.
  - **Localized / selective amnesia** = failure to recall **specific events**, usually traumatic, within a specific period. **Far more common.** Common during war.
- **Case (The Woman Who Lost Her Memory):** mother (early 50s) in a violent family; ~every 6 months after a family battle she lost her memory, was hospitalized, recovered after a few days away from turmoil; resolved when children moved out (stress decreased). Note: amnesia driven by **current** stress.
- **Sackeim & Devanand (1991) case:** amnesia not for the events but for the **intense emotional reactions** to them (recovered under hypnosis). Most dissociative amnesia is **selective for traumatic events/emotions**, not generalized.
- **Dissociative fugue** (a subtype; "fugue" = flight): memory loss around an **unexpected trip**; person travels, ends up in a new place unable to recall why/how; may assume a new identity or become confused about the old; usually leaving an intolerable situation; ends abruptly with recovery of most/all memory.
- **Case (Jeffrey Ingram, "A Troubled Trip"):** 40-yr-old; found himself in Denver not knowing who he was; diagnosed **dissociative fugue**, stress-triggered (en route to visit a dying friend); had a prior 9-month episode (1995); never fully regained earlier memory.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Dissociative Amnesia (Table 6.6):**
> A. Inability to recall important **autobiographical information**, usually of a traumatic/stressful nature, inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. (Most often **localized/selective**; sometimes **generalized** for identity and life history.)
> B. Clinically significant distress/impairment.
> C. Not due to a substance or neurological/medical condition (partial complex seizures, transient global amnesia, head injury, etc.).
> D. Not better explained by DID, PTSD, acute stress disorder, SSD, or major/mild neurocognitive disorder.
> **Specify if:** With **dissociative fugue** (purposeful travel/bewildered wandering with amnesia for identity/autobiographical info).
> **DSM-IV → DSM-5 change (tested):** DSM-IV **dissociative fugue** was a **separate disorder**; DSM-5 made it a **specifier** of dissociative amnesia. DSM-5-TR: unchanged.

- **Dissociative amnesia vs amnesia in cognitive disorders (Table 6.4):** DA → no medical cause, onset linked to psychological trauma, deficits mainly in **autobiographical** recall, **reversible with hypnosis**, ability to learn new info intact, disorientation to identity present; cognitive disorders → known medical cause, not trauma-linked, NOT reversible with hypnosis, may worsen with sedatives, learning impaired, identity disorientation only in late phase.
- **Statistics:** seldom appears before adolescence; rare to first appear after age 50; prevalence ~**1.8%-7.3%** (most prevalent dissociative disorder).
- **Culture-bound "running" syndromes:**
  - **Amok** ("running amok") — non-Western; mostly **males**; trancelike state with brutal assault/killing; no memory afterward.
  - **Pivloktoq** (Arctic peoples); **frenzy witchcraft** (Navajo). Most running disorders (except amok) more common in **women**; resemble dissociative fugue.
- **Trance / possession:** dissociative symptoms attributed to spirit possession; common in **India, Nigeria (vinvusa), Thailand (phii pob)**, and at African American prayer meetings, Native American rituals, Puerto Rican spiritist sessions; "falling out" (Bahamians, African Americans from the South). Not abnormal in accepted religious context; diagnosed as **"other specified dissociative disorder (dissociative trance)"** only when undesirable/pathological (e.g., possession by an evil spirit/another person).

### Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID)
- Up to **100** identities (average ~**15**); often only partially independent (so not truly "multiple complete personalities"). **Renamed in DSM-IV** from **Multiple Personality Disorder → DID.**
- **Alters** = the different identities. **Host** identity = the one who seeks treatment, tries to hold fragments together, usually develops **later** (not the original). Many have an **impulsive alter** handling sexuality/income; **cross-gendered alters** common (e.g., a small woman with a strong male protector alter).
- **Switch** = transition between identities; usually **instantaneous**; physical changes possible (posture, facial expression, wrinkling, disabilities); **handedness changed in 37%** of cases (Putnam et al. 1986).
- **Case (Jonah, "Bewildering Blackouts"):** 27-yr-old; severe headaches with amnesia; violent acts under the name **Usoffa Abdulla, son of Omega.** Staff identified 3 alters besides Jonah: **Sammy** (rational/calm/in control; appeared at age ~6 after Jonah saw his mother stab his father), **King Young** (sexual; emerged after mother dressed Jonah as a girl), **Usoffa Abdulla** (violent protector; emerged ~age 9-10 after a brutal attack by white youths). Jonah knew nothing of the alters.

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Dissociative Identity Disorder (Table 6.7):**
> A. **Disruption of identity** = ≥2 distinct **personality states** (described in some cultures as **possession**); marked discontinuity in sense of self/agency with alterations in affect, behavior, consciousness, memory, perception, cognition, and/or sensory-motor functioning. May be observed by others or self-reported.
> B. **Recurrent gaps** in recall of everyday events, important personal info, and/or traumatic events, inconsistent with ordinary forgetting.
> C. Clinically significant distress/impairment.
> D. Not a normal part of broadly accepted cultural/religious practice (in children, not better explained by imaginary playmates/fantasy play).
> E. Not due to a substance or another medical condition (e.g., complex partial seizures).
> **DSM-IV → DSM-5 change:** DSM-5 added that the identity disruption may be **self-reported** (not only observed) and explicitly allows the cultural **possession** presentation. DSM-5-TR: unchanged.

#### Can DID Be Faked?
- DID patients are **suggestible**; alters may be created via leading questions (therapy or hypnosis).
- **Case (Kenneth Bianchi, the "Hillside Strangler"):** murdered 10 women; under hypnosis produced alter "Steve" who claimed all killing → insanity plea. **Martin Orne** (hypnosis expert) for prosecution: suggested a "true" multiple personality has ≥3 personalities → Bianchi promptly produced a third; **no corroboration** of alters before arrest; psychological tests showed **no differences** among personalities (true DID often scores differently); Bianchi had psychopathology textbooks; responded like someone **simulating** hypnosis → found **guilty.**
- **Spanos, Weeks & Bertrand (1985):** college students given Bianchi-style interview → **>80%** simulated an alter to avoid conviction; vaguer instructions → far fewer.
- Cognitive-science memory tests: DID patients' **implicit memory** doesn't differ from normals; **inter-identity amnesia** often not borne out (Huntjens; Kong, Allen & Glisky 2008: words learned as one identity recalled after switching, contrary to self-report).
- **Sociocognitive model (Spanos 1996):** DID symptoms largely explained by **therapists inadvertently suggesting alters** to suggestible patients (socially reinforced). Survey (Pope et al. 1999): only **one-third** of psychiatrists thought DID belonged in DSM without reservation.
- **Counter-evidence DID is real:** Eve Black (*The Three Faces of Eve*, **Chris Sizemore**) showed transient **microstrabismus** absent in other personalities; **S.D. Miller (1989):** DID patients had **4.5×** the optical-function changes of simulators (acuity, refraction, eye-muscle balance — hard to fake); Jonah's alters had different physiological responses (electrodermal, EEG); **fMRI** changes in **hippocampal/medial temporal** activity across switches (Tsai et al. 1999); malingerers eagerly display symptoms while DID patients **hide** them (Kluft 1999).
- **Case (Anna O. Revealed):** Bertha Pappenheim was **never fully cured** by Breuer (gave up 1882), reinstitutionalized, became a pioneering **social worker/feminist** fighting sexual slavery; Breuer's notes show "two Anna O.'s" → she had **DID** (one relatively normal, one dark/hallucinating/abusive; switches triggered by e.g. the sight of an orange; left **two wills in different handwriting** at death from cancer, 1936).

#### Statistics (DID)
- Female:male ratio as high as **9:1** (from case studies). Onset almost always in **childhood** (as young as 4), but ~**7 years** between symptom appearance and diagnosis. Lifelong without treatment; switching frequency decreases with age.
- Prevalence: **3%-6%** of severely disturbed inpatients in North America, ~**2%** in Holland; **1.5%** in the best community survey (Johnson et al. 2006).
- High **comorbidity** (anxiety, substance abuse, depression, personality disorders); avg >7 additional diagnoses in one sample; strong overlap with **borderline personality disorder** (self-destructive/suicidal behavior, emotional instability) — some argue BPD accounts for most DID symptoms.
- **Auditory hallucinations** common → often misdiagnosed as **psychotic**, but DID voices come from **inside** the head (patient aware they are hallucinations, tries to suppress) vs outside in psychosis. Reported in **21 different countries** (Coons et al. 1991).

#### Causes (DID)
- **Almost every DID patient reports severe childhood abuse.** Putnam et al. (1986): **97%** experienced significant trauma (usually sexual/physical abuse), **68% incest.** Ross et al. (1990): **95%** of 97 reported physical/sexual abuse. (Some reports confabulated — Kluft.) Not all trauma is abuse (e.g., child who saw parents killed in a minefield).
- DID = a natural tendency to **escape/dissociate** from unremitting negative affect of severe abuse; **lack of social support** also implicated. Twin study (Waller & Ross 1997): cause largely attributable to **chaotic, nonsupportive family environment** (environmental, not heritable, variance).

#### Suggestibility
- **Suggestibility** = a normally distributed personality trait (≈ **hypnotizability**). Imaginary childhood playmates correlate with rich fantasy life and suggestibility; **self-hypnosis** lets one dissociate (e.g., suggest no pain in a hand).
- **Autohypnotic model (Putnam 1991):** suggestible people use dissociation to defend against extreme trauma; identity splits when trauma is unbearable. **~50%** of DID patients recall imaginary playmates. The **developmental window** for DID closes ~**age 9** (when children reliably distinguish reality/fantasy). Less suggestible people may develop **PTSD** instead of a dissociative reaction.

#### Biological Contributions
- Almost certainly a biological vulnerability, hard to pinpoint (twin study found variance fully **environmental**).
- Consistent with **diathesis-stress**: only with appropriate vulnerability does extreme stress produce **pathological** dissociation (normal dissociation differs statistically from pathological).
- **DID ↔ PTSD:** both = strong emotional reactions to severe trauma; DID may be an **extreme subtype of PTSD** emphasizing dissociation over anxiety. Developmental window for DID closes ~age 9, after which severe PTSD (not DID) may develop.
- Smaller **hippocampal and amygdala volume** (Vermetten et al. 2006), as in PTSD. **Temporal lobe epilepsy:** ~6% report out-of-body experiences; ~50% show dissociative symptoms — but seizure-related dissociation appears in **adulthood without trauma** (different from DID). Head injury can cause amnesia but is generalized/irreversible. **Sleep deprivation** produces dissociative symptoms; DID worsens when tired ("like bad jet lag").

> **ICD-11 note (current-classification overlay, NOT in the 2015 book):** ICD-11 keeps the dissociative disorders grouping and is largely convergent with DSM-5-TR, but with notable differences Sihan should flag:
> • ICD-11 distinguishes **Dissociative Identity Disorder** from **Partial Dissociative Identity Disorder** (in partial DID, non-dominant personality states recurrently intrude but do not take executive control — roughly analogous to DSM's "other specified" presentations).
> • ICD-11 separates **Trance Disorder** and **Possession Trance Disorder** as distinct named categories (DSM-5-TR folds these into *other specified dissociative disorder, dissociative trance*).
> • **Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder** exists in ICD-11 similarly.
> • For somatic presentations, ICD-11 uses **Bodily Distress Disorder** (graded mild/moderate/severe by distress and preoccupation) instead of the DSM "somatic symptom disorder" construct; **Hypochondriasis (Health Anxiety Disorder)** is classified by ICD-11 **with the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders**, not with the bodily distress group (a contrast worth memorizing). Conversion symptoms appear in ICD-11 as **Dissociative Neurological Symptom Disorder** (grouped under dissociative, not somatic, disorders — opposite of DSM's placement).

#### Real Memories and False (the recovered-memory controversy)
- Central controversy: are recovered memories of early trauma (esp. sexual abuse) **accurate**? Stakes are high on both sides (untreated suffering vs false accusations/wrongful imprisonment).
- **Strong evidence false memories can be created:** **Loftus, Coan & Pickrell (1996)** — implanted a false "lost in a shopping mall at age 5" memory via a trusted companion; a 14-yr-old "remembered" it, added details, persisted after debriefing. **Bernstein & Loftus (2009):** false memory of getting ill from egg salad → ate less egg salad up to 4 months later.
- **Children unreliable:** **Bruck et al. (1995)** — after genital exams, ~60% of touched girls refused to indicate it; ~60% of **control** girls falsely reported intrusive touching. **Ceci (2003)** — repeated "think hard" sessions over 10 weeks → **58%** of preschoolers described a **fictitious** event as real; 27% still claimed to remember it after being told it was false.
- **Alien abduction study (Clancy/McNally et al. 2002):** people reporting recovered/repressed memories of abduction showed more **false recall/recognition** and higher **suggestibility/depression** → memories are malleable, esp. in people high in **absorption** (vivid imagery) and openness to unusual ideas.
- **But trauma can be real and forgotten:** **Williams (1994)** — 38% of 129 women with documented childhood sexual abuse did not recall it 17 yrs later (more so if young + knew the abuser). **Goodman et al. (2003)** — 81% of 175 with documented abuse **did** remember/report it (better recall with older age at end of abuse + emotional support). **McNally & Geraerts (2009):** some simply **forgot** and later recalled without repression/false memory.
- **Four pathways for those reporting abuse memories:** (1) experienced + always remembered; (2) false memories; (3) recovered "repressed" memories in therapy; (4) simply forgot and later remembered.
- **False Memory Syndrome Foundation** formed by the falsely accused. Satanic-day-care abuse tales are likely **implanted** by careless/aggressive therapists or law enforcement (some elderly caregivers wrongly imprisoned for life). Therapists must understand memory mechanisms to avoid implanting false memories while remaining sensitive to genuine dissociated trauma.

#### Treatment (Dissociative Disorders)
- **Dissociative amnesia / fugue:** usually **resolve on their own**; prevention via resolving current life stress and strengthening coping; therapy helps recall events (sometimes with friends/family corroboration); difficult cases may use **hypnosis or benzodiazepines** (with suggestions that it's okay to remember).
- **DID:** much harder; goal = **reintegrate** the identities via **long-term psychotherapy** (no controlled trials; prognosis **guarded**). Coons (1986): only 5/20 fully integrated. Ellason & Ross (1997): 12/54 (**22.2%**) integrated at 2 years.
- DID strategy (from clinical wisdom + PTSD methods): **identify cues/triggers** of trauma/dissociation and neutralize them; patient must **confront and relive** early trauma and gain **control** (turn a current-feeling event into a "terrible memory"). **Hypnosis** often used to access unconscious memories/alters (dissociation ≈ hypnosis) but **not proven necessary.** Reemerging memories can trigger further dissociation — therapist must guard against this; **trust is essential.** Medication (esp. antidepressants) may help some cases but evidence is weak. DID runs a **chronic** course and seldom remits spontaneously (suggesting current treatments do help somewhat).

## DSM Controversies: Radical Changes in Classification
- Both groupings are among the **oldest** recognized disorders, yet neither may be a uniform category with shared characteristics.
- Old assumption: **"somatization"** = a common process whereby a mental disorder manifests as physical symptoms; specific disorders just reflect different physical expressions. This was questioned.
- DSM-5 redefined somatic symptom disorders around **two factors**: (1) severity/number of **physical symptoms**, and (2) severity of **anxiety** focused on symptoms + degree of **behavior change.** IAD needs no real physical symptoms — focus is solely on illness anxiety.
- Advantages: a **dimensional** approach (physical + psychological symptom severity) may better predict course and guide treatment; **less burden on physicians** to determine whether symptoms have a physical cause (DSM-IV's tricky problem). The category also now houses **psychological factors affecting medical condition** and **factitious disorders.**
- The change is **controversial** because little data exist on validity/reliability of the new categories, but it appears to be an improvement.

## Exam Quick-Reference Summary
- **5 somatic symptom & related disorders:** SSD, IAD, psychological factors affecting medical condition, conversion disorder, factitious disorder.
- **SSD vs IAD:** SSD = distressing somatic symptoms + excessive cognitive/behavioral response (≥6 months symptomatic). IAD = preoccupation with **having/acquiring** illness, symptoms absent/mild (≥6 months); subtypes care-seeking / care-avoidant.
- **Conversion disorder:** altered voluntary motor/sensory function **incompatible** with neurological disease; *la belle indifférence* NOT diagnostic; **preceding stress dropped** as criterion in DSM-5; astasia-abasia, globus hystericus, psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, aphonia.
- **Factitious disorder:** deception with no external reward (sick role); **imposed on another** = Munchausen by proxy = atypical child abuse.
- **Blindness test rule:** truly blind = chance; conversion = above chance (good); malingerer = below chance.
- **Dissociative disorders:** depersonalization-derealization (reality testing intact), dissociative amnesia (localized/selective most common; **fugue** now a specifier), DID.
- **DID:** avg ~15 alters; female:male up to 9:1; onset in childhood; ~97% report childhood trauma/abuse; developmental window closes ~age 9; **autohypnotic** vs **sociocognitive** models; strong PTSD/borderline overlap.
- **False memory:** Loftus (mall study), Ceci, Bruck — false memories implantable; but Williams/Goodman — real abuse can be forgotten then recalled.
- **Treatment headlines:** SSD/IAD → CBT (+ gatekeeper physician, reduce reassurance-seeking) > SSRIs > placebo; conversion → catharsis + remove secondary gain + CBT (hypnosis adds little); dissociative amnesia/fugue → often self-resolve; DID → long-term integrative psychotherapy (guarded prognosis).
