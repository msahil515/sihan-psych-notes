# Ch 2: An Integrative Approach to Psychopathology

This chapter is the conceptual spine of the whole book: it argues that no psychological disorder has a single cause, and lays out the **multidimensional integrative model** in which biological, psychological, emotional, social/cultural, and developmental influences weave together in a feedback system. It surveys each "dimension" in turn (genetics, neuroscience/neurotransmitters, behavioral-cognitive science, emotions, sociocultural factors, life-span development) and grounds them in Judy's blood-injection-injury phobia from Ch 1. For MET/NIMHANS this is a high-yield, definition-dense chapter: examiners love the **diathesis-stress model, gene-environment correlation model, epigenetics, the Caspi 5-HTT study, neurotransmitter functions, brain structures, learned helplessness, prepared learning, the components of emotion, and equifinality**. Note: this is an etiology/foundations chapter, so it contains **no formal diagnostic criteria**; DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 overlays are limited and flagged at the end.

## One-Dimensional versus Multidimensional Models

### Linear vs systemic causality
- **One-dimensional (linear) model** — attempts to trace the origin of behavior to a **single cause** (e.g., "schizophrenia/phobia is caused by a chemical imbalance" OR "by overwhelming family conflict"). Still encountered, but rejected by most scientists.
- **Systemic perspective** — derives from the word *system*; any influence contributing to psychopathology **cannot be considered out of context**. A system (feedback loop) has independent inputs at many points, but once an input becomes part of the whole it can **no longer be considered independent**.
- **Context** = the biology + behavior of the individual, plus the cognitive, emotional, social, and cultural environment; any one component inevitably affects the others.
- **Multidimensional integrative approach** (KEY TERM, p. 29) — the model adopted by the book: disorders arise from the **interaction** of biological, psychological, emotional, social, and developmental dimensions.

### What caused Judy's phobia? (running case study)
- **Behavioral influences** — Judy saw a graphic blood/injury film; her reaction (an unconditioned response) became associated, via **classical conditioning** and **stimulus generalization**, with similar situations (even the words "Cut it out!" evoked queasiness). Puzzle: *why didn't her classmates develop the same phobia?* → conditioning alone is insufficient.
- **Biological influences** — Physiologically she had a **vasovagal syncope** (common cause of fainting): heart rate/BP rose, then her body overcompensated by lowering vascular resistance, heart rate, and BP until blood to the brain dropped and she fainted. *Syncope* = "sinking feeling"/swoon from low BP in the head. Possible cause: an overreactive **sinoaortic baroreflex arc** (compensates for sudden BP increases by lowering BP). This overcompensation tendency seems **inherited** → explains family clustering: **61% of family members** of people with the phobia had a similar (usually milder) condition (Öst, 1992). But many with severe syncope tendencies never develop a phobia (they cope, e.g., **tensing muscles to raise BP** and prevent fainting), and some with little syncope reaction develop the phobia anyway → biology alone insufficient.
- **Emotional influences** — Behavior/thoughts/feelings also influence biology. Judy's fear/anxiety could trigger a stronger baroreflex via rapid heart-rate increases, changed how she appraised blood situations, and motivated avoidance.
- **Social influences** — Friends/family rushed to aid her; principal **rejected/dismissed** her (rejection by authority figures can worsen disorders); being supportive **only during symptoms** can increase the frequency/intensity of the reaction (social attention as reinforcement).
- **Developmental influences** — The passage of time; a **developmental critical period** may make us more/less reactive. Why at age 16 and not before? Her vasovagal susceptibility may have peaked in the teen years; timing + the disturbing film = the unfortunate combination.
- **Outcome** — Judy responded to brief intensive treatment (graded **exposure** to blood words/images/situations while **preventing the BP drop**, starting with "Cut it out!", ending with witnessing surgery); back in school within **7 days** despite a setback (passing a car crash, nightmares).

## Genetic Contributions to Psychopathology

### The nature of genes
- **Genes** (KEY TERM, p. 31) — long molecules of **deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)** at various locations on chromosomes, within the cell nucleus.
- Each normal human cell has **46 chromosomes** in **23 pairs** (one of each pair from each parent).
- First **22 pairs** = programs for development of body and brain; the **23rd pair = sex chromosomes**. Females = **XX**; males = **XY** (mother always gives X; father gives X or Y). Abnormalities in the sex-chromosome pair can cause ambiguous sexual characteristics (Ch 10).
- **DNA** structure = a **double helix** (two intertwined spirals turning in opposite directions); ~**160 million base pairs** on the X chromosome; base-pair ordering influences development.
- **Dominant gene** — strongly influences a trait; only **one** needed (e.g., brown-eye gene dominant over blue). **Recessive gene** — must be paired with another recessive gene to have effect. Mendelian laws allow prediction of trait inheritance for dominant genes.
- **Polygenic** — most development, behavior, personality, and even **IQ** are influenced by **many genes**, each a tiny effect, all influenced by environment. The **human genome** = an individual's complete set of genes, **>20,000 genes**.
- **Single-gene disorders** (rare): **Huntington's disease** (degenerative brain disease, onset early 40s; genetic defect → deterioration of the **basal ganglia**; personality, cognitive, motor changes incl. involuntary jerking; no known environmental cure) and **phenylketonuria (PKU)** (present at birth; body cannot metabolize **phenylalanine**; inherited when both parents are carriers; can cause intellectual disability; **correctable** by restricting dietary phenylalanine until ~age 6-7).

> **Quantitative genetics** sums up the tiny effects across many genes without identifying which genes do what. **Molecular genetics** examines the actual structure of genes (e.g., **DNA microarrays** analyze thousands of genes at once). Hundreds of genes can contribute to a single trait's heritability.

### New developments in genes and behavior
- Best estimates: ~**half** of enduring personality traits and cognitive abilities are attributable to genetic influence (Rutter, 2006).
- McClearn et al. (1997): Swedish twins ≥80 yrs (110 identical pairs vs 130 fraternal pairs); heritability of specific cognitive abilities (memory, spatial) ranged **32%-62%**.
- Personality traits (shyness, activity level) heritability ~**30%-50%** (Bouchard et al., 1990).
- **Genes work by producing proteins**; all cells contain the full genome but only a small proportion of genes are **"turned on" (expressed)** → cell specialization. **Environment (social/cultural) can determine whether genes are turned on** (Cole, 2011).
  - Rat-pup example: absence of normal maternal **"licking and grooming"** prevents expression of a **glucocorticoid receptor** that modulates stress hormones → greater stress sensitivity (Meaney & Szyf, 2005).
- For psychological disorders: genes contribute to **all** disorders but account for **less than half** the explanation. If one identical twin has **schizophrenia**, the other has a **less-than-50%** chance (Gottesman, 1991). Adverse life events (a "chaotic" childhood) can **overwhelm** the influence of genes (Turkheimer et al., 2003).
- **Linkage studies** — study individuals with the same disorder who also share a feature with a known gene location (e.g., eye color) to "link" known gene locations with the disorder gene's possible location.

### The interaction of genes and the environment
- **Eric Kandel** (Nobel laureate, 1983) speculated that **learning can change the genetic structure of cells** by turning on dormant genes → changing the number of receptors at a neuron's end. The brain is **plastic** (subject to continual change, even at the genetic level), not fully "hardwired" after maturity.

#### The diathesis-stress model (KEY TERM, p. 34) — HIGH-YIELD
> **Diathesis-stress model** — individuals **inherit tendencies** (diatheses) to express certain traits/behaviors, which are then **activated under conditions of stress**.
- **Diathesis** = a condition (genetically based **vulnerability**, KEY TERM p. 34) that makes someone susceptible to developing a disorder.
- **Stress** = the environmental life event that activates the diathesis.
- Both must **interact** to produce a disorder. Judy inherited the fainting tendency (diathesis); the dissection (stressor, escape not acceptable) activated it.
- **Rule:** the **smaller** the vulnerability, the **greater** the life stress needed to produce the disorder; conversely, greater vulnerability needs less stress.
- Having a vulnerability does **not** guarantee the disorder (alcoholism example: only the friend with "addictive genes" spirals down). Considered **greatly oversimplified** given environment's effect on brain structure.

> **Caspi et al. (2003) landmark study (5-HTT / serotonin transporter):** 847 New Zealanders assessed for >2 decades from age 3. The serotonin-transporter gene comes in two **alleles**: **long (L)** and **short (S)**. **LL** = better stress coping; **SS** = worse. In people with **two S alleles**, risk of a major depressive episode **doubled** if they had ≥4 stressful life events (vs LL with 4 events). With **severe childhood maltreatment**, SS individuals' depression risk more than doubled (**63% vs 30%** not maltreated). **LL** individuals: childhood stress did **not** affect depression incidence (**30%** depressed regardless). Conclusion: **neither genes nor environment alone** explains onset; it takes a **complex interaction**.
- Replications/extensions: Kilpatrick et al. (2007) — 589 adults after the 2004 Florida hurricanes; **SS** more likely to develop **PTSD**; strong **social support protected** even high-risk people; high-risk (high exposure + SS + low social support) = **4.5× risk** of PTSD and depression. Caspi et al. (2002) — a different gene set + **childhood maltreatment** → **4× more likely** to be violent/antisocial. Later work (Risch et al., 2009): not just any single variant; a **larger gene network** is involved.

#### The gene-environment correlation model (KEY TERM, p. 36)
> **Gene-environment correlation model** (a.k.a. **reciprocal gene-environment model**) — genetic endowment may **increase the probability that a person experiences stressful life events**, which then trigger the genetic vulnerability.
- Example: someone genetically vulnerable to blood-injection-injury phobia may also be genetically **impulsive/accident-prone**, so they create the very blood-exposure situations that trigger the phobia.
- Applied to **depression** (people may seek out difficult relationships) and to **divorce** (McGue & Lykken, 1992): if your identical twin is divorced, your divorce probability increases **sixfold** (fraternal twin = doubles); if your identical twin + both sets of parents + spouse divorced → **77.5%**; if no family divorce → **5.3%**. No "divorce gene" — mediated by inherited traits (high-strung, impulsive, short-tempered) or mate choice.

### Epigenetics and the nongenomic "inheritance" of behavior
- **Epigenetics** (KEY TERM, p. 37) — genes are turned on/off by cellular material located **just outside the genome** ("epi" = on/around). Stress, nutrition, or other factors affect this **epigenome**, which is **passed down to the next generation** (and maybe several). The **genome itself is NOT changed**, so if the adverse environment disappears, the epigenome eventually **fades**.
- Crabbe, Wahlsten & Dudek (1999): genetically identical mice raised in near-identical environments at 3 universities still **behaved differently** on some tests → "genetic influences are often a lot less powerful than is commonly believed" (Sapolsky).
- **Cross-fostering** studies (Francis et al., 1999; Cameron et al., 2005): a rat pup born to one mother is reared by another. Calm/supportive maternal behavior could be **passed down across generations independent of genes** — pups of easily stressed mothers reared by calm mothers grew up calm. Maternal behavior altered the **endocrine stress response** via gene expression, **but only if the mother was calm during the pups' first week of life** (early-experience critical window).
- Suomi (1999/2000) with rhesus monkeys: genetically reactive monkeys reared by calm mothers for the first 6 months behaved later as if non-reactive — environment can **override and even reverse** the genetic contribution, and this is **transmitted across generations**. For non-reactive-genotype monkeys, maternal deprivation had little effect. **Extremely chaotic** early environments can override genetic factors and increase later disorder risk.
- Tienari et al. (1994): adopted children of parents with schizophrenia developed psychiatric disorders (incl. schizophrenia) **only if adopted into dysfunctional families**; high-quality parenting protected them. → It is too simplistic to say genetic contribution is "~50%"; heritability is meaningful **only in the context of the individual's environment**.
- **Chang & Eng** ("Siamese twins," b. 1810, Thailand/Siam, joined at chest): identical genes + nearly identical environments, yet **distinct personalities** (Chang moody/depressed/heavy drinker; Eng cheerful/quiet/thoughtful).

## Neuroscience and Its Contributions to Psychopathology

### The central nervous system (CNS)
- **Neuroscience** (KEY TERM, p. 38) — study of the nervous system and its role in behavior, emotion, cognition.
- **CNS** = brain + spinal cord (spinal cord mainly relays messages to/from the brain). Brain uses ~**140 billion** nerve cells (**neurons**, KEY TERM p. 38; >100 billion by some estimates).
- **Neuron** structure: central **cell body** + **dendrites** (receptors that **receive** chemical impulses, converting them to electrical) + **axon** (**transmits** impulses to other neurons).
- **Synaptic cleft** (KEY TERM, p. 38) — the small space between the axon of one neuron and the dendrite of another; the impulse must cross it.
- **Neurotransmitters** (KEY TERM, p. 39) — biochemicals released from the axon that transmit the impulse to the dendrite receptors of the next neuron.
- **Glia (glial) cells** — outnumber neurons ~**10:1**; once thought passive (insulation), now known to play **active roles** in neural activity and modulate neurotransmitter activity.
- Major neurotransmitters relevant to psychopathology: **norepinephrine (noradrenaline), serotonin, dopamine, GABA, glutamate.** Estimated **>100 different neurotransmitters**, each with multiple receptors.

### The structure of the brain
- Two big parts: **brain stem** (lower, more ancient; automatic functions like breathing, sleeping, coordinated movement) and **forebrain** (advanced, recent).
- **Hindbrain** (lowest brain stem): **medulla, pons, cerebellum**. Regulates automatic activities (breathing, heartbeat, digestion). **Cerebellum** controls **motor coordination**; abnormalities may be associated with **autism** (Ch 14).
- **Midbrain** (in brain stem): coordinates movement with sensory input; contains parts of the **reticular activating system** (arousal/tension, awake vs asleep).
- **Thalamus and hypothalamus** (top of brain stem): broadly regulate behavior and emotion; act as a relay between forebrain and lower brain stem.
- **Limbic system** (base of forebrain; *limbic* = "border"): includes **hippocampus** (sea horse), **cingulate gyrus** (girdle), **septum** (partition), **amygdala** (almond). Regulates emotional experience/expression, some learning and impulse control; involved with basic drives (**sex, aggression, hunger, thirst**).
- **Basal ganglia** (base of forebrain; includes the **caudate nucleus**): control **motor activity**; linked later to **OCD**.
- **Cerebral cortex** (KEY TERM context, p. 42): largest part of the forebrain; contains **>80%** of all CNS neurons; gives distinctly human qualities (planning, reasoning, creating). Divided into **two hemispheres** — **left** = verbal/cognitive processes; **right** = perceiving the world / creating images.
- Four **lobes** per hemisphere: **temporal** (recognizing sights/sounds, long-term memory), **parietal** (touch sensations, body positioning), **occipital** (integrating visual input), **frontal** (most relevant to psychopathology). The **prefrontal cortex** (anterior frontal lobe) = higher cognitive functions (thinking, reasoning, planning, long-term memory); synthesizes information and decides responses; lets us behave as social animals.

### The peripheral nervous system (PNS)
- Two components: **somatic nervous system** (controls voluntary muscles; damage impairs voluntary movement incl. talking) and **autonomic nervous system (ANS)**.
- **ANS** = **sympathetic** + **parasympathetic** divisions; regulates cardiovascular and endocrine systems, digestion, body temperature.
- **Endocrine system**: each gland releases its **hormone** (KEY TERM, p. 43) directly into the bloodstream.
  - **Adrenal glands** → **epinephrine (adrenaline)** in response to stress + salt-regulating hormones + the stress hormone **cortisol**.
  - **Thyroid gland** → **thyroxine** (energy metabolism, growth).
  - **Pituitary** = master gland (regulatory hormones).
  - **Gonadal glands** → sex hormones (estrogen, testosterone).
  - **Psychoneuroendocrinology** = subfield linking endocrine regulation to depression, anxiety, schizophrenia. (Depressed patients may respond better to an antidepressant + thyroid hormone; some older depressed men benefit from added testosterone.)
- **Sympathetic nervous system** — mobilizes the body during stress/danger; on alert, three things happen: **heart beats faster** (more blood to muscles), **respiration increases** (more oxygen to blood/brain), **adrenal glands stimulated**. Mediates the "emergency"/"alarm" reaction.
- **Parasympathetic nervous system** — **balances** the sympathetic system; takes over after sympathetic activity, normalizing arousal and facilitating energy storage/digestion.
- **HPA axis** = **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical axis**: hypothalamus → pituitary → adrenal cortex → cortisol; implicated in several disorders (Chs 5, 7, 9).

### Neurotransmitters (mechanisms & drug actions)
- Study methods: **agonist** (KEY TERM, p. 45) = increases/mimics a neurotransmitter's activity; **antagonist** (KEY TERM, p. 45) = decreases/blocks it; **inverse agonist** (KEY TERM, p. 45) = produces effects **opposite** to the neurotransmitter.
- **Reuptake** (KEY TERM, p. 45) — after release, a neurotransmitter is quickly drawn back from the synaptic cleft into the same neuron; some drugs **block reuptake** → continued stimulation.
- Two studied classes: **monoamines** (norepinephrine, serotonin, dopamine) and **amino acids** (GABA, glutamate); called "classic" because synthesized in the nerve.
- **Brain circuits** (KEY TERM, p. 44) — neurons sensitive to one neurotransmitter cluster into paths ("currents") running through the brain; thousands-to-tens-of-thousands exist.
- CAUTION (recurring theme): early "imbalance" claims (serotonin→depression, dopamine→schizophrenia, GABA→anxiety, norepinephrine→depression) are **oversimplifications**; neurotransmitter effects are **less specific** (related to how we **process information**) and almost always involve **interactions** among systems.

#### Glutamate and GABA (the "chemical brothers")
- **Glutamate** (KEY TERM, p. 45) — **excitatory** amino-acid transmitter; "turns on" neurons → action. Overactivity could "burn out" sections of the nervous system. **MSG (monosodium glutamate)** can raise body glutamate → headaches/tinnitus in sensitive people.
- **GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid)** (KEY TERM, p. 45) — **inhibitory** amino-acid transmitter; **reduces postsynaptic activity**, inhibiting behaviors/emotions; best-known effect = **reduces anxiety**. **Benzodiazepines** (minor tranquilizers) make it easier for GABA to attach to receptors → calming + anticonvulsant effects; addictive. GABA also reduces anger, hostility, aggression, and even some positive emotions → a **general inhibitory** transmitter; composed of subsystems (different GABA receptor types).

#### Serotonin
- **Serotonin** (KEY TERM, p. 46) = **5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)**; monoamine; ~6 major circuits from the **midbrain**, many ending in the cortex → influences much behavior (esp. **information processing**).
- Regulates behavior, moods, thought processes. **Extremely low** activity → less inhibition, instability, **impulsivity, overreaction**; associated with **aggression, suicide, impulsive overeating, excessive sexual behavior** (but does not necessarily cause them). High serotonin may interact with GABA to counteract glutamate.
- ~**15 different serotonin receptors**. Drugs: **tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., imipramine/Tofranil) and, more directly, **SSRIs (selective-serotonin reuptake inhibitors)** e.g., **fluoxetine/Prozac** — used for anxiety, mood, and eating disorders. **St. John's wort** also affects serotonin.

#### Norepinephrine (noradrenaline)
- **Norepinephrine** (KEY TERM, p. 46) — monoamine/**catecholamine**; part of the endocrine system. Stimulates **alpha-adrenergic** and **beta-adrenergic** receptors.
- **Beta-blockers** block beta-receptors → reduce response to norepinephrine surges → lower BP/heart rate (used for hypertension).
- One circuit begins in the hindbrain (basic functions); another influences **emergency/alarm responses** → possible relationship to **panic**. Acts in a **general modulating** way, not in specific behavior patterns.

#### Dopamine
- **Dopamine** (KEY TERM, p. 47) — monoamine/catecholamine; implicated in the **pathophysiology of schizophrenia** and **addiction**; possible role in depression and ADHD.
- **Reserpine** and modern antipsychotics **block dopamine receptors** → lower dopamine activity → the old idea that schizophrenia = **overactive dopamine circuits**. **Clozapine** (second-generation antipsychotic, only weak dopamine-receptor effects) suggests the dopamine hypothesis needs revising (Ch 13).
- Acts as a **"switch"** turning on brain circuits; ≥**5 receptor sites**. **L-dopa** = dopamine **agonist**. The **locomotor system** is one circuit dopamine switches on; **dopamine deficiency** → **Parkinson's disease** (tremors, muscle rigidity, judgment difficulty), helped by **L-dopa**.
- Dopamine = exploratory/outgoing/pleasure-seeking behavior; serotonin = inhibition/constraint → they **balance** each other.

### Implications for psychopathology
- Identifiable **lesions** in specific brain structures usually do **not** cause psychological disorders (which mix emotional/behavioral/cognitive symptoms); widespread damage usually causes **motor/sensory deficits** (the province of **neurology**/neuropsychology).
- Focus has shifted to the **general role of brain function in personality** (e.g., impulsive risk-takers may have **low serotonergic + high dopaminergic** activity).
- **OCD imaging**: patients have **increased activity** in the **orbital surface** of the frontal lobe, the **cingulate gyrus**, and (less so) the **caudate nucleus** — a correlated circuit rich in **serotonin**. Eslinger & Damasio (1985)/Insel (1992) case: an accountant developed classic OCD after removal of an **orbital frontal cortex** tumor. CAUTION: one case; brain-imaging is often inconsistent; the orbital frontal cortex is implicated in other anxiety disorders too; **effect does not imply cause** (the activity could be a *result* of the repetitive thinking, not its cause — running-to-class analogy).

### Psychosocial influences on brain structure and function
- **Psychological treatment can normalize ("re-wire") brain circuits**: Baxter et al. (1992) — **exposure and response prevention** (CBT for OCD) **normalized** the OCD brain circuit (replicated by Schwartz et al., 1996). Similar normalization after psychological treatment for depression, PTSD, social anxiety, and specific phobia; as little as **2 hours of exposure** changed phobia brain function, persisting **6 months**.
- **Placebo effects** as a window: placebos (sugar pills/sham treatments) change behavior/emotion via psychological factors (hope, expectation, conditioning). Knowing you are receiving a drug (visible vs hidden infusion pump) produces a greater effect (Colloca et al., 2004). A warm therapeutic relationship adds substantial benefit to sham acupuncture for IBS (Kaptchuk et al., 2008).
- Leuchter et al. (2002): antidepressants and placebos both changed brain function but in **different brain regions** (different mechanisms in depression). Petrovic et al. (2002): for **pain**, opioid and placebo both activated **overlapping** regions (**anterior cingulate cortex + brain stem**) → cognitive expectation of relief turns these circuits on.
- **Top-down vs bottom-up** (Kennedy et al., 2007): **CBT** works **"top down"** (cortex → lower brain, changing thinking patterns that affect the emotional brain); **drugs** work **"bottom up"** (reaching the cortex last).

### Interactions of psychosocial factors and neurotransmitter systems
- Insel, Scanlan, Champoux & Suomi (1988): rhesus monkeys raised **with vs without control** over their environment, then given a **benzodiazepine inverse agonist** (extreme anxiety burst). No-control monkeys = severe **anxiety/panic** (crouched in corner); control monkeys = **anger/aggression** (attacked others). Same neurochemical, **different effects** depending on psychological history.
- Yeh, Fricke & Edwards (1996): in crayfish, **serotonin** made neurons **more** likely to fire in winners of dominance battles and **less** likely in losers; effects **reversible** if losers become dominant → naturally occurring neurotransmitters have different effects depending on prior **psychosocial experience**, by altering serotonin receptor sensitivity.
- Berton et al. (2006): "bullied" smaller mice → **BDNF (brain-derived/development neurotrophic factor)** turned on in the **mesolimbic dopamine system** by a psychological experience (bullying), switching that system from facilitating reward/addiction to facilitating **avoidance/isolation** (the mouse became a recluse).

### Psychosocial effects on the development of brain structure and function
- Neuron structure (incl. number of receptors) can be **changed by learning and experience** throughout life.
- Greenough et al. (1990): rats raised in **enriched, learning-rich environments** developed **more connections + more dendrites** in the **cerebellum** vs "couch potato" rats; structural changes began in as little as **4 days** (Wallace et al., 1992) → enormous **plasticity**.
- Early-development stress changes **HPA-axis** function → more/less susceptibility to later stress.
- Sallet et al. (2011): housing monkeys in larger groups increased **gray matter** in social-cognition brain regions (and social ranking). Kanai et al. (2012): correlational — larger Facebook networks ↔ increased temporal-lobe gray-matter density.
- Conclusion: **early psychological experience affects nervous-system development → vulnerability to later disorders**; the CNS is constantly changing via learning/experience, even into old age (plasticity helps adaptation).

## Behavioral and Cognitive Science

### Conditioning and cognitive processes
- **Cognitive science** (KEY TERM, p. 53) — how we acquire, process, store, and retrieve information.
- **Rescorla (1988)**: simply **pairing two events in time (contiguity)** is **not** what matters in classical conditioning; a variety of **judgments and cognitive processes** determine the outcome, even in rats. Example: if meat powder appears **only** after the metronome (animal 1) vs **with and without** the metronome (animal 2), the two animals learn **different things** (the metronome is **less meaningful** to animal 2). Conditioning teaches the **relationship among environmental events**.

### Learned helplessness (KEY TERM, p. 53)
> **Learned helplessness** — occurs when animals (or people) encounter conditions over which they have **no control** and **give up** trying to cope (Maier & Seligman, 1976); the animal develops the equivalent of **depression**.
- In humans, depression follows when people make an **attribution** that they have **no control** over stress, even when others see that something could be done (Abramson, Seligman & Teasdale, 1978). Revisited in Ch 7.
- **Learned optimism** (Seligman): an optimistic, upbeat attitude → better psychological/physical functioning. Levy et al. (2002): positive self/aging attitudes → lived **7.5 years longer** (exceeds the 1-4 years from low BP, low cholesterol, no obesity/smoking). Launched the field of **positive psychology**.

### Social learning
- **Albert Bandura** — organisms can learn by **observing** others, not just by direct experience: **modeling / observational learning** (KEY TERM, p. 54). Even animals must make a **symbolic** decision about whether their own situation matches the observed one. Behavior + cognitive factors + environment converge; importance of the **social context** of learning → field of **social neuroscience**.

### Prepared learning (KEY TERM, p. 55)
> **Prepared learning** — over evolution we have become **highly prepared** to learn fears of certain objects/situations because that knowledge aided **survival of the species**.
- We learn to fear **snakes/spiders** more readily than rocks/flowers (even when rationally harmless), and **less** readily fear guns/electrical outlets (though deadlier). Possible cave-ancestor explanation.
- **Sex difference** (Rakison, 2009): females are particularly sensitive to this learning, showing it as early as **11 months** → may explain the **greater incidence of snake/spider phobias in women** (foragers/gatherers vs risk-taking male hunters).
- **One-trial (taste-aversion) learning**: quickly learning to avoid food that tastes bad/causes nausea (survival-relevant); animals **don't** learn the association nearly as fast if **shocked** rather than poisoned (shock isn't a natural consequence of eating).

### Cognitive science and the unconscious
- The unconscious is **not** Freud's "seething cauldron"; we simply process/store/act on information **without awareness**.
- **Blind sight (unconscious vision)** (Weiskrantz, 1992): a man blinded after **visual cortex** removal could still reach for, distinguish, and respond to objects while insisting he was only "guessing." Same occurs in **hypnotized** people (suggested blindness) → illustrates **dissociation** between behavior and consciousness (basis of dissociative disorders, Ch 6).
- **Implicit memory** (KEY TERM, p. 55) — acting on the basis of past events **without remembering** them (vs **explicit memory** = good memory for events). Anna O.'s paralysis was connected to implicit memories of her father's death.
- **The "black box"** = unobservable feelings/cognitions inferred from self-report.
- **Stroop color-naming paradigm** — naming the ink color of words is **delayed** when the word's meaning grabs attention; people are **slower** to name colors of **disorder-relevant** words (e.g., *blood, injury, dissect* for Judy) → reveals emotional significance even outside awareness.
- fMRI work: the greater the **duration, intensity, and coherence** of a neural representation, the more likely the person is **conscious** of the information. Supports Freud's unconscious **up to a point**, but **no evidence** for an elaborate id/ego/superego structure.

## Emotions

### The physiology and purpose of fear
- **Flight or fight response** (KEY TERM, p. 56) — the alarm reaction during life-threatening emergencies; programmed in all animals (Darwin, 1872); purpose = mobilize to escape (flight) or fend off (fight).
- **Walter Cannon (1929)** physiology of fear: blood vessels **constrict** (↑ arterial pressure, ↓ blood to extremities → "white with fear"); blood redirected to **skeletal muscles/vital organs**; **piloerection** (hair stands on end, conserves heat); **breathing faster/deeper** (more oxygen); **glucose** released from the liver; **pupils dilate**; hearing sharpens; **digestion suspended** ("dry mouth"); pressure to urinate/defecate/vomit. Survival value → ancestors with strong reactions survived and passed on genes.

### Emotional phenomena
- **Emotion** (KEY TERM, p. 57) — an **action tendency** (Barlow; Lang): a tendency to behave a certain way (e.g., escape), elicited by an **external event** (threat) + a **feeling state** (terror) + a (possibly) characteristic **physiological response**. Feeling states **motivate** behavior (escaping lowers unpleasant terror). Function (Öhman): an evolution-guided means to do what is needed to pass on genes.
- **Mood** (KEY TERM, p. 57) — a more **persistent** period of affect/emotionality (vs emotions, which are short-lived states lasting minutes to hours).
- **Affect** (KEY TERM, p. 57) — the **momentary emotional tone** accompanying what we say/do (e.g., looking sad after an A = inappropriate affect). Also summarizes characteristic emotional states: **negative affect** (fearful/anxious/depressed) vs **positive affect** (pleasant/joyful/excited).

### The components of emotion (three overlapping; Figure 2.16)
- **Behavior** — basic emotion patterns differ fundamentally (freeze, escape, approach, attack); emotion is a means of **communication** between members of a species (a scared face warns others → emotions are **contagious**, cf. mass hysteria).
- **Physiology** (Cannon) — emotion is a **brain function**; emotional areas are more **ancient/primitive** than higher-cognition areas. Direct neurobiological connections between emotional centers and the eye (retina)/ear allow **emotional activation without higher cognition** (LeDoux, Zajonc).
- **Cognition** (Lazarus) — environmental changes are **appraised** for their potential impact; the **type of appraisal determines the emotion** (a gun in a dark alley = fear; a gun in a museum display = not). Lazarus: thinking and feeling cannot be separated; others (Teasdale) argue the systems interact/overlap but are fundamentally separate.

### Anger and your heart
- Negative emotions (**hostility, anger**) increase **heart-disease** risk; sustained hostility + suppressed anger contribute **more** to heart-disease death than smoking, high BP, or high cholesterol.
- Ironson et al. (1992): **anger** (even just **remembering** being angry) dropped the heart's **pumping efficiency** in heart-disease patients (not stress or exercise) → risk of **arrhythmias**.
- Williams et al. (1980): often-angry medical students were **7× more likely to die by age 50**. Suarez et al. (2002): inflammation from an overactive immune system in hostile people may clog arteries.
- Larsen et al. (2012): a **forgiving attitude** neutralized anger's toxic cardiovascular effects (reduced BP/HR reactivity), with effects persisting into later rumination. CAUTION: anger contributes to heart disease but so do other factors (incl. genetic vulnerability) — not one-dimensional.

### Emotions and psychopathology
- **Suppressing** almost any emotion (anger, fear) increases **sympathetic activity** → may contribute to psychopathology.
- A **panic attack** may be the normal emotion of **fear occurring at the wrong time** (nothing to fear) (Ch 5).
- **Mania** (overexcitement/grandiosity) is part of **bipolar disorder**; alternates with extreme sadness/distress (**depression** = inability to feel pleasure, hopelessness, suicide risk) → mood disorders (Ch 7).
- Basic emotions (fear, anger, sadness/distress, excitement) may **contribute to and even define** disorders. Mood colors cognition: positive mood → positive associations/memories; depressed → unpleasant memories ("bottle half empty" vs "half full"). Leading psychopathologists study **emotion dysregulation**.

## Cultural, Social, and Interpersonal Factors

### Voodoo, the evil eye, and other fears
- **Fright disorders** — exaggerated startle + fear/anxiety reactions found across cultures.
- **Susto** (Latin American) — anxiety-based symptoms (insomnia, irritability, phobias, sweating, **tachycardia**); cause = belief that one has become the object of **black magic/witchcraft** and is suddenly badly frightened. The sinister influence is sometimes called the **evil eye**; the fright disorder can be **fatal**.
- **Voodoo death** (Cannon, 1942, Haiti): a medicine man's death "sentence" creates **intolerable autonomic arousal** with no ability to cope because there is **no social support** (friends/family withdraw, assuming death has occurred) → organ damage and death. A physically/psychologically healthy person dies from **changes in the social environment**.

### Gender
- Fear/phobias are **universal**, but **what** we fear is shaped by the social environment. Elbedour et al. (1997): **Jewish children** (individualistic society) had **fewer** fears than **Bedouin children** (paternalistic, group/family-centered society), whose fears centered on **family disintegration**.
- **Gender roles** strongly shape psychopathology. Insect/small-animal phobia severe enough to limit life is **~90% female**; **social phobia** affects men and women **roughly equally**. Likely due to **cultural expectations**: men hide/endure fear (less acceptable to admit), women acknowledge it → phobia develops; men more likely to admit **shyness** than fear, or to **self-medicate** fear/panic with **alcohol** → contributes to **alcoholism** (more males).
- Felmingham & Bryant (2012): after PTSD exposure therapy, **women maintained gains better** at follow-up (possibly better recall of emotional memories).
- **Bulimia nervosa** occurs almost entirely in young females (cultural emphasis on female thinness, Ch 8); of the few male cases, a substantial percentage are gay.
- **"Tend and befriend"** (Taylor et al., 2000) — a female-typical stress response: protecting self/young via nurturing (**tend**) + forming alliances, esp. with other females (**befriend**); builds on the brain's **attachment-caregiving** system; gender-specific neurobiology. Gender does **not cause** psychopathology but shapes its **form and content**.

### Social effects on health and behavior
- The greater the **number/frequency of social relationships**, the **longer** you live; low social-index scores → shorter life expectancy (controlling for physical health, BP, cholesterol, smoking). Social relationships protect against high BP, depression, alcoholism, arthritis, AIDS progression, and low-birth-weight babies.
- Risk of depression for people who **live alone** is ~**80% higher** (Pulkki-Raback et al., 2012). **Social isolation** raises death risk about as much as **smoking** and more than inactivity/obesity (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). Key factor is the **perception of loneliness**, not the absolute number of contacts.
- Cohen et al. (1997): exposed 276 volunteers to cold viruses; those with the **fewest social ties** were **>4× more likely** to catch a cold. Pet ownership → lower resting HR/BP and smaller stress increases.
- Haber & Barchas (1983): **amphetamine** had no average effect on monkeys, but **increased dominant behavior** in high-hierarchy monkeys and **submissive behavior** in low-hierarchy ones → a biological factor's effect is uninterpretable without **social context**.
- Steinglass et al. (1988): in a threatened Israeli community, **perceived** disintegration of one's social network (whether or not it was actually disintegrating) best predicted poor adjustment.
- **Urbanicity & schizophrenia**: Lewis et al. (1992) — schizophrenia incidence **38% greater** in men raised in cities vs rural areas (after controlling for drift, drug use, family instability); something about cities may contribute (cause unknown).
- **Social stigma** — psychological disorders carry substantial stigma (anxious/depressed = "weak/cowardly"; schizophrenic = "unpredictable/crazy"); psychological war injuries earn scorn vs medals for physical injuries → less support, less recovery, greater suicide risk.
- Culture shapes the **expression** of disorders: schizophrenia and major depression occur in **all cultures** but look different (Western depression = **guilt/inadequacy**; developing countries = **physical distress** like fatigue/illness).

### Global incidence (WHO)
- Mental disorders = **13% of the global burden of disease** (WHO).
- **10-20%** of all primary medical services in poor countries are sought by patients with psychological disorders (anxiety, mood, suicide attempts, alcoholism, drug abuse, childhood developmental disorders).
- Severe workforce shortages: Cambodia (~2006) had only **26 psychiatrists for 12 million people**; sub-Saharan Africa ~**1 psychiatrist per 2 million**. In the US, ~200,000 mental-health professionals for ~300 million people, yet only **1 in 3** with a disorder has ever received any treatment.

## Life-Span Development

- Avoid the **"snapshot" perspective** (judging the whole person from one life point). The "**end of history**" illusion makes us think we will change little going forward (Quoidbach et al., 2013).
- **Erik Erikson (1982)** — **8 major psychosocial crises** across life, each shaped by biological maturation + social demands; unlike Freud (no stages beyond adolescence), Erikson held that we grow/change **beyond age 65**. Criticized as vague/under-researched but illustrates the comprehensive life-span approach.
- Kolb, Gibb & Gorny (2003): enriched environments had **different brain effects depending on developmental stage** (juvenile vs adult vs aged); even **prenatal** experience (mother in a complex environment during pregnancy) → more complex cortical circuits in offspring. Cameron et al. (2005): mother rats' first-week behavior (not later) strongly influenced pups' lifelong stress handling.
- Developmental specifics: children/adolescents do **not** benefit from antidepressants the same way adults do (and face risks adults don't); the **gender distribution in depression is roughly equal until puberty**, then becomes more common in **girls**.

### The principle of equifinality (KEY TERM, p. 63) — HIGH-YIELD
> **Equifinality** — used in developmental psychopathology to indicate that a given outcome can be reached by **a number of different paths** (a behavior/disorder may have many causes, like a fever) (Cicchetti, 1991).
- Examples: a **delusional syndrome** can be schizophrenia OR amphetamine abuse; **delirium** can follow surgery in older adults OR thiamine deficiency OR renal disease; **autism** can follow prenatal rubella exposure OR labor difficulties.
- Psychological + biological factors interact across development: people with equally severe **brain damage** can have **different** outcomes depending on **social support** and **adaptive personality** (e.g., paraplegics who become athletes vs those who withdraw).
- **Resilience research** — studying people who do **not** develop disorders despite similar stress. A caring adult friend/relative and the child's own coping ability can offset stress (e.g., a parent's psychiatric disturbance); biological differences in stress responsiveness arise from protective factors like **social support** and a strong sense of **purpose/meaning**.

## Conclusions

- Seven contributing traditions must all be considered: (1) **psychoanalytic theory**, (2) **behavioral and cognitive science**, (3) **emotional influences**, (4) **social and cultural influences**, (5) **genetics**, (6) **neuroscience**, (7) **life-span developmental factors**.
- It is **inaccurate and misleading** to say a disorder is "caused by a chemical imbalance" — chemical imbalances almost certainly exist, but they can be **caused by** psychological/social factors (stress, emotion, family interactions, aging) or their interaction. Likewise, "alcoholism is caused by lack of willpower" is "highly simplistic and just plain wrong."
- **Core take-home:** psychological disorders have **many interacting causes** → a **multidimensional integrative approach** is required. (Next chapter: assessment and diagnosis.)

## DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 overlay (current-classification notes, NOT in the 2015 book text)

> **DSM-5-TR note:** This is a foundations/etiology chapter and contains **no formal diagnostic criteria**, so there are **no criteria-level DSM-5-TR changes** to flag for this chapter specifically. General terminology the chapter uses that DSM-5-TR has updated: the book already uses **"intellectual disability"** (in the PKU example) in place of the obsolete "mental retardation" — DSM-5-TR formalizes this as **intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability)**, aligning with ICD-11's "disorders of intellectual development" (covered fully in Ch 14). The chapter's **multidimensional integrative / biopsychosocial framing** is fully consistent with DSM-5-TR, which retains a categorical diagnostic system while explicitly acknowledging dimensional and developmental/contextual considerations.

> **DSM-5-TR note (forward references):** The disorders this chapter previews are where DSM-5-TR criteria changes actually live, e.g., **Prolonged Grief Disorder** added in DSM-5-TR (relevant when grief/depression is discussed in Ch 7), explicit **suicidal behavior** and **nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI)** as conditions for further study/coding, and updated **ICD-10-CM codes** throughout. None of these alter Ch 2's etiological content; they apply when the specific disorders are reached.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 (in effect since 2022) is the current global classification used in India/NIMHANS settings and differs from DSM-5-TR in several ways relevant to the disorders previewed here: ICD-11 distinguishes **PTSD vs Complex PTSD** (the chapter's hurricane/maltreatment + social-support discussion maps onto trauma-related disorders); ICD-11 adopts a **fully dimensional model of personality disorders** (severity + trait domains) vs DSM-5-TR's categorical clusters (DSM-5-TR also offers the **Alternative Model for Personality Disorders** in Section III); ICD-11 added **Gaming Disorder**; and ICD-11 frames intellectual disability as **disorders of intellectual development**. These are the **current-nosology overlay** only — the 2015 Barlow & Durand text predates ICD-11 and is written to DSM-5.

> **ICD-11 note (culture-bound syndromes):** The chapter's **susto** and **voodoo death** fall under what DSM-5-TR now calls **cultural concepts of distress** (replacing the older "culture-bound syndromes" label); ICD-11 similarly emphasizes **cultural formulation** rather than fixed culture-specific diagnostic categories. Both current systems treat these as culturally-shaped expressions of distress rather than discrete disease entities — consistent with the chapter's point that culture shapes the **form and content** of psychopathology.
