# Ch 7: Mood Disorders and Suicide

This chapter covers the **mood disorders** (unipolar depressive disorders and bipolar disorders), defined by gross deviations in mood, plus a major section on **suicide**. It moves from clinical description and DSM-5 criteria of every disorder, through prevalence and life-span/cultural patterns, into an **integrative biological–psychological–social etiology** (genes, HPA axis, neurotransmitters, learned helplessness, Beck's cognitive triad, stressful life events), and finishes with biological and psychological treatments. It is one of the highest-yield, most disorder-dense and treatment-dense chapters in the book and a perennial exam favourite.

> **DSM-5-TR note (chapter-wide):** This text is written to DSM-5 (2013). DSM-5-TR (2022) kept all of these mood-disorder criteria essentially unchanged but **added Prolonged Grief Disorder** as an official diagnosis (the chapter's "complicated grief," which was in DSM-5 Section III for further study). DSM-5-TR also clarified suicidal-behaviour and nonsuicidal self-injury reporting, and updated ICD-10-CM codes. Where a criterion below has no DSM-5-TR change, it is unchanged.

## Understanding and Defining Mood Disorders

### The Two Fundamental States: Depression and Mania
- Beginning with **DSM-III (1980)**, these problems are grouped as **mood disorders** (older labels: "affective disorders," "depressive neuroses"); characterized by **gross deviations in mood**.
- **Major depressive episode** = most commonly diagnosed and most severe depression.
  - Extremely depressed mood lasting **at least 2 weeks**, plus cognitive symptoms (worthlessness, indecisiveness) and disturbed physical functions (sleep, appetite/weight, energy).
  - Central indicators are the **physical/somatic/vegetative symptoms** and the behavioral/emotional "shutdown"; **anhedonia** (loss of energy + inability to feel pleasure) is more characteristic of severe episodes than sadness itself. Reflects **low positive affect**, not just high negative affect.
  - Crying does NOT reflect severity (occurs equally in depressed and nondepressed people).
  - Untreated duration ≈ **4 to 9 months**.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Major Depressive Episode (Table 7.1):**
> **A.** ≥5 of the following in the same **2-week** period, a change from prior functioning; at least one symptom is (1) **depressed mood** or (2) **loss of interest/pleasure**:
> 1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (in children/adolescents can be **irritable mood**)
> 2. Markedly diminished interest/pleasure in all/almost all activities
> 3. Significant weight change (>5% body weight/month) or appetite change
> 4. Insomnia or hypersomnia
> 5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation (observable by others)
> 6. Fatigue/loss of energy
> 7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt
> 8. Diminished ability to think/concentrate, or indecisiveness
> 9. Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, or a suicide attempt/plan
> **B.** Clinically significant distress/impairment. **C.** Not due to substance or medical condition (e.g., hypothyroidism).

- **Mania** = abnormally exaggerated elation, joy, or euphoria; some compare it to "continuous sexual orgasm." Hyperactive, little sleep, **grandiose plans**, **flight of ideas** (rapid, sometimes incoherent speech). DSM-5 added "**persistently increased goal-directed activity or energy**" to criterion A.
  - Manic episode duration: **at least 1 week** (less if hospitalization needed). Irritability often appears near the end; anxiety/depression ("dysphoric mania") is common. Untreated ≈ **3 to 4 months**.
- **Hypomanic episode** = less severe version; **at least 4 days**, no marked impairment, no psychosis ("hypo" = below). Not itself problematic but defines several disorders.

### The Structure of Mood Disorders
- **Unipolar mood disorder**: mood stays at one "pole." Unipolar mania is rare (most develop depression eventually).
- **Bipolar mood disorder**: alternates between depression and mania. Depression and elation are **relatively independent**, not exact opposites → an episode can have **"mixed features."**
- One study: **30%** of 1,090 acute-mania patients had mixed episodes; in another, up to **two-thirds** of bipolar-depressed patients also had manic symptoms (racing thoughts, distractibility, agitation).
- **Course/temporal patterning** matters: full remission (recover ≥2 months between episodes) vs partial remission. Goal of treatment is not only relief of current episode but **preventing future episodes**.

## Depressive Disorders

### Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)
- Defined by absence of manic/hypomanic episodes. A single isolated lifetime episode is now known to be **relatively rare**.
- **Recurrent** = ≥2 episodes separated by ≥2 symptom-free months. **35%–85%** of single-episode cases have a second episode. Recurrence risk: 20% first year, rising to **40%** in the second year.
- Median lifetime number of episodes: **4 to 7** (25% have ≥6). Median duration of recurrent episodes ≈ 4–5 months.
- About **20%** of MDD episodes are **chronic** (≥2 years) → meet criteria for persistent depressive disorder. Unipolar depression is increasingly viewed as a **chronic condition that waxes and wanes**.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — MDD (Table 7.3):** **A.** ≥1 major depressive episode. **B.** Not better explained by schizoaffective/schizophrenia spectrum disorders. **C.** Never a manic or hypomanic episode. Specifiers: single/recurrent; mild/moderate/severe; with anxious distress, mixed features, melancholic features, atypical features, mood-congruent/incongruent psychotic features, catatonia, peripartum onset, seasonal pattern; in partial/full remission.

### Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)
- Shares MDD symptoms but differs in **course**: depressed mood for **≥2 years** (1 year in children/adolescents), never symptom-free >2 months at a time. May have **as few as 2 symptoms**.
- Considered **more severe than MDD** because: higher comorbidity, less treatment-responsive, slower improvement. Can last **20–30 years**.
- **Double depression**: persistent depression (fewer symptoms, "dysthymia") + superimposed major depressive episodes. Few dysthymic symptoms develop first (often early age), then MDEs occur and revert to baseline. Associated with more severe psychopathology, worse course; **61%** had not recovered the underlying pattern 2 years post follow-up (Keller et al., 1983).
- **22%** of people with persistent depression with fewer symptoms eventually had an MDE; among those who had dysthymia, up to **79%** also had an MDE at some point.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Persistent Depressive Disorder (Table 7.4):** **A.** Depressed mood most days for ≥2 years (≥1 year in youth, can be irritable). **B.** ≥2 of: poor appetite/overeating; insomnia/hypersomnia; low energy; low self-esteem; poor concentration/indecision; hopelessness. **C.** Never symptom-free >2 months in the period. Specifiers: with pure dysthymic syndrome / persistent MDE / intermittent MDEs (with or without current episode); early onset (<21) vs late onset (≥21).

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5 (and DSM-5-TR) **merged** DSM-IV's chronic major depressive disorder + dysthymic disorder into one **Persistent Depressive Disorder** category. DSM-5-TR retains this; no substantive change to the criteria here.

### Additional Defining Criteria (Specifiers)
Rated severity (mild/moderate/severe) plus **eight specifiers**:
1. **Psychotic features** — hallucinations/delusions; **mood-congruent** (e.g., somatic delusion of body rotting; auditory voices of guilt) vs **mood-incongruent** (e.g., grandeur in depression — rare, may progress to/indicate schizophrenia). Grandeur in **mania is mood-congruent**. Psychosis occurs in **5%–20%** of depression; predicts poor treatment response.
2. **Anxious distress** — "perhaps the most important addition to specifiers" in DSM-5. Anxiety → more severe, more suicide, poorer outcome.
3. **Mixed features** — ≥3 manic symptoms in a depressive episode.
4. **Melancholic features** — severe somatic symptoms: early-morning awakening, weight loss, loss of libido, excessive guilt, anhedonia.
5. **Catatonic features** — absence of movement (stupor) or **catalepsy** (waxy, semirigid muscles) or excessive purposeless movement; rare in mania.
6. **Atypical features** — **oversleep and overeat** (weight gain, higher diabetes risk), mood reactivity (can react with pleasure), more women, earlier onset, more suicide attempts, more comorbid alcohol abuse.
7. **Peripartum onset** — surrounding birth. **13%–19% (1 in 8)** of women giving birth meet criteria for depression (**peripartum depression**); 7.2% full MDE. Postpartum > pregnancy incidence. Fathers also affected (~10% over first year vs ~40% mothers). Distinguish from **"baby blues"** (40%–80% of women, days 1–5, normal, transient — specifier does NOT apply). In rare tragic cases, a psychotic mother kills the newborn. Peripartum depression did NOT get a separate category (just a specifier); treatment is the same as non-peripartum.
8. **Seasonal pattern** — episodes tied to seasons (usually **winter depression**); → **Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)**. Affects ~2.7% of North Americans; 15%–25% have subclinical vulnerability. Like atypical depression: oversleep + overeat. Linked to **melatonin** (pineal gland, increased in winter / less light) and delayed **circadian rhythms**. Prevalence higher at extreme latitudes (e.g., <2% Florida vs ~10% New Hampshire; 9% Fairbanks). Treatment: **phototherapy** (~2 hr of 2,500 lux bright light on awakening; lift in 3–4 days, remission 1–2 weeks; side effects: headaches 19%, eyestrain 17%, "wired" 14%); **morning light > evening light > placebo**; CBT also effective and more enduring (only 5–6% relapse with CBT vs 39% with light alone).

### Onset and Duration (MDD)
- Risk low until early teens, then rises **linearly**. Mean age of onset ≈ **30**; 10% have first episode at ≥55.
- Incidence rising: 25% of 18–29-year-olds had already experienced MDD. By age band (Rohde 2013): children 5–12 = 5%; adolescents 13–17 = 19%; emerging adults 18–23 = 24%; young adults 24–30 = 16%.
- Even severe episodes: ~**90%** probability of remission within 1 year. Episodes lasting ≥5 years → only 38% eventually recover.
- **Early-onset persistent depression (<21 yrs)**: greater chronicity, poorer prognosis, stronger familial loading, more comorbid personality disorders. 76% of children with persistent mild depressive symptoms later develop MDD.

### From Grief to Depression
- After bereavement, normal grief peaks within the **first 6 months**; acute grief evolves into **integrated grief** (death's finality acknowledged, bittersweet but positive memories incorporated; recurs at anniversaries — normal).
- Concern when someone does NOT grieve, or when grief lasts beyond typical time. After 6 months–1 year, ~**7%** of bereaved develop a disorder ("**complicated grief**" / syndrome of complicated grief) — suicidal thoughts increase (focused on joining the deceased), impaired future-imagining, rigid emotion regulation.
- Distinct from MDD: strong **yearning** linked to **dopamine** activation (reduced in MDD); attachment-related brain areas active. Treated like PTSD: reexperience the loss under supervision until integrated grief is achieved.

> **DSM-5-TR note (HIGH YIELD):** The chapter's "complicated grief" was in **DSM-5 Section III** (conditions for further study). **DSM-5-TR (2022) added Prolonged Grief Disorder** as an official diagnosis: persistent grief ≥12 months in adults (≥6 months in children/adolescents) with intense yearning/preoccupation plus identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance, emotional pain, loneliness, etc.

### Other Depressive Disorders (NEW in DSM-5)
- **Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD):** affects ~**2%–5%** of women (distinct from the 20%–40% with non-impairing PMS). ≥5 symptoms in the final week before menses, improving after onset, minimal postmenses. Must include ≥1 of: marked affective lability, irritability/anger, depressed mood/hopelessness, anxiety/tension; plus others to total 5 (anhedonia, concentration difficulty, lethargy, appetite change, hyper/insomnia, feeling overwhelmed, physical symptoms). Confirmed by **prospective daily ratings over ≥2 cycles**. Best considered a **mood disorder**, not endocrine.
- **Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD):** created to **stop over-diagnosis of pediatric bipolar disorder** (diagnoses rose **40-fold, 1995–2005**). These children show chronic irritability, anger, temper tantrums but **NO periods of elevated mood (mania)** and no excess bipolar in families; instead at risk for future depressive/anxiety disorders.
  - **DSM-5-TR Criteria (Table 7.6):** severe recurrent temper outbursts (verbal/behavioral) grossly out of proportion; inconsistent with developmental level; **≥3×/week**; persistently irritable/angry mood between outbursts; present **≥12 months** (no symptom-free period ≥3 months); in ≥2 of 3 settings; **not first diagnosed before age 6 or after 18**; onset before age 10; never a full manic/hypomanic episode.

## Bipolar Disorders

- Key feature: **manic episodes alternate with major depressive episodes** ("roller-coaster"). Parallel to depressive disorders.
- **Bipolar I disorder**: full **manic** episode (with or without MDEs). A full manic episode emerging during antidepressant treatment that persists beyond the drug's physiological effect = sufficient for Bipolar I.
- **Bipolar II disorder**: ≥1 **hypomanic** episode + ≥1 MDE; **never a full manic episode** (case of Jane).

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Manic Episode (Table 7.2):** **A.** Distinct period of abnormally elevated/expansive/irritable mood **AND** increased goal-directed activity/energy, ≥1 week (any duration if hospitalized). **B.** ≥3 symptoms (≥4 if mood only irritable): (1) inflated self-esteem/grandiosity, (2) decreased need for sleep, (3) more talkative/pressured speech, (4) flight of ideas/racing thoughts, (5) distractibility, (6) increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation, (7) excessive risky pleasurable activities (buying sprees, sexual indiscretions, foolish investments). **C.** Marked impairment / hospitalization / psychotic features. **D.** Not attributable to substance/medical condition.

> **DSM-5-TR Criteria — Bipolar II (Table 7.8):** ≥1 hypomanic episode (min 4 days, no marked impairment, no psychosis) + ≥1 MDE; never a manic episode.

### Cyclothymic Disorder
- Milder, chronic version (analog of persistent depressive disorder). Chronic alternation of mood elevation and depression **not reaching** manic or MDE severity; ≥2 years (1 year in youth), present at least half the time, never symptom-free >2 months. Often seen as "moody." Treat because of **risk of progressing to full bipolar I/II** (one-third to one-half progress).

### Additional Specifiers for Bipolar
- All depressive-disorder specifiers apply. **Rapid-cycling specifier (unique to bipolar):** **≥4 manic/depressive episodes within a year**; severe, treatment-resistant; higher suicide risk; **60%–90% female**; ~20%–50% of bipolar patients; can worsen to **rapid switching**. **Antidepressants may precipitate rapid cycling** → anticonvulsants/mood stabilizers preferred. Only 3%–5% continue rapid cycling across 5 years (80% revert within 2 years).

### Onset, Duration, Prognosis (Bipolar)
- Age of onset: Bipolar I ≈ **15–18**, Bipolar II ≈ **19–22** (younger than MDD; onset more **acute**). Rare after age 40; course is **chronic**. Cyclothymia onset often ~12–14, lifelong.
- **10%–25%** of Bipolar II progress to Bipolar I. Unipolar/bipolar may be a **spectrum/continuum** (Cassano: up to 67.5% of unipolar patients had some manic symptoms).
- **Suicide is an all-too-common consequence**, almost always during depressive episodes. Lifetime attempt estimates: **12%–48%** (~20× higher than non-bipolar). Completed suicide **4× higher** than in recurrent MDD. Poor outcomes even with treatment.

## Prevalence of Mood Disorders

- **MDD:** ~**16% lifetime**, ~6% past year worldwide.
- **Persistent depressive disorder** (dysthymia + chronic MDD): ~**3.5%** lifetime and past year.
- **Bipolar disorder:** ~**1% lifetime**, 0.8% past year.
- **Women twice as likely** as men to have mood disorders — but this imbalance is due **solely to MDD and dysthymia**; **bipolar is ~equal across gender**.
- MDD/dysthymia significantly **lower among Blacks than Whites**; **higher among Native Americans** (translation/cultural issues noted).

### Children, Adolescents, Older Adults
- Depression less common pre-puberty (MDD ~1.5% ages 2–5), **rises dramatically in adolescence** (adolescents ≈ adults). Child sex ratio ~50:50; **puberty triggers the female predominance**. 20%–50% of children have subclinical depressive symptoms.
- **Bipolar ~1%** in childhood/adolescence (same as adults); clinic diagnosis rates inflated (now → DMDD).
- **Over-65 MDD ≈ half** the general rate (fewer triggering events), but **milder symptoms more common** and tied to illness/infirmity.

### Life-Span Developmental Influences
- **3-month-old babies can become depressed** (infants of depressed mothers show sad faces, slow movement, unresponsiveness even with nondepressed adults). Mood disorders are **fundamentally similar in children and adults** (only DMDD is developmentally limited, ≤12 yrs). For preschoolers (≤6): set aside strict 2-week rule; 4 symptoms (not 5) may suffice if core sadness/irritability + anhedonia present.
- Childhood depression/mania often misdiagnosed as **ADHD or conduct disorder**; many now fit DMDD. Mania in children <9 presents as more **irritability and emotional swings**; irritability alone is **nonspecific** and insufficient to diagnose mania.
- **Older adults:** 14%–42% of nursing-home residents may have MDEs; ~80% of depressed elderly did not remit over 6 years. Late-onset depression linked to sleep problems, illness anxiety, agitation. Up to **50% of Alzheimer's patients** have comorbid depression. Depression **doubles death risk** after heart attack/stroke. **Suicide rates highest in older adults**. Gender imbalance **lessens after 65**.

### Across Cultures
- Depression often takes **somatic forms** (stomachaches, headaches, weakness/tiredness). Cultural idioms: Hopi say "**heartbroken**"; Aboriginal Australian men attribute it to "**weakness/injury of the spirit**." Individualist cultures → "I feel blue"; collectivist → "our life has lost its meaning." Native American village lifetime prevalence ~28% (≈4× general population), driven by major depression + chronic life stress.

### Among Creative Individuals
- Long-standing belief linking **genius to "madness."** ~20% of 36 major 20th-century American poets (e.g., Plath, Sexton, Berryman, Lowell) had **bipolar disorder** (vs <1% population). Creativity is specifically associated with **manic (not depressive) states**; possibly a shared **genetic** vulnerability — heightened creativity persists in euthymic bipolar (but not unipolar) patients.

## Causes of Mood Disorders

> **Equifinality:** the same outcome (depression) can arise from different causes. Etiology is **integrative**: biological + psychological + social.

### Biological Dimensions

**Familial & Genetic Influences**
- Rate in first-degree relatives of probands ≈ **2–3× greater** than controls; higher with greater severity, recurrence, earlier onset.
- **Twin study (McGuffin 2003):** if first twin bipolar → **66.7% identical vs 18.9% fraternal**; if unipolar → **45.6% vs 20.2%**. Bipolar confers risk of *some* mood disorder, not necessarily bipolar. **80%** of concordant identical twins also concordant for polarity. Conclusion: genetic contribution to **depression is shared** across unipolar/bipolar, but genetics of **mania are distinct**.
- **Heritability estimates: ~40% women, ~20% men** (so 60%–80% of causes are environmental). **Nonshared** (unique) events matter more than shared ones. Sex difference: environment plays a larger role in men.
- **Depression and anxiety: same genes** — Kendler's twin data show the same genetic factors underlie both; social/psychological factors differentiate them. Suggests a general predisposition (basic temperament = **neuroticism / negative affect**), not a disorder-specific one (except possibly mania).

**Neurotransmitter Systems**
- **Permissive hypothesis:** low **serotonin** "permits" other neurotransmitters (norepinephrine, dopamine) to become dysregulated → mood irregularities. Low serotonin → more impulsivity, wider mood swings. Current view: the **balance/interaction** of neurotransmitters matters more than absolute level of any one.
- **Dopamine** implicated in mania, atypical/psychotic depression (L-dopa can induce hypomania; chronic stress lowers dopamine → depressive-like behavior).

**The Endocrine System ("stress hypothesis")**
- Focus shifted to overactivity in the **HPA axis** (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical) → stress hormone **cortisol** (elevated in depressed patients). Cushing's disease / hypothyroidism → depression.
- **Dexamethasone Suppression Test (DST):** dexamethasone normally suppresses cortisol; ~**50%** of (especially severe) depressed patients show **reduced/non-suppression**. Failed as a diagnostic test because anxiety-disorder patients also show non-suppression.
- **Neurogenesis hypothesis:** chronic stress hormones harm neurons and suppress **neurogenesis in the hippocampus** (which itself keeps stress hormones in check); hippocampal **shrinkage**. At-risk girls (depressed mothers) show **reduced hippocampal volume** *before* depression. Successful treatments (incl. ECT, exercise) **promote neurogenesis**, reversing this.

**Sleep & Circadian Rhythms**
- Hallmark: **shorter REM latency** (REM begins sooner after falling asleep), more intense REM, delayed/absent **slow-wave (deep) sleep**. Some disturbances persist even when not depressed in severe recurrent cases.
- Insomnia is a **risk factor** for onset/persistence; treating insomnia directly may enhance depression treatment. Bipolar: decreased REM latency + severe insomnia AND hypersomnia. Sleep–mood relationship is **bidirectional**. **Sleep deprivation** (2nd half of night) → temporary improvement (esp. bipolar depression), returns with normal sleep.

**Brain Structure & Function**
- Depressed individuals show **greater right-sided anterior (prefrontal) activation** and **less left-sided activation / less alpha-wave activity**; persists after recovery and in at-risk offspring → possible **vulnerability marker**. (Bipolar-spectrum: *elevated* left-frontal activity predicts onset of Bipolar I.) Also implicated: **anterior cingulate cortex** and **amygdala** (increased inhibition, deficits pursuing goals).

### Psychological Dimensions

**Stressful Life Events**
- **Diathesis–stress model**: vulnerability + activating stressor. **Context and meaning** of an event matter more than the event itself (George W. Brown's method). Memory of events is biased by current mood → **prospective** study needed.
- Severe events precede nearly all depression (except some melancholic/psychotic recurrences). Stronger predictor for **initial** than recurrent episodes. **Humiliation, loss, social rejection** are the most potent. Twin study: a loss → 10× risk; loss **+ humiliation** → 20× risk.
- **Gene–environment correlation / reciprocal model:** genetically influenced personality leads people to *create/seek* stressful environments. ~1/3 of the stress–depression link is people placing themselves in high-risk situations. Stress triggers depression AND depressed people generate stress.
- **Stress & bipolar:** **positive goal-striving events** (new job, promotion, marriage, acceptance) trigger **mania** in vulnerable people; once cycling begins, episodes "develop a life of their own"; **sleep loss / disrupted circadian rhythm** (postpartum, jet lag) can precipitate mania.
- Only **20%–50%** of people who experience severe events develop a mood disorder → vulnerability matters.

**Learned Helplessness (Seligman)**
- Animals (and humans) exposed to uncontrollable aversive events become **helpless** → animal analog of depression. Humans become anxious/depressed when they **decide they have no control**. Anxiety is the *first* response; depression follows **hopelessness**.
- **Depressive attributional style:** (1) **internal** ("it's all my fault"), (2) **stable** (will always be my fault), (3) **global** (across many issues).
- Negative cognitive styles **precede and predict** depression (a risk factor), but emerge more with age/stress; not specific to depression (also in anxiety). Revised **hopelessness theory** (Abramson, Metalsky, Alloy 1989): attributions matter only insofar as they create **hopelessness** — anxious AND depressed people feel helpless, but only the depressed give up / become hopeless.

**Negative Cognitive Styles (Aaron T. Beck)**
- Depression = tendency to interpret events negatively. **Cognitive errors**: e.g., **arbitrary inference** (emphasizing the negative without evidence) and **overgeneralization** (one event → sweeping conclusion).
- **Depressive cognitive triad:** negative thoughts about (1) **self**, (2) **world**, (3) **future**.
- Underlying **negative schemas** (e.g., self-blame schema, negative self-evaluation schema), often **automatic** and not conscious. Beck = **father of cognitive therapy.** Bipolar adds ambitious goal-striving, perfectionism, self-criticism.

**Cognitive Vulnerability — Integration**
- Seligman and Beck models overlap; cognitive vulnerabilities predispose to depression. **Temple-Wisconsin study (Alloy & Abramson):** high-risk freshmen were **6–12× more likely** to have an MDE (16% vs 2.7%; minor symptoms 46% vs 14%). Cognitive vulnerability may even be **"contagious"** (roommates).

### Social and Cultural Dimensions
- **Marital relations:** dissatisfaction strongly related to depression (bidirectional). Marital split → severe depression 3× (women) and **9×** (men) higher; for first-onset, **only men** had heightened risk after a split. Depression for **men** disrupts relationships; for **women**, relationship problems cause depression. Recommend treating marital problems alongside the mood disorder.
- **Mood disorders in women:** ~**70%** of MDD/dysthymia are women (bipolar even). Constant ratio worldwide. Linked to **perceptions of uncontrollability**, culturally assigned passive/affiliative sex roles, smothering/overprotective parenting, discrimination/poverty. Girls' "sudden surge" at puberty; **rumination + self-blame** (Nolen-Hoeksema) predicts later depression vs men's distracting activity (which may be therapeutic — "activation").
- **Social support:** living alone → ~**80%** higher depression risk. Brown & Harris (1978): only **10%** of stressed women with a confidant became depressed vs **37%** without. Support speeds recovery (universal across countries, incl. China) — but for bipolar, support speeds recovery from **depressive** but **not manic** episodes → led to **Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)**.

### An Integrative Theory
- Shared, genetically determined **biological vulnerability** (overactive neurobiological stress response; serotonin transporter gene-linked polymorphic region) + **psychological vulnerability** (perceived inadequacy, depressive cognitive style) = the temperament of **neuroticism / negative affect** → triggered by stressful life events → stress hormones → wide neurotransmitter effects.
- **Acute Tryptophan Depletion (ATD)** study (Booij & Van der Does): lowering serotonin induced depressive symptoms **only in those with cognitive vulnerability** (none in healthy controls) → a mechanism for diathesis–stress.
- Bipolar adds an overactive **Behavioral Approach System (BAS)** → hypersensitivity to **goal-attainment events** and **circadian disruption**.

## Treatment of Mood Disorders

> Most depression goes untreated (under-recognized). Outpatient treatment rose 1987–2007, almost all via antidepressants (~75% of treated patients); psychotherapy share declined.

### Medications

**Antidepressants — four classes.** No major differences in effectiveness; ~**50%** benefit, ~half of those reach remission (60%–70% among completers). Meta-analysis (Fournier 2010): relatively **ineffective vs placebo for mild–moderate depression**; clear advantage only in **severe** depression.
- **SSRIs** (first choice): block **presynaptic reuptake of serotonin**. **Fluoxetine (Prozac)** best known. Concern about **suicidality in adolescents** is justified → **FDA warning**; yet higher-SSRI regions had lower suicide rates (correlational). Side effects: agitation, **sexual dysfunction (50%–75%)**, insomnia, GI upset. Others: citalopram (Celexa), escitalopram (Lexapro), fluvoxamine (Luvox), paroxetine (Paxil), sertraline (Zoloft).
- **Mixed reuptake inhibitors:** **venlafaxine (Effexor)** blocks norepinephrine + serotonin; bupropion (Wellbutrin); duloxetine.
- **MAO inhibitors:** block the enzyme MAO. As effective as tricyclics, somewhat fewer side effects, relatively better for **atypical** features — but **dangerous with tyramine** (cheese, red wine, beer → hypertensive crisis/death) and many drug interactions → used only when others fail.
- **Tricyclics** (imipramine/Tofranil, amitriptyline/Elavil): down-regulate norepinephrine. Side effects: blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation, weight gain, sexual dysfunction; **lethal in overdose** → caution in suicidal patients. ~40% stop the drug.
- **St. John's Wort (hypericum):** popular in Europe; large NIH trial found **no benefit over placebo**.
- **Treatment-resistant depression:** **STAR*D** trial — adding/switching a second drug → 20%–30% remission; third drug 10%–20%. Continue meds **6–12 months** beyond episode to prevent relapse. Drugs effective with adults **not necessarily effective with children**; **fluoxetine** has efficacy in adolescents (esp. + CBT).

**Lithium (mood stabilizer):** common salt; narrow therapeutic window (toxicity, lowered thyroid, weight gain). Key advantage: **also prevents/treats manic episodes** → **gold standard for bipolar.** ~**50%** respond initially. Alternatives: anticonvulsants (**carbamazepine, valproate/Divalproex**), calcium-channel blockers (verapamil). **Valproate** is now the most commonly prescribed mood stabilizer (equally effective, incl. rapid cycling) but **less effective than lithium at preventing suicide** (completed suicide **2.7× higher** on valproate). ~70% relapse over 5 years even on lithium; **noncompliance** is a major problem (patients miss the "high"). Adding an antidepressant to a mood stabilizer gives **no advantage** (Sachs 2007).

### Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) & Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)
- **ECT:** most controversial treatment after psychosurgery; now safe (anesthesia + muscle relaxants), **6–10 treatments every other day**. For severe/psychotic depression not responding to meds, ~**50%** benefit; **relapse ~60%+** → needs follow-up med/psychotherapy. Mechanism unknown (↑ serotonin, blocks stress hormones, promotes hippocampal neurogenesis). Side effects: short-term memory loss/confusion (some long-term). Appropriate for **acutely suicidal/psychotic inpatients** who can't wait weeks.
- **TMS:** magnetic coil → localized electromagnetic pulse; no anesthesia, side effect = headaches. Effective, but **ECT clearly more effective for severe/treatment-resistant psychotic depression** (TMS ≈ more comparable to medication).
- Other developmental approaches: **vagus nerve stimulation** (FDA-approved, weak results) and **deep brain stimulation** (electrodes in limbic system; experimental).

### Psychological Treatments for Depression
- **Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (Beck):** identify automatic thoughts → recognize cognitive errors → substitute realistic appraisals → target underlying schemas. **Socratic** method, collaborative, highly trained therapists (Beck & Irene dialogue illustrates eliciting automatic thoughts/hopelessness).
  - Related: **CBASP** (Cognitive-Behavioral Analysis System of Psychotherapy — McCullough; for chronic depression), **Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)** (effective preventing relapse, esp. with ≥3 prior episodes).
  - **Behavioral activation** (Jacobson): increase activities, face avoided cues; as/more effective than cognitive approaches. **Programmed aerobic exercise** ≈ medication (Zoloft) and **better at preventing relapse** (Babyak 2000); increases hippocampal neurogenesis.
- **Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)** (Weissman & Klerman): structured, 15–20 sessions; resolves current interpersonal problems in 4 areas: **role disputes, grief/loss, role transitions (acquiring relationships), social-skill deficits.** Dispute stages: **negotiation → impasse → resolution.** Marital therapy applies to ~50% of depressed patients (esp. women).
- **Efficacy:** psychotherapy and medication are **equally effective** post-treatment, both > placebo. ~50%+ benefit vs ~30% control. CBT > switching antidepressants in adolescents who failed an SSRI. **No difference by severity.** IPT especially valuable for **postpartum/pregnant** women avoiding meds (60% recovered).
- **Prevention (IOM 3 types):** **universal** (everyone), **selected** (at-risk by factors), **indicated** (already mildly symptomatic). Teaching cognitive/social skills can **"psychologically immunize"** at-risk youth. CBT prevention worked **only when the parent was not currently depressed** → treat the whole family. Preventing depression is a **global public-health priority**.

### Combined Treatments
- For **chronic (persistent)** major depression (Keller 2000): each treatment alone ~48% response vs **73% combined** (nefazodone + CBASP). Consensus: combination gives some advantage but is expensive → favor a **sequential strategy** (start one, switch/add if unsatisfactory). (Contrast Ch 5: no combination advantage for anxiety.)

### Preventing Relapse
- Drugs work faster; **CBT/IPT give enduring protection** against relapse/recurrence. Fava: CBT for residual symptoms → 35% relapse vs 70% clinical management (40% vs 90% at 6 years). Hollon (2005): in year 2 off all treatment, **17.5% recurrence (prior CT) vs 56.3% (prior medication)** → prior cognitive therapy has an **enduring effect**.

### Psychological Treatments for Bipolar Disorder
- Medication (esp. lithium) necessary, but psychological treatment manages interpersonal/practical problems and **increases medication compliance.**
- **Interpersonal & Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT)** (Ellen Frank): regulates **circadian rhythms** (eating, sleep, schedules) + interpersonal coping → longer time without new episodes.
- **Family-Focused Treatment (FFT)** (Miklowitz): family tension predicts relapse; FFT + medication → only **35% relapse vs 54%** comparison, longer survival to relapse (73.5 weeks). Intensive psychotherapy (up to 30 sessions) > usual care for **bipolar depression** (which antidepressants treat poorly).

## Suicide

### Statistics
- ~**40,000** suicides/year in the U.S. Per 1,000 world population/year: **4 die by suicide, 7 make plans, 20 seriously consider it.** Officially the **11th** leading cause of death in the U.S. (actual likely 2–3× reported). Worldwide kills more than homicide or HIV/AIDS.
- **Overwhelmingly a White phenomenon** (Native Americans a major exception — far higher; Apache ~4× national average). **Rises dramatically from adolescence** (1.29/100,000 ages 10–14 → 12.35 ages 20–24); 3rd leading cause of death for teens; 5th for ages 5–14. **Highest in the elderly** (medical illness + lost social support + depression).
- **Males complete suicide ~4× more** than females (everywhere except **China**, where rural women predominate — no stigma, available pesticides). **Females attempt ~3× more often**; nonlethal thoughts/plans/attempts 40%–60% higher in women.
- Four indices: **completed suicide, suicidal ideation (9.2%), suicidal plans (3.1%), suicidal attempts (2.7% lifetime).** Distinguish **"attempters"** (intent to die) from **"gesturers"** (intent to influence/communicate, not die). Adolescent thoughts:attempts ratio ≈ 3:1 to 6:1. Methods: males → violent (guns, hanging); females → less violent (overdose). **No** ethnic/racial differences in ideation/plans/attempts despite higher White completion.

> **DSM-5-TR note:** DSM-5-TR added **Suicidal Behavior** and **Nonsuicidal Self-Injury (NSSI)** as coded conditions (in "Other Conditions That May Be a Focus of Clinical Attention" / Section III), formalizing the attempter-vs-gesturer/NSSI distinction the chapter describes informally.

### Causes
- **Durkheim's social types:** **altruistic** (formalized/approved, e.g., hara-kiri), **egoistic** (loss of social supports — e.g., isolated elderly), **anomic** (sudden disruption, e.g., loss of high-prestige job; *anomie* = lost/confused), **fatalistic** (loss of control over destiny — e.g., Heaven's Gate cult). Only **13%** of serious attempters had an adequate social network.
- **Freud:** suicide = unconscious **hostility turned inward**.

### Risk Factors
- **Method of study:** **psychological autopsy** (Shneidman) — reconstruct the profile via interviews with family/friends.
- **Family history:** strongest predictor among depressed patients was **family history of suicide**; offspring of attempters had **6×** the risk. Adoption studies → some **genetic** contribution (possibly the trait of **impulsivity**, not independent of depression genetics).
- **Neurobiology:** **low serotonin** linked to impulsivity, overreaction, and violent suicide attempts.
- **Existing disorders:** **>80%** of suicides have a psychological disorder (mood, substance use, impulse control). **60% of suicides (75% of adolescent suicides)** associated with a mood disorder — but depression and suicide are still **independent**. **Hopelessness** strongly predicts suicide (even without depression). **Interpersonal theory of suicide** (Van Orden/Joiner): **perceived burdensomeness + thwarted belonging** → hopelessness → suicide.
  - **Alcohol** involved in 25%–50% of suicides (esp. students/adolescents; ~1/3 of teen suicides intoxicated). **Combinations** of disorders (depression + impulse control + anxiety/agitation) raise risk more than any single one. **Sensation-seeking** and **past attempts** are strong predictors (deliberate self-harm patients: 30× the general rate). **Borderline PD** + depression is "particularly deadly" (~10% die "by mistake").
- **Stressful life events:** the most important risk factor = a severe event experienced as **shameful or humiliating** (failure, arrest, rejection); physical/sexual abuse; natural disasters (suicide +13.8% after floods, +31% after hurricanes, +62.9% first year after earthquake).
- **Threshold model** (Blumenthal & Kupfer): predisposing risk factors + precipitating humiliating event, offset by **protective factors** (cognitive flexibility, strong social support, hopefulness, treatment).

### Is Suicide Contagious?
- Suicides cluster, especially among teens (~**5%** of teen suicides are imitation); media coverage correlates with increases. Causes: romanticization, detailed method reporting, omission of failed-attempt consequences. Not "contagious" in the infectious sense — rather, **shared stress affects multiple vulnerable individuals**. Intervene immediately in schools after a suicide.

### Treatment
- Predicting suicide is uncertain. **Ask directly** — screening does **not** increase suicidal thoughts (Gould: no risk increase in 1,000 screened students). Assess **(1) suicidal desire** (ideation, hopelessness, burdensomeness, feeling trapped), **(2) suicidal capability** (past attempts, high anxiety/rage, available means), **(3) suicidal intent** (plan, expressed intent, preparatory behavior) — all three → **immediate action**.
- Higher risk: detailed plan (time/place/method), putting affairs in order, more lethal/violent method, precautions against discovery.
- **Implicit measures** (Cha/Nock Stroop death–self association) predicted attempts **6×** better than patient or clinician predictions.
- Clinical steps: **no-suicide contract**; if refused or high risk → **immediate hospitalization (even involuntary)**; treat underlying stressors and disorders.
- **Prevention programs:** universal curriculum programs are **generally not effective**; **targeted** programs work better. **Limiting access to lethal means** may be the most powerful element (Mann 2005). **Hotlines** useful if backed by professionals. **Cognitive therapy** (10 sessions) for recent attempters **cut repeat attempts by 50%** over 18 months (24% vs 42%). **DBT** (Linehan) for borderline-related impulsive suicidal behavior.

### DSM Controversy: Grief vs Major Depression
- DSM-IV had a **bereavement exclusion** (no MDD diagnosis within 2 months of a loss unless severe). **DSM-5 dropped it** because: depression triggered by loss is indistinguishable from depression triggered by other stressors; same vulnerability factors apply; removing it wouldn't greatly increase treatment numbers. **Controversial** — critics feared pathologizing normal grief / boosting antidepressant prescriptions.

> **ICD-11 note:** ICD-11 keeps **single episode depressive disorder, recurrent depressive disorder, dysthymic disorder, mixed depressive and anxiety disorder, bipolar type I, bipolar type II, and cyclothymic disorder.** Key contrasts with DSM-5/5-TR: (1) ICD-11 has a dedicated **Mixed depressive and anxiety disorder** category (DSM uses an anxious-distress specifier instead); (2) ICD-11 formally includes **Prolonged Grief Disorder** (aligned with DSM-5-TR); (3) ICD-11 does **NOT** include PMDD as a mental disorder — it is classified under genitourinary conditions; (4) ICD-11 includes **Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder–equivalent** content but historically classified severe childhood irritability differently; (5) ICD-11 places mood disorders on more **dimensional severity** lines and uses qualifiers ("with prominent anxiety symptoms," "with panic attacks," "with melancholia," "with seasonal pattern").

## Exam-High-Yield Recap
- **2-week** MDE / **2-year** persistent depressive disorder / **1-week** mania / **4-day** hypomania / **≥4 episodes/yr** = rapid cycling / **2-year** cyclothymia.
- **Double depression** = dysthymia + superimposed MDEs (worst long-term course). **Bipolar I** = mania; **Bipolar II** = hypomania + MDE, never mania.
- New in DSM-5: **PMDD, DMDD,** anxious-distress and mixed-features specifiers, merged persistent depressive disorder, dropped bereavement exclusion. New in **DSM-5-TR: Prolonged Grief Disorder.**
- Etiology = **diathesis–stress**: genes (~40% women/~20% men) + HPA/cortisol + low serotonin (permissive hypothesis) + learned helplessness/hopelessness + Beck's cognitive triad + humiliating loss + low social support; bipolar adds **BAS hypersensitivity** + circadian disruption + distinct mania genetics.
- Treatment: SSRIs first-line (weak vs placebo in mild cases); **lithium = gold standard for bipolar** and best for suicide prevention; **ECT** for severe/psychotic/acutely suicidal; **CBT/IPT** equal to meds acutely but **enduring relapse protection**; combination best for chronic depression. **FFT/IPSRT** for bipolar.
- Suicide: ~40,000/yr US; males complete 4× / females attempt 3×; >80% have a disorder; **hopelessness + perceived burdensomeness/thwarted belonging** predict; **limiting access to lethal means** + **cognitive therapy** + **DBT** are key interventions; **Durkheim's 4 types**: altruistic, egoistic, anomic, fatalistic.

## Supplement: Psychodynamic depression theories (NIMHANS high-yield, not in Barlow)

> **Why this is here:** NIMHANS has directly tested **Blatt** and **Spitz**. Barlow & Durand is an integrative/biopsychosocial text and does not cover these psychodynamic models, so they are added here as a high-yield overlay. Treat them as exam additions, not Barlow's own content.

### Sidney Blatt — two-polarities model of depression
- Blatt argued depression is **not one thing**; it splits by **what the person is depressed about**, mapping onto two lifelong personality "polarities": **relatedness** vs **self-definition**.
- **Anaclitic (dependent) depression** — disturbance of **relatedness**. Core themes: helplessness, fear of **abandonment**, loneliness, wanting to be **cared for, loved, nurtured**. Feels **depleted**, not bad. Vulnerable to **loss** of a relationship. ("Anaclitic" = leaning on another for support.)
- **Introjective (self-critical) depression** — disturbance of **self-definition**. Core themes: harsh **superego**, **guilt**, worthlessness, failure to live up to standards, fear of **disapproval**. Feels **bad/inadequate**, not unloved. Vulnerable to **failure** and loss of self-worth.
- The two polarities (relatedness vs self-definition) parallel other dual models: **Bowlby** (attachment vs autonomy), **Beck** (sociotropy vs autonomy), **Arieti & Bemporad** (dominant other vs dominant goal).
- **Clinical use:** anaclitic patients respond better to the **relationship** in therapy (supportive, the bond itself heals); introjective patients respond better to **insight/interpretation** addressing self-criticism.

### René Spitz — anaclitic depression in infants / hospitalism
- **Anaclitic depression (Spitz):** the depressive syndrome in **infants (~6-18 months)** separated from the mother for a prolonged period after a normal early bond, e.g. institutional care.
- Sequence: apprehension/weepiness → withdrawal, refusal of contact, **weight loss**, insomnia, developmental slowing → frozen, **expressionless** stupor if separation continues.
- **Reversible** if the mother (or a substitute) returns within ~3-5 months; beyond that it can progress to **hospitalism** (severe, sometimes irreversible physical and developmental deterioration, raised mortality in institutionalised infants despite adequate physical care).
- Spitz's films/observations were foundational evidence for **maternal deprivation** effects and fed into **Bowlby's attachment theory**.
- **Exam contrast:** Spitz's "anaclitic depression" = **infant** syndrome of maternal separation; Blatt's "anaclitic depression" = **adult** depressive subtype centred on dependency/relatedness. Same word, two different referents; NIMHANS likes this distinction.
