# Ch 12: Personality Disorders

This chapter covers the **10 DSM-5 personality disorders** grouped into three clusters (A = odd/eccentric, B = dramatic/emotional/erratic, C = anxious/fearful), plus the cross-cutting issues that make this category controversial: categorical vs. dimensional models, the five-factor model, gender bias in diagnosis, comorbidity, and the failed push to dimensionalize the whole category in DSM-5. For the exam, master each disorder's clinical description, DSM-5(-TR) criteria, causes (integrative bio-psycho-social), and treatment; know the special bodies of research on **antisocial PD / psychopathy** (Cleckley/Hare, arousal & fearlessness theories, conduct disorder) and **borderline PD** (triple vulnerability, DBT). The DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 overlays below mark where the current 2026 nosology differs from this 2015 book text.

## An Overview of Personality Disorders

### What a Personality Disorder Is
- A **personality disorder** = a persistent pattern of **emotions, cognitions, and behavior** that results in enduring emotional distress for the person and/or others, and may cause difficulties with work and relationships (APA, 2013).
- **Chronic**: unlike most disorders, they do **not come and go**; they originate in **childhood** and continue throughout **adulthood** (Widiger, 2012). They **pervade every aspect** of a person's life (employment, relationships, where one lives).
- The affected person may feel **no subjective distress**; instead **others** often feel the distress, especially with **antisocial PD** (blatant disregard for others' rights, no remorse). In such cases someone *other* than the patient must judge whether functional impairment exists.
- **DSM-5 lists 10 specific personality disorders.**
- **Countertransference** (Freud's term for therapist emotions evoked by clients) tends to be **negative** for PD clients, especially **Cluster A** (odd/eccentric) and **Cluster B** (dramatic/emotional/erratic) (Liebman & Burnette, 2013). Therapists must guard against letting personal feelings interfere.
- **Historical note (axis system):** Prior to DSM-5, most disorders sat on **Axis I** of DSM-IV-TR; personality disorders sat on a separate **Axis II** because their traits were seen as more ingrained, inflexible, and less modifiable. **DSM-5 eliminated the multiaxial system**, listing PDs alongside all other disorders.

### Categorical vs. Dimensional Models
- Core debate: are PDs **extreme versions of normal personality variation (dimensions)** or **qualitatively different ways of relating (categories)** (Widiger & Trull, 2007)? Difference of **degree vs. kind**.
- Analogy: gender/height can be viewed categorically (male/female; tall/short) *or* dimensionally (testosterone level; inches).
- The **DSM is categorical**: you do (**yes**) or do not (**no**) have a disorder; no "somewhat." Convenient but leads clinicians to **reify** disorders (treat them as real "things" like an infection or broken arm).
- **Widiger (1991)** argued a dimensional system has 3 advantages: (1) retains more information about each individual, (2) more flexible (permits both categorical and dimensional differentiation), (3) avoids arbitrary cutoff decisions.
- DSM-5's **alternative model** (in "emerging measures and models," for further study) focuses on a continuum of disturbance in **"self"** (identity, self-direction) and **interpersonal** (empathy, intimacy) functioning.

> ICD-11 note (current nosology): ICD-11 made the dimensional shift DSM-5 *declined* to make. ICD-11 **abolished the discrete categorical personality disorder types entirely** (no more separate "borderline," "antisocial," etc. as standalone diagnoses). Instead a clinician rates **severity** (mild / moderate / severe personality disorder, plus "personality difficulty") and then specifies up to five **trait domains**: Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, and Anankastia. A single **"Borderline pattern" qualifier** was retained largely to preserve treatment links (e.g., DBT). This is the biggest classification divergence in the chapter: DSM-5/DSM-5-TR stayed categorical (10 disorders) while ICD-11 is fully dimensional.

### The Five-Factor Model ("Big Five")
- Most widely accepted dimensional contender, drawn from **normal** personality (McCrae & Costa). Five dimensions:
  - **Extroversion** (talkative, assertive, active vs. silent, passive, reserved)
  - **Agreeableness** (kind, trusting, warm vs. hostile, selfish, mistrustful)
  - **Conscientiousness** (organized, thorough, reliable vs. careless, negligent, unreliable)
  - **Neuroticism** (even-tempered vs. nervous, moody, temperamental)
  - **Openness to experience** (imaginative, curious, creative vs. shallow, imperceptive)
- People rated **high, low, or in between** on each. **Cross-culturally universal** (Hofstede & McCrae, 2004), though with cultural variation (e.g., Austrian/Swiss/Dutch highest on Openness; Danes/Malaysians/Telugu Indians lowest).

### Personality Disorder Clusters (DSM-5)
- **Cluster A — Odd or Eccentric:** Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal.
- **Cluster B — Dramatic, Emotional, or Erratic:** Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, Narcissistic.
- **Cluster C — Anxious or Fearful:** Avoidant, Dependent, Obsessive-Compulsive.

### Statistics & Development
- **~1 in 10 (10%)** U.S. adults may have a diagnosable PD (Lenzenweger et al., 2007); **worldwide ~6%** of adults have at least one PD (Huang et al., 2009).
- Many do **not seek help** on their own → prevalence data are difficult and variable.
- PDs can **remit over time** but may be **replaced by other PDs** (a person can shift from one PD diagnosis to another over years) (Torgersen, 2012). Course is poorly understood for ~half the disorders.
- **Borderline PD:** turbulent, unstable relationships; frequent hospitalizations; severe depression; suicidal gestures. **~10% attempt suicide, ~6% complete suicide** (Skodol & Gunderson, 2008). Symptoms gradually improve if they survive into their **30s** (Zanarini et al., 2006).
- **Antisocial PD:** chronic disregard for others' rights; lying/manipulation; some **"burn out" after ~age 40** with fewer criminal activities.

### Gender Differences
- Men with a PD tend toward **aggressive, structured, self-assertive, detached** traits; women toward **submissive, emotional, insecure** traits (Torgersen, 2012).
- **Antisocial PD** more common in **males**; **dependent PD** more common in **females**.
- Historically **histrionic and borderline** were diagnosed more in **women**, but recent general-population studies suggest roughly **equal** rates → raises the **gender bias** question.
- **Classic Ford & Widiger (1989) study:** identical case histories sent to clinicians, sex varied. An antisocial case labeled **male** got the correct diagnosis; the *same* case labeled **female** was misdiagnosed as **histrionic**. Histrionic diagnosis was more likely when labeled a woman → clinicians incorrectly over-diagnosed women with histrionic PD.
- **Kaplan (1983):** histrionic PD features (overdramatization, vanity, seductiveness, overconcern with appearance) embody the Western **"stereotypical female"** → may be biased against women. Note the absence of any "macho" personality (Mosher & Sirkin, 1984) disorder in the DSM.
- **Bias can occur at two stages** (Widiger & Spitzer, 1991): **criterion gender bias** (biased criteria) vs. **assessment gender bias** (biased measures/use). Criteria themselves don't show strong bias; clinicians may apply their own bias.

### Comorbidity
- Summing prevalence rates suggests ~25% affected, but true figure is closer to **10%** — discrepancy is because people are **diagnosed with more than one PD**.
- **Comorbidity** historically = having multiple diseases (Caron & Rutter, 1991); debated for psychological disorders due to overlap.
- **Zimmerman, Rothschild & Chelminski (2005):** 859 psychiatric outpatients; high diagnostic overlap (odds ratios). E.g., borderline PD overlaps with paranoid, schizotypal, antisocial, narcissistic, avoidant, dependent.

### Personality Disorders Under Study (not in DSM-5)
- **Sadistic personality disorder** — pleasure from inflicting pain on others (Morey et al., 2007).
- **Passive-aggressive personality disorder** — defiant, refuses to cooperate, undermines authority (Wetzler & Jose, 2012).
- Both remain controversial → **excluded from DSM-5**.

> DSM-5-TR note: Personality disorder **criteria are unchanged in DSM-5-TR** from DSM-5 — the categorical 10-disorder system and the dimensional Alternative Model (Section III) both carried over without revision. DSM-5-TR's general updates (e.g., addition of Prolonged Grief Disorder, "intellectual developmental disorder" terminology, suicidal-behaviour/nonsuicidal-self-injury conditions for further study, updated ICD-10-CM codes) belong to other chapters and **do not alter this chapter's diagnostic criteria.**

## Cluster A Personality Disorders (Odd / Eccentric)
- Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal share features resembling **psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia**.
- **Grouping schema (Siever):** *positive* psychotic-like symptoms (ideas of reference, magical thinking, perceptual distortions) vs. *negative* (social isolation, poor rapport, constricted affect):
  - **Paranoid** — positive **Yes**, negative **Yes**
  - **Schizoid** — positive **No**, negative **Yes**
  - **Schizotypal** — positive **Yes**, negative **No**

### Paranoid Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** pervasive, **unjustified distrust and suspiciousness**; assume others are out to harm/trick them; won't confide in others. Even unrelated events read as personal attacks (a barking dog, a delayed flight). Sensitive to criticism; excessive need for **autonomy**. Increased risk of **suicide attempts and violence**; poor quality of life. (Case: **Jake** — CIA/microwave conspiracy theories, diagnosed paranoid PD + major depression.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Paranoid PD:** A pervasive distrust and suspiciousness such that motives are interpreted as malevolent, by early adulthood, ≥4 of: (1) suspects others are exploiting/harming/deceiving; (2) preoccupied with doubts about loyalty/trustworthiness of friends; (3) reluctant to confide for fear info used against them; (4) reads hidden demeaning/threatening meanings into benign remarks; (5) persistently bears grudges; (6) perceives attacks on character not apparent to others, reacts angrily; (7) recurrent unjustified suspicions about fidelity of partner. B: not exclusively during schizophrenia/psychotic/mood-with-psychotic disorder; not due to another medical condition. Specifier "**(premorbid)**" if criteria met before schizophrenia onset.

- **Causes:** limited biological evidence; slightly more common among **relatives of people with schizophrenia** (weak association); strong role for **genetics** (Kendler et al., 2006). Psychological: retrospective reports of **early mistreatment/trauma** (caution — recall bias). Cognitive **schemas**: "People are malevolent and deceptive," "They'll attack you if they get the chance," "You can be okay only if you stay on your toes." **Cultural factors:** prisoners, refugees, people with hearing impairments, older adults more susceptible (Rogler, 2007).
- **Treatment:** mistrust → unlikely to seek help; usually present in a **crisis** or for anxiety/depression. **Therapeutic alliance / trust** is the crucial first step. **Cognitive therapy** to counter mistaken assumptions. No confirmed treatment significantly improves their lives; only **11%** of therapists thought these clients would stay in therapy long enough to be helped (Quality Assurance Project, 1990).

### Schizoid Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** pattern of **detachment from social relationships** + **restricted range of emotional expression**; aloof, cold, indifferent; neither desire nor enjoy closeness (incl. sexual); unaffected by praise or criticism. Term coined by **Bleuler (1924)**. Some are actually sensitive to others' opinions but unable to express it. **Homelessness** prevalent. Beck & Freeman: they "consider themselves to be observers rather than participants." Share negative symptoms (social isolation, poor rapport, **constricted affect**) with paranoid PD but **lack** the unusual thought processes (no ideas of reference). (Case: **Mr. Z.** — Antarctica scientist, solitary, chess his only contact, indifferent to colleagues' anger.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Schizoid PD:** Pervasive detachment from social relationships + restricted emotional expression, ≥4 of: (1) neither desires nor enjoys close relationships (incl. family); (2) almost always chooses solitary activities; (3) little/no interest in sexual experiences with another; (4) takes pleasure in few activities; (5) lacks close friends/confidants other than first-degree relatives; (6) indifferent to praise/criticism; (7) emotional coldness, detachment, flattened affect. B: not exclusively during schizophrenia/psychotic/mood-with-psychotic disorder or **autism spectrum disorder**; not due to medical condition. Specifier "(premorbid)."

- **Causes:** little empirical research. **Childhood shyness** a possible precursor; abuse/neglect reported. Parents of children with **autism** are more likely to have schizoid PD (Constantino et al., 2009) → possible shared biological dysfunction interacting with early learning.
- **Treatment:** rarely seek help except in a crisis (depression, job loss). Therapists point out value of relationships; may need to be **taught the emotions felt by others** (empathy). **Social skills training** + **role-playing**; identify a supportive **social network**. Outcome research limited.

### Schizotypal Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** socially isolated (like schizoid) **plus** odd/unusual behavior, suspiciousness, odd beliefs. **Psychotic-like (but not psychotic)** symptoms: **ideas of reference** (but can reality-test, e.g., acknowledge the bus passengers probably aren't talking about them), **magical thinking** (clairvoyance, telepathy), **unusual perceptual experiences** (sensing a presence), odd speech, paranoid thinking, little emotion, odd dress/behavior. **DSM-5 lists schizotypal under BOTH personality disorders AND schizophrenia spectrum disorders.** Children who later develop it: passive, unengaged, hypersensitive to criticism (Olin et al., 1997). **Cultural caution:** religious/spiritual practices (speaking in tongues, voodoo, mind reading) can cause **misdiagnosis**. (Case: **Mr. S.** — avoided foods "contaminated by machine," "opened my third eye," vitamin deficiency.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Schizotypal PD:** Pervasive social/interpersonal deficits (acute discomfort with + reduced capacity for close relationships) plus cognitive/perceptual distortions and eccentric behavior, ≥5 of: (1) ideas of reference (excluding delusions); (2) odd beliefs/magical thinking inconsistent with subcultural norms; (3) unusual perceptual experiences incl. bodily illusions; (4) odd thinking and speech (vague, circumstantial, metaphorical, overelaborate, stereotyped); (5) suspiciousness/paranoid ideation; (6) inappropriate or constricted affect; (7) odd/eccentric/peculiar behavior or appearance; (8) lack of close friends/confidants other than first-degree relatives; (9) excessive social anxiety that does not diminish with familiarity and is associated with paranoid fears. B: not exclusively during schizophrenia/psychotic/mood-with-psychotic disorder or autism spectrum disorder. Specifier "(premorbid)."

- **Causes:** historically **"schizotype"** = predisposed to schizophrenia (Meehl, 1962; Rado). Viewed as one **phenotype** of a schizophrenia **genotype** — those with "schizophrenia genes" but relatively low biological/environmental stress get the milder schizotypal PD. Family/twin/adoption studies show **increased prevalence among relatives of people with schizophrenia**. Environment matters: schizotypal symptoms strongly linked to **childhood maltreatment** in men, and to **PTSD symptoms** in women (Berenbaum et al., 2008). Cognitive testing → mild-moderate **memory/learning** deficits (left-hemisphere damage); MRI → generalized brain abnormalities.
- **Treatment:** **30%–50%** of those seeking help also meet criteria for **major depressive disorder**; treat with depression treatments. Combination approaches — **antipsychotic medication + community treatment + social skills training** — reduced symptoms or **postponed onset of schizophrenia** (Nordentoft et al., 2006). Treating young symptomatic people with antipsychotics + CBT is a **promising schizophrenia-prevention strategy**.

> ICD-11 note: ICD-11 classifies **schizotypal disorder under "Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders,"** NOT as a personality disorder at all — consistent with viewing it as part of the schizophrenia spectrum. (DSM-5/DSM-5-TR double-list it under both personality disorders and the schizophrenia spectrum.)

## Cluster B Personality Disorders (Dramatic / Emotional / Erratic)

### Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD)
- **Clinical description:** long history of **failing to comply with social norms** and **violating the rights of others**; aggressive, irresponsible, impulsive, deceitful; **no remorse**. Hare: "social predators who charm, manipulate, and ruthlessly plow their way through life... completely lacking in conscience and empathy." **Substance abuse in 60%** (Taylor & Lang, 2006). Long-term outcome poor regardless of gender. First identified medically by **Philippe Pinel (1801)** as **"manie sans délire"** (mania without delirium — impulsive rages, no reasoning deficit); ancient descriptions trace to Mesopotamia ~670 B.C. Other historical labels: moral insanity, egopathy, sociopathy, psychopathy. (Case: **Ryan** — thrill seeker, no genuine remorse, conned parents repeatedly, loved PCP, diagnosed ASPD in his 20s.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Antisocial PD:** A: pervasive disregard for and violation of others' rights since **age 15**, ≥3 of: (1) failure to conform to social norms re lawful behavior (repeated arrestable acts); (2) deceitfulness (lying, aliases, conning); (3) impulsivity/failure to plan; (4) irritability and aggressiveness (repeated fights/assaults); (5) reckless disregard for safety of self/others; (6) consistent irresponsibility (work, financial obligations); (7) lack of remorse. B: individual is **≥18**. C: evidence of **conduct disorder with onset before age 15**. D: not exclusively during schizophrenia or bipolar disorder.

- **Psychopathy vs. ASPD (defining criteria):**
  - **Cleckley (1941):** 16 major characteristics ("Cleckley criteria"), mostly **personality traits**.
  - **Hare's Psychopathy Checklist–Revised (PCL-R):** 20 items; high scores = psychopathy. Six listed: (1) glibness/superficial charm; (2) grandiose sense of self-worth; (3) pathological lying; (4) conning/manipulative; (5) lack of remorse/guilt; (6) callous/lack of empathy.
  - Cleckley/Hare focus on **underlying traits**; earlier DSM criteria focused almost entirely on **observable behaviors** (easier to assess reliably). **DSM-5 moved closer to trait-based** criteria (callousness, manipulativeness, deceitfulness, like Hare's PCL-R) — but research suggests this **reduced diagnostic reliability** (Regier et al., 2013).
  - **Successful / subclinical psychopaths:** some psychopaths are **not criminals** and function successfully (politics, business, entertainment). **Widom (1977)** recruited subclinical psychopaths via newspaper ads ("charming, aggressive, carefree... good at looking after number one") — similar low empathy/socialization as imprisoned psychopaths, but many had stable jobs and stayed out of prison.
  - **IQ as protective:** White, Moffitt & Silva (1989) followed ~1,000 children from age 5; of high-risk 5-year-olds, **16%** had run-ins with the law by 15 and **84%** did not — the at-risk kids who got in trouble had **lower IQs** (higher IQ may protect or help avoid getting caught).
- **Conduct Disorder (CD):** DSM-5 diagnosis for children violating societal norms; **precursor** to adult ASPD. Subtypes: **childhood-onset** (≥1 criterion before age 10) and **adolescent-onset** (none before age 10), plus **"with limited prosocial emotions"** (the chapter calls it the new **callous-unemotional presentation** — added in DSM-5 to flag psychopathy-like youth). Most often diagnosed in **boys**. Adult ASPD likelihood increases if child had **both CD and ADHD** (Biederman et al.; Moffitt et al.).

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Conduct Disorder:** A: repetitive, persistent pattern violating others' basic rights or major societal norms, ≥3 of **15 criteria** in past 12 months (≥1 in past 6 months), across four categories: **Aggression to people/animals** (bullies; initiates fights; used a weapon; cruel to people; cruel to animals; stolen confronting a victim; forced sexual activity); **Destruction of property** (fire setting; destroying property); **Deceitfulness/theft** (broken into house/building/car; lies/cons; stolen without confronting victim — shoplifting/forgery); **Serious rule violations** (stays out at night before age 13; runs away overnight ≥2x; truant before age 13). B: clinically significant impairment. C: if ≥18, criteria for ASPD not met. Specify **childhood-onset / adolescent-onset / unspecified onset** and severity **mild / moderate / severe**.

- **Genetic influences:** family/twin/adoption studies support genetic influence on ASPD and criminality. **Crowe (1974):** adopted offspring of felon mothers had higher arrest/conviction/antisocial rates — *and* those who became criminals had spent **more time in interim orphanages** → **gene–environment interaction** (genes matter in the presence of certain environments). **Cadoret et al. (1995):** biological antisocial parents + adoptive family chronic stress → greater conduct-problem risk. **Endophenotype** approach: hunting genes for **serotonin/dopamine** levels and the lack of anxiety/fear — i.e., genes for fearlessness, aggressiveness, impulsivity, lack of remorse, not for "ASPD" per se.
- **Neurobiological influences — arousal theories:**
  - **Underarousal hypothesis:** psychopaths have abnormally **low cortical arousal**; seek stimulation to boost it (relates to the **Yerkes-Dodson** inverted-U: too-high or too-low arousal → poor performance/negative affect). Raine, Venables & Williams (1990): future criminals showed **lower skin conductance, lower resting heart rate, more slow-frequency brain waves** = low arousal.
  - **Fearlessness hypothesis (Lykken):** psychopaths have a **higher threshold for fear**; things that frighten others have little effect.
  - **Gray's model:** three brain systems — **Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS)** (stops/slows behavior under impending punishment/nonreward; septohippocampal; noradrenergic/serotonergic; produces anxiety), the **reward system** (approach to reward; mesolimbic dopamine "pleasure pathway"), and **fight/flight**. An **imbalance** (weak BIS, dominant reward system) makes fear/anxiety less apparent and rewards more salient → explains why psychopaths aren't anxious about antisocial acts.
- **Psychological/social dimensions:** Newman, Patterson & Kosson (1987) card-game study — psychopaths **kept playing and losing** even after reward dropped to 0% (failure to abandon an unattainable goal once a reward is set). **Patterson's coercive family process:** parents give in to a child's aversive behavior → short-term gains for both, long-term escalation of aggression; combines with genetics, parental depression, poor monitoring.
- **Developmental influences:** antisocial behavior **declines markedly around age 40** (**Hare, McPherson & Forth, 1988** — psychopath conviction rates constant to ~40, then drop sharply; nonpsychopath rates constant 16–45). Reason unknown.
- **Integrative model:** genetic vulnerability → neurotransmitter/neurohormone differences (dopamine, serotonin, **cortisol**) → traits (callousness, impulsivity, aggressiveness). **Gao et al. (2010):** poor **fear conditioning at age 3** predicted criminal record at 23; **amygdala** deficits → can't recognize threat cues → fearlessness. Bio factors interact with early adversity, difficult temperament, alienation of good role models, school dropout.
- **Prevention:** aggressive behavior is remarkably **stable**; school/preschool programs + **parent training for toddlers (1½–2½)** reduce aggression and improve social competence — given poor adult treatment outcomes, **prevention is the best approach**.
- **Treatment:** rarely self-identify as needing help; manipulative even with therapists → most clinicians **pessimistic**; few documented successes. CBT reduced violence 5 years post-treatment in one study, BUT success was **negatively correlated** with PCL-R "selfish, callous, remorseless" trait scores. Most common child treatment = **parent training** (praise/privileges to reduce problems). Risk factors for treatment failure/dropout: family dysfunction, socioeconomic disadvantage, family stress, parental antisocial history, severe CD.

### Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)
- **Clinical description:** tumultuous lives; **unstable moods and relationships**, poor **self-image**, chronic **emptiness**, great suicide risk. One of the **most common PDs in clinical settings**; seen in **every culture**; **~1%–2%** of general population. Fear **abandonment** but lack emotional control. **Self-harm** (cutting, burning, punching). **~6% complete suicide** (McGirr et al., 2009). Long-term outlook encouraging: up to **88% achieve remission >10 years** after initial treatment (Zanarini et al., 2006). Intense, rapid swings (anger → depression). Described as **"stably unstable."** Self-injury described as **tension-reducing**. Comorbidity: **~20% major depression, ~40% bipolar**, almost **25% of bulimia** patients have BPD, up to **67%** have a substance use disorder. (Case: **Claire** — self-cut hair/arms to "fill the void," abusive alcoholic father, stormy marriages, suicidal depression.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Borderline PD:** Pervasive pattern of **instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image, and affects, and marked impulsivity**, by early adulthood, ≥5 of: (1) frantic efforts to avoid real/imagined abandonment; (2) unstable, intense relationships alternating between **idealization and devaluation**; (3) identity disturbance (unstable self-image); (4) impulsivity in ≥2 self-damaging areas (spending, sex, substance abuse, reckless driving, binge eating); (5) recurrent suicidal behavior/gestures/threats or self-mutilation; (6) affective instability (intense episodic dysphoria/irritability/anxiety, usually a few hours, rarely >a few days); (7) chronic feelings of emptiness; (8) inappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger; (9) transient stress-related **paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms**.

- **Causes:** more prevalent in families with the disorder; linked with **mood disorders**; higher **MZ > DZ** twin concordance. **Serotonin** genes (low serotonergic activity → mood/impulsivity dysregulation). Neuroimaging → **limbic network** (emotion regulation). Emotional reactivity = central; greater fluctuations/intensity of **negative emotions** (anger, anxiety). **More accurate** than controls at reading morphing facial emotions (Fertuck et al., 2009). Elevated **shame** proneness (Rusch et al., 2007) linked to low self-esteem, low quality of life, anger, self-injury. **Memory bias** (Korfine & Hooley, 2000): remembered disorder-relevant words ("abandon," "suicidal," "emptiness") even when told to forget. Major environmental risk: **early trauma — sexual and physical abuse** (Widom et al., 2009 prospective study: significantly more abused/neglected children developed BPD; girls 2–3x more likely to be sexually abused). BUT abuse is **neither necessary nor sufficient** — some BPD patients report no abuse; trauma also occurs in other disorders.
- **Integrative model (triple vulnerability, borrowed from anxiety):** (1) generalized **biological** vulnerability (genetic emotional reactivity); (2) generalized **psychological** vulnerability (world seen as threatening); (3) **specific psychological** vulnerability from early **trauma/abuse**. Stress + biological over-reactivity + psychological threat-sensitivity → outbursts and suicidal behaviors.
- **Treatment:** unlike ASPD, BPD patients **are distressed and seek treatment** (more than even anxiety/mood disorder patients). **Mood stabilizers** (some anticonvulsants/antipsychotics) help affective disturbance. Treatment complicated by drug abuse, noncompliance, suicide attempts. Flagship treatment: **Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)** by **Marsha Linehan** — treatment-priority hierarchy: (1) **life-threatening/suicidal behaviors**, (2) therapy-interfering behaviors, (3) quality-of-life-interfering behaviors. Weekly individual sessions, emotion identification/regulation, problem solving, PTSD-style reexperiencing of trauma; final stage = trusting one's own responses over others' validation. DBT reduces suicide attempts, dropouts, hospitalizations. Brain-imaging pilot (Schnell & Herpertz, 2007): **amygdala/hippocampus** arousal to upsetting photos improved with successful treatment.

### Histrionic Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** overly **dramatic / theatrical** ("histrionic" = theatrical in manner); exaggerated emotional expression; **vain, self-centered**, uncomfortable when not the center of attention; **seductive** appearance/behavior; constant need for reassurance/approval; impulsive, poor delay of gratification. Cognitive style is **impressionistic** (global, black-and-white); speech **vague, lacking detail, exaggerated**. Diagnosed far more in **women** → possible **overdiagnosis/bias** (Sprock, 2000). (Case: **Pat** — flamboyant, flattering, weekly boyfriends with instant wedding plans, fabricated illnesses, manipulative.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Histrionic PD:** Pervasive **excessive emotionality and attention seeking**, by early adulthood, ≥5 of: (1) uncomfortable when not the center of attention; (2) inappropriate sexually seductive/provocative behavior; (3) rapidly shifting, shallow emotions; (4) uses physical appearance to draw attention; (5) speech excessively impressionistic, lacking detail; (6) self-dramatization, theatricality, exaggerated emotion; (7) suggestible (easily influenced); (8) considers relationships more intimate than they are.

- **Causes:** little research. Greek origin "**hysteria**" (wandering uterus) — but it occurs in men too. **Strong co-occurrence with antisocial PD** (Lilienfeld et al., 1986: ~two-thirds of histrionic also met ASPD criteria) → hypothesis that histrionic and antisocial may be **sex-typed alternative expressions of the same underlying condition** (females → histrionic, males → antisocial). Controversial.
- **Treatment:** little research shows success. Behavioral approaches reward appropriate interactions and **fine attention-getting behavior** (Kass et al., 1972). Therapy focuses on **problematic interpersonal relationships**; show how short-term manipulative gains create long-term costs; teach appropriate ways to meet needs.

### Narcissistic Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** exaggerated, **unreasonable sense of self-importance**; preoccupied with themselves; **lack empathy/compassion**; need constant admiration. **Grandiosity** (exaggerated feelings, fantasies of greatness); expect **special treatment**; **exploit** others; envious and **arrogant** toward other successful people; often **depressed** (fail to meet own expectations). Named for **Narcissus** (Greek youth in love with his reflection). (Case: **Willie** — claimed to run the office, called coworkers "his staff," controlling, blamed others, explosively abusive, diagnosed major depression + NPD.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Narcissistic PD:** Pervasive pattern of **grandiosity (fantasy or behavior), need for admiration, and lack of empathy**, by early adulthood, ≥5 of: (1) grandiose sense of self-importance; (2) preoccupied with fantasies of unlimited success/power/brilliance/beauty/ideal love; (3) believes self **special/unique**, can only associate with high-status others; (4) requires excessive admiration; (5) sense of **entitlement**; (6) interpersonally **exploitative**; (7) lacks empathy; (8) often envious or believes others envy him/her; (9) arrogant, haughty behaviors/attitudes.

- **Causes:** **Kohut (1971, 1977)** — arises from parental **failure to model empathy** early; child stays fixated at a self-centered, grandiose stage and endlessly seeks the ideal person to meet unmet empathic needs. **Lasch (1978), The Culture of Narcissism** — sociological view: rising in Western societies due to short-term hedonism, individualism, competitiveness, success ("me generation"). Reports confirm rising prevalence (Huang et al., 2009), though this may reflect increased research interest.
- **Treatment:** very limited research/success. Cognitive therapy targets grandiosity, hypersensitivity to evaluation, lack of empathy; replace fantasies with attainable day-to-day pleasures; relaxation training to accept criticism; focus on others' feelings. Vulnerable to **severe depression in middle age** → treatment often initiated for depression.

## Cluster C Personality Disorders (Anxious / Fearful)
- Avoidant, Dependent, Obsessive-Compulsive share features with **anxiety disorders**.

### Avoidant Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** extremely **sensitive to others' opinions**; **desire** social relationships but **anxiety** drives avoidance; very **low self-esteem** + fear of rejection → few friendships, dependent on the few they trust. **Millon (1981):** distinguish the **avoidant** person (asocial because interpersonally **anxious/fearful of rejection**) from the **schizoid** person (asocial because **apathetic, flat, uninterested**). Feel chronically rejected; pessimistic about the future. (Case: **Jane** — abused by alcoholic borderline mother, believed herself intrinsically unworthy, discounted positive feedback.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Avoidant PD:** Pervasive **social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation**, by early adulthood, ≥4 of: (1) avoids occupational activities with significant interpersonal contact (fear of criticism/disapproval/rejection); (2) unwilling to get involved unless certain of being liked; (3) restraint in intimate relationships (fear of shame/ridicule); (4) preoccupied with being criticized/rejected; (5) inhibited in new interpersonal situations (feelings of inadequacy); (6) views self as socially inept/unappealing/inferior; (7) unusually reluctant to take risks or try new activities (may be embarrassing).

- **Causes:** related to **subschizophrenia-related disorders** (more common in relatives of people with schizophrenia). Millon: born with **difficult temperament** → parental rejection / insufficient uncritical love → low self-esteem and social alienation. Stravynski et al. (1989): remembered parents as more **rejecting, guilt-engendering, less affectionate**; Meyer & Carver (2000): more childhood isolation/rejection/conflict.
- **Treatment:** relatively **well-researched** (Leahy & McGinn). Behavioral techniques for anxiety + **social skills training** have success; overlaps heavily with **social phobia** treatments. **Therapeutic alliance** an important predictor of success.

### Dependent Personality Disorder
- **Clinical description:** rely on others for **ordinary and important decisions**; unreasonable **fear of abandonment**; **submissive, timid, passive, clinging**; agree with others even against own opinion to avoid rejection. Similar to avoidant in feelings of inadequacy/sensitivity to criticism/need for reassurance, BUT avoidant **avoids** relationships while dependent **clings** to them. **Cultural caution:** in some cultures (e.g., **East Asian Confucianism**) dependence/submission is a valued state. (Case: **Karen** — stayed 10 years in an abusive first marriage, preferred others to make decisions, feared being left alone.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Dependent PD:** Pervasive, **excessive need to be taken care of** → submissive, clinging behavior and fears of separation, by early adulthood, ≥5 of: (1) difficulty making everyday decisions without excessive advice/reassurance; (2) needs others to assume responsibility for most major life areas; (3) difficulty expressing disagreement (fear of loss of support); (4) difficulty initiating projects on own (lack of self-confidence, not motivation); (5) goes to excessive lengths to obtain nurturance (volunteers for unpleasant things); (6) uncomfortable/helpless when alone (fear of being unable to care for self); (7) urgently seeks a new relationship when one ends; (8) unrealistically preoccupied with fears of being left to care for self.

- **Causes & treatment:** socialization normally builds independence. Early **death of a parent / neglect / rejection** may breed fear of abandonment; **genetic influences** also important (Gjerde et al., 2012). Treatment literature mostly descriptive. Patients seem like **ideal patients** (eager to hand responsibility to therapist), but that very **submissiveness** undermines the goal of building independence. Therapy progresses **gradually** toward independent decision-making; guard against the patient becoming overly dependent on the therapist.

### Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder (OCPD)
- **Clinical description:** fixation on doing things **"the right way"**; preoccupation with **details/orderliness/perfectionism** prevents completing tasks; **work-oriented, rigid**, poor interpersonal relationships. **Only distantly related** to obsessive-compulsive *disorder* (OCD) — OCPD patients **lack the obsessions/compulsions** of OCD. Intriguingly linked to some **serial killers** ("masters of control," need to control all aspects of the crime) and some **pedophiles** (brain functioning resembles OCPD; Schiffer et al., 2007), and also to **gifted children** (debilitating perfectionism). (Case: **Daniel** — rigid daily routine, dissertation 4.5 years overdue, marriage threatened.)

> **DSM-5-TR criteria — Obsessive-Compulsive PD:** Pervasive **preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism, and mental/interpersonal control** at the expense of flexibility, openness, efficiency, by early adulthood, ≥4 of: (1) preoccupied with details/rules/lists/order/organization/schedules to the point the main point is lost; (2) perfectionism that interferes with task completion; (3) excessively devoted to work/productivity (excludes leisure/friendships); (4) overconscientious, scrupulous, inflexible about morality/ethics/values; (5) unable to discard worn-out/worthless objects; (6) reluctant to delegate unless others do it exactly his/her way; (7) **miserly** spending style (money hoarded for future catastrophes); (8) rigidity and stubbornness.

- **Causes & treatment:** **weak genetic contribution** (Cloninger & Svakic, 2009); predisposition toward structure + parental reinforcement of conformity/neatness. Therapy attacks the **fears underlying the need for orderliness** (fear of inadequacy → procrastination, rumination); use **relaxation/distraction** to redirect compulsive thoughts. **CBT** (along OCD-treatment lines) appears effective (Svartberg et al., 2004).

> DSM-5-TR note: Cluster C criteria are **unchanged from DSM-5**. Note the persistent naming confusion students must avoid: **OCPD (a personality disorder, ego-syntonic, about perfectionism/control)** is distinct from **OCD (an anxiety-related disorder, ego-dystonic, with true obsessions and compulsions)**.

> ICD-11 note: ICD-11's **Anankastia** trait domain corresponds to the OCPD/Cluster-C anankastic presentation; **Detachment** captures schizoid/avoidant features; **Negative Affectivity** captures dependent/borderline-type emotional features. Under ICD-11 a clinician would not diagnose "avoidant PD" or "OCPD" by name but would rate PD **severity** plus the relevant **trait domain(s)**.

## DSM Controversies: The Battle for the Personality Disorders
- DSM-5 development proposed major changes to this category, most of which **did not happen**:
  - The **multiaxial split** (Axis I vs. Axis II) **was eliminated**, elevating PDs into the mainstream. (This change *did* occur.)
  - The plan to **replace specific PDs with Big-Five-style dimensions never materialized** — too many permutations made diagnosis hard and complicated treatment planning (Skodol, 2012).
  - A proposal to **eliminate several PDs** (paranoid, schizoid, histrionic, avoidant, dependent), rediagnosing them as "general personality disorder with traits specified," was rejected. Rationale for proposed removal: lack of research + heavy comorbidity/overlap. (Blashfield et al. even published **"The Death of Histrionic Personality Disorder."**)
- Net result: DSM-5 kept the **categorical 10-disorder system** in the main text and placed the **dimensional Alternative Model in Section III** ("emerging measures and models") for further study.

> Exam synthesis — categorical vs. dimensional showdown: The chapter ends on the unresolved tension that ICD-11 later resolved in favor of dimensions. For MET-2026, be ready to (a) name all 10 DSM categories by cluster, (b) state that DSM-5/DSM-5-TR remained categorical with a Section-III dimensional alternative, and (c) contrast this with ICD-11's fully dimensional **severity + 5 trait domains (Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, Anankastia) + Borderline pattern qualifier** model.
