# Ch 16: Mental Health Services: Legal and Ethical Issues

This final chapter covers the intersection of the **mental health system** and the **legal system**, the area called **mental health law / forensic psychology**. It explains how society balances the **rights of people with psychological disorders** against the **responsibility of society to protect itself and them**: civil commitment (involuntary hospitalization), criminal commitment (the insanity defense, competence to stand trial), the therapist's **duty to warn**, the role of **expert witnesses**, **patients' rights** (right to treatment, right to refuse treatment, rights of research participants), and **evidence-based practice / clinical practice guidelines**. High-yield for MET/NIMHANS because it is the source of nearly all classic forensic-psychology landmark cases (M'Naghten, Durham, ALI, Tarasoff, O'Connor v. Donaldson, Dusky, Wyatt v. Stickney) and recurring legal/ethical exam concepts.

> **Note on classification overlays:** This chapter is about **law and ethics**, not DSM disorders, so there are **almost no diagnostic criteria** here and therefore **very few DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 changes** to flag. The one relevant point (made repeatedly by the book) is that **"mental illness" is a LEGAL concept, NOT a DSM/ICD diagnosis** — this remains true under DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 overlay notes are added only where the book references diagnostic systems directly.

## Perspectives on Mental Health Law
### Core framing (the Arthur case)
- **Opening case — Arthur** (revisited from Ch 13): had **psychotic symptoms** (talked of a "secret plan" to save starving children, planned to break into the German embassy). Family **could not force him into a psychiatric hospital** because he was **not in danger of harming himself or others**. He could only **admit himself voluntarily** (unlikely, since he believed nothing was wrong). Diagnosis = **brief psychotic disorder** (one of the few psychotic disorders that is **NOT chronic**; resolved in weeks).
- Lesson: the mental health system **could not act** because Arthur had not hurt himself or anyone, and **family could not authorize** involuntary admission. Illustrates the constant tension between **individual rights** and **societal responsibility**.
- Mental health professionals must **diagnose and treat** AND weigh **individual vs. societal rights/responsibilities**. Laws **change over time** with shifting societal and political views of mental illness.
- Central question: do people with mental illness **need help and protection**, or does **society need protection from them**?
- **Confidentiality** links research and practice: protects the **identity of a research participant** AND of a **patient seeking help**; people receiving services often also participate in research, so both constituencies' concerns matter.

### The two historical "eras" (La Fond & Durham, 1992)
- **Liberal era (1960–1980):** commitment to **individual rights and fairness**; the **rights of people with mental illness dominated**.
- **Neoconservative era (1980–present):** reaction to liberal reforms; focuses on **majority concerns, law and order**; the rights of people with mental illness have been **limited to provide greater protection to society**.

## Civil Commitment
- **Civil commitment laws** (Key Term, p. 571): each state's laws detailing **when a person can be legally declared to have a mental illness and placed in a hospital for treatment**, sometimes against the person's wishes.
- The legal system exerts significant influence over the mental health system; protecting both the individual and society is a **delicate balancing act**.
- **History:** US civil commitment laws date to the **late 19th century**. Before, people with severe mental illness were cared for by **family/community** or left to fend for themselves. Rise of large public hospitals brought an **alarming trend: involuntary commitment for reasons unrelated to mental illness** (e.g., women committed by husbands for differing views). **Mrs. E. P. W. Packard** (1800s) crusaded for better commitment laws after being confined **3 years** because her husband found her religious views "dangerous."

### Criteria for Civil Commitment
- Historically, commitment permitted when several conditions met:
  1. The person has a **"mental illness" and is in need of treatment**.
  2. The person is **dangerous to himself/herself or others**.
  3. The person is **unable to care for himself** — a **"grave disability."**
- Two types of government authority to act against a citizen's will:
  - **Police power:** government protects **public health, safety, and welfare**; can hold criminal offenders / threats to society.
  - **Parens patriae power** ("state or country as the parent"): the state acts as a **surrogate parent** when citizens cannot act in their own best interest (e.g., custody of orphaned children; committing those with severe mental illness who face **grave disability** or who **do not recognize their need for treatment**).
- **Voluntary admission** is always available after evaluation by a mental health professional.
- **Formal civil commitment process** (varies by state) usually begins with a **petition by a relative or mental health professional to a judge** → court requests an examination (psychological status, self-care ability, need for treatment, potential for harm) → judge decides.
- **Procedural safeguards** (like other legal proceedings): the person can **request a jury**, must be **notified**, must be **present during the trial**, must have **representation by an attorney**, can **examine witnesses**, and can **request an independent evaluation**.
- **Assisted outpatient treatment (AOT):** court-ordered; the person with severe mental illness **agrees to receive treatment as a condition of continuing to live in the community** — balances individual independence vs. community safety.
- **Emergency / short-term commitment:** in clear **immediate danger**, family or police can certify a **"clear and present danger"** without the full formal proceedings. Arthur's family **failed** here because only **possible** harm (not immediate) was shown.

### Defining Mental Illness (LEGAL concept)
- **Mental illness** (Key Term, p. 573) = a **LEGAL concept**, typically **severe emotional or thought disturbances that negatively affect an individual's health and safety**. **Each state has its own definition.**
- **New York:** "affliction with a mental disease or mental condition... manifested by a disorder or disturbance in behavior, feeling, thinking, or judgment to such an extent that the person... requires care, treatment and rehabilitation."
- **Connecticut:** a "mental or emotional condition that has substantial adverse effects on his or her ability to function and who requires care and treatment," and **specifically excludes** an **alcohol-dependent or drug-dependent person**.
- **Many states exclude cognitive disability or substance-related disorders** from the legal definition of mental illness.
- **KEY EXAM POINT — Mental illness ≠ psychological disorder.** Having a **DSM-5 diagnosis does NOT mean** the person fits the legal definition of mental illness. The DSM is specific, but legal terms like "mental condition" / "adverse effects on ability to function" are **ambiguous**, allowing flexibility but also **subjective bias**.

> **DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 note:** This separation is unchanged. Neither **DSM-5-TR** nor **ICD-11** defines "mental illness" as used by courts — they classify *mental disorders*. A court's finding of legal "mental illness" is independent of whether the person meets DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 criteria for any disorder.

### Dangerousness
- **Dangerousness** (Key Term, p. 573) = whether someone is a danger to self or others; a **critical determinant** of civil commitment and a **controversial concept**.
- **Popular opinion** (Kobau et al., 2010): people who are mentally ill are **more dangerous** — widespread, partly due to **sensational media**, but **questionable**.
- Research is **mixed**, but points to a **moderately increased rate of violence** among people with mental illness (Elbogen & Johnson, 2009). **Specific symptoms** raise risk: **hallucinations, delusions, or a comorbid personality disorder** (Lurigio & Harris, 2009). Even previously violent individuals are **not necessarily** going to reoffend.
- **Ethnic disparity:** the misperception of dangerousness differentially affects ethnic minorities; **Black males** are often perceived as dangerous even without violent behavior, partly explaining their **overrepresentation among involuntary commitments** (Lindsey et al., 2010; Vinkers et al., 2010).
- **Predicting dangerousness:** Brain/empathy research — "feeling the pain" of others (empathy) involves the **prefrontal cortex** (Robertson et al., 2007); damage prevents empathy-based moral decisions (Damasio, 2007). Used in court ("**brain blame**"); e.g., should defendants with **psychopathy** be judged not responsible because their inability to empathize has a **brain origin**? Our definitions of insanity **have not kept up** with the biology + environment understanding (Greely & Simpson, 2012).
- **Risk assessment tools:** e.g., the **Psychopathy Checklist–Revised (PCL-R)** (Hare & Vertommen, 2003; Ch 12). These tools are **best at identifying people at LOW risk** of violence but only **marginally successful at predicting who WILL be violent later** (Fazel et al., 2012). Clinicians can identify **higher-risk GROUPS** (e.g., prior violence + drug/alcohol dependence) but **cannot predict with certainty** for a particular individual.

### The Supreme Court and Civil Commitment (landmark cases)
- **O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975):** Kenneth Donaldson committed (1957) for paranoid schizophrenia to Florida State Hospital; **not dangerous**; Dr. O'Connor refused to release him for **~15 years** with **virtually no treatment** despite offers of a halfway house/friend. Donaldson sued and won **$48,500**. Court held: a state **cannot constitutionally confine a non-dangerous individual who can survive safely in freedom** by himself or with help of willing family/friends.
- **Addington v. Texas (1979):** more than a **promise of improving quality of life** is required to commit someone involuntarily. **Needing treatment or having a grave disability was NOT sufficient** — substantially **limited the government's ability to commit** unless the person was **dangerous**.

### Procedural Changes Affecting Civil Commitment
**Criminalization**
- Tightened 1960s–70s restrictions meant many who would have been committed were instead **handled by the criminal justice system** — the **"criminalization" of the mentally ill** (Lamb & Weinberger, 2009). The criminal justice system was **not prepared** to care for them; families frustrated as loved ones **languished in jail without help**.
- **Larry Hogue** example: involuntarily committed because, homeless and on drugs, he **terrorized a NYC neighborhood**; off drugs he could control himself.

**Deinstitutionalization and Homelessness**
- **Deinstitutionalization** (Key Term, p. 574) = the **movement of people with severe mental illness OUT of institutions** (started escalating in the 1980s).
- Two goals: (1) **close large state mental hospitals**; (2) **create community mental health centers** for the released individuals.
- **Goal 1 largely achieved:** ~**75% decrease** in number of hospitalized patients (Kiesler & Sibulkin, 1987). **Goal 2 NOT attained** — community care was deficient.
- Instead → **transinstitutionalization** (Key Term, p. 575) = the **movement of people with severe mental illness FROM large psychiatric hospitals TO nursing homes / group residences / jails / prisons**, many providing only **marginal services**. Because of deteriorated care, **deinstitutionalization is largely considered a FAILURE.**
- **Homelessness facts:** ~**2–3 million** Americans experience a night of homelessness each year; **>400,000** homeless on any given night (SAMHSA, 2011). Homelessness is **NOT exclusively a mental-illness problem**. Severe mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar) among homeless ≈ **30%**. Ethnicity matters: in San Diego County, **Latinos and Asian Americans** with mental illness were **less likely** to become homeless; **African Americans more likely** (Folsom et al., 2005).
- Homelessness was once blamed on strict commitment criteria + deinstitutionalization, but the rise is **also** due to **economic factors** — increased unemployment and shortage of low-income housing (Wright, 2009).

### Reactions to Strict Commitment Procedures
- Backlash arose from the **failure of deinstitutionalization, rise in homelessness, and criminalization** → calls to make involuntary commitment **easier**.
- **Joyce Brown case (1987–88):** During a 1988 NYC winter emergency, **Mayor Ed Koch** ordered all seemingly mentally ill homeless people **involuntarily committed for their protection**, citing **parens patriae**. **40-year-old Joyce Brown** (called herself "Billie Boggs") was admitted to **Bellevue Hospital**, diagnosed **paranoid schizophrenia**; supported by the **NY Civil Liberties Union**, she contested and was **released after 3 months**. Re-committed in 1994; repeated pattern for years. Illustrates the **clash between individual freedom and society's responsibility to treat**.
- The **National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)** (coalition of family members) pushed for legal reform to make involuntary commitment **easier**.
- **Washington State (1979):** revised laws to allow commitment of people **"in need of treatment"** → **91% increase** in involuntary commitments in the first year (Durham & La Fond, 1985). Hospital population size **did not change** — only the **status** (now **involuntary** under **parens patriae** instead of voluntary). Result: only changed the **authority** under which people were admitted.

### Sex offenders and "sexual predator" laws
- **1930–1960: "sexual psychopath laws"** — hospitalization instead of incarceration, but for an **indefinite period**; offenders held until treatment was "effective." Repealed/unused as treatment proved often **unsuccessful** with uncooperative clients and public opinion shifted **from treat to punish**.
- **"Sexual predator" laws (first enacted 1990):** incarcerate sex offenders for the crime, then, if still judged **dangerous at the end of the sentence**, **civilly commit** them.
- **Kansas v. Hendricks (1997):** US Supreme Court **upheld** the Kansas version as constitutional. Confinement viewed as acceptable because seen as **treatment** — even though justices **conceded such treatment is often ineffective** (Zonana & Buchanan, 2009). Critics (La Fond, 2005): gives government **too much latitude** to keep certain individuals away from society via civil commitment rather than incarceration.

> **An Overview of Civil Commitment / optimistic note:** Periodic change in laws is a sign of a **healthy, self-correcting system** that responds to limitations of prior decisions, even if slow.

## Criminal Commitment
- **Criminal commitment** (Key Term, p. 577) = the process by which people are held because **(1)** they are **accused of a crime and detained in a mental health facility until assessed as fit/unfit** to participate in legal proceedings, OR **(2)** they have been **found not guilty by reason of insanity (NGRI)**.
- Framing cases: **Andrea Yates** — first convicted/sentenced to life for **drowning her five children in a bathtub (2001)**, later found **NGRI**. Raises whether laws have "gone too far."

### The Insanity Defense
- Law recognizes that under some circumstances people are **not responsible** for their behavior and punishing them would be unfair/ineffective.
- **M'Naghten rule (1843, England):** **Daniel M'Naghten** (would today likely be diagnosed **paranoid schizophrenia**) believed the **Tory party** persecuted him; set out to kill the British PM but **mistook and killed the PM's secretary**. Rule: people are **not responsible** if they **do not know what they are doing** OR **do not know that what they are doing is wrong** (a **defect of reason, from disease of the mind**). Basis of the insanity defense; dominant for **>100 years**. Criticized as **too limited** (focuses only on knowledge of right/wrong, ignoring emotional functioning).
- **Durham rule (1954, Durham v. United States; Judge David Bazelon):** the **"accused is not criminally responsible if his unlawful act was the PRODUCT of mental disease or mental defect."** Initially hailed (let clinicians present a complete picture) but **abandoned** because mental health professionals **could not reliably assess whether illness CAUSED the behavior** → decisions based on **unscientific opinions** (Gunn & Wheat, 2012).
- **American Law Institute (ALI) rule / ALI test (1962):** developed by attorneys, judges, law scholars. A person is **not responsible** if, **as a result of mental disease or defect, he lacks substantial capacity EITHER to APPRECIATE the criminality (wrongfulness) of his conduct OR to CONFORM his conduct to the requirements of law.** Combines **M'Naghten (cognitive)** + **inability to self-control (volitional)**. Note: "mental disease or defect" **excludes** an abnormality manifested **only by repeated criminal/antisocial conduct** (i.e., excludes pure psychopathy/ASPD by itself). ALI argued threat of punishment **won't deter** someone with severe mental illness — they should be **treated until improved, then released**.
- **Diminished capacity (1978):** a person's ability to understand the nature of their behavior — and thus their **criminal intent** — can be **diminished by mental illness**. Tied to **mens rea** ("guilty mind"). To convict, there must be proof of **both** the physical act (**actus rea**) **and** the mental state (**mens rea**) (Gunn & Wheat, 2012). Example: a driver who **accidentally** kills a pedestrian who steps in front of the car is **not criminally responsible** — there was **no criminal intent**.

> **Table 16.1 — Evolution of the Insanity Defense (memorize the dates/quotes):**
> - **M'Naghten rule — 1843:** at the time of the act, the accused, from disease of the mind, did **not know the nature and quality of the act**, or if he did, **did not know it was wrong**.
> - **Durham rule — 1954:** not responsible if the act was the **product of mental disease or defect**.
> - **ALI rule — 1962:** lacks substantial capacity to **appreciate criminality** OR **conform conduct to law**; excludes abnormality shown only by repeated antisocial conduct.
> - **Diminished capacity — 1978:** abnormal mental condition can **reduce the degree of crime** (e.g., to offenses requiring only recklessness/criminal neglect).
> - **Insanity Defense Reform Act — 1984:** NGRI only if, due to mental disease/retardation, the person was **unable to appreciate the wrongfulness** of conduct at the time (moves back toward M'Naghten; drops the volitional "control" prong).

### Reactions to the Insanity Defense
- 1960s–70s rulings paralleled civil commitment — treatment over punishment for offenders with mental illness; this **alarmed** the public.
- Successful but controversial uses: **1979** man pleaded **NGRI for writing bad checks** based on **pathological gambling disorder** (State v. Campanaro, 1980). Other defenses: **PTSD, kleptomania** (DSM disorders), and **battered wife syndrome** (NOT in DSM).
- **John W. Hinckley, Jr. (March 31, 1981):** shot and seriously wounded **President Ronald Reagan**, a Secret Service agent, a police officer, and press secretary **James Brady**, while obsessed with actress **Jodie Foster** (claimed to impress her). Judged **NGRI using the ALI standard** — **national outrage**. James and Sarah Brady → gun control advocacy → **Brady Act (1994)**.
- After Hinckley, ~**75% of states** changed insanity rules to make the defense **harder** (Simon & Shuman, 2008). Highly publicized cases (Hinckley, **Charles Manson, Jeffrey Dahmer, Ted Kaczynski**) link mental illness with violence in public perception.
- **Public misperceptions:** 91% agreed judges/juries can't tell if defendants are really sane/insane; ~90% called the insanity plea a **"loophole."**

> **Table 16.2 — Public Perception vs. Actual Occurrence (Silver, Cirincione, & Steadman, 1994) — HIGH-YIELD:**
> - Felony indictments resulting in an **insanity plea**: perceived **37.0%** vs. **actual 0.9%** (less than 1%).
> - Insanity pleas resulting in **acquittal**: perceived **44.0%** vs. actual **26.0%**.
> - Acquittees **sent to a mental hospital**: perceived **50.6%** vs. actual **84.7%**.
> - Acquittees **set free**: perceived **25.6%** vs. actual **15.3%**.
> - Length of confinement (months): **all crimes 21.8**; **murder 76.4**.
- Reality: NGRI is **NOT used excessively** and does **not** free dangerous people. Confinement after NGRI may **exceed** the prison time for conviction. **Hinckley** has been a patient at **St. Elizabeth's Hospital for >30 years**. People with mental illness found guilty of nonviolent crimes can be committed **>8× as long** as those without mental illness in prison (Perlin, 2000). They do **not** "beat the rap."

### Guilty But Mentally Ill (GBMI)
- A reform replacing NGRI with **guilty but mentally ill (GBMI)** to close the perceived loophole (Torry & Billick, 2010).
- **NGRI:** not sent to prison; evaluated at a psychiatric facility until ready for release; **if no longer mentally ill, must be released** (e.g., Arthur's brief psychotic disorder resolved → would be released immediately).
- **GBMI version 1:** allows the system to **both treat AND punish** — the person gets a **prison term as if no mental illness**; legal authorities decide whether confinement is in prison or a mental health facility; if recovered before sentence ends, can be **held in prison for the maximum term** (Arthur could serve a full sentence even after recovering).
- **GBMI version 2 (harsher):** convicted individuals **imprisoned**; prison may provide mental health services **if available**; the verdict is just a **declaration** that the person was mentally ill at the time, with **no differential treatment**. **Idaho, Montana, and Utah** have **abandoned the insanity defense altogether** and adopted this version ("The Evolving Insanity Defense," 2006).
- Research: GBMI recipients are **more likely to be imprisoned and get longer sentences** than NGRI pleaders (Callahan et al., 1992; Keilitz, 1987), and are **no more likely to receive treatment** than other mentally ill prisoners (Keilitz, 1987; Smith & Hall, 1982).
- **>3× as many** people with severe mental illness are in **jails than hospitals** (Torrey et al., 2010). **Figure 16.1:** incarceration rates of the severely mentally ill are approaching levels of **150+ years ago**.
- **Jared Loughner:** shot Congresswoman **Gabrielle Giffords**, killed six others; **forcibly medicated** to be made competent; ultimately **pleaded guilty**, life without parole.

### Therapeutic Jurisprudence
- Built-in tension: the legal system is **adversarial** (winners/losers); the mental health system seeks **solutions without blame** (both sides "win").
- **Therapeutic jurisprudence** = using what we know about **behavior change** to help people in trouble with the law. Implemented through **"problem-solving courts."**
- **Problem-solving courts:** **drug treatment courts, domestic violence courts, mental health courts**, etc. — designed for the unique needs of specific populations. Roots in **tribal legal systems** of the US, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand (King & Wexler, 2010).
- Example: a **drug treatment court** judge may **delay sentencing** if the defendant holds a job 6 months, gets drug treatment, and stays drug-free. A **mental health court** may refer to community programs and involve family.
- Challenge: mental health professionals **cannot assess mental health retrospectively** (can't reliably reconstruct whether someone knew right from wrong and could control behavior at the time of the act).

### Competence to Stand Trial
- **Competence** (Key Term, p. 581) — before trial, people must be **able to understand the charges against them** and **assist with their own defense** (criteria from **Dusky v. United States, 1960**).
- Distinct from insanity: a person could be **NGRI** (mentally ill **at the time of the crime**) yet still **competent to stand trial** (mental state **during proceedings**) — would have applied to Arthur.
- A person found **incompetent** typically **loses decision-making authority and faces commitment**. Most people with obvious severe impairments are **never tried**. Estimate: for **every NGRI verdict, ~45 others** are committed with a diagnosis of severe mental illness (Butler, 2006).
- Length of stay = time to **regain competence**. Because this could be indefinite, **Jackson v. Indiana (1972):** confinement **cannot be indefinite**; after a **"reasonable amount of time"** the person must be found competent, **set free, or committed under civil law**. ("Reasonable amount of time" is vague.)
- **Burden of proof:** the weight of evidence needed to win a case. **Medina v. California (1992):** placed the burden of proof on the **defendant** to show that **she is incompetent** to stand trial.

### Duty to Warn
- **Duty to warn** (Key Term, p. 581) = the therapist's responsibility to **warn potential victims** that a client may attempt to hurt or kill them.
- **Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1974, 1976):** In 1969, **Prosenjit Poddar**, a UC Berkeley grad student (diagnosed **paranoid schizophrenia**), killed **Tatiana Tarasoff**, who had rejected his romantic advances. At his last session he **hinted he would kill Tarasoff**; the therapist alerted **campus police**, who investigated and got assurances from Poddar; weeks later Poddar **shot and stabbed Tarasoff** to death. Family sued; **the court agreed the therapists should have warned Tarasoff** → established the **standard duty to warn**.
- **Thompson v. County of Alameda (1980):** a therapist does **NOT** have a duty to warn when the person makes **nonspecific threats against nonspecific people** — threats must be **specific**.
- Good practice: when in doubt, **consult colleagues** (a second opinion).

### Mental Health Professionals as Expert Witnesses
- **Expert witnesses** (Key Term, p. 581) = individuals with **specialized knowledge** who assist judges/juries (e.g., on dangerousness, ability to understand/participate in defense, competence, malingering).
- Public views them **ambivalently** — valuable educators vs. **"hired guns"** whose opinions suit whoever pays.
- **Dangerousness predictions:** reliable over the **short term (2–20 days)** post-evaluation (Scott et al., 2008); **NOT** reliable over **longer periods** (Fazel et al.).
- **Diagnosis:** DSM-IV-TR and **DSM-5** improved reliability, but again the **legal "mental illness" is not matched by a DSM-5 disorder** — statements about "mental illness" are **court determinations, not clinical ones**.
- **Malingering** = to **fake or grossly exaggerate symptoms**, usually to be **absolved from blame** (e.g., claiming to have been hallucinating during the crime). The **MMPI (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory)** is **extremely accurate** at detecting malingering: examiners look for **true symptoms that genuinely ill people rarely report**, which **malingerers overreport** in their rush to fake illness (Sellbom et al., 2010).
- Mental health professionals also reliably assess **competence** (Shulman et al., 2007). BUT real-world testimony can be biased by the witness's **personal/professional opinions** (e.g., an anti-commitment bias shaping how clinical info is presented; Drogin et al., 2012).

> **DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 note:** Where the book credits **DSM-IV-TR / DSM-5** with improving diagnostic reliability, **DSM-5-TR** continues that aim (clarified criteria text, harmonized **ICD-10-CM codes**), and **ICD-11** provides the parallel international diagnostic system. None of this changes the chapter's core point: a forensic finding of legal "mental illness" is **separate** from any DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 diagnosis.

## Patients' Rights and Clinical Practice Guidelines
- Until ~**40 years ago**, people in mental health facilities had **few rights** (phone calls, mail, visitors, treatment decided by staff). Abuses → legal action and court rulings.

### The Right to Treatment
- **Right to treatment** = one of the most fundamental rights of people in mental health facilities (Bloch & Green, 2012). Early **1970s class-action lawsuits** established rights for people with mental illness and intellectual disability.
- **Wyatt v. Stickney (1972)** — landmark. Grew from a lawsuit by **fired Alabama institution employees**. Established the first **minimum standards** facilities must meet: **minimum staff–patient ratios**, **physical requirements** (e.g., number of showers/toilets per residents), and a mandate to make **positive efforts to attain treatment goals**.
  - Expanded the **"least restrictive alternative"** concept — people should get care in the **least confining/limiting environment possible**; the court listed a progression: more → less structured living; large → smaller facilities; large → smaller living units; group → individual residences; segregated → community-integrated; dependent → independent living.
- **Youngberg v. Romeo (1982):** reaffirmed treating people in **nonrestrictive settings** but left the **type of treatment to professional judgment** — concerning to advocates because professional judgment hasn't always served patients.
- **Protection and Advocacy for Mentally Ill Individuals Act (1986):** Congress established **protection and advocacy agencies in each state** to investigate **abuse and neglect** and act as **legal advocates** (Woodside & Legg, 1990) — balancing professional needs against patients' rights.

### The Right to Refuse Treatment
- **Right to refuse treatment** = one of the most controversial issues, centered on **antipsychotic medications** (Bloch & Green, 2012; Simon & Shuman, 2008).
  - **Pro-treatment side:** under some circumstances people with severe mental illness **cannot decide in their own best interest**, so clinicians are responsible for providing treatment despite protests.
  - **Patient/advocate side:** all people have a **fundamental right** to make decisions about their own treatment, **even if not in their best medical interest**.
- **"Forced competence" dilemma:** can delusional/hallucinating defendants be **forced to take medication** to become competent to stand trial?
- **Riggins v. Nevada (1992):** because of potential **negative side effects** (e.g., involuntary movements of **tardive dyskinesia**), people **cannot be forced to take antipsychotic medication**.
- **Washington v. Harper (1990) / "Harper hearing":** allows **involuntary medication** following a **due-process hearing** where professionals argue the merits of medication and the patient gives a counterargument. Used to involuntarily medicate **Jared Loughner** (later pleaded guilty to **19 charges** of murder/attempted murder; 2011 Tucson attack injuring **Rep. Gabrielle Giffords**, six killed).

### The Rights of Research Participants
- Rights of psychological research participants (APA, 2010a, 2010b):
  1. Right to be **informed about the purpose** of the study.
  2. Right to **privacy**.
  3. Right to be **treated with respect and dignity**.
  4. Right to be **protected from physical and mental harm**.
  5. Right to **choose to participate or refuse** without prejudice/reprisal.
  6. Right to **anonymity** in reporting results.
  7. Right to **safeguarding of records**.
- **Informed consent** = formal agreement to participate **after being fully apprised of all important aspects**, including any possibility of harm. **Simple consent is not enough.**
- **Greg Aller case:** In **1988**, 23-year-old Greg Aller consented to a **UCLA Neuropsychiatric Institute** study on **early schizophrenia and medication withdrawal**. On **Prolixin** he improved dramatically (hallucinations/delusions gone; enrolled in college, made the dean's list). In the **medication-withdrawal phase (late 1989)** he relapsed (delusions about Reagan and space aliens), **threatened to kill his parents**, and never fully returned to his improved state. NIH found UCLA researchers **did not give Greg and his family all the information** about risks and alternatives (Aller & Aller, 1997). Warning about informed-consent responsibilities; researchers now explore **formal assessment of whether participants with mental illness understand risks/benefits** (Harmell, Palmer, & Jeste, 2012; Jeste et al., 2009).

### Evidence-Based Practice and Clinical Practice Guidelines
- **Evidence-based practice (EBP)** = health-care practices **supported by research findings demonstrating effectiveness**. Driven by rising health-care costs and government funding. Formalized as a systematic method only in the **past ~15 years** (Institute of Medicine, 2001; Sackett et al., 2000).
- US **President's New Freedom Commission on Mental Health (2003)**: principal recommendation = advance EBPs and expand the EBP workforce. **APA Presidential Task Force (2006)**: adopted EBP in psychology as policy.
- **Clinical practice guidelines** = recommendations on how to treat a particular problem, derived from accumulated effectiveness evidence.
- US agency history: **Agency for Health Care Policy and Research (1989)** → reauthorized/renamed **Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (1999)** — establishes uniformity in effective care delivery and disseminates the latest developments.
- **Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (2010):** national health insurance; created the **Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute** to research which treatments work for which conditions and disseminate widely (Dickersin, 2010). Billions allocated to disseminate EBP psychological treatments (e.g., **Veterans Health Administration** in the US, **National Health Service** in the UK; McHugh & Barlow, 2010).
- **APA template (published 1995, revised 2002):** guidelines should be built on **two axes**:
  - **Clinical efficacy axis = INTERNAL validity.** Is the treatment effective vs. an alternative or vs. no treatment in a **controlled clinical research context**? Established via **clinical trials** showing the intervention is **better than no therapy, better than nonspecific therapy, or better than an alternative therapy** (the last = **highest level of evidence**). Other sources: **quantified clinical observations / clinical replication series** and **clinical consensus** of experts (less valuable than RCTs or quantified observations). Guards against false effectiveness from **passage of time / natural healing** or **nonspecific effects** (e.g., just meeting a caring professional).
  - **Clinical utility axis = EXTERNAL validity.** Will an efficacious intervention work in the **actual practice settings** where it's applied, and is it **feasible and cost-effective**? Two main issues:
    - **Feasibility:** will patients **accept** and **comply** with it; is it **easy to administer**? Example: **electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** is effective for severe depression but **frightening** (many refuse) and requires sophisticated procedures/medical supervision → used **only as a last resort**.
    - **Generalizability:** effectiveness across patients of differing **ethnicity, age, sex**, across **settings** (inpatient/outpatient/community), and across **therapists**.
- A treatment can be **highly efficacious** yet still **not be disseminated** unless it is **generalizable, feasible, and cost-effective**.
- **2010:** APA decided to develop **its own clinical practice guidelines** for evidence-based psychological care.
- **Scientist-practitioners** (Ch 1): take a scientific approach to clinical work; will contribute to guidelines development, especially by collecting **clinical-utility / external-validity data** in routine practice.

> **Table 16.3 — Template for Constructing Psychological Intervention Guidelines (two axes):**
> - **Clinical Efficacy (Internal Validity):** A. Better than alternative therapy (RCTs); B. Better than nonspecific therapy (RCTs); C. Better than no therapy (RCTs); D. Quantified clinical observations; E. Clinical consensus (strongly positive / mixed / strongly negative / contradictory).
> - **Clinical Utility (External Validity):** A. **Feasibility** (patient acceptability — cost, pain, duration, side effects; patient choice at equal efficacy; probability of compliance; ease of dissemination); B. **Generalizability** (patient characteristics — culture, gender, developmental level; therapist characteristics; robustness across settings; contextual factors); C. **Costs and benefits** (cost of delivering vs. cost of withholding the intervention).

## Conclusions
- Therapy and scientific progress do **not occur in a vacuum**. Those who study/treat abnormal behavior must master the information **and** understand their **role in society, politics, and law**. Every facet of life — biological, social, political, legal — **interacts with every other**; helping people requires appreciating this complexity.

## Key Terms (with page numbers)
- **civil commitment laws** (571) — state laws certifying a person legally as mentally ill for hospitalization, possibly against their wishes.
- **mental illness** (573) — a **legal** concept: severe emotional/thought disturbances negatively affecting health and safety (definition varies by state; ≠ DSM diagnosis).
- **dangerousness** (573) — tendency to commit violent acts; a key (and controversial) civil-commitment criterion.
- **deinstitutionalization** (574) — movement of people with severe mental illness **out of** institutions.
- **transinstitutionalization** (575) — movement of people with severe mental illness **to a less restrictive (or marginal) facility** (nursing homes, group residences, jails).
- **criminal commitment** (577) — holding people because they are accused of a crime and detained for competence assessment, or found NGRI.
- **diminished capacity** (578) — mental illness can lessen a person's ability to understand behavior and form criminal intent (**mens rea**).
- **competence** (581) — ability to understand the charges and assist in one's own defense (Dusky v. US, 1960).
- **duty to warn** (581) — therapist's responsibility to warn a client's potential victims (Tarasoff).
- **expert witnesses** (581) — people with specialized knowledge who assist judges/juries (e.g., on competence, malingering).
- **clinical efficacy axis** (584) — internal-validity axis: does the treatment work in controlled research?
- **clinical utility axis** (585) — external-validity axis: is the treatment effective, feasible, and disseminable in real practice settings?

## Exam-Focused Landmark Case / Rule Summary (rapid revision)
- **M'Naghten (1843):** didn't know nature/quality of act, or didn't know it was wrong.
- **Durham (1954):** act was a "product of mental disease or defect" (abandoned).
- **ALI (1962):** lacked capacity to **appreciate wrongfulness** OR **conform conduct to law**.
- **Insanity Defense Reform Act (1984):** drops volitional prong; back to appreciating wrongfulness (post-Hinckley).
- **O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975):** can't confine a **non-dangerous** person who can survive in freedom.
- **Addington v. Texas (1979):** need-for-treatment/grave-disability alone insufficient to commit.
- **Kansas v. Hendricks (1997):** upheld sexual-predator civil commitment as "treatment."
- **Dusky v. US (1960):** standard for **competence to stand trial**.
- **Jackson v. Indiana (1972):** competence-restoration confinement can't be **indefinite**.
- **Medina v. California (1992):** **defendant** bears burden of proving incompetence.
- **Tarasoff v. Regents (1974/1976):** established **duty to warn**.
- **Thompson v. County of Alameda (1980):** no duty to warn for **nonspecific** threats.
- **Wyatt v. Stickney (1972):** **right to treatment** + minimum standards + least restrictive alternative.
- **Youngberg v. Romeo (1982):** treatment type left to professional judgment.
- **Riggins v. Nevada (1992):** can't force antipsychotics (side-effect risk).
- **Washington v. Harper (1990):** involuntary medication allowed after a **due-process (Harper) hearing**.
