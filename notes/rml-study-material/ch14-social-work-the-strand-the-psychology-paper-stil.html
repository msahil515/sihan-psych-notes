Question 4 (Joel Fischer's casework review), question 18 (the first school of social work in India), question 32 (what case management does and does not achieve) and question 82 (the common properties of a group) are all social work content. Three of those four were in reach with ten minutes of reading. None of them were hard. They were simply from a discipline nobody told you was on the paper.

From scratch: why a psychology entrance asks social work at all. In India, mental health service delivery was built as a three-professional team: the psychiatrist (medical), the clinical psychologist (assessment and psychotherapy) and the psychiatric social worker (family, environment, rehabilitation, welfare linkage). All three are regulated together, the first by the National Medical Commission and the latter two by the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI). Because the professions were built as one team, entrance papers for clinical psychology in India routinely carry a slice of social work: its methods, its founders, its Indian institutional history. RML is one of the papers that does this. It is not a mistake in the paper. It is the syllabus.

Key: Social work is a separate profession with its own founders, its own methods and its own Indian timeline. It is not "applied social psychology" and it is not counselling. Learning it as a distinct discipline, with its own names attached to its own dates, is what makes these questions free marks instead of coin flips.

What social work is, and how it split from charity

Social work is the organised, method-driven profession of helping people cope with problems in their social functioning: the fit between a person and the environment they live in. That last phrase is the whole definition. A clinical psychologist asks what is happening inside the person. A social worker asks what is happening between the person and their family, workplace, neighbourhood, welfare entitlements and law. The two overlap heavily in mental health, which is exactly why the entrance paper mixes them.

The profession grew out of nineteenth-century charity, and its founding argument was that charity should stop being sentiment and start being method. Two rival movements did this in parallel, and RML-type papers like to contrast them:

MovementFoundedCore ideaDescendant method

Trap: Toynbee Hall (1884, London) is the first settlement house; Hull House (1889, Chicago) is the famous American one. Papers swap the two and swap the founders. Barnett is London, Addams is Chicago.

Method 1: Social case work, and the Fischer question

Social case work is social work with the individual and the family as the unit of attention. Its founding text is Mary Ellen Richmond's Social Diagnosis (1917), which imported the medical logic of study → diagnosis → treatment into helping work, and her What Is Social Case Work? (1922), which named the method. Richmond's definition is the one to be able to recognise: case work is the set of processes that develop personality through conscious, individual by individual adjustments between people and their social environment.

After Richmond the method split into two schools, and this split is examinable:

Key: the two schools of case work. The Diagnostic school (Freudian, Smith College and New York School of Social Work; Gordon Hamilton, later Florence Hollis) treats the worker as the expert who diagnoses and treats an intrapsychic problem; Hollis's version is called psychosocial case work, remembered by the phrase "the person-in-situation configuration". The Functional school (Rankian, University of Pennsylvania; Jessie Taft and Virginia Robinson) rejects diagnosis, puts the client's own will at the centre, and uses the agency function and above all time (a defined beginning, middle and end) as the therapeutic instrument.
Who → what: Mary Ellen Richmond → Social Diagnosis (1917), What Is Social Case Work? (1922), founder of case work. Gordon Hamilton → diagnostic school, Theory and Practice of Social Case Work. Florence Hollis → psychosocial case work, person-in-situation. Jessie Taft and Virginia Robinson → functional school, will and time, out of Otto Rank. Helen Harris Perlman → the problem-solving model (1957), the compromise between the two schools, remembered as the four Ps: Person, Problem, Place, Process (she later added a fifth and sixth P, Professional and Provisions). Felix Biestek → The Casework Relationship (1957) and the seven principles.
Key: Biestek's seven principles of the casework relationship. Individualisation, purposeful expression of feelings, controlled emotional involvement, acceptance, non-judgemental attitude, client self-determination, confidentiality. If a question asks for "a principle of case work" and one option is something like "worker direction" or "moral evaluation", that option is the answer to an EXCEPT stem.
From scratch: the Fischer question, which is the one that cost you a mark. By the 1960s case work had been the profession's flagship method for fifty years, but nobody had audited whether it worked. Joel Fischer did. His paper Is Casework Effective? A Review, published in the journal Social Work in 1973, pulled together the controlled evaluation studies of professional casework and reported that the evidence did not show casework to be effective; in a number of the studies the treated clients did no better, and in some they did slightly worse, than untreated controls. The finding detonated inside the profession and is credited with pushing social work toward empirical practice, measurable outcomes and, eventually, evidence-based practice. So the correct association is: Fischer 1973 → casework effectiveness questioned → the empirical practice movement.

Trap: The Fischer stem is almost always written so that the "nice" option is wrong. Fischer did not establish that casework works, did not develop a casework model, and is not a founder of casework. He is the man who asked whether it worked and answered "the evidence does not say so". If the options offer Richmond, Perlman, Hollis and Fischer against a stem about effectiveness or evaluation, it is Fischer.

Method 2: Social group work, and what a group actually is

Social group work is social work practised through the group: the worker uses the group experience itself as the instrument of change, so members grow through what happens between them rather than through what the worker does to them. It came out of the settlement houses, the YMCA/YWCA and the recreation movement, and was made a formal method largely by Grace Longwell Coyle (Social Process in Organized Groups, 1930), with Gisela Konopka writing the standard mid-century text on group work in the treatment setting.

Who → what: Grace Longwell Coyle → the founder of social group work as a method (1930s). Gisela Konopka → group work as a treatment method, group work with the emotionally disturbed. William Schwartz → the mediating/interactionist model. Robert Vinter → the remedial model. Charles Garvin and Paul Glasser → later systematisation. Compare with Kurt Lewin (group dynamics, T-groups, National Training Laboratories, 1946), who is psychology's side of the same coin.
Key: the three classical models of group work. Social goals model (the settlement inheritance: build citizenship and social conscience, the group serves the community). Remedial model (Vinter: the group is a means of treating individual members, the worker is a change agent). Reciprocal/mediating model (Schwartz: the worker mediates between the individual and the group, both are the client).
From scratch: the common properties of a group, which is question 82. Ask what makes a collection of people an actual group rather than a queue at a bus stop, and every textbook, sociological or social work, returns broadly the same list. A group has: (1) two or more people; (2) interaction, they act on one another; (3) shared or common goals; (4) a sense of "we-feeling", belonging or common identity; (5) interdependence, what one does affects the others; (6) norms, shared rules of conduct; (7) roles and usually some structure or status differentiation; and (8) some stability over time. What is not on that list is anything technical or theoretical: a group does not require an analytic base, a therapist, a formal theory, a written constitution, a fixed size or a professional worker. Groups exist without any of those.

Trap: this is an EXCEPT question and you must pick the odd one out, not the weakest one. On a stem like "all of the following are common properties of a group EXCEPT", the four options will typically be three ordinary properties (interaction, common goal, we-feeling, norms) and one that sounds impressively technical. The technical-sounding one, analytic base, is the answer. Under time pressure the pull is to choose whichever property feels least important; that is the wrong instinct. On an EXCEPT stem your job is to find the option that is false, not the option that is weakest. Read every option, tick the three that are genuinely true, and take what is left.
Key: stages of group development. Bruce Tuckman (1965): forming, storming, norming, performing, plus adjourning added in 1977 with Mary Ann Jensen. The social work version is the Boston model of James Garland, Hubert Jones and Ralph Kolodny (1965): pre-affiliation, power and control, intimacy, differentiation, separation. Know both, and know which is which: Tuckman is the psychology/management sequence, Garland-Jones-Kolodny is the social group work sequence.

Method 3: Community organisation, and the rest of the method set

Community organisation is social work with a community as the unit: identifying needs, building local capacity and matching resources to those needs. Murray G. Ross wrote the classic definition (a community identifies its needs, orders them, develops the confidence and will to work at them, and takes action, thereby extending cooperative attitudes). Jack Rothman (1968) gave the three models that get asked most:

Rothman's modelWhat it assumesWorker's role
Locality developmentThe community lacks skills and cohesion; consensus is possibleEnabler, catalyst, teacher
Social planningThe problem is technical and substantive; needs expert dataFact-gatherer, analyst, programme implementer
Social actionThe community is an oppressed group facing a power structure; conflict is expectedActivist, advocate, negotiator, agitator
Key: the methods of social work, primary and secondary. Primary (direct) methods: social case work, social group work, community organisation. Secondary (auxiliary, indirect) methods: social welfare administration, social action, social work research. Six in total. A stem asking for "the primary methods" wants the first three; a stem asking which is not a primary method is usually pointing at social work research or social welfare administration.
Who → what: Murray G. Ross → the classic definition of community organisation. Jack Rothman → the three models (locality development, social planning, social action). Saul Alinsky → radical community organising, Rules for Radicals. Paulo Freire → Pedagogy of the Oppressed, conscientisation, the theory behind social action practice.

Case management: what it does, and the thing it does not do

Case management is the coordinating method that took over from classical casework in mental health, disability and long-term care after deinstitutionalisation. A person with a severe, long-running condition needs many services at once (medication, housing, income support, vocational training, family work) delivered by many agencies that do not talk to each other. The case manager is the single accountable point that assembles and holds that package together.

Key: the core functions of case management. Assessment of need, planning (a written, individualised service plan), linkage/brokerage to services, coordination across providers, monitoring and review, advocacy for entitlements, and evaluation. The named models are the brokerage model (thin, link and refer), the clinical/therapeutic model (the manager also provides treatment), intensive case management, the strengths model (Charles Rapp and Ronna Chamberlain, University of Kansas: build on what the person and the environment already have) and Assertive Community Treatment (ACT), developed by Leonard Stein and Mary Ann Test in Madison, Wisconsin, where a multidisciplinary team delivers services directly in the community with a low caseload and no time limit.
From scratch: the outcomes question, which is question 32. What case management is evidenced to achieve is a narrow and specific list: it reduces hospitalisation and readmission, it improves continuity of care and engagement with services, it improves access to and coordination of services, it improves social functioning, housing stability and quality of life, it increases client and family satisfaction, and it can reduce total service costs by substituting community care for inpatient beds. What it does not do is cure anything. Case management is a service-delivery and coordination technology, not a treatment for the underlying disorder. It changes where and how care reaches a person; it does not change the natural history of schizophrenia.

Trap: On "all of the following are outcomes of case management EXCEPT", the answer is the option that promises a clinical cure, remission, symptom elimination or "recovery from the illness". Every other option will be a coordination, access, continuity, satisfaction or cost outcome. Same discipline as question 82: find the false option, not the weak one.

Social work in India: the dates and the institutions

From scratch: the founding of social work education in India, which is question 18. Indian social work education begins in Bombay in 1936 with the Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work, the first school of social work in India and in Asia. It was founded by the Sir Dorabji Tata Trust and its first director was the American Protestant missionary Clifford Manshardt, who had been running the Nagpada Neighbourhood House, a settlement in Bombay; the settlement-house lineage runs straight into it. In 1944 the school was renamed the Tata Institute of Social Sciences (TISS), and it was granted deemed-university status in 1964. So a single chain to memorise: 1936 Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work → 1944 renamed TISS → 1964 deemed university, founder-director Clifford Manshardt, city Bombay/Mumbai.

Trap: The distractors on this question are always the other plausible institutions and the other plausible years. It is not Delhi School of Social Work (founded 1946, later University of Delhi), not TISS-under-that-name (that name only exists from 1944), not NIMHANS (Bangalore, and a mental health institute, not a school of social work), and not 1947 or 1950. First school, first year: Bombay, 1936.

Psychiatric social work: where the two professions touch

Psychiatric social work is the specialisation you will actually work beside. Its unit of attention is the patient's environment: it takes the social history and family assessment, does psychoeducation with the family, works on expressed emotion (George Brown and colleagues) and family burden, arranges welfare entitlements and disability certification, runs vocational rehabilitation and aftercare, and does the community follow-up that stops relapse. In an Indian mental health team the division of labour that the paper expects you to know is: the psychiatrist diagnoses and prescribes; the clinical psychologist does psychological assessment and psychotherapy; the psychiatric social worker does the family, the environment, the entitlements and the rehabilitation.

RML tilt: RML is a Delhi government teaching hospital with a service orientation, so it asks social work the way a hospital asks it: who does what on the team, what case management delivers, what the methods are called, and when Indian social work education began. Learn the names, the dates and the method labels. There is no reasoning to do on these questions; either you have the fact or you do not, which is exactly why they are the cheapest marks in the paper to recover.

Recap: Social work is a separate profession organised around social functioning, born when nineteenth-century charity split into the investigating Charity Organisation Society (London 1869) and the live-among-them settlement movement (Toynbee Hall 1884, Samuel Barnett; Hull House 1889, Jane Addams). Its three primary methods are social case work, social group work and community organisation; its three secondary methods are social welfare administration, social action and social work research. Case work was founded by Mary Ellen Richmond (Social Diagnosis 1917, What Is Social Case Work? 1922), split into the diagnostic school (Gordon Hamilton, Florence Hollis, person-in-situation) and the functional school (Jessie Taft, Virginia Robinson, will and time), was reconciled by Helen Harris Perlman's problem-solving model and its four Ps, given its seven relationship principles by Felix Biestek, and was then put on trial by Joel Fischer in 1973, whose negative review pushed the profession toward empirical practice. Group work was founded by Grace Longwell Coyle and runs on three models (social goals, remedial after Robert Vinter, reciprocal after William Schwartz); a group needs interaction, common goals, we-feeling, norms, roles and interdependence, and needs no analytic base. Community organisation follows Murray Ross and Jack Rothman's three models: locality development, social planning, social action. Case management assesses, plans, links, coordinates, monitors and advocates, reducing hospitalisation and improving continuity and quality of life, but it never cures. In India the chain is 1936 Sir Dorabji Tata Graduate School of Social Work in Bombay under Clifford Manshardt, renamed TISS in 1944, with Delhi School of Social Work in 1946, RCI regulation from 1992 and the Mental Healthcare Act in 2017.
