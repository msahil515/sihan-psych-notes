Social psychology is the scientific study of how our thoughts, feelings, and behaviour are shaped by the real, imagined, or implied presence of others. It sits between the individual (personality) and the group (sociology): the same person behaves very differently alone versus in a crowd, and that difference is the subject matter here.

From scratch: Three questions organise the whole field. How do we evaluate the social world (attitudes)? How do we explain it (attribution)? And how does it change us (influence, groups, prejudice, aggression, helping)? Keep those buckets in mind and the classic studies slot in cleanly.

Attitudes

An attitude is a learned, relatively stable evaluation of a person, object, or idea. The classic tri-component (ABC) model breaks it into Affect (feelings), Behaviour (action tendencies), and Cognition (beliefs). "I feel disgust at smoking (A), I refuse cigarettes (B), and I believe it causes cancer (C)."

Attitudes form through classical conditioning (pairing), operant conditioning (reward/punishment), observational learning (Albert Bandura), and mere exposure (Robert Zajonc: familiarity breeds liking). But attitudes predict behaviour weakly, the attitude-behaviour gap. Richard LaPiere's 1934 study is the classic demonstration: a Chinese couple was served at nearly every establishment they visited, yet those same places later said in writing they would refuse Chinese guests.

Persuasion: the Elaboration Likelihood Model

Richard Petty and John Cacioppo proposed two routes to persuasion. The central route works through careful thinking about argument quality (used when motivation and ability are high) and produces lasting change. The peripheral route relies on surface cues, source attractiveness, length of message, number of arguments, and produces flimsy, temporary change.

Cognitive dissonance

Leon Festinger argued that holding two inconsistent cognitions creates an uncomfortable tension (dissonance) that we reduce by changing an attitude. In the famous Festinger & J. Merrill Carlsmith (1959) study, people paid only $1 to lie that a boring task was fun later rated it as more enjoyable than those paid $20, because the $1 group had no external justification and had to change their attitude to resolve the tension.

Trap: Lower reward produces more attitude change in dissonance (insufficient justification). Bigger incentive gives an external excuse, so no internal change is needed.

Attribution

Attribution is how we infer the causes of behaviour. Fritz Heider, the founder, distinguished internal (dispositional) from external (situational) causes, calling ordinary people "naive scientists."

Who → what: Heider → naive-scientist attribution; Edward E. Jones & Keith Davis → correspondent inference; Harold Kelley → covariation model.

Edward Jones and Keith Davis's correspondent inference theory asks when an act reflects a stable trait: we infer disposition when behaviour is freely chosen, unexpected (low social desirability), and produces non-common effects. Harold Kelley's covariation model says we weigh three kinds of information: consensus (do others act this way?), consistency (does this person always act this way?), and distinctiveness (only towards this target?). High consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency → external attribution; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → internal.

Three biases recur:

  • Fundamental attribution error (FAE): over-crediting disposition and under-weighting situation when explaining others (Lee Ross's term).
  • Actor-observer bias (Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett, 1971): we explain our own behaviour situationally but others' dispositionally. "I failed because the exam was unfair; he failed because he is lazy."
  • Self-serving bias: we attribute our successes internally and our failures externally, protecting self-esteem.

Social influence

Conformity is yielding to real or imagined group pressure. Solomon Asch's line-judgement study had participants match a line to one of three comparisons; with confederates giving obviously wrong answers, about 75% conformed at least once, and roughly a third of responses matched the majority. Two motives underlie it: informational influence (others are a source of truth, especially in ambiguity) and normative influence (wanting acceptance).

Obedience is following a direct order from authority. Stanley Milgram's shock experiments found 65% of ordinary people delivered the maximum 450 volts to a screaming "learner" when instructed by an experimenter, demonstrating the power of situational authority over conscience.

Compliance techniques exploit consistency and contrast: the foot-in-the-door (a small request first makes a later large one likely) and the door-in-the-face (an unreasonable request first makes a modest follow-up seem fair).

Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) assigned students to be guards or prisoners; guards turned cruel and the study was halted after six days, showing how roles and situations override individual character. Muzafer Sherif earlier used the autokinetic effect (an illusory moving dot) to show groups converge on a shared norm, and his Robbers Cave study created intergroup hostility between boys' camps, then dissolved it through superordinate goals (shared tasks needing cooperation).

Group processes

Norman Triplett (1898) ran what is usually called the first social psychology experiment: cyclists rode faster with others present. This is social facilitation, the presence of others improves performance on easy/well-learned tasks but impairs it on hard ones (Zajonc explained it via heightened arousal boosting the dominant response).

Its mirror image is social loafing: people exert less effort in a group when individual contributions cannot be identified (Max Ringelmann's rope-pulling). Irving Janis coined groupthink, the drive for consensus in cohesive groups that suppresses dissent and produces bad decisions (symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, pressure on dissenters). Group polarization (Serge Moscovici and Marisa Zavalloni, 1969) is the tendency for group discussion to push members towards a more extreme version of their initial leaning. Deindividuation (Leon Festinger, Albert Pepitone and Theodore Newcomb, 1952) is the loss of self-awareness and restraint in crowds and anonymity.

The bystander effect, that people are less likely to help as the number of onlookers grows, was studied by John Darley and Bibb Latané after the 1964 Kitty Genovese case. The mechanism is diffusion of responsibility: with many present, each person assumes someone else will act.

Prejudice

Distinguish three levels: a stereotype is a belief (cognition), prejudice is a negative attitude/feeling (affect), and discrimination is unfair behaviour. Realistic conflict theory traces prejudice to competition over scarce resources (Sherif's Robbers Cave is the evidence). Henri Tajfel and John Turner's social identity theory shows that merely categorising people into groups, even arbitrary "minimal groups," produces in-group favouritism, because we derive self-esteem from group membership. Gordon Allport's contact hypothesis holds that prejudice falls when groups meet under four conditions: equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.

Aggression

John Dollard and Neal Miller's frustration-aggression hypothesis stated that frustration (a blocked goal) always leads to aggression, later softened by Leonard Berkowitz to "frustration creates readiness for aggression, triggered by cues." Albert Bandura's Bobo doll study showed social learning: children who watched an adult beat an inflatable doll imitated that aggression, proving behaviour is learned by observation without direct reinforcement.

Prosocial behaviour

Prosocial behaviour is any act intended to benefit another; altruism is helping with no expectation of reward. Daniel Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis argues that when we feel genuine empathy for someone, we help for their sake, not merely to relieve our own distress, supporting the existence of true altruism against purely egoistic accounts.

The Self

Social psychology treats the self as both the knower (the "I" who experiences) and the known (the "me" that is thought about). The topics below cover how people build a picture of themselves, evaluate it, and manage it in front of others.

From scratch: A self-concept is the total set of beliefs a person holds about who they are. It is organised into self-schemas (Hazel Markus, 1977): mental frameworks about specific traits (for example "I am athletic" or "I am shy") that guide how quickly and confidently we process self-relevant information. If a trait is central to you, you are said to be schematic on it; if it is irrelevant, you are aschematic.

Self-esteem is the evaluative and emotional part of the self: how positively or negatively a person judges their own worth. It differs from self-concept (the description) by adding a value judgement (the appraisal). Self-awareness is the state of focusing attention on the self as an object; it rises in front of a mirror, a camera, or an audience and tends to make behaviour match personal standards more closely.

Key theorists of the self

Who → what: Daryl Bem → self-perception theory: when internal cues are weak or ambiguous, people infer their own attitudes and feelings by observing their own behaviour, exactly as an outside observer would ("I have eaten three biscuits, so I must like them"). This is the reverse of the usual assumption that attitudes drive behaviour, and it is the main rival explanation to cognitive dissonance.
Who → what: Leon Festinger → social comparison theory: people evaluate their own opinions and abilities by comparing themselves with others, especially when objective yardsticks are missing. Upward comparison (with someone better) can motivate or deflate; downward comparison (with someone worse) can protect self-esteem.
Who → what: Mark Snyder → self-monitoring: a personality dimension describing how much people adjust their behaviour to fit the social situation. High self-monitors read the room and change accordingly; low self-monitors act from inner attitudes regardless of audience.

Self-serving biases and strategies

  • Self-serving bias: the tendency to take credit for successes (internal attribution) but blame failures on external causes. It protects self-esteem.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecy: an initially false expectation that causes the very behaviour that makes it come true. Demonstrated by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson in the "Pygmalion in the classroom" study, where teachers' raised expectations lifted pupils' actual performance.
  • Self-handicapping (Steven Berglas and Edward Jones, 1978): creating an obstacle to one's own success (for example not studying, or drinking before a test) so that failure can be blamed on the obstacle rather than on low ability, while success looks even more impressive.
Trap: Do not confuse self-serving bias (protecting the self after an outcome) with the fundamental attribution error (misjudging other people). Self-serving bias is about you; the attribution error is about them.

Social Cognition & Perception

Social cognition is the study of how people select, store, and use information about other people and social situations. Because we cannot process everything, we rely on schemas (organised knowledge structures) and mental shortcuts.

Impression formation

Who → what: Solomon Asch → classic impression-formation work. He showed a primacy effect: traits presented first weigh more heavily than later ones. He also identified central traits (such as "warm" versus "cold") that reshape the whole impression, versus peripheral traits (such as "polite" versus "blunt") that change it little.

The halo effect (Edward Thorndike, 1920) is a related bias: one positive quality (often physical attractiveness) leads us to assume other unrelated positive qualities, so good-looking people are rated as kinder or more competent without evidence.

Heuristics in social judgement

Heuristics are quick rules of thumb, described by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, that usually work but can bias judgement.

Stereotypes can be seen as cognitive shortcuts: category-based beliefs that save effort but overgeneralise and feed prejudice. Priming is the process by which a recent or frequent concept becomes temporarily more accessible and shapes how the next person or event is interpreted, often outside awareness.

Attribution, re-framed as social cognition

Two well-known attribution biases are really products of how we process social information:

  • Fundamental attribution error: overestimating personality and underestimating the situation when explaining others' behaviour.
  • Actor-observer bias: attributing our own behaviour to the situation, but others' behaviour to their character, partly because we literally see the situation from our own viewpoint.
RML tilt: Expect a matching-type item pairing each heuristic or bias with its definition, and a single-line question naming the theorist (Asch for central traits, Bem for self-perception, Snyder for self-monitoring). Learn the names, not just the ideas.

Interpersonal Attraction & Close Relationships

Determinants of attraction

  • Proximity and mere exposure: we like those who are physically near, largely through the mere exposure effect (Robert Zajonc): repeated contact with a stimulus increases liking.
  • Physical attractiveness: a powerful first-stage cue, amplified by the halo effect.
  • Similarity: shared attitudes, values, and background predict attraction far more than the folk idea that "opposites attract".
  • Reciprocity: we tend to like those who like us.

Theories of love and bonding

Who → what: Robert Sternberg → the triangular theory of love. Three components combine into different kinds of love: intimacy (closeness, warmth), passion (physical and emotional arousal), and commitment (the decision to maintain the relationship). All three together give consummate love.

A common distinction is between passionate love (intense, arousing, often early in a relationship) and companionate love (steady affection and deep friendship that endures).

Who → what: Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver → extended attachment theory (John Bowlby) to adults, proposing that adult romantic bonds echo infant attachment. Adults show secure, anxious (ambivalent), or avoidant styles that shape trust and intimacy.

Economic models of relationships

Social exchange theory analyses relationships as a ledger of rewards minus costs, judged against a comparison level (what we expect) and a comparison level for alternatives (what else is available). Equity theory adds that people are most satisfied when the ratio of their own outcomes to inputs feels roughly fair compared with the partner's.

Key. Attraction is driven by proximity, similarity, physical cues, and reciprocity; love is best split into Sternberg's intimacy, passion, and commitment; long relationships run on companionate love, fair exchange, and secure attachment.

Applied Social Psychology (Brief)

Applied social psychology uses these principles to solve real problems in specific settings.

  • Health: attitude-change and social-norm campaigns promote vaccination, exercise, and safe behaviour; perceived social support buffers stress and improves recovery.
  • Environment: feedback, commitment, and descriptive norms ("most guests reuse their towels") are used to cut energy use and encourage conservation.
  • Law: research on eyewitness memory, line-up construction, jury decision-making, and conformity within juries informs fairer legal procedures.
Recap: The self is described by self-concept and self-schemas, valued through self-esteem, and inferred through Bem's self-perception and Festinger's social comparison, then defended by self-serving bias and self-handicapping. Social cognition runs on schemas, Asch's primacy and central traits, the halo effect, and the availability, representativeness, and anchoring heuristics. Attraction rests on proximity, similarity, and reciprocity; Sternberg's triangle and Hazan and Phillip Shaver's adult attachment explain love; and applied social psychology carries all of this into health, environment, and law.

Attitude Formation and the Deep Theories of Attitude Change

An attitude is a learned, relatively enduring evaluation of a person, object, or idea that predisposes a person to respond in a favourable or unfavourable way. Psychologists describe attitudes with the ABC model: an Affective component (feelings), a Behavioural component (action tendencies), and a Cognitive component (beliefs). The existing chapter introduced the elaboration likelihood model and dissonance in outline. This section teaches the full family of change theories from scratch so that no exam question on the topic can catch you.

From scratch: The core puzzle of attitude research is consistency. Humans dislike holding beliefs, feelings, and behaviours that clash with one another. Almost every classic theory below is a different answer to one question: what does a person do when two mental elements do not fit together?

Fritz Heider: Balance Theory

Fritz Heider (1946, 1958) proposed balance theory, the earliest of the cognitive-consistency theories. It concerns a triad of relations between a Person (P), an Other (O), and an attitude object (X). Each link between two elements is either positive (liking, agreement) or negative (disliking, disagreement). A triad is balanced when the product of the three signs is positive, and unbalanced when the product is negative. Balanced states are stable and pleasant; unbalanced states create tension that pushes the person to change one relation to restore balance.

Example: You (P) like a friend (O). Your friend praises a film (X) that you dislike. The signs are P–O positive, O–X positive, P–X negative; the product is negative, so the triad is unbalanced. You can restore balance by starting to like the film, by deciding you dislike the friend, or by deciding the friend does not really care for the film.

Rule: "A friend of my friend is my friend; an enemy of my enemy is my friend." Balanced triads have an even number (zero or two) of negative links.

Leon Festinger: Cognitive Dissonance and the Classic $1 versus $20 Study

Leon Festinger (1957) argued that when a person holds two psychologically inconsistent cognitions, an uncomfortable drive state called cognitive dissonance arises, and the person is motivated to reduce it by changing a cognition, adding new consonant cognitions, or trivialising the conflict. The landmark demonstration is Festinger and James Carlsmith (1959). Participants performed a genuinely boring peg-turning task, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell a waiting participant that the task was enjoyable. Counter-intuitively, the $1 group later rated the task as more enjoyable than the $20 group.

From scratch: The $20 participants had a large external justification ("I lied for the money"), so little dissonance. The $1 participants had insufficient justification for lying, so they resolved the dissonance between "the task was dull" and "I said it was fun" by genuinely revising their attitude: "it actually was quite interesting." This is induced compliance, and it shows that attitudes follow behaviour when external justification is weak.

Dissonance also explains effort justification (Aronson and Mills, 1959: people who endured a severe initiation valued a dull group more), post-decision dissonance (chosen alternatives become more attractive after a decision, the "spreading of alternatives"), and the free-choice paradigm.

Daryl Bem: Self-Perception Theory

Daryl Bem (1967) offered a rival explanation for the same findings. Self-perception theory holds that people do not always have privileged access to their own attitudes; instead, when internal cues are weak or ambiguous, they infer their attitudes by observing their own behaviour and the situation, exactly as an outside observer would. The $1 participant reasons, "I said it was interesting for almost no money, so I must have found it interesting." No tension or drive is required.

Trap: Do not treat dissonance and self-perception as simply "right versus wrong." The modern resolution (Fazio, Zanna, Cooper) is that dissonance operates when behaviour is discrepant with a clearly held attitude (producing real arousal), while self-perception operates in the ambiguous middle range where the person has no strong prior attitude.

Carl Hovland: The Yale Attitude-Change Approach

Carl Hovland and the Yale Communication and Attitude Change Program (1950s) launched the systematic study of persuasion, framed as "who says what to whom." They isolated three sets of variables. Source factors: credibility (expertise and trustworthiness) and attractiveness. Hovland and Weiss demonstrated the sleeper effect: a message from a low-credibility source has little immediate impact, but its persuasive power can grow over time as the message dissociates from its discredited source. Message factors: one-sided versus two-sided arguments, order of presentation (primacy versus recency), and emotional appeals. Audience factors: intelligence, self-esteem, and initial position.

Fear Appeals

A fear appeal is a persuasive message that arouses fear about a threat to motivate protective behaviour (anti-smoking, road-safety, health campaigns). Early work suggested moderate fear works best, and that fear appeals succeed only when they also provide a clear, achievable recommendation for avoiding the threat. Ronald Rogers systematised this in Protection Motivation Theory: persuasion depends on perceived severity of the threat, perceived vulnerability, response efficacy (the action works), and self-efficacy (I can do the action). Very high fear without an efficacy route triggers defensive avoidance rather than action.

The Attitude–Behaviour Gap: Reasoned Action and Planned Behaviour

Attitudes often predict behaviour poorly (the famous LaPiere study of the 1930s found restaurateurs served a Chinese couple in person yet said in writing they would not). Martin Fishbein and Icek Ajzen addressed this with the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA): the immediate predictor of behaviour is behavioural intention, which is determined by (1) one's attitude toward the specific behaviour and (2) subjective norms (perceived social pressure). Ajzen later extended it into the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) by adding perceived behavioural control (how easy or hard the person believes the behaviour is), which improves prediction for behaviours not fully under voluntary control.

Rule: Attitudes predict behaviour best when they are specific to the behaviour, strong and accessible, based on direct experience, and measured close in time to the action (the principle of compatibility).

Attribution: How People Explain Behaviour, in Depth

Attribution is the process of inferring the causes of one's own and others' behaviour. The existing chapter summarised the topic; here each formal model is taught in full.

Fritz Heider: Naive Psychology and the Dispositional–Situational Split

Fritz Heider (1958), often called the father of attribution theory, described ordinary people as "naive scientists" who seek stable causes for events. His key distinction is between internal (dispositional) causes, located in the person (ability, effort, personality), and external (situational) causes, located in the environment (luck, task difficulty, social pressure).

Harold Kelley: The Covariation Model and Causal Schemata

Harold Kelley (1967) proposed the covariation model for cases where we have information across time and situations. We attribute an effect to the factor with which it covaries, using three kinds of information:

DistinctivenessDoes this person react this way only to this stimulus?External (stimulus) cause

High consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency yields an external attribution; low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency yields an internal (person) attribution. Kelley added causal schemata for single-observation cases, including the discounting principle (the role of a given cause is discounted if other plausible causes are present) and the augmenting principle (a cause is weighted more heavily if it produced an effect despite an inhibitory factor).

Edward Jones and Keith Davis: Correspondent Inference Theory

Edward Jones and Keith Davis (1965) explained how we infer that a behaviour corresponds to a stable disposition from a single act. Correspondent inferences are stronger when the behaviour is freely chosen, produces non-common effects (outcomes unique to that choice), is low in social desirability (unexpected behaviour is more informative), and is high in hedonic relevance to the perceiver.

Bernard Weiner: Attribution of Achievement

Bernard Weiner (1972, 1985) applied attribution to success and failure in achievement settings. He classified causes along dimensions: locus (internal versus external), stability (stable versus unstable over time), and controllability. The four classic causes are ability (internal, stable), effort (internal, unstable), task difficulty (external, stable), and luck (external, unstable). The stability dimension governs expectancy of future outcomes; the locus and controllability dimensions govern emotions such as pride, shame, and gratitude. Attributing failure to lack of effort (unstable, controllable) is adaptive; attributing it to lack of ability (stable) fosters learned helplessness (Martin Seligman and Steven Maier, 1967).

Systematic Attribution Biases

Fundamental attribution error (Lee Ross, 1977): observers overestimate dispositional causes and underestimate situational ones when explaining others' behaviour. Actor–observer bias (Jones and Nisbett): we attribute our own behaviour to the situation but others' identical behaviour to their disposition, partly because of differing perceptual focus. Self-serving bias: we attribute our successes internally and our failures externally, protecting self-esteem. Just-world hypothesis (Melvin Lerner, 1980): the belief that the world is fair and people get what they deserve, which leads observers to blame innocent victims ("she must have done something").

RML tilt: Expect a matching item pairing the theorist to the model: Heider → naive psychology/balance, Kelley → covariation (consensus/distinctiveness/consistency), Jones and Davis → correspondent inference, Weiner → achievement attribution, Lerner → just-world.

Social Influence: Conformity, Obedience, and Minority Power in Depth

Muzafer Sherif and Solomon Asch: Two Faces of Conformity

Muzafer Sherif (1935) used the autokinetic effect (a stationary point of light appears to move in a dark room). Individual estimates of the movement converged over trials into a shared group norm that persisted even when participants were later tested alone, demonstrating informational social influence in an ambiguous situation. Solomon Asch (1951) removed the ambiguity: participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard, an easy perceptual task. With confederates unanimously giving a wrong answer, about 75% of participants conformed at least once and roughly a third of responses overall were conforming, revealing normative social influence, conforming to be accepted despite knowing the answer.

Rule: Informational influence produces genuine private acceptance and rises with ambiguity; normative influence produces public compliance to gain approval and falls sharply when even one ally breaks the unanimity (Asch found a single dissenter cut conformity dramatically).

Stanley Milgram: Obedience and Its Variations

Stanley Milgram (1963) studied destructive obedience to authority. Participants ("teachers") were ordered by an experimenter to deliver escalating "shocks" to a learner. In the baseline, 65% went to the maximum 450 volts. Milgram ran many variations that reveal the causes: obedience fell when the experimenter gave orders by telephone, when the study moved from Yale to a run-down office, when the learner was in the same room (proximity), and above all when a peer confederate defied the experimenter. Milgram explained results through the agentic state, in which a person sees themselves as an instrument carrying out another's wishes and shifts responsibility upward.

Philip Zimbardo: The Stanford Prison Experiment

Philip Zimbardo (1971) randomly assigned students to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a simulated prison. The study, planned for two weeks, was halted after six days because guards became abusive and prisoners showed extreme stress. Zimbardo argued that situational forces and assigned social roles, plus deindividuation, can override individual dispositions. (Note the modern critique: demand characteristics and explicit guard coaching mean the effect was less "spontaneous" than first claimed.)

Serge Moscovici: Minority Influence

Serge Moscovici (1969) showed that a numerical minority can influence a majority through consistency (holding firmly to a position over time and within the group). In his blue-green colour study, a consistent minority calling blue slides "green" led some majority members to shift. Minority influence tends to produce conversion (deep, private, delayed attitude change through the validation process) rather than the surface compliance produced by majorities.

Compliance Techniques and Reactance

Foot-in-the-door (Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser): agreeing to a small request raises the chance of agreeing to a larger one later, via the consistency principle. Door-in-the-face (Robert Cialdini): a large request is refused, then a smaller one is accepted, via reciprocal concessions. Reactance (Jack Brehm, 1966): when people feel a freedom is threatened or removed, they experience an aroused motivational state to reassert it, sometimes doing the very opposite of what was demanded.

Group Processes in Depth

Norman Triplett and Robert Zajonc: Social Facilitation

Norman Triplett (1898) ran what is often called social psychology's first experiment, finding cyclists rode faster when racing others. Robert Zajonc (1965) resolved contradictory findings with drive theory: the mere presence of others raises arousal, which strengthens the dominant response. On easy or well-learned tasks the dominant response is correct, so presence improves performance (social facilitation); on hard or novel tasks the dominant response is often wrong, so presence impairs performance (social inhibition).

Bibb Latane: Social Loafing

Bibb Latane, Kipling Williams, and Stephen Harkins (1979) documented social loafing: individuals exert less effort on a collective task (rope-pulling, clapping, shouting) when their individual contribution cannot be identified. It is reduced by making contributions identifiable, increasing task meaningfulness, and keeping groups small.

Irving Janis: Groupthink

Irving Janis (1972) coined groupthink: a mode of thinking in highly cohesive, insulated groups under a directive leader where the drive for unanimity overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives. Symptoms include the illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalisation, belief in the group's morality, stereotyping of out-groups, self-censorship, an illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters, and self-appointed "mindguards." Historical cases include the Bay of Pigs invasion.

Group Polarization, Risky Shift, and Deindividuation

Group polarization is the tendency for group discussion to strengthen the members' initially dominant leaning, producing a more extreme group decision; the risky shift is its earliest-noticed form (Stoner). Deindividuation (built on Le Bon and developed by Festinger, Pepitone, Newcomb, and Zimbardo) is a loss of self-awareness and individual accountability in groups or anonymous conditions, lowering restraints on impulsive and antisocial behaviour.

Muzafer Sherif: The Robbers Cave Study

Muzafer Sherif (1954) ran the Robbers Cave field study with two groups of boys at a summer camp. Competition for scarce resources rapidly produced hostility (support for realistic conflict theory), and simple contact did not reduce it. Only superordinate goals, shared aims that required both groups to cooperate (fixing the water supply, pulling a stalled truck), reduced intergroup conflict.

Kurt Lewin: Leadership Styles

Kurt Lewin, Ronald Lippitt, and Ralph White (1939) compared autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire leadership in boys' clubs. Democratic leadership produced the most satisfaction and sustained productivity; autocratic leadership produced high output only under supervision plus aggression or apathy; laissez-faire produced the least work.

Prejudice and Intergroup Relations in Depth

Realistic Conflict Theory

Realistic conflict theory (Sherif; Donald Campbell) holds that intergroup hostility arises from real competition over limited resources (jobs, land, status). Robbers Cave is its central demonstration.

Henri Tajfel: Social Identity Theory and the Minimal Group Paradigm

Henri Tajfel and John Turner (1979) proposed social identity theory: part of our self-concept comes from group memberships, and we boost self-esteem through favourable in-group/out-group comparisons. In the minimal group paradigm, Tajfel showed that merely categorising strangers into arbitrary groups (even by a coin toss or a preference for one painter) was enough to trigger in-group favouritism in resource allocation, with no competition or prior contact required. The process runs categorisation → identification → comparison.

Gordon Allport: The Contact Hypothesis

Gordon Allport (1954) argued that contact between groups reduces prejudice only under four conditions: equal status within the situation, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and support of authorities, law, or custom. Mere contact without these can worsen prejudice.

Claude Steele: Stereotype Threat

Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995) demonstrated stereotype threat: when a negative stereotype about one's group is made salient, the fear of confirming it disrupts performance. African-American students scored lower on a test framed as diagnostic of ability, but not when the same test was framed as non-diagnostic; the effect also appears for women in mathematics.

Scapegoat Theory and the Authoritarian Personality

Scapegoat theory links prejudice to displaced frustration onto a weaker out-group. Theodor Adorno and colleagues (1950) proposed the authoritarian personality, measured by the F-scale, a syndrome of rigid conventionalism, submission to authority, and hostility to out-groups, rooted in harsh childhood discipline.

Aggression: Theories from Scratch

John Dollard, Neal Miller and colleagues (1939) proposed the frustration–aggression hypothesis: frustration (blocked goal-directed behaviour) always produces a readiness to aggress, and aggression is always preceded by frustration. Leonard Berkowitz revised it: frustration produces anger, which yields aggression only when aggressive cues are present (the weapons effect). Albert Bandura's Bobo doll studies (1961) showed aggression is learned through observation and imitation of models, especially when the model is rewarded (social learning theory). The General Aggression Model (Anderson and Bushman) integrates person and situation inputs acting through arousal, cognition, and affect. The catharsis hypothesis, the idea that venting aggression discharges it, is largely a myth: acting aggressively tends to increase, not decrease, later aggression.

Prosocial Behaviour in Depth

John Darley and Bibb Latane: The Bystander Effect

Spurred by the Kitty Genovese case, John Darley and Bibb Latane (1968, 1970) showed the bystander effect: the more bystanders present, the less likely any one of them helps. Their five-step decision model requires the bystander to (1) notice the event, (2) interpret it as an emergency (blocked by pluralistic ignorance, where each person, seeing others calm, decides it is not serious), (3) assume responsibility (blocked by diffusion of responsibility across the group), (4) know how to help, and (5) decide to act.

Models of Helping

The arousal: cost–reward model (Piliavin) holds that witnessing distress creates unpleasant arousal that the bystander is motivated to reduce, weighing the costs of helping against the costs of not helping. Daniel Batson's empathy–altruism hypothesis argues that feeling genuine empathy for a sufferer produces truly altruistic motivation to help for the other's sake, not merely to relieve one's own distress. Evolutionary accounts add kin selection (helping relatives preserves shared genes) and reciprocal altruism (helping non-kin who may help back).

Interpersonal Attraction and Close Relationships in Depth

Robert Sternberg: The Triangular Theory of Love

Robert Sternberg (1986) proposed that love has three components: intimacy (closeness and warmth), passion (physical and romantic arousal), and commitment (the decision to maintain the relationship). Their combinations yield seven kinds of love: liking (intimacy alone), infatuation (passion alone), empty love (commitment alone), romantic love (intimacy + passion), companionate love (intimacy + commitment), fatuous love (passion + commitment), and consummate love (all three).

Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver: Adult Attachment Styles

Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) extended Bowlby and Ainsworth's infant attachment to adult romantic relationships, describing three styles: secure (comfortable with closeness and trust), anxious/ambivalent (craves closeness, fears abandonment, clingy), and avoidant (uncomfortable with intimacy and dependence). Adult attachment style predicts jealousy, trust, and relationship stability.

Economic and Perceptual Determinants

Social exchange theory (John Thibaut and Kelley) treats relationships as an exchange in which people seek to maximise rewards and minimise costs, judging satisfaction against a comparison level and commitment against the comparison level for alternatives. Equity theory (Walster/Hatfield) adds that people are most satisfied when the ratio of each partner's outcomes to inputs feels fair, and both under-benefited and over-benefited partners feel distress. The matching hypothesis holds that people tend to form relationships with others of similar physical attractiveness. The mere exposure effect (Robert Zajonc) shows that repeated exposure to a stimulus, including a person, increases liking, which is why proximity is such a strong predictor of friendship.
