Psychotherapy is the planned, systematic use of a trained relationship and psychological methods to relieve emotional distress, change unhelpful thoughts, feelings and behaviour, and help a person live more fully. It is sometimes called "the talking cure," a phrase that goes back to one of Sigmund Freud's early patients. Counselling is a close cousin: it also uses a helping relationship, but tends to focus on specific problems, decisions and adjustment rather than deep personality change. This whole section builds each major approach from the ground up, always naming who developed what, because "match the therapy to its founder" is a favourite question type in this paper.

6.1 What psychotherapy is, and what all therapies share

There are hundreds of named therapies, yet research repeatedly shows they work partly through the same underlying ingredients. These shared ingredients are called the common factors. Saul Rosenzweig first argued in 1936 that different therapies succeed for reasons they hold in common, and he jokingly used the Dodo bird from Alice in Wonderland ("Everybody has won, and all must have prizes") to name the finding that most bona fide therapies produce roughly similar outcomes. This is called the Dodo bird verdict.

  • The therapeutic alliance (working alliance). The bond of trust between client and therapist, plus their agreement on the goals and tasks of therapy. Edward Bordin described its three parts as bond, goals and tasks. The alliance is one of the strongest predictors of good outcome, regardless of the school of therapy.
  • An emotionally charged, confiding relationship with a helping person.
  • A healing setting that raises the client's expectation of being helped.
  • A rationale or "myth" – a believable explanation of the problem.
  • A ritual or procedure that both parties believe in and carry out.
  • Hope, catharsis (emotional release) and new learning.
Key. Common factors (especially the therapeutic alliance) explain a large share of why therapy works. The specific techniques matter too, but no technique works well without the relationship carrying it.

Therapies also differ on two practical dimensions you should be able to name:

  • Directive vs non-directive. A directive therapist actively guides, teaches, assigns homework and challenges thoughts (behaviour therapy, Aaron Beck's and Albert Ellis's cognitive work). A non-directive therapist mainly follows the client, reflecting and clarifying rather than steering (Carl Rogers's person-centred therapy is the classic example).
  • Individual vs group. Individual therapy is one client with one therapist. Group therapy treats several clients together, adding unique healing forces such as universality and peer feedback (see Irvin Yalom below). Related formats include couples and family therapy.
Why. Examiners love the directive/non-directive split because it cleanly separates the humanistic camp (non-directive) from the behavioural and cognitive camps (directive). Anchor Carl Rogers at the non-directive pole and you can place everyone else relative to him.

6.2 Psychodynamic therapy: Sigmund Freud and after

Who → what. Sigmund Freud developed psychoanalysis, the first formal system of psychotherapy, in Vienna around the 1890s–1900s.

Freud held that much of mental life is unconscious and that current symptoms grow from unresolved childhood conflicts, especially around sexual and aggressive urges. The aim of psychoanalysis is to make the unconscious conscious, so that buried conflict can be understood and worked through. The mind, in his structural model, has three parts:

  • Id – the primitive, unconscious reservoir of drives, ruled by the pleasure principle (wants satisfaction now).
  • Ego – the reality-based executive that mediates between id, superego and the outer world, ruled by the reality principle.
  • Superego – the internalised moral conscience and ideals, largely from the parents.

Core techniques of psychoanalysis:

  • Free association. The client says whatever comes to mind, without censoring, so hidden material surfaces.
  • Dream analysis. Freud called dreams "the royal road to the unconscious." The remembered dream (manifest content) is interpreted for its disguised meaning (latent content).
  • Transference. The client unconsciously transfers feelings about important early figures (often parents) onto the therapist. Analysing it is central to the cure.
  • Resistance. Anything that blocks the work – falling silent, missing sessions, changing the subject – signals a defended conflict worth exploring.
  • Interpretation. The analyst offers tentative explanations that link present feelings to unconscious past conflict, timed for when the client can accept them.
  • Working through. Repeatedly re-examining the same conflict across many situations until insight becomes lasting change.

Defense mechanisms are the ego's unconscious strategies for keeping anxiety-provoking material out of awareness. Freud named them and his daughter Anna Freud catalogued them systematically:

  • Repression – pushing threatening thoughts out of consciousness (the master defense).
  • Denial – refusing to accept an external reality.
  • Projection – attributing one's own unacceptable impulses to someone else.
  • Displacement – redirecting an impulse onto a safer target (shouting at the dog after a bad day at work).
  • Sublimation – channelling an impulse into a socially valued activity (the healthiest defense).
  • Reaction formation – turning an impulse into its opposite.
  • Rationalisation – inventing acceptable reasons for unacceptable behaviour.
  • Regression – retreating to an earlier, more childish stage.
  • Intellectualisation – dealing with a threat through abstract thought, stripped of feeling.

The neo-Freudians kept the idea of the unconscious but broke from Freud's heavy emphasis on sexuality:

Who → what. Carl Jung founded analytical psychology, adding the collective unconscious and its archetypes, plus the ideas of introversion/extraversion and individuation.
Who → what. Alfred Adler founded individual psychology, stressing the inferiority complex, striving for superiority, birth order and social interest.
Who → what. Melanie Klein pioneered object relations and child play therapy, focusing on the infant's inner world of internalised "objects" (people). Later object-relations and attachment thinkers include Donald Winnicott and John Bowlby.
Trap. Do not confuse the founders. Sigmund Freud = psychoanalysis; Carl Jung = analytical psychology; Alfred Adler = individual psychology. "Individual psychology" is Adler's label and has nothing to do with individual (one-to-one) therapy format.

6.3 Behaviour therapy

Behaviour therapy applies learning theory to change behaviour directly, holding that maladaptive behaviour is learned and can therefore be unlearned. It is present-focused, directive and measurable. It has two engines: classical conditioning (Ivan Pavlov) and operant conditioning (B. F. Skinner).

Who → what. Mary Cover Jones is called the "mother of behaviour therapy." Working with the boy "Peter," she reduced a child's fear of a rabbit by gradually pairing the feared object with pleasant eating – an early demonstration of counter-conditioning that inspired later desensitization.

Classical-conditioning based methods:

Who → what. Joseph Wolpe developed systematic desensitization, built on reciprocal inhibition (you cannot be relaxed and anxious at the same time).
  1. Teach deep relaxation (often progressive muscle relaxation).
  2. Build an anxiety hierarchy, ranking feared situations from mild to terrifying.
  3. While relaxed, the client imagines each step, moving up the ladder only when the lower step no longer triggers anxiety. Relaxation replaces fear at every rung.
  • Exposure therapy. Facing the feared stimulus (in imagination, in vivo, or via virtual reality) until anxiety fades through habituation and extinction.
  • Flooding. Intense, prolonged exposure to the most feared situation at full strength (no gentle ladder), continued until the fear burns out.
  • Aversion therapy. Pairing an unwanted behaviour (drinking, smoking) with an unpleasant stimulus so the behaviour loses its appeal (a drug that causes nausea with alcohol).

Operant-conditioning based methods rest on B.F. Skinner's principle that behaviour is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement increases behaviour, punishment decreases it):

Who → what. B.F. Skinner established operant conditioning, the basis for token economies (Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin, 1968), shaping and contingency management.
  • Token economy. Desired behaviours earn tokens that are later exchanged for rewards; widely used on wards and in classrooms.
  • Shaping. Reinforcing successive approximations that get closer and closer to a target behaviour.
  • Contingency management. Systematically arranging rewards and consequences so they follow (are contingent on) specific behaviours; a contingency contract writes this down.
Why. Classical-conditioning methods change reflexive, emotional responses like fear; operant methods change voluntary behaviour through its consequences. Sort any technique by asking: is it treating an automatic fear (classical) or a chosen action being rewarded/punished (operant)?

6.4 Cognitive therapy and CBT

Cognitive approaches say it is not events themselves but our interpretations of events that drive our feelings. Change the thinking and the emotion changes. Two founders dominate.

Who → what. Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy, originally for depression, later expanded into Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT).

Beck argued that depressed people are trapped in the cognitive triad: negative views of the self, the world, and the future. These are fed by automatic thoughts (quick, involuntary negative appraisals) and sustained by cognitive distortions, systematic errors in thinking such as:

  • All-or-nothing (black-and-white) thinking.
  • Overgeneralisation – one setback means "always."
  • Catastrophising – expecting the worst.
  • Mental filter – focusing only on the negative.
  • Personalisation – blaming oneself for things outside one's control.
  • "Should" statements and mind-reading.

Beck's therapist uses Socratic questioning (guided discovery through gentle questions), collaborative empiricism (client and therapist test beliefs together like scientists), thought records and behavioural experiments to examine and revise these thoughts.

Who → what. Albert Ellis developed Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT), built on the ABC model.
  • A – Activating event. Something happens.
  • B – Belief. An irrational belief about the event ("I must be approved by everyone").
  • C – Consequence. The emotional and behavioural result.

Ellis added D – Disputing the irrational belief and E – the new Effect. His signature target is "musturbation" – rigid demands phrased as musts and shoulds. His style is far more forceful and confrontational than Beck's gentle questioning.

Trap. Aaron Beck vs Albert Ellis is a classic exam contrast. Both are cognitive and both use the idea that beliefs cause distress, but: Aaron Beck uses gentle Socratic questioning / collaborative empiricism and speaks of automatic thoughts and the cognitive triad (therapy: cognitive therapy / CBT). Albert Ellis is directive and confrontational, uses the ABC(DE) model and attacks irrational beliefs / "musts" (therapy: REBT). Remember Ellis came first (1950s) and Beck a little later (1960s).

6.5 Humanistic and existential therapies

These approaches reject both the determinism of psychoanalysis and the mechanism of behaviourism (John B. Watson, 1913). They see people as basically good, free and driven toward growth. The therapist's job is to create conditions for that growth rather than to fix or condition the client.

Who → what. Carl Rogers developed person-centred (client-centred) therapy, the model non-directive humanistic therapy.

Rogers believed every person has an actualizing tendency, an inbuilt drive to grow toward their full potential, which unfolds when the relationship offers three core conditions:

  • Unconditional positive regard – warm, non-judgemental acceptance of the client as a person.
  • Empathy – accurately sensing and reflecting the client's inner world.
  • Congruence (genuineness) – the therapist is real and transparent, not hiding behind a professional mask.
Key. Rogers's three core conditions – unconditional positive regard, empathy, congruence – are among the most tested facts in this whole topic. Memorise all three by name.
Who → what. Fritz Perls (with Laura Perls) developed Gestalt therapy, focused on the here-and-now and taking responsibility for present experience.

Gestalt therapy aims for awareness of what one is feeling and doing right now, completing "unfinished business." Its best-known technique is the empty chair, where the client speaks to an imagined person (or part of the self) seated in an empty chair, then switches seats to reply.

Who → what. Victor Frankl developed logotherapy, the "third Viennese school," centred on the will to meaning – the idea that finding meaning, even in suffering, is the primary human drive. Frankl drew on his own survival in Nazi concentration camps.

Existential therapy (associated with Rollo May and, in the group/existential blend, Irvin Yalom) confronts the "givens" of existence – death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness – and helps the client live authentically in the face of them. Logotherapy is one existential branch.

6.6 Third-wave and newer approaches

The "third wave" of behaviour therapy keeps CBT's rigor but adds acceptance, mindfulness and values rather than only trying to change thoughts.

Who → what. Marsha Linehan developed Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), first for chronically suicidal clients and borderline personality disorder. It balances (the "dialectic" of) acceptance and change, and teaches four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness.
Who → what. Steven Hayes developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), which teaches psychological flexibility: accept inner experiences, defuse from thoughts, and commit to action guided by personal values.
  • Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT). Developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale to prevent relapse in recurrent depression; it blends CBT with the mindfulness training of Jon Kabat-Zinn's MBSR.
Who → what. William Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed Motivational Interviewing (MI), a collaborative, client-centred style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change, especially in addictions. It works with ambivalence and "rolls with resistance" rather than arguing.
Who → what. James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente developed the Transtheoretical Model / Stages of Change, often paired with Motivational Interviewing.
  1. Precontemplation – not yet considering change.
  2. Contemplation – weighing it up.
  3. Preparation – getting ready to act.
  4. Action – actively changing.
  5. Maintenance – sustaining the change (with possible relapse and recycling).

6.7 Family and group therapy

Systemic and family therapies treat the family as the unit, not the individual. The idea is that a symptom in one member (the "identified patient") often expresses a problem in the whole family system.

Who → what. Salvador Minuchin developed structural family therapy, mapping the family's structure – its subsystems, boundaries (too rigid or too enmeshed) and hierarchies – and actively restructuring them.
Who → what. Murray Bowen developed Bowenian (intergenerational) family systems therapy, with key ideas of differentiation of self, triangulation, and the multigenerational transmission of patterns (mapped on a genogram).
Who → what. Irvin Yalom is the key name for group therapy, cataloguing the therapeutic factors that make groups heal.

Yalom's therapeutic factors include universality ("I am not the only one"), instillation of hope, imparting information, altruism (helping others helps you), group cohesiveness, interpersonal learning, catharsis, and the corrective recapitulation of the primary family group (the group re-plays and repairs old family dynamics).

Trap. Match the family-therapy founders precisely: Salvador Minuchin = structural (boundaries, enmeshment); Bowen = intergenerational (differentiation, triangles, genogram). For group work, the name to give is Irvin Yalom and his therapeutic factors (universality is the most-quoted one).

6.8 The counselling process, and counselling vs psychotherapy

Counselling typically unfolds in identifiable stages. A common way to remember them:

  1. Rapport / relationship building. Establish trust, warmth and the working alliance.
  2. Assessment / exploration. Understand the problem, history and context; clarify what the client wants.
  3. Goal-setting. Agree clear, realistic goals together.
  4. Intervention / working phase. Apply the chosen techniques and support change.
  5. Termination and follow-up. Consolidate gains, prepare for ending, and plan for maintenance and relapse prevention.

Effective counselling rests on micro-skills (systematised by Allen Ivey and echoing Carl Rogers). Learn these named skills:

  • Attending – posture, eye contact and presence (often taught with the SOLER acronym).
  • Active listening.
  • Open and closed questions.
  • Reflection of feeling – naming the emotion the client expresses.
  • Paraphrasing – restating content in your own words.
  • Summarising.
  • Clarifying, confronting (gently), and silence.

How counselling differs from psychotherapy. The two overlap heavily and use many of the same skills, but examiners expect you to state the classic distinctions:

6.10 Cognitive and behavioural techniques in depth

The earlier subsections introduced cognitive therapy and behaviour therapy as broad families. This subsection goes inside them, to the actual techniques a therapist uses in the room. Two names dominate the cognitive side: Aaron Temkin Beck, an American psychiatrist who built Cognitive Therapy, and Albert Ellis, an American clinical psychologist who built Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy. On the behavioural side the central name is Joseph Wolpe, a South African psychiatrist who created systematic desensitization. Learn to tell these approaches apart, because entrance papers love to swap them.

Aaron Beck's cognitive model

Who → what. Aaron Temkin Beck (1921–2021) developed Cognitive Therapy in the 1960s, originally for depression. He proposed that emotional disorders are maintained not by events themselves but by the biased thoughts a person has about those events.

Beck's model rests on three linked ideas. First, the cognitive triad: in depression a person holds negative views of the self ("I am worthless"), the world or current experience ("everything is against me"), and the future ("nothing will ever improve"). These three negative streams feed one another. Second, automatic thoughts: rapid, involuntary appraisals that pop into the mind without deliberate reasoning. They feel true and go unquestioned, which is exactly why they are powerful. Third, deeper schemas or core beliefs: stable underlying assumptions ("I am unlovable") laid down early in life that generate the automatic thoughts.

Beck catalogued recurring errors of reasoning, called cognitive distortions. A working list every candidate should recognise:

  • All-or-nothing thinking (also called black-and-white or dichotomous thinking): seeing things in only two categories, with no middle ground. "If I am not perfect I am a total failure."
  • Overgeneralization: treating one negative event as a never-ending pattern. "I failed this test, so I fail at everything."
  • Catastrophizing (magnification): expecting the worst possible outcome and treating it as certain. "If I stumble in the interview my whole career is over."
  • Personalization: taking responsibility for events that were not one's fault. "My friend is quiet today; it must be because of me."
  • Mental filter (selective abstraction): focusing on a single negative detail while ignoring the whole picture. Dwelling on one critical remark inside a page of praise.
  • Mind reading and fortune telling: assuming you know others' thoughts, or predicting the future negatively, without evidence.
  • Emotional reasoning: treating a feeling as proof of fact. "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong."
  • "Should" statements: rigid rules about how oneself and others must behave, producing guilt or anger when broken.
  • Labelling: attaching a fixed global label to oneself. "I am a loser," rather than "I made a mistake."

Beck's therapy trains the client to catch automatic thoughts, test them against evidence, and generate more balanced alternatives, often recorded on a thought record (a worksheet with columns for the situation, the emotion, the automatic thought, evidence for and against, and a balanced response).

Albert Ellis and Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy

Who → what. Albert Ellis (1913–2007) founded Rational Emotive Therapy in 1955, later renamed Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT). It is usually considered the earliest form of cognitive behaviour therapy, predating Beck's clinical work.

Ellis's central tool is the ABC framework:

  • A – Activating event: the situation that seems to trigger distress.
  • B – Belief: the thought or, more precisely, the irrational belief the person holds about the event.
  • C – Consequence: the emotional and behavioural result.

Ellis's key claim is that A does not cause C. Instead B causes C. The same event (failing an exam) produces despair in one person and mild disappointment in another, because their beliefs differ. Ellis argued that distress flows from rigid, absolute demands he summed up as "musts" (which he playfully called musturbation): "I must succeed," "others must treat me well," "the world must be fair." From these grow secondary irrational beliefs such as awfulizing ("it is awful and unbearable"), low frustration tolerance, and global self-rating ("I am worthless").

To ABC he added D and E. D – Disputing: actively challenging the irrational belief. The therapist asks whether the belief is logical, whether there is evidence for it, and whether it is helpful. E – Effective new belief (and new emotion): a flexible preference ("I would strongly prefer to succeed, but I can stand it if I do not") replacing the rigid demand. REBT disputing is comparatively direct and confrontational, in keeping with Ellis's style.

Trap. Beck vs Albert Ellis. Beck → Cognitive Therapy, cognitive triad, automatic thoughts and distortions, a gentle collaborative-empirical style using guided discovery. Ellis → REBT, the ABC(DE) model, irrational "must" beliefs, a more direct disputing style. Both attack thinking, but the labels and vocabulary are distinct. Do not credit the ABC model to Beck or the cognitive triad to Ellis.

Socratic questioning and guided discovery

Socratic questioning is the signature conversational method of Beckian cognitive therapy. Rather than telling the client that a thought is wrong, the therapist asks a series of open questions that lead the client to examine the thought and reach a new conclusion themselves: "What is the evidence for that? Is there another way to see this? What would you say to a friend who thought this?" This process of leading someone to their own insight through questioning is called guided discovery. It is collaborative, not lecturing, and it builds the client's own reasoning skill.

Behavioural experiments

A behavioural experiment is a planned real-world test of a belief. If a socially anxious client believes "if I speak up people will laugh at me," the therapist and client design a small experiment (asking a question in a group) and observe what actually happens. Experience, not argument, updates the belief. Behavioural experiments bridge the cognitive and behavioural halves of CBT: the thought is cognitive, but the test is behavioural.

The behaviour-therapy toolkit

Who → what. Joseph Wolpe (1915–1997) developed systematic desensitization in the 1950s, applying reciprocal inhibition to human fears. It is the classic behavioural treatment for phobias.

Reciprocal inhibition is the principle that two opposing responses cannot occur strongly at the same time: a person cannot be deeply relaxed and intensely anxious simultaneously. Wolpe used relaxation to inhibit anxiety. Systematic desensitization combines three steps:

  1. Train the client in deep relaxation (usually progressive muscle relaxation).
  2. Build an exposure hierarchy (also called an anxiety or fear hierarchy): a ranked ladder of feared situations from least to most frightening, each rated on a subjective scale.
  3. Pair relaxation with the feared images or situations, starting at the bottom of the ladder and climbing only when each step is comfortable, so relaxation replaces anxiety at every rung.

Because desensitization is graded (gradual) and paired with relaxation, it is gentle. Contrast this with the intense exposure methods:

  • Flooding: exposing the client directly to the most feared real situation at full intensity, with no gradual ladder, until anxiety naturally subsides through extinction and habituation. There is no relaxation pairing.
  • Implosion (implosive therapy): like flooding but conducted in imagination rather than real life, often with vividly exaggerated feared scenes.
Trap. Systematic desensitization vs flooding. Desensitization → gradual, uses a hierarchy, pairs exposure with relaxation (reciprocal inhibition), starts at the easy end. Flooding → maximum intensity at once, no hierarchy, no relaxation, relies on habituation. Implosion is flooding done in imagination.

The remaining tools rest on operant conditioning (consequences shape behaviour) or classical conditioning (associations shape responses):

  • Aversion therapy: pairing an unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus so the behaviour becomes distasteful. A drug that induces nausea when alcohol is drunk. This uses classical conditioning to attach a negative response.
  • Token economy: a structured programme, common on inpatient wards, in which desired behaviours earn tokens (secondary reinforcers) that can later be exchanged for privileges or goods. It is an application of operant reinforcement, associated with Burrhus Frederic Skinner's principles and developed clinically by Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin.
  • Contingency management: arranging clear consequences (rewards, or loss of rewards) so they follow specific behaviours, making the link between action and outcome explicit. A token economy is one form of it.
  • Relaxation training: teaching bodily calm, most often through progressive muscle relaxation (tensing and releasing muscle groups in sequence), described by Edmund Jacobson, plus controlled breathing. It underpins desensitization and is a treatment for anxiety in its own right.
Key. Cognitive techniques change thoughts (distortions, irrational beliefs) through questioning and testing. Behavioural techniques change behaviour through conditioning (exposure, reinforcement, association). CBT deliberately uses both, because thoughts and actions maintain each other.

6.11 Specialised and process-focused approaches

Beyond the main schools sit several targeted methods and, cutting across every therapy, a set of process constructs that describe what actually happens in the relationship. Both are heavily examined.

Motivational interviewing

Who → what. William Richard Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed Motivational Interviewing (MI), first for addictions. It is a collaborative, client-centred style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change.

MI assumes that lecturing a reluctant person increases resistance. Instead the therapist works with the client's ambivalence (the coexisting wish to change and wish to stay the same), guiding them to voice their own reasons for change, called change talk. Its spirit is often summarised as partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation (drawing motivation out of the client rather than imposing it). A well-known principle is rolling with resistance rather than arguing against it.

Crisis intervention

Crisis intervention is brief, immediate, active help for a person overwhelmed by an acute crisis (a suicidal state, disaster, sudden loss). Its goals are short-term: ensure safety, reduce immediate distress, mobilise coping and support, and restore the person to at least their pre-crisis level of functioning. It is time-limited and problem-focused, not deep personality change.

Grief and bereavement work

Grief counselling helps a person mourn a loss and adapt to life without the person or thing lost. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described commonly cited stages of dying and grief (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance), though these are now understood as possible reactions rather than a fixed sequence everyone must pass through. Modern approaches also speak of the tasks of mourning (accepting the reality of the loss, working through the pain, adjusting, and finding an enduring connection while moving on), associated with J. William Worden. A key clinical distinction is between normal grief and complicated or prolonged grief that does not ease over time.

Dialectical Behaviour Therapy in depth

Who → what. Marsha M. Linehan developed Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), originally for chronically suicidal clients and borderline personality disorder. Its "dialectic" is the balance between acceptance of the client as they are and the push for change.

Standard DBT combines individual therapy with a skills group teaching four skill modules:

  • Mindfulness: observing and describing present experience without judgement; the core skill underlying the others.
  • Distress tolerance: surviving crises without making them worse (self-soothing, distraction, radical acceptance).
  • Emotion regulation: understanding, reducing vulnerability to, and changing painful emotions.
  • Interpersonal effectiveness: asking for what one needs and saying no while keeping relationships and self-respect.

DBT is usually classed as a "third-wave" cognitive behavioural therapy because of its heavy use of mindfulness and acceptance.

Eye-Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing

Who → what. Francine Shapiro developed Eye-Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) in the late 1980s, primarily for trauma and post-traumatic stress disorder.

In EMDR the client holds a distressing memory in mind while following a repeated bilateral stimulation (typically the therapist's finger moving side to side, producing back-and-forth eye movements, or alternating taps or tones). Over sets, the memory is meant to lose its intensity and be reprocessed into a less disturbing, more adaptive form. EMDR is a recognised trauma treatment; note that its distinctive ingredient is the bilateral eye-movement component paired with structured recall.

Interpersonal Psychotherapy

Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT), developed by Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman, is a brief, structured, evidence-based treatment for depression. It links mood symptoms to current relationship problems and focuses on a small number of problem areas: grief (complicated bereavement), role disputes (conflict with a significant person), role transitions (a major life change such as divorce or retirement), and interpersonal deficits (loneliness and difficulty forming relationships). It works on the present social world, not childhood.

Process constructs that cut across all therapies

Whatever the school, certain relationship processes appear in every therapy, and questions often test the definitions.

  • Therapeutic alliance (working alliance): the collaborative bond between client and therapist, made of an emotional bond plus agreement on the goals and tasks of therapy. Its strength is one of the most reliable predictors of good outcome, described in this three-part form by Edward Bordin.
  • Transference: the client unconsciously redirects feelings from important past figures (often a parent) onto the therapist. A concept from Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis.
  • Countertransference: the therapist's own emotional reactions to the client, whether stirred up by the client or arising from the therapist's own history. It must be recognised so it does not distort the work.
  • Resistance: anything in the client that works against the progress of therapy (missing sessions, changing the subject, falling silent). Psychodynamic therapy treats resistance as meaningful material to be understood, not merely an obstacle.
  • Cultural competence: the therapist's awareness of, respect for, and skill in working across the client's culture, language, values, and identity, so that therapy fits the client rather than imposing the therapist's assumptions.
  • Ethical boundaries and confidentiality: keeping the relationship professional (no exploitation, no dual relationships), and protecting the privacy of what the client discloses. Confidentiality has recognised limits: risk of serious harm to self or others, abuse of a child, or a legal order can require disclosure. Beyond these limits, privacy is a foundation of trust.
Trap. Transference vs countertransference. Transference → the client's feelings displaced onto the therapist. Countertransference → the therapist's feelings toward the client. Match the direction to the correct person.

The common-factors view

Who → what. Saul Rosenzweig in 1936 argued that different therapies work about equally well and that their shared ingredients matter more than their unique techniques. He borrowed the Dodo Bird's line from Alice in Wonderland: "Everybody has won, and all must have prizes," giving the idea its nickname, the Dodo Bird Verdict.

Decades later Bruce E. Wampold gathered the modern evidence for this position in his "contextual model," arguing that factors common to all effective therapies (a strong alliance, the therapist's empathy and belief in the treatment, a shared rationale that makes sense to the client, and the client's own expectation of change) account for much of therapy's benefit, alongside specific techniques. The common-factors view does not say technique is worthless; it says the relationship and shared ingredients carry a great deal of the weight.

Recap. Beck built Cognitive Therapy (cognitive triad, automatic thoughts, distortions, guided discovery via Socratic questioning); Ellis built REBT (the ABC, then D disputing and E, targeting irrational "must" beliefs). Wolpe's systematic desensitization pairs a graded exposure hierarchy with relaxation through reciprocal inhibition, unlike full-intensity flooding or imaginal implosion; the behavioural toolkit also holds aversion therapy, token economies, contingency management, and relaxation training. Specialised methods include Miller and Rollnick's motivational interviewing, crisis intervention, grief work, Linehan's DBT with its four skill modules, Shapiro's EMDR, and Klerman and Weissman's interpersonal psychotherapy. Cutting across all of them are the alliance, transference and countertransference, resistance, cultural competence, ethical boundaries and confidentiality, and the common-factors "Dodo Bird" view from Rosenzweig to Wampold.
