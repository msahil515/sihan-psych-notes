# Chapter 1: Personality: What It Is and Why You Should Care

**What this chapter is really about.** Every later chapter in Schultz and Schultz presents one theorist (Freud, Jung, Adler, Horney, Erikson, Allport, Maslow, Rogers, Skinner, Bandura and the rest), and every one of those chapters is built on the same skeleton: a definition of what personality is, a view of how it is shaped, methods for measuring it, research that tests it, and a stand on six old questions about human nature. Chapter 1 hands you that skeleton before any flesh goes on it. It asks what the word *personality* means and lands on a working definition (an enduring, unique cluster of characteristics that can still shift with the situation). It shows that personality is shaped by **gender, race and culture** and, in the 11th edition, by life on **social media**. It surveys the tools for **assessing** personality (self-report inventories such as the MMPI, projective tests such as the Rorschach and the TAT, interviews, behavioural observation, thought and experience sampling) and the yardsticks of **reliability and validity** by which they are judged. It sets out the **research methods** (clinical, experimental, virtual, correlational) used to test every theory in the book. And it closes with the **six questions about human nature** that serve as the scoring grid for each theorist.

This is not a soft introduction. The assessment and research modules are among the most examined material in the book, because they double as the foundation of psychological testing and research methodology papers: the MMPI's versions and dates, the Rorschach's ten cards, the TAT's origin, reliability versus validity, the range of the correlation coefficient and why correlation does not prove causation turn up year after year, in MCQs and in vivas.

**How to read this chapter.** Module 1.1 builds the definition of personality step by step, from the Latin *persona* to the person-situation debate. 1.2 covers personality and social media. 1.3 and 1.4 deal with race, gender and culture, including the individualism-collectivism research the book keeps returning to. 1.5 is the long assessment module: almost every sentence carries a testable fact. 1.6 teaches the research methods and their logic. 1.7 explains what a theory is for and why personality theories are partly autobiographical. 1.8 sets up the six human-nature issues, with a summary table to reproduce from memory.

## 1.1 Take a Look at the Word

### Three dictionary definitions and the plan of the book

Dictionaries define personality in three ways: as *the condition of being a person*, as *the qualities that make someone's character distinctive*, and as *the total of a person's physical, mental, emotional and social characteristics*. The common thread is that personality is whatever makes you the particular individual you are. The word is simple; the concept is hard enough to need a whole book of competing theories.

The book's route is organised by each theory's outlook on human nature. It starts with **Sigmund Freud** and psychoanalysis, moves to the theorists who revised or rebelled against him, then to the **life-span approach** (birth to old age), then to theories centred on **traits**, **psychological health**, **predetermined behaviour patterns** and **cognitive learning from social situations**, and ends with current 21st-century ideas.

One early warning sets the tone. The great 20th-century theorists seldom considered ethnic or cultural difference, yet it is not meaningful to generalise to all humanity from observations of neurotic European women (Freud's practice) or from tests given to American male college students. So the book tries throughout to show the influence of **age, gender, race, ethnic and national origin, religious beliefs and sexual orientation**. That list is why modules 1.3, 1.4 and the last third of 1.5 exist.

### Everybody has one

Your personality is one of your most important assets: it has shaped your experience so far and will help set the limits of your success and fulfilment, influencing your career, your relationships, your parenting and even your health. It can open up opportunities or close them off. In daily life we sort people crudely into "great personality" (pleasant, easy company, someone you would hire, befriend or marry) and "terrible personality" (cold, hostile, difficult, and often avoided). The twist is that the judging is mutual: while you size others up, they size you up, every time you meet someone new. Even how many social situations you enter depends on personality, since the sociable walk into far more of them than the shy.

### Describing your personality

"Terrific" and "terrible" are too blunt, because people are complex and behave differently across situations and companions. Precise description is why psychologists invest so much in **personality tests**. Try listing honest adjectives for what you are really like (not how you would like to be seen); most people manage between six and a dozen. Compare the widely used **Adjective Check List**, which offers **300 adjectives** for the test-taker to tick.

**Table 1.1** Adjective check list (sample)

Table 1.1 reproduces **30** of those 300 adjectives, arranged roughly alphabetically, for you to tick; the real test would offer another **270**. The list mixes flattering words (one meaning warm and loving, say) with unflattering ones (one meaning spiteful and out for revenge), and dominant qualities with submissive ones, so an honest respondent cannot simply tick every desirable word. Even this short sample yields a more detailed self-portrait than most people produce unaided.

### How does personality develop?

The book's interest is not in *what* your personality is like but in the **forces and factors that shape it**: whether we are born with a personality or learn it from our parents, whether unconscious forces drive it, and whether it changes as we age. Each theory will be described (what it says, how it arose, where it stands now) and then judged on how well it answers such questions. The authors compare the theorists to people each contributing pieces of a huge jigsaw puzzle; some pieces are decades old, but psychologists are still fitting them together.

### Ways of looking at personality

How do we use the word in everyday life? **Adams (1954)** suggested examining what we intend when we say "**I**". Saying "I" sums you up (likes and dislikes, fears and virtues, strengths and weaknesses) and marks you off as a separate individual. The rest of the book can be read as competing accounts of what lies behind that "I".

### How others see us

The word *personality* dates from about the year **1500** and derives from the Latin **persona**, the **mask worn by actors**. Taken literally, personality would then mean only our **external, visible characteristics**, the impression we make on others: the mask we wear to face the world.

The authors reject this as too narrow. Personality also includes subjective social and emotional qualities that cannot be seen directly, that a person may hide from us, or that we may hide from others. A colleague who is charming in meetings but privately consumed by envy has a personality that includes the envy, not only the charm.

> **Trap.** The Latin **persona** (actor's mask, root of the word, about 1500) is not **Jung's persona archetype** (Chapter 3), the public face a person adopts to meet society's expectations. A question on the *etymology* of personality wants the theatrical mask. And "personality is only the mask" is the view Schultz reject as incomplete.

### Stable and predictable characteristics

We also assume personality is **enduring**: relatively **stable and predictable**. A usually calm friend may sometimes become excited or panicky, so personality varies with the situation and is not rigid, but it resists sudden change.

How much weight to give the stable person versus the situation became a famous argument. In the **1960s** a debate broke out over the relative impact of **enduring personal variables** (**traits and needs**) and **situational variables** (see **Mischel, 1968, 1973**). It ran for about **20 years** and ended with **Funder's (2001)** verdict that the long-standing person-versus-situation dichotomy was, and always had been, a fake. The resolution is the **interactionist approach**: a full explanation needs stable traits, the changing situation **and the interaction between them**. A socially anxious student (stable trait) may be relaxed with close friends, tense in a seminar and nearly mute in a job interview; what explains the pattern is how her trait meets each kind of situation.

> **Trap.** Mischel (1968, 1973) is cited as **starting** the debate; the "always was a fake" verdict is **Funder (2001)**; the winner is **interactionism**, not either side. Classic background: Mischel's *Personality and Assessment* (1968) argued that trait scores predict behaviour only weakly (correlations around .30), which is why it was read as an attack on trait theory; Indian papers call this the **person-situation** or **consistency** debate.

### Unique characteristics

Finally, we sense that each person has qualities that set them apart. Combining the elements gives the working definition: personality is an **enduring and unique cluster of characteristics that may change in response to different situations**. The glossary puts it more formally: **personality** is the set of **unique, relatively enduring internal and external aspects of a person's character that influence behaviour in different situations**. Each phrase matters: *unique* (no two people alike), *relatively enduring* (stable but not frozen), *internal and external* (hidden qualities as well as the visible mask), *in different situations* (the interactionist clause).

Not all psychologists accept even this. Each theorist offers a personal vision of personality that becomes, in effect, his or her definition; the book's aim is to understand these different ways of defining the word "I".

> **Classic background:** The definition most quoted in Indian exams is **Allport's (1937)**: personality is "the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment." For *Schultz's* definition, give the "unique, relatively enduring internal and external aspects" wording.

## 1.2 Personality and the Social Media

So much social life now happens online that a large research literature links personality to the virtual world. Schultz frame it as **three questions**:

1. Do we present our **real selves** on social media?
2. Does social media use **influence or change** our personalities?
3. Do people with **different personalities** use social media differently?

The short answer: social media both **reflects** personality and **shapes** it, and the online self mixes accurate self-display with selective presentation.

### Are you the same person online?

If personality is partly the face we show others, then the face shown on sites such as Facebook becomes part of it. Is that face accurate or idealised?

- **Mostly honest.** Studies in the **United States and Germany** found that networking profiles convey accurate impressions of personality, at least as accurate as face-to-face impressions (**Gosling, Gaddis, and Vazire, 2007; Back et al., 2010**).
- **Flattering on stability.** A later large **German** study found many people present themselves online as much **more emotionally stable** than they are (**Blumer and Doring, 2012**).
- **The shy find a voice.** **Introverted, neurotic, lonely and socially awkward** people find it easier to express their true selves online than in person (**Marriott and Buchanan, 2014**).
- **Feeling real drives activity.** Those who feel able to express their true selves online are **more active** on social media (**Seidman, 2014**).

**Selfies**: **more women than men** send them, and heavy use can make the sender **less likeable**, **reduce intimacy** in friendships and reinforce the idea that looks matter more than how one treats friends (**Drexler, 2013; Rutledge, 2013**).

We are not fully honest face to face either, especially when impressing a date or an employer; the real difference online may be the **size and immediacy of the audience**. That audience includes employers. In **Goodman, Smith, Ivancevich and Lundberg (2014)**, Facebook pages with negative content (drunkenness, sexual display, profanity) led evaluators to **false perceptions** of the owner's personality, while clean pages produced **more accurate** judgements, a difference that can decide a job or a graduate place.

### How does social media influence our personality?

On the shaping side, the findings concern mainly **excessive** use:

- **Lam and Peng (2010):** among Chinese adolescents aged **13 to 18**, **excessive Internet use** went with significant **anxiety and depression** compared with light users.
- **Blais, Craig, Pepler, and Connolly (2008); Huang (2010a); Kross et al. (2013):** heavy social media use can **lower psychological well-being** and **worsen relationships** with friends and romantic partners.
- **Gentzler, Oberhauser, Westerman, and Nadorff (2011):** US college students who kept in touch with parents **by phone** had more satisfying, supportive relationships than those using **social networking sites**, who reported more **loneliness, anxiety and conflict** with parents.
- **Four countries:** in the **Netherlands, Serbia, Hong Kong and Korea**, excessive users were **lonelier, more introverted and lower in self-esteem** (**Baek, Bae, and Jang, 2013; Milosevic-Dordevic and Zezelj, 2013; Muusses et al., 2014; Yao and Zhong, 2014**).
- **Mosher (2011):** excessive use can become an **addiction** as compulsive as alcohol, drug or gambling addiction, and has been shown to **alter brain regions linked to depression and irritability**.

> **Trap.** The four-country result is one pooled conclusion; the book does not pair authors with countries. Excessive users are *more introverted*, whereas ordinary Facebook use goes with *extraversion* (below): one finding is about overuse, the other about use. And most of these studies are **correlational** (1.6), so "excessive users are lonelier" does not by itself show social media *causes* loneliness.

### How does our personality influence our use of social media?

- **Extraversion and narcissism.** In **Eastern and Western** cultures, more **extraverted** and more **narcissistic** people (narcissism being an inflated, unrealistic self-concept) used Facebook much more, and more narcissistic teenagers **updated their status more often** (**Kuo and Tang, 2014; Michikyan, Subrahmanyam, and Dennis, 2014; Ong et al., 2011; Panek, Nardis, and Konrath, 2014; Winter et al., 2014**).
- **Heavy users** tend to be **more extraverted** and **more open to experience**, but **lower in self-esteem and social skills**, **less conscientious** and **less emotionally stable** than light users (Blackhart and colleagues, 2014; Correa and colleagues, 2010; Mehdizadeh, 2010; Papastylianou, 2013; Ross and colleagues, 2009; Weiss, 2014; Wilson, Fornasier, and White, 2010).
- **Mobile phones (Australia).** **Extraverts** and people with a **strong self-identity** spent more time **calling** and changing **ringtones and wallpaper**; the **more neurotic, less conscientious and shy** spent more time **texting** (**Bardi and Brady, 2010; Butt and Phillips, 2008; Walsh, White, Cox, and Young, 2011**).
- **Internet trolls**, who post hateful, inflammatory comments to hurt others, are **mostly male**, **average age 29**, and **high in sadism**: degrading others pleases them (**Buckels, Trapnell, and Paulhus, 2014; Lewis, 2014**).

The two directions form a loop: a narcissistic teenager posts constantly because of who he is (personality reflected), while a lonely user who retreats online may grow more isolated (personality shaped).

## 1.3 The Role of Race and Gender in Shaping Personality

### Who built the theories, and on whom

The theorists in this book disagree about almost everything, yet share one profile: **all are White, of European or American heritage, and almost all are men**. Schultz treat this as a fact of history rather than a scandal. While these theories were being written, nearly every major advance in the arts, philosophy, literature and science (including scientific method itself) came from White men of European or American background, because education and the professions were largely closed to women and ethnic minorities.

The **patients and research participants** behind the early theories were also almost all **White**, and mostly **men** (the authors note drily that even the laboratory rats were white). Yet the theories were offered as valid for **everyone**. No theorist said a theory applied only to men, Whites or Americans; they granted some role to social and environmental forces but **ignored or minimised gender and ethnic background**.

> **Trap.** "Almost all" men, not all: the book includes **Karen Horney** (who has her own chapter and challenged Freud's view of women) and **Anna Freud** (within the Freud chapter). "All were White and of European or American heritage" matches the book.

### Why background must matter

Your siblings and friends grew up under different influences and became different people. Whole environments differ far more: a child in a mostly White Midwestern town, one in a Los Angeles *barrio* (Spanish-speaking neighbourhood), one in an Appalachian mountain village and one in an affluent Black suburb meet very different influences, and their personalities differ accordingly.

Gender works the same way, since boys and girls are usually raised according to **traditional gender stereotypes**. Two studies illustrate documented sex differences:

- **Barrett, Lane, Sechrest, and Schwartz (2000)** compared men and women among **undergraduates at two American universities** and **medical students in the United States and Germany**. In **both countries**, **women showed greater emotional complexity and intensity**.
- **Alansari (2006)** studied **more than 7,000 college students in 16 Islamic nations**: **women scored significantly higher in anxiety in 11 of the 16 samples**.

The lesson: a theory built on men cannot be assumed to fit women, nor one built on one ethnic group to fit another.

## 1.4 The Role of Culture in Shaping Personality

### Cross-cultural psychology and what it has shown

The specialty of **cross-cultural psychology** has produced extensive evidence that personality is formed by both **genetic and environmental** influences, and that **cultural influences** are among the most important environmental ones (**Triandis and Suh, 2002**). The most persuasive evidence comes from people who **move between cultures**, since background stays roughly constant while the culture changes:

- **Japanese emigrants to the United States** became much more "American" in personality than those who stayed in Japan (**Gungor, Bornstein, De Leersnyder, Cote, Ceulemans, and Mesquita, 2013**).
- **Chinese immigrants to Canada.** **Recent** immigrants were as low in extraversion as **Hong Kong Chinese** who had not emigrated, but those who had lived in Canada **at least 10 years** scored **significantly higher in extraversion** than both (**McCrae, Yi, Trapnell, Bond, and Paulhus, 1998**). Exposure time, not migration as such, shifted a basic trait. (The book's sentence literally says recent immigrants showed a low level of "introversion", a slip; the comparison only makes sense as low extraversion.)

Culture also colours emotion and self-evaluation. In a **daily diary study**, **Asian-American** students reported far **more negative emotions in social situations** than **European-American** students (**Lee, Okazaki, and Yoo, 2006**). Westerners, and Americans especially, are **more optimistic** and view themselves and their futures more positively, even rating **their sports teams, cities and friends** as superior, more than Asians do (**Endo, Heine, and Lehman, 2000**).

### Cultural neuroscience

The field of **cultural neuroscience** has shown large cultural differences in **brain activity and genetic makeup** (**Azar, 2010**). **Brain-wave** measures show Eastern and Western participants responding differently to the **same stimuli** (**Park and Huang, 2010**). In one study, Japanese and American brain-wave responses to the same visual stimuli differed in ways that **paralleled each culture's measured submissiveness or dominance** (**Freeman, Rule, and Ambady, 2009**).

### Different cultural beliefs about destiny

The book's example here is Indian. For centuries the concept of **karma** has shaped outlook in **India** and other **Hindu and Buddhist** societies. Schultz present it as a **fatalistic, deterministic** view: present and past actions fix one's destiny, so events happen because they were **destined**, and fortune or misfortune, health or sickness are **preordained** regardless of present effort. Such a belief, they suggest, may foster a **passive, resigned** personality that accepts whatever comes, in contrast to the American emphasis on **free choice, action and personal initiative**.

Research shows substantial **East-West differences in fate attribution** (**Norenzayan and Lee, 2010**), but as Eastern cultures such as **China** modernise and Westernise, the belief **weakens** (**Wong, Shaw, and Ng, 2010**).

> **Trap.** Karma here illustrates the **determinism** pole of free will versus determinism (1.8), at the level of a culture; it is not **historical determinism** (personality fixed in childhood). Modernisation **reduces** fate attribution. In an interview you may add that Indian philosophy also stresses that present action shapes future outcomes, but in an MCQ reproduce the book's framing.

### Individualism

The central cultural dimension of the chapter is **individualism versus collectivism**. An **individualistic** society stresses **personal freedom, choice and action**; a **collectivist** society stresses **group norms and values, role expectations and other cultural constraints**. Competitiveness and assertiveness tend to be frowned on in Asian cultures and prized in Western ones. People in individualistic cultures show greater **extraversion, self-esteem, happiness (subjective well-being), optimism** about the future, and **belief in their ability to control it**.

The studies, each worth knowing by its comparison:

- **Australia versus Japan:** Australian students stressed **individuality**, Japanese the **collective** (**Kashima, Kokubo, Kashima, Boxall, Yamaguchi, and Macrae, 2004**).
- **The immigrant job applicant** (an illustration, not a study): a recent, unacculturated Asian immigrant is likely to score **low** on tests of **competitiveness, assertiveness and self-promotion**, be judged deficient by American standards, and miss the job. This is the chapter's clearest example of **cultural bias in assessment**.
- **63 countries:** a study of **over 400 million people in 63 countries** found **individualism strongly and consistently related to positive well-being** (**Fischer and Boer, 2011**).
- **Genes and culture:** genetic differences between cultures have been linked to **lower anxiety and depression** in **collectivist** cultures and **higher** levels in **individualistic** ones (**Chiao and Blizinsky, 2010**).
- **Self-efficacy:** US students scored significantly **higher** than Japanese students on **self-efficacy**, the sense of being adequate, efficient and competent in coping with life and controlling events (**Morling, Kitayama, and Miyamoto, 2002**).
- **Australia versus Singapore:** Australian students were **more agreeable, conscientious, optimistic and satisfied** with life (**Wong, Lee, Ang, Oei, and Ng, 2009**).
- **Whose happiness?** At the same US university, **European-American** students found well-being in goals pursued for **personal satisfaction**; **Asian-American** students in goals that **pleased important others** such as parents and met their expectations (**Oishi and Diener, 2001**).
- **Self-description:** **American** students described themselves in far more **positive** terms, **Japanese** students more often in **negative** terms (**Kanagawa, Cross, and Markus, 2001**).

> **Trap.** Two findings that seem to clash are both in the book: individualism goes with **greater well-being** (Fischer and Boer, 2011), yet collectivist cultures go with **lower anxiety and depression** (Chiao and Blizinsky, 2010). Learn them with their authors. **Self-efficacy** is Bandura's term (Chapter 13). Classic background: the individualism-collectivism dimension is most associated with **Hofstede** and with **Triandis**.

### Child-rearing practices

In the individualistic **United States**, parenting tends to be **noncoercive, democratic and permissive**; in collectivist **Asian and Arab** societies, **authoritarian, restrictive and controlling**. The striking finding is that authoritarian parenting does not mean the same everywhere:

- **Dwairy, Achoui, Abouserie, and Farah (2006):** adolescents in several **Arab countries** felt **more connected to their parents** than American adolescents, followed parental direction in nearly every domain (social life, relationships, marriage, career, politics), did not feel they suffered from it and were often content. The authoritarian style **did not harm** their mental health and well-being, as it might in liberal Western cultures.
- **Liu and Guo (2010):** Chinese mothers in Canada were **more authoritarian** than non-Chinese Canadian mothers.
- **Durgel, Leyendecker, Yagmurlu, and Harwood (2009):** Turkish mothers in Germany who were **more assimilated** stressed **individualistic goals** for their children much more than less assimilated mothers.

The effect of a parenting style depends on the cultural meaning the child gives it. (Classic background: authoritarian and permissive are two of **Baumrind's** parenting styles, alongside authoritative.)

### Self-enhancement

**Self-enhancement** is the tendency to **promote oneself aggressively and make oneself conspicuous**; its opposite, **self-effacement** (self-criticism, modesty), fits **Asian** values better.

- **Heine, Takata, and Lehman (2000):** in a laboratory study, self-enhancement was far more common among **Canadian** students, self-criticism among **Japanese** students.
- **Heine and Renshaw (2002); Kurman (2001):** comparing **Japanese with American** students, and **Singapore Chinese** with **Israeli Jewish** high-school and college students, the **collectivist** groups showed **more self-criticism and less self-enhancement**.
- **Church et al. (2014):** across the **United States, Mexico, Venezuela and China**, the **Chinese** were the most **self-effacing**.
- **Janteloven (Nordic).** **Norway, Sweden and Denmark** have a cultural code, **Janteloven**, against putting one's own interests above the community's and in favour of humility. **American** students rated themselves further **above average on positive traits and below average on negative traits** than **Norwegian** students did, a self-enhancement the authors judged **culturally induced** (**Silvera and Seger, 2004**).
- **Spain versus the Netherlands:** even neighbouring European cultures differ. **Spanish** respondents cared more about **honour and family values** (family security, respect for parents, recognition from others); **Dutch** respondents scored much higher on **ambition, capability and independence** (**Rodriguez-Mosquera, Manstead, and Fischer, 2000**).

Self-enhancement is not simply a vice. In several **European** countries, others rated self-enhancers as **emotionally stable, socially attractive and influential** (**Dufner, Denissen and colleagues, 2013**), and **American** students high in self-esteem and self-enhancement sought **mates with the same qualities**, partners as impressive as they believe themselves to be (**Brown, Brown, and Kovatch, 2013**).

> **Trap.** **Janteloven** is **Nordic**, not Japanese. Pairings to keep straight: **Canadians** vs Japanese (Heine et al., 2000); **Israelis** vs Singapore Chinese (Kurman, 2001); **Chinese** most self-effacing (Church et al., 2014); **Norwegians** (Silvera and Seger, 2004); **Spain** honour vs **Netherlands** independence.

### A diversity of cultures

Gaps remain: far less personality research exists for **African and South American** nations than for English-speaking countries and much of Europe and Asia, and much of it is not published in English. Most studies still use **American college students**. The book responds by drawing on research from **more than 40 countries** and many age groups, religions and ethnic backgrounds. (Classic background: this sampling problem is now called reliance on **WEIRD** samples, Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic; Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan, 2010.)

## 1.5 Assessing Your Personality

To **assess** is to measure or evaluate. Personality assessment is one of psychology's main real-world applications, and the book names five groups who use it:

| Who assesses | Why |
|---|---|
| **Clinical psychologists** | To understand symptoms by distinguishing normal from abnormal behaviour and feeling, so as to diagnose and choose therapy |
| **School psychologists** | To find the causes of students' adjustment or learning problems |
| **Industrial/organisational psychologists** | To select the best candidate for a job |
| **Counselling psychologists** | To find the best job for an applicant, matching the job's demands to the person's interests and needs |
| **Research psychologists** | To explain participants' behaviour in experiments, or correlate their traits with other measures |

> **Trap.** **I/O** psychologists seek the best *person for the job*; **counselling** psychologists seek the best *job for the person*. They are mirror images and easily swapped.

Almost nobody escapes being assessed at some point in a working life, which is why you need to know how tests work and how to judge them.

### The concepts of reliability and validity

The best assessment techniques meet two principles, and every test in the book should be judged against them.

#### Reliability

**Reliability** is the **consistency of response** to an assessment device. Suppose you take the same test on two days and get two very different scores: which is your real score? Nobody could rely on such a test. Slight variation between occasions is normal, but a large variation means something is wrong with the test or its scoring.

The book names one further kind in its discussion of projective tests: **inter-scorer reliability**, the extent to which different examiners interpreting the same responses reach the same conclusions. When two psychologists read the same Rorschach record and form different impressions, inter-scorer reliability is low.

> **Classic background:** Psychometrics distinguishes four ways of estimating reliability, each expressed as a correlation (the **reliability coefficient**): **test-retest** (same test, two occasions; the book's example), **alternate or parallel forms** (two equivalent versions), **split-half and internal consistency** (within one administration; Spearman-Brown, Kuder-Richardson, **Cronbach's alpha**), and **inter-scorer (inter-rater)** reliability.

#### Validity

**Validity** is the extent to which a device **measures what it is intended to measure**. Does an intelligence test measure intelligence? Does an anxiety scale measure anxiety? An invalid test cannot **predict behaviour**: a high score on an invalid intelligence test says nothing about how you will do in college, and an invalid personality test can paint a completely misleading picture of your emotional strengths and weaknesses.

> **Classic background:** Exam papers divide validity into **content validity** (items sample the whole domain), **criterion-related validity**, either **concurrent** (criterion measured at the same time) or **predictive** (criterion measured later; the book's intelligence-and-college example), and **construct validity** (the test behaves as the theory of the trait predicts, with **convergent** and **discriminant** evidence; Cronbach and Meehl, 1955). **Face validity** (looking as if it measures the trait) is not validity in the technical sense.

> **Trap.** **Reliability = consistency; validity = measuring the right thing.** A bathroom scale that always reads 5 kg too heavy is perfectly reliable and invalid. A test can be reliable without being valid, but not valid without being reliable, so reliability is **necessary but not sufficient**. Also: **test-retest** concerns the test-taker's consistency over time; **inter-scorer** concerns examiners' agreement on one record.

#### Standardisation

The book invokes standardisation when it describes efforts to **standardise the administration, scoring and interpretation of the Rorschach** (the Comprehensive System), when its summary calls projective techniques **usually poorly standardised**, and when it reports that Hispanic Rorschach scores differ from the system's **norms**.

> **Classic background:** A test is **standardised** when it is administered and scored uniformly for everyone (same instructions, materials, time limits and scoring rules) and has **norms**, the score distribution of a large, representative **standardisation sample** against which an individual score is read. A raw score means nothing until you know how the reference group scored, and norms from an unrepresentative group (White Americans, say, applied to a recent immigrant) can bias interpretation even when the test is reliable.

### Methods of assessment

Each theorist developed the methods most useful for his or her theory and built the theory on the data they yielded. The methods vary in **objectivity, reliability and validity**, from **dream interpretation** and **childhood recollections** to **computer-administered objective tests**. The book groups them into **five major approaches**:

1. **Self-report** (objective) inventories
2. **Projective** techniques
3. **Clinical interviews**
4. **Behavioural assessment** procedures
5. **Thought and experience sampling** procedures

### Self-report personality tests

In a **self-report inventory**, people answer questions about their own **behaviour and feelings** in various situations. Items cover **symptoms, attitudes, interests, fears and values**, and the person marks how well each statement describes them or how far they agree. The book singles out one of the most useful.

#### The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)

Translated into **more than 140 languages**, the MMPI is the **world's most widely used psychological test** (see Butcher, 2010; Cox, Weed, and Butcher, 2009).

| Version | Year | What the book says |
|---|---|---|
| **MMPI** | **1943** | First published |

**Format and content.** It is a **true-false** test, given in the book as **567 statements**, covering physical and psychological health; political and social attitudes; educational, occupational, family and marital matters; and neurotic and psychotic tendencies. The **clinical scales** measure such characteristics as **gender role, defensiveness, depression, hysteria, paranoia, hypochondriasis and schizophrenia**, and some items detect whether the person is **deliberately faking**, **careless**, or has **misunderstood the instructions**.

**What the clinician does.** The client marks each statement true or false (today usually on a computer). Scoring against the scales yields a **profile** comparing the person with norms. The clinician checks the faking and carelessness indicators first, then reads the pattern of clinical scale elevations, and carries the resulting hypotheses into interview.

**Evidence on faking.** The **MMPI-2-RF** distinguished people with **genuine physical pain** from those **faking pain for disability payments** (**Crighton, Applegate, Wygant, Granacher, and Ulauf, 2013**) and genuine from **faked PTSD** (**Mason et al., 2013**). Yet **mentally ill** people can learn through **online instruction** to answer so as to **hide their symptoms** (**Hartmann and Hartmann, 2014**).

**Uses, limits, verdict.** The MMPI-2 is used in **research**, as a **diagnostic tool**, for **employee selection**, and in **vocational and personal counselling**. Its drawbacks are **length** (many people lose interest and motivation before the end) and **privacy** (very personal items feel intrusive, especially when the test is a condition of employment). Even so, the MMPI in its various forms is **valid**: it discriminates **neurotics from psychotics** and the **emotionally healthy from the disturbed**, and remains a highly valuable diagnostic and research tool.

> **Classic background:** The MMPI was built at the **University of Minnesota** by **Starke Hathaway** (psychologist) and **J. Charnley McKinley** (psychiatrist) by **empirical criterion keying**: an item was kept if a diagnostic group answered it differently from normal respondents, whatever its content. The profile has **10 clinical scales** (1 Hypochondriasis, 2 Depression, 3 Hysteria, 4 Psychopathic Deviate, 5 Masculinity-Femininity, 6 Paranoia, 7 Psychasthenia, 8 Schizophrenia, 9 Hypomania, 0 Social Introversion) plus **validity scales**, classically **? (Cannot Say), L (Lie), F (Infrequency)** and **K (Correction)**. The book's "gender role" is the Mf scale and its "defensiveness" the K scale (strictly a validity scale). The MMPI-2-RF has **338** items; an **MMPI-3** (**335** items) appeared in **2020**, after this edition.

> **Trap.** The book says "the MMPI" has **567** items, but strictly **567 is the MMPI-2**; the **original 1943 MMPI** had **550** distinct items (**566** printed, 16 repeated), so older keys may give 550 or 566. For Schultz-based questions: **567** (MMPI/MMPI-2), **478** (MMPI-A, **1992**, adolescents), **1943**, **1989** (nonsexist language), **2008** (MMPI-2-RF), **140+** languages. The "A" in MMPI-A means **adolescent**, not abbreviated.

#### Assessment of self-report inventories: limits, response sets and faking

- **Who can take them.** They may not suit people of **below-normal intelligence** or with **limited reading skills**.
- **Wording effects.** Small changes in wording or response options can transform results. Asked to pick the most important thing for children to learn **from a list**, **61.5 percent** of adults chose "to think for themselves"; asked to **supply their own answer**, only **4.6 percent** said this or something similar (**Schwarz, 1999**). The same opinion looks majority or fringe depending on format.
- **Social desirability.** Test-takers tend to choose the more **socially desirable** answer, especially in job applications. Few applicants would mark as true that they are often very tense at work.
- **Deliberate faking.** Students told to appear as good and acceptable as possible answered **more carefully** and **took longer** than students answering normally (**Holtgraves, 2004**), and most research participants find it **easy to give false answers** when asked to (**McDaniel, Beier, Perkins, Goggins, and Frankel, 2009**).

**The verdict.** For all these problems, no assessment approach is more objective than the self-report inventory. Its chief strength is that scoring needs no judgement: the answers can be machine-scored rapidly into a full diagnostic profile, and, as the summary puts it, the score is untouched by the examiner's personal or theoretical biases.

> **Classic background:** A **response set** is a tendency to answer in a particular way regardless of content: **social desirability** (Edwards, 1957), **acquiescence** ("yea-saying"), **nay-saying** and **extreme responding**. **Faking good** (hiding problems, as in job applications) and **faking bad** (exaggerating them, as in **malingering**) are deliberate distortions that validity scales try to catch.

> **Trap.** **Objective** refers to **scoring** (a fixed key, no examiner judgement), not to truthfulness. An objective test can still be distorted by the respondent's social desirability or faking. That is why self-report tests are called objective and projective tests are not.

### Online test administration

Self-report inventories can now be taken **online**, and many employers use them that way for **pre-screening**. The book lists **five advantages**:

1. **Less time-consuming** for applicant and organisation
2. **Less expensive**
3. **More objective scoring**
4. **Readily accepted by younger workers**
5. Prevents **looking ahead** at later questions and **changing earlier answers**

Research finds **no significant differences** between paper-and-pencil and online versions of most inventories (for example **Chuah, Drasgow, and Roberts, 2006; Clough, 2009; Luce et al., 2007; Naus, Philipp, and Samsi, 2009**). People are also significantly **more willing to disclose sensitive or embarrassing information online** than to a live administrator, apparently because a computer feels more **anonymous and private**.

> **Trap.** Online testing does **not** change scores on most inventories; what changes is **disclosure**, which **increases** online.

### Projective techniques

#### The projective hypothesis

**Projective tests** were developed by clinical psychologists working with emotionally disturbed patients, inspired by **Freud's** emphasis on the **unconscious**. They aim at the part of personality a self-report inventory cannot reach, because the person is unaware of it.

The underlying idea is the **projective hypothesis**: shown an **ambiguous stimulus** (an inkblot, a vague picture) and asked to describe it, a person cannot take the answer from the stimulus, so they supply it from within, **projecting** their **needs, fears and values** onto it. A man preoccupied with being attacked may see two creatures about to fight; a grieving woman may tell a story about someone waiting for a person who never comes.

That strength is also the weakness. Interpretation is so **subjective** that projective tests are **not high in reliability or validity**: different examiners often form quite different impressions of the same person, so **inter-scorer reliability is low**. The summary adds that they are usually **poorly standardised**. They remain widely used nonetheless, above all the **Rorschach Inkblot Technique** and the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)**.

> **Classic background:** The term "projective methods" is **Lawrence Frank's (1939)**. **Lindzey (1959)** classified projectives by response type: **association** (Rorschach, word association), **construction** (TAT), **completion** (sentence completion), **choice or ordering**, and **expression** (drawing and play, such as Draw-A-Person). This classification is a favourite in Indian testing papers.

> **Trap.** **Objective versus projective.** Objective (self-report) tests use **structured** items with fixed options, are **scored by key**, and are **higher in reliability and validity**, but their purpose is transparent, so answers can be distorted. Projective tests use **ambiguous** stimuli and **open** responses, are **interpreted** by the examiner, target **unconscious** material, and are **lower in reliability (especially inter-scorer) and validity**; their disguised purpose makes conscious faking harder.

#### Rorschach and his inkblots

The **Rorschach** was developed in **1921** by the **Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach (1884 to 1922)**, whose interest in inkblots began in childhood. As a boy he played **Klecksographie**, or **Blotto**, a popular game of saying what one sees in inkblots, and as a teenager he was so taken with blots that he was nicknamed **Klecks**, German for an ink blot. During his **psychiatric residency**, after qualifying in medicine, he and a friend played Blotto with patients to pass the time, and Rorschach noticed that **patients' responses differed consistently from schoolchildren's** to the same blots. That observation, that what people see in an ambiguous shape reflects their mental state, is the core of the test.

He made his blots by **dropping ink onto paper and folding the sheet in half**, producing a symmetrical shape like the one in Figure 1.1.

The figure is not a real Rorschach card (those are protected) but a blot made the same way. Two features make it work. It is **symmetrical** about the fold, which invites paired figures, faces, animals or bodies; and it is **meaningless in itself**, so whatever a viewer reports (dancers, a bat, a mask) comes from the viewer. Whether the person uses the whole blot or a fragment, sees movement or stillness, people or animals, is what the examiner records.

After experimenting, Rorschach settled on **10 blots** because he **could not afford to have more printed**. His publication was a **failure**: few copies sold, and the few reviews were negative. He became depressed and **died 9 months after the work appeared**, long before the test became enormously popular.

> **Classic background:** The monograph was **Psychodiagnostik (1921)**. Rorschach died in **1922**, aged 37, of **peritonitis** after a ruptured appendix. Of the 10 cards, **5 are achromatic** (I, IV, V, VI, VII), **2 black and red** (II, III) and **3 multicoloured** (VIII, IX, X); the book says only that some are black and others coloured. The number 10 was set by **printing cost**, not by theory, a detail examiners love.

#### Using the Rorschach

What the clinician does:

1. **Response phase.** Cards are shown **one at a time** and the person says what they see.
2. **Inquiry.** The cards are shown **a second time** and the psychologist asks **specific questions about the earlier answers** (where on the blot, and what made it look that way).
3. **Observation throughout** of **gestures**, **reactions to particular blots** (hesitation, rejecting a card) and **general attitude**.

Responses are interpreted by whether the person reports **movement**, **human or animal figures**, **animate or inanimate objects**, and **whole or partial figures**. Someone who uses only tiny fragments of each blot, sees no movement and gives few responses presents very differently from someone who builds whole-blot scenes full of active people.

The most successful attempt to standardise administration, scoring and interpretation is the **Comprehensive System**, which on the basis of considerable research claims improved reliability and validity (see **Exner, 1993**). Even so there is **no universal agreement** on the test's usefulness: some researchers find **no scientific basis** for it, others insist it is **as valid as any other** measure. It remains popular in research and clinical practice, and is widely used in research in **Europe and South America**. Overall, though, **validity research supports the MMPI more than the Rorschach**, so the MMPI can be used with **greater confidence**, particularly with **ethnic minority and culturally diverse groups** (see **Wood, Garb, Lilienfeld, and Nezworski, 2002**).

> **Classic background:** The Comprehensive System codes each response for **location** (whole W, common detail D, unusual detail Dd, white space S), **determinants** (form, movement, colour, shading), **content** (human, animal and so on), **popular** responses and **form quality**. Exner first published it in **1974** (the book cites 1993); a newer system, **R-PAS**, appeared in **2011**.

#### The Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

The **TAT** was developed by **Henry Murray and Christiana Morgan (Morgan and Murray, 1935)**. In the book's description it has **19 ambiguous pictures**, each showing one or more people, plus **1 blank card**, and the pictures are deliberately vague about what is happening.

What the clinician does: the person **tells a story** about each picture, covering **what led up to the scene**, **what the characters are thinking and feeling**, and **how it will turn out** (for the blank card, the person imagines a picture first). In interpreting the stories, the clinician considers the **kinds of personal relationships** involved, the **characters' motivations**, and the **degree of contact with reality** they show. The storyteller is assumed to identify with a central character and lend it his own needs and conflicts: a client whose every story ends with someone abandoned or punished for wanting something is revealing what he might not say directly.

**Strengths and limits.** There are **no objective scoring systems** for diagnostic use, and the TAT's **reliability and validity are low for diagnosis**. It has proved **useful in research**, where scoring systems measure specific needs such as **achievement, affiliation and power**, and it remains useful clinically (**Gieser and Wyatt-Gieser, 2013**).

> **Classic background:** The TAT came from the **Harvard Psychological Clinic** to assess the needs of **Murray's need-press theory**; "apperception" means perceiving in the light of past experience. The **full published set has 31 cards (30 pictures plus 1 blank)**, marked for boys, girls, men or women, and a person traditionally receives **20** (Murray's 1943 manual), which is why the book speaks of 19 pictures and a blank card. The achievement scoring system is **McClelland and Atkinson's**; the **Children's Apperception Test** with animal pictures is **Bellak's (1949)**.

> **Trap.** **Rorschach versus TAT.** **Rorschach**: Hermann Rorschach alone, Swiss psychiatrist, **1921**, **10 inkblots** (limit set by printing cost), the person says **what they see**, **Comprehensive System (Exner)**, an **association** technique. **TAT**: **Morgan and Murray, 1935** (Morgan is first author), **19 pictures + 1 blank** (31 in the full set), the person **tells stories**, research scoring for **achievement, affiliation and power**, a **construction** technique. Validity research favours the **MMPI over the Rorschach**.

#### Other projective techniques

- **Word-association test.** Words are read **one at a time** and the person replies with the **first word that comes to mind**. Replies are analysed for being **commonplace or unusual**, for signs of **emotional tension**, and for links to **sexual conflicts**, and **speed of response** matters: a long pause before a charged word suggests it has touched something.
- **Sentence-completion test.** The person completes **stems** such as "My ambition is ..." or "What worries me is ...". Interpretation of both methods can be highly **subjective**, but some sentence-completion tests, such as the **Rotter Incomplete Sentence Blank**, allow **more objective scoring**.

> **Classic background:** Word association was first studied by **Galton (1879)** and turned into a clinical tool by **Jung**, who used **100 stimulus words**, timed each reply and treated delays and odd answers as signs of emotionally charged **complexes** (Chapter 3). The **Rotter Incomplete Sentence Blank** (Rotter and Rafferty, **1950**) has **40** stems, scored for adjustment. Galton's was a study of association in general; Jung's was the clinical, projective test.

### Clinical interviews

Testing is usually combined with **clinical interviews**: talking to the person about **past and present experiences**, **social and family relationships**, and the **problems that brought them for help**. The clinician attends to **general appearance, demeanour and attitude**; **facial expressions, posture and gestures**; **preoccupations**; **degree of self-insight**; and **level of contact with reality**.

Tests such as the MMPI are usually given **before or during** the interview sessions, so the psychologist can **focus on the problems the results flag**. If a profile shows a raised depression scale, for example, the interview probes sleep, appetite, hopelessness and suicidal thinking.

**Limits.** Interpreting interviews is **subjective** and can be swayed by the interviewer's **theoretical orientation and personality**. Still, interviews remain widely used and are a useful tool when **supplemented by more objective procedures**.

> **Classic background:** The interviewer's checklist is essentially the **mental status examination** taught in Indian clinical training. Interviews range from **unstructured** to **structured** (fixed questions in fixed order, as in the SCID); structure raises reliability at the cost of flexibility.

### Behavioural assessment

In **behavioural assessment**, an **observer** evaluates a person's **behaviour in a given situation**. The **better observers know** the person and the **more often** they interact, the **more accurate** their ratings (**Connelly and Ones, 2010**).

The book's example: **Arnold Buss and Robert Plomin** devised a questionnaire to assess **temperament** in **same-sex twins** (**Buss and Plomin, 1984**). The twins' **mothers**, drawing on their own observation, ticked the items describing **specific, easily observed** instances of their children's behaviour.

**Table 1.3** Sample items from the Buss and Plomin EASI Temperament Survey

Table 1.3 lists **six** short statements about a child that a mother can confirm from daily observation rather than abstract judgement. They sample emotional reactivity (for example, a child who bursts into tears readily), activity level (a child who cannot stay seated for long), sociability and shyness, and how quickly attention jumps from one plaything to the next. Each item names a concrete, visible behaviour, which is what makes this behavioural rather than self-report assessment. (Classic background: **EASI** = **Emotionality, Activity, Sociability, Impulsivity**, Buss and Plomin's 1975 dimensions; their **1984** revision dropped impulsivity to give the **EAS** survey.)

Informal observation happens too: counsellors routinely note **facial expressions, nervous gestures and general appearance** when forming a diagnosis. This is **less systematic** than formal behavioural assessment but can still be valuable.

### Thought and experience assessment

#### Thought sampling

Where behavioural assessment has observers watch actions, **thought sampling** records a person's **thoughts** systematically over time. Thoughts are private, so the only possible observer is the thinker: **observer and observed are the same person**. The method is **typically used with groups**, but it also helps diagnosis and treatment of **individuals**: a client may record thoughts and moods for the psychologist to analyse later. A client with panic attacks who notes her thoughts in the minutes before each attack may reveal a pattern of catastrophic thoughts about her heart.

#### Experience sampling

The **experience sampling method** adds the **social and environmental context**. Participants note, for instance, whether they were **alone or with others** when an **electronic beeper** signalled, or **what they were doing** and **where**. The aim is to see **how context influences thoughts and moods**.

**Smartphones** now make recording quick, and entries are **time- and date-stamped**, so researchers can check that entries were made when requested; entries made much later would be bent by the **vagaries of memory**.

- **Park, Armeli, and Tennen (2004):** college students kept **daily Internet diaries for 28 days** on their moods, stressful events and coping. The **most common negative events** were **academic**; the **second most common**, **interpersonal**. Other methods might not have revealed this so readily.
- **Takano, Sakamoto, and Tanno (2014):** **Japanese students** who reported **repetitive night-time thoughts**, dwelling obsessively on something, had trouble **falling asleep**, slept **fitfully** and slept **less**.

**Limitations.** Busy participants may **forget to record** when signalled, so useful data may come only from the **most conscientious**; and **moods** such as anger or sadness may colour what is reported (**Scollon, Kim-Prieto, and Diener, 2009**). Overall, the method is useful and gives data **comparable to self-report inventories**.

> **Classic background:** Experience sampling was developed by **Larson and Csikszentmihalyi**, using pagers that beeped at random; the clinical version is often called **ecological momentary assessment (EMA)**. Its advantage is capturing experience in the moment, reducing reliance on memory.

> **Trap.** **Behavioural assessment**: an **outside observer** records **overt behaviour**. **Thought sampling**: the **person observes himself or herself**. **Experience sampling**: thought sampling **plus context**.

### Gender and ethnic issues that affect assessment

A score is produced by someone of a particular gender and culture, interpreted by a clinician with his or her own assumptions, and compared with norms from a particular population; each step can bend the result. The book's summary names three influences: the **test-taker's gender**, the **test-taker's ethnic identity**, and the **administrator's attitudes and beliefs**.

#### Gender

**Women tend to score lower than men on assertiveness**, perhaps because of **sex-role training** that in some cultures teaches girls not to assert themselves. Whatever the cause, tests often show male-female differences on many characteristics **at every age**. In a study of **474 children** with a **median age of 11**, **girls** showed **more depression** and **more concern with what others thought of them** (**Rudolph and Conley, 2005**).

Gender also shows in **diagnosis**: **women are diagnosed with depression, anxiety and related disorders more often than men**. The book offers three explanations, which examiners ask you to distinguish:

1. **A real difference** in incidence.
2. **Interpretive bias**: gender bias or stereotyping in reading assessment results.
3. **Treatment bias**: therapists may be **biased against women**; the average **course of therapy is longer** for women, and **medication doses are higher**.

#### Asians

The **Asian-American** population is heterogeneous (**Chinese, Japanese, Filipino, Thai, Korean, Vietnamese** and others), and that creates a validity problem. Validation is local: an MMPI version that works in one large Chinese city carries no guarantee for Chinese Americans, or even for Chinese people elsewhere in China. Translations into Asian languages exist, but **little research** has checked their reliability and validity for Asian Americans.

Asian cultures also differ in **attitudes to mental illness and treatment**:

- **Shame and under-use.** Mental disorder tends to be seen as **shameful**, so Asian Americans, especially **first-generation immigrants**, **underuse** mental health services. Those **born in the United States** are **almost twice as likely** to seek treatment as those born abroad (**Meyer, Zane, Cho, and Takeuchi, 2009**).
- **First-generation Chinese students** were significantly less likely to seek help than **first-generation European students** (**Hsu and Alden, 2008**).
- Asian Americans tend to **wait until problems are severe**, and then are **less likely to benefit** (**Hwang, 2006**).
- **Somatic presentation.** A **New York** psychologist found that immigrant **Chinese** patients at first reported only **physical complaints** (backache, stomach ache) and needed **several sessions** of trust-building before describing depression. Some Asian languages, such as **Korean**, have **no specific word for depression**; one **Korean** client struck his chest and spoke of a "**down heart**" (**Kershaw, 2013**).
- Asian Americans are **far less likely to take antidepressants** than Whites (**Gonzalez, Tarraf, Brady, Chan, Miranda, and Leong, 2010**).

Given such different beliefs about disorder, different scores are unsurprising, and using **American values and norms as the universal standard** may explain why Asian Americans tend to receive **different psychiatric diagnoses** from European-heritage patients. (Classic background: voicing distress through bodily complaints is **somatisation**, familiar in Indian practice too, and the "down heart" is a classic **idiom of distress**.)

#### Blacks

**1990s** research found consistent Black-White differences on self-report tests, and some psychologists concluded that tests such as the **MMPI are biased against African Americans**. **Later research contradicted this**: among Black and White **psychiatric inpatients** at a **Veterans Administration (VA)** centre there were **no significant differences on any MMPI scale** (**Arbisi, Ben-Porath, and McNulty, 2002**).

A difference did appear on a **paranoia** measure: **Black college students** scored significantly higher on **distrust** of others, **suspicion of motives** and being **on guard**, and similar distrust and paranoid patterns were later found in **Black adults aged 55 to 64** (**Iacovino, Jackson, and Oltmanns, 2014**). This does **not** mean Black people are more paranoid. The researchers attributed the difference to **mistrust and wariness produced by pervasive discrimination and perceived racism** (**Combs, Penn, and Fenigstein, 2002**), and the 2014 study agreed. Realistic wariness is not a symptom.

- **Cultural identification protects:** African-American students who **identified strongly with Black cultural values** had **lower depression and hopelessness** (**Walker, Alabi, Roberts, and Obasi, 2010**).
- **Perceived discrimination harms:** **Black teenagers** high in **perceived discrimination** reported **more depression** and **lower self-esteem and life satisfaction** (**Seaton, Caldwell, Sellers, and Jackson, 2010**).
- **No interpreter bias:** White researchers did **not** interpret MMPI results differently according to the test-taker's race (**Knaster, 2013; Knaster and Micucci, 2013**).
- **Therapist match:** **Black students** rated **Black therapists** more favourably, accepted treatment options more readily with them and expected more benefit (**Thompson and Alexander, 2006; Want, Parham, Baker, and Sherman, 2004**).

> **Trap.** The book reverses the 1990s claim: **no Black-White differences on any MMPI scale** (Arbisi et al., 2002) and **no race effect on interpretation** (Knaster, 2013). The consistent difference (higher **distrust** scores) is read as a response to **discrimination**, not pathology.

#### Hispanics

**Hispanic** respondents score **similarly to Whites on the MMPI** (for example **Handel and Ben-Porath, 2000**), but on the **Rorschach**, respondents from **Mexico and Central and South America** differ significantly from the **Comprehensive System norms**, so those norms may not suit Hispanic populations (**Wood et al., 2002**).

Help-seeking is again low:

- Hispanics are **less likely than other minority groups** to seek counselling, and **half** of those who do **never return after the first visit** (**Dingfelder, 2005**).
- Significantly **more White than Hispanic adolescents** received adequate care (**Alexandre, Martins, Silvia, and Richard, 2009**).
- **Foreign-born** Hispanics use services even **less** than US-born (**Bridges, deArellano, Rheingold, Danielson, and Silcott, 2010**).
- Hispanic adolescents in treatment are seen for **shorter periods** than White adolescents (**Edman, Adams, Park, and Irwin, 2010**).
- **Mexican-American** students grew **more favourable to counselling** as they **integrated** into mainstream US culture (**Ramos-Sanchez and Atkinson, 2009**).

A confounding factor is the **shortage of Spanish-speaking** clinicians. Hispanic clients are more satisfied with staff who understand their **highly collectivist** culture (see **Malloy, Albright, Diaz-Loving, Dong, and Lee, 2004**) and benefit more from **Hispanic psychologists**, who are only **1 percent** of US psychologists. This may be why **Hispanics aged 65 and over** prefer to take mental health problems to their **family physician** (**Dupree, Herrera, Tyson, Jang, and King-Kallimans, 2010**).

Collectivism may also explain a **PTSD** finding. **Hispanic police officers** had **higher PTSD** rates than **Black** and **non-Hispanic White** officers and reported **significantly less social support** after critical incidents; the researchers suggested that valuing collectivity made them **especially sensitive to social isolation** (**Pole, Best, Metzler, and Marmar, 2005**). Among **civilian survivors of physical injury**, too, Hispanics showed **more PTSD symptoms** than Whites (**Marshall, Schell, and Miles, 2009**).

#### Other cultural issues in assessment

**Hermann Rorschach** was among the first to notice culture's effect on test performance: in **1921** he found different inkblot responses in **two culturally distinct regions of Switzerland** and predicted large differences across peoples and races (quoted in **Allen and Dana, 2004**). Normality itself is culture-bound: an **MMPI-2** study of **American Indians** showed responses reflecting behaviour **normal in their culture** but **pathological** by mainstream White standards (**Hill, Pace, and Robbins, 2010**), and what is normal in one culture may seem wrong, sick or simply strange in another (**Cheung, 2009**).

**Translation** brings its own problems (see **Gudmundsson, 2009**), especially when Western tests go to non-Western cultures such as Chinese or Filipino. For **traditional Chinese** people, key characteristics include being **gracious**, **family-oriented**, **harmonious** with others and **frugal**, and **none** of these is typical of what American inventories measure. (Classic background: this gap led **Fanny Cheung** and colleagues to build the **Chinese Personality Assessment Inventory**, the standard example of an **emic**, culture-specific test as against an **etic** one exported unchanged.)

#### Highlights: personality assessment across groups

| Group | Tends to score |
|---|---|
| **Asians** | **High** in collectivism; **low** in individual competitiveness and assertiveness; **low** in self-enhancement and optimism; **low** in seeking mental health treatment |
| **African Americans** | **Low** in trust of others; **low** in hopelessness and depression **if** they identify with Black cultural values; **high** in depression; **low** in self-esteem **if** they perceive discrimination |
| **Hispanics** | **Low** in seeking mental health treatment; **high** in collectivism; **high** in PTSD symptoms after injuries |

The African American row lists depression as both low and high: the low entry is conditional on cultural identification (Walker et al., 2010), the high entry matches the perceived-discrimination research (Seaton et al., 2010). The chapter summary adds that people from **collectivist** societies tend to score **lower on self-enhancement** and **higher on pessimism, negative affectivity and psychological distress** than people from individualistic ones.

#### Translation, format and content problems

- **Content.** Translating the **MMPI-2** into **Arabic** raised the problem of **sex** items, since open discussion of sex is considered inappropriate or offensive in Arab countries. The items were **kept** but made **optional**; in a study of **Iranian college students**, **more than 90 percent** skipped them (**Nezami, Zamani, and DeFrank, 2008**).
- **Stimulus.** The **TAT cannot be used in Islamic cultures** because of the prohibition on **depicting humans**. Asked to make up TAT stories, **European** women did so easily, while **Muslim** women hesitated and consistently **declined to invent** interpretations (**Bullard, 2005**).
- **Idiom.** American **slang** (an item about having the blues, or about keeping up with the neighbours) may mean little in translation. Yet many American tests translate well into **Arabic** where the trait is shared: the US-developed **Internet Addiction Test** predicted Internet addiction equally well in **Arab teenagers** (**Hawi, 2013**).
- **Format.** **True-false** and **multiple-choice** formats feel natural to American students but alien elsewhere. When the **MMPI** reached **Israel in the 1970s**, many respondents struggled with the **true-false format** and the **instructions had to be rewritten** (**Butcher, 2004**).

Adapting a test so that it validly measures relevant variables in another culture is difficult and demands real cultural knowledge and sensitivity.

## 1.6 Research in the Study of Personality

### Choosing a method

Researchers use different methods because they study different things. Some care only about **overt behaviour**, what people do and say in response to stimuli. Others study **feelings and conscious experience** through tests and questionnaires (self-report inventories are among the most used research tools). Others try to reach **unconscious forces**. A method suited to one aspect may be useless for another.

The book names **four major methods**: **clinical**, **experimental**, **virtual (online)** and **correlational**. All depend, to differing degrees, on **objective observation**, which the authors call the defining characteristic of scientific research in any field. The chapter summary lists the requirements of research as **objective observation**, **controlled and systematic conditions**, and **duplication and verifiability** (a study can be repeated and its results checked). These three are the yardstick for what follows: the experiment meets them best, the clinical method least.

### The clinical method

#### The case study

The main clinical method is the **case study** (or **case history**): a **detailed history of an individual built from data from many sources**. The psychologist searches the patient's past and present for clues to current emotional problems, writing in effect a short biography of the person's emotional life, including feelings, fears and experiences.

**Freud** built psychoanalysis largely on case studies (Chapter 2), searching patients' childhoods for the events and conflicts behind their adult neuroses:

- **Katharina**, **18**, suffered **anxiety attacks and shortness of breath**. Freud traced them to **early sexual experiences** she reported, including a **seduction attempt by her father when she was 14**.
- **Lucy**: Freud linked her **hallucinations** to past events involving her **love for her employer**, which had been **rebuffed**.

From cases like these Freud derived a theory that puts **sexual conflicts and traumas** at the root of neurosis. He and later case-study theorists looked for **consistencies and patterns** across patients and then **generalised** what they saw **to everyone**.

> **Classic background:** Both cases are in **Studies on Hysteria (Breuer and Freud, 1895)**. Freud met **Katharina** at an inn in the Alps while on holiday; the 1895 text disguised the abuser as her **uncle**, and a **1924 footnote** revealed it was her **father**. **Lucy R.** was an English governess in a widowed factory director's household, and her hallucinations were **olfactory** (a smell of burnt pudding, later of cigar smoke).

#### Other clinical methods, and the verdict

Clinicians also use **tests, interviews and dream analysis**. The clinical method tries to be scientific but lacks the **precision and control** of experimental and correlational research. Its data are **subjective**, concerning mental, largely **unconscious** events and early experiences; they are open to interpretations that may reflect the **therapist's biases** more than other methods allow; and **childhood memories** may be **distorted by time** and are hard to verify. Its strength is depth: it opens a window onto the deeper layers of personality, and it is used above all by the **psychoanalytic and neo-psychoanalytic** theorists.

> **Trap.** The case study's specific weaknesses: **no control** of variables; **subjective, unverifiable** data such as retrospective memories; interpretation shaped by the **therapist's theory and bias**; and **generalisation** from a small, unusual sample (often neurotic patients) to everyone. Its strength: rich, deep data on one person. In the book's ranking it meets the requirements of research **less well** than the experimental and correlational methods.

### The experimental method

#### The logic of an experiment

An **experiment** determines the **effect of one or more variables** on behaviour. Everyday life bombards us with stimuli (lights, sounds, screens, voices, smells, instructions, chatter), so ordinarily we cannot tell which one changed behaviour. The experimenter therefore lets **only one variable operate**, **eliminating or holding constant** all others; if behaviour changes, that variable must be the cause.

- The **independent variable** (stimulus variable) is the condition the experimenter **manipulates**.
- The **dependent variable** is what the experimenter **measures**, usually the participants' **behaviour** in response.

Two groups, both drawn **at random from the same population**, rule out other influences. The **experimental group** receives the **treatment** (the independent variable); the **control group** does **not**. Behaviour is measured in **both groups before and after**. If some other variable were operating (time of day, a news event, practice), **both** groups would change; if only the independent variable matters, **only the experimental group** changes.

> **Trap.** **Independent** = **manipulated** (the cause tested); **dependent** = **measured** (the effect, which *depends* on it). "The effect of X on Y": X is independent, Y dependent. In correlational research the "independent variable" (age, birth order, neuroticism) is **measured as it exists, not manipulated**. Classic background: distinguish **random selection** (drawing a sample so results generalise) from **random assignment** (allocating participants to groups by chance so they start equivalent); the book's phrase covers both, but random assignment is what lets an experiment show cause and effect.

#### Applying the experimental method: Bandura and imitated aggression

**Albert Bandura** (Chapter 13) wanted to know whether **children imitate aggressive behaviour they see in adults**. He could have watched children on streets or playgrounds, hoping they would witness violence, and waited to see whether they copied it. That would fail twice over. The sample would be wrong: some children might be aggressive already, so later aggression could not be credited to the incident. And the stimulus would be uncontrolled: children see many kinds of violence daily (games, films, television, streets), whereas a fair test needs every child to see the **same** aggressive act.

Bandura's design fixed both problems. Children's **pre-experiment aggression was measured**. The **experimental group** watched the **same display of adult aggression**; the **control group** watched **non-aggressive adults in the same setting**. **Trained observers** recorded both groups' behaviour. Children who saw the aggressive adult **behaved aggressively**; the control group **did not change**. Conclusion: aggressiveness **can be learned by imitating** others.

In the vocabulary: the **independent variable** is the type of adult model; the **dependent variable** is the children's later aggression; the **pre-measure** rules out "they were aggressive anyway". (Classic background: these are the **Bobo doll** studies, Bandura, Ross and Ross, **1961**.)

#### Limitations of the experimental method

1. **Ethics and safety.** Clinicians would value experimental data on which **child-rearing** practices cause adult problems, but nobody can take babies from their parents and raise them under different regimes to find out.
2. **Being watched changes behaviour.** Participants who know they are observed may act differently, **guess the purpose** and try to **please or frustrate** the experimenter, so the dependent variable reflects their attitudes, not the treatment.

When it is **well controlled and systematic**, however, the experiment yields **excellent data**; the summary calls it **the most precise research method**. (Classic background: the guessing problem is **demand characteristics**, Orne, 1962; the effect of being observed is often called the **Hawthorne effect**; artificial settings lower **ecological validity**.)

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of the experiment?" Ethical limits and participants' awareness of being observed are named. **Inability to establish cause and effect** is **not**: that is the limitation of the **correlational** method. Showing causation is the experiment's great advantage.

### Virtual research

Psychologists now run tests, surveys and experiments **online**, much as they once could only in a laboratory, recruiting participants quickly and cheaply through **crowd-sourcing**. A popular source is **Amazon's Mechanical Turk**, which pays people small sums for tasks including research participation (see **Emanuel, 2014; Mason and Suri, 2012; Paolacci and Chandler, 2014**).

**Advantages.** Faster responses, lower costs and access to a **broader range** of people by age, education, employment, income, class and ethnicity, far more diverse than a campus sample (**Crump and colleagues, 2013**).

**Disadvantages.**

- **Who is online.** Web users are **younger, more affluent and better educated** than non-users, so online samples are not fully representative (though likely more so than college students). Often missing: **older adults, low-income people, the visually impaired and non-English speakers** (**Buhrmester, Kwang, and Gosling, 2011; Suarez-Balcazar, Balcazar, and Taylor-Ritzler, 2009**).
- **Who responds.** In **Germany and the United States**, people who **ignored** an online survey were judged from their **personal websites** to be **more introverted and disagreeable, less open and lower in self-esteem** than responders (**Marcus and Schutz, 2005**).
- **Who chooses online.** Given a choice, **more extraverted** American students took part **in person**, **more introverted** ones **online** (**Witt, Donnellan, and Orlando, 2011**).
- **Response quality.** Online and **telephone** respondents did **not differ** in willingness to disclose sensitive information, but online **response rates** averaged about **10 percent lower** than telephone or mail surveys, and about **10 percent** of online participants showed **excessive clicking and mouse movement** (**Fan and Yan, 2010; Hines, Douglas, and Mahmood, 2010; Stieger and Reips, 2010**).
- **Honesty** about age, gender, ethnicity, education or income is hard to verify.

**Verdict.** Many comparisons find online and laboratory results **generally consistent and similar**.

> **Trap.** Two different "10 percent" figures: online **response rate about 10 percent lower**, and **about 10 percent** of participants clicking excessively. Introverts **choose** online participation (Witt et al., 2011), and online **non-responders** were judged more introverted (Marcus and Schutz, 2005): one finding is about mode, the other about who ignores a survey.

### The correlational method

#### What the method does

The **correlational method** studies **relationships that already exist** among variables; the glossary defines it as a statistical technique measuring the **degree of relationship between two variables**, expressed by the **correlation coefficient**. Nothing is manipulated. Instead of creating stress in the laboratory, researchers study people already under stress, such as **police officers, racing drivers or test-anxious students**. Nor are participants assigned to experimental and control groups: people who already differ on a variable such as **age, gender, birth order, aggressiveness or neuroticism** are compared on another, such as **personality test responses** or **job performance**.

#### Applying the correlational method

The question is how one variable **changes as a function of** another: Is **birth order** related to aggressiveness? Do high-**IQ** scorers make better computer scientists? Do highly **anxious** people use social media more? Such answers matter wherever success must be predicted; **college entrance examinations** rest on correlations between **standardised test scores and classroom success**.

The book's worked example: a psychologist measures the **need for achievement** of students **already admitted** to college, using a **self-report inventory**, and compares it with their **grades**. Achievement need is measured, not manipulated. Students **high in the need for achievement earned higher grades** (**Atkinson, Lens, and O'Malley, 1976**).

Correlation also underpins assessment: **the reliability and validity of tests are typically established by correlation** (scores on two occasions, or test scores against a criterion).

#### The correlation coefficient

The **correlation coefficient** gives the **direction** and **strength** of a relationship.

- **Direction.** High scores on one variable with **high** scores on the other: **positive**. High with **low**: **negative**.
- **Range.** From **+1.00** (**perfect positive**) to **-1.00** (**perfect negative**).
- **Strength.** The **closer to +1.00 or -1.00**, the **stronger** the relationship and the more confidently one variable predicts the other.

> **Trap.** A **negative** correlation is neither weak nor "no relationship". **Sign = direction; distance from zero = strength**, so **-.70 is stronger than +.30**. Values outside **-1.00 to +1.00** are impossible. Classic background: the usual statistic is **Pearson's r**; squaring it gives the **coefficient of determination** (r = .50 means 25 percent shared variance).

#### Cause and effect

The **primary limitation** of the correlational method is that a correlation, however high, does **not show that one variable caused the other**. A causal link may exist, but it cannot be assumed as it can after a well-controlled experiment.

The book's example: a strong **negative** correlation between **shyness** and **self-esteem** (the shyer, the lower the self-esteem). Three causal stories fit it:

1. Shyness may **lower self-esteem**.
2. **Low self-esteem** may make people **shy**.
3. A **third variable**, such as **physical appearance** or **parental rejection**, may cause **both**.

This troubles **researchers**, who want causes, but for **practitioners**, who need to **predict**, correlation is entirely satisfactory. To predict college grades from achievement need, a high correlation is all that is required; whether the need *causes* the grades does not matter.

> **Trap.** "Correlation does not imply causation" is the **primary** limitation, but correlational research is fully adequate for **prediction** (selection tests, entrance examinations, reliability and validity). Classic background: the two obstacles to causal inference are the **directionality problem** (stories 1 and 2) and the **third-variable problem** (story 3).

### Idiographic and nomothetic approaches

> **Classic background:** Not drawn in Schultz's Chapter 1, but a staple of Indian papers. The terms come from the philosopher **Windelband** and were brought into personality psychology by **Allport (1937)**. **Idiographic** research studies the **individual case in depth** (the case study; Allport's *Letters from Jenny*). **Nomothetic** research seeks **general laws** across many people, comparing them on common dimensions (experiments, correlational studies, inventories such as the MMPI). Idiographic fits the **uniqueness** pole of the human-nature issues (1.8), nomothetic the **universality** pole. **Idiographic** (Greek *idios*, one's own) is not "ideographic"; **nomothetic** comes from *nomos*, law.

## 1.7 The Role of Theory in Personality Theories

### What a theory is, and what it is for

People dismiss ideas as "only a theory", as if a theory were a vague hunch, the opposite of a fact. The authors grant that a theory with **no research evidence** is indeed speculation. But the reverse problem is as serious: a mass of data means **nothing** until it is organised into an explanatory framework. A theory is that framework, a **map** that represents and explains the data and their interrelationships and fits them into a meaningful pattern. Thousands of separate findings on birth order, anxiety, dreams and parenting, with no account of how they connect, are data without theory.

Formally, **theories are sets of principles used to explain a particular class of phenomena**, here the behaviours and experiences of personality. A useful personality theory must:

1. **Be testable**, capable of **stimulating research** so that its propositions can be **accepted or rejected** using the methods of 1.6.
2. **Clarify and explain** the data by organising them into a **coherent framework**.
3. Help us **understand and predict** behaviour.
4. Then be **applied** to help people **change** behaviour, feelings and emotions from harmful to helpful.

The summary condenses this: a theory provides a framework for **simplifying and describing data** meaningfully.

> **Classic background:** Other textbooks list **criteria for evaluating theories**. **Hjelle and Ziegler**: **verifiability**, **heuristic value** (stimulating research), **internal consistency**, **parsimony**, **comprehensiveness** and **functional significance**. **Feist and Feist**: generates research, is falsifiable, organises data, guides action, is internally consistent, is parsimonious. A **hypothesis** is a specific testable prediction derived from a theory.

> **Trap.** A good theory must be **testable**, not "proven true"; parts of it may be rejected by research. **Data without theory are meaningless; theory without data is speculation.**

### The autobiographical nature of personality theories

Theories aim at objectivity, but some have a **subjective component** reflecting the theorist's own life, a **disguised autobiography** in which personal events serve as data. However impartial scientists try to be, their viewpoint colours their perception; theorists are human and, like the rest of us, find it hard to accept ideas that clash with their own experience.

So to understand a theory fully, learn about its author's **life** and ask how specific events shaped it. The typical sequence is that the theorist **first describes himself or herself** and **later seeks data from others** to justify generalising that personal view.

- **William James**, considered by many the greatest American psychologist, held that **biography** is crucial for anyone studying human nature, more important than knowing eminent people's theories or systems.
- **Freud** said his **most important patient**, the one who taught him most about personality, was **himself**.
- The historian **Friedman (1996)** noted that psychologists publish biographical and autobiographical sketches of their colleagues more than any other profession, as though conceding that their **lives and values** hold the key to their scientific knowledge.

**The caution.** The arrow may point the other way: the **theory may shape what the theorist remembers** and reports about the life. Most of our biographical information comes from **autobiographies written late in life**, after the theory was proposed and defended, and years of public commitment may **distort** memories. Are only supporting events recalled? Are awkward ones forgotten, or experiences even invented to lend credibility? These questions often cannot be answered, but they should be kept in mind; and your own experiences may shape how you judge theorists' lives and theories.

**Preview from later chapters.** The pairings of life and theory most often asked:

| Theorist | Life event | Idea linked to it |
|---|---|---|
| **Freud** | Young, adoring mother; older, stern father | **Oedipus complex** |
| **Adler** | Sickly childhood with **rickets**, jealous of a healthy older brother | **Inferiority feelings**, **compensation** |
| **Horney** | Envy of an adored older brother; a domineering father | **Basic anxiety**, neurotic **need for affection** |
| **Erikson** | Never knew his biological father; rejected by both Jewish and German classmates | **Identity crisis** |
| **Jung** | Lonely childhood; inner turmoil after the **1913** break with Freud | **Collective unconscious**, archetypes |

> **Trap.** The autobiographical argument cuts **both ways**: the life may shape the theory, and the theory may shape the **remembered** life, because memoirs are written late. "My most important patient was myself" is **Freud**; "biography matters more than theories" is **William James**.

## 1.8 Questions about Human Nature: What Are We Like?

### Why images of human nature matter

Every personality theory carries an **image of human nature**, an answer to what being human fundamentally means. Poets, philosophers and artists have debated it for centuries, and the theorists agree no better. Their images allow a **meaningful comparison** among them, because each image is the framework within which a theorist sees people and builds a theory.

Schultz define a theorist's image by **six issues**, each a continuum between two poles, and rate every later theorist on them. Many theorists take moderate or intermediate positions. **Chapter 1 does not place any theorist** on the issues; that is done chapter by chapter. What you need here is each issue, its two poles, and what each side implies.

### Issue 1. Are we in charge of our lives? Free will versus determinism

Can we **consciously direct** our actions, **choose** our thoughts and behaviour, rationally selecting among alternatives, with awareness and **self-control**? Are we **masters of our fate**, or **victims** of past experience, biology, unconscious forces or external stimuli beyond conscious control? Have outside events shaped us so that we **cannot change**?

- **Free will**: people consciously choose and are responsible for their actions; therapy helps them recognise and use choice.
- **Determinism**: behaviour is caused by forces outside awareness (drives, biology, early experience, environment); change comes from altering those causes.
- **Moderate positions**: some behaviour is determined by the past, some is spontaneous and under our control.

The book's reading of **karma** (1.4) is a cultural instance of the determinist pole.

### Issue 2. What dominates us? Nature versus nurture

Is personality shaped more by **inherited** traits and attributes (**nature**, genetic endowment) or by **environment** (the **nurturing** influences of upbringing, education and training)? The same controversy runs through **intelligence**: genetic endowment versus stimulation at home and school.

- **Nature**: temperament, genes and biology set personality, which is then relatively hard to change.
- **Nurture**: learning, parenting and culture shape personality, which can change when the environment changes.
- **Both**: many theorists accept both forces but weight them differently, some treating inheritance as dominant, others environment.

### Issue 3. Are we dependent on or independent of childhood? Past versus present

How important are **early childhood experiences** compared with **later events**?

- **Childhood pole: historical determinism**, the view that personality is **basically fixed in the early years** and **subject to little change** thereafter. On this view personality is largely settled **by about age 5**, and adult development merely elaborates themes laid down then; a clinician holding it treats adult problems by going back to childhood.
- **Present pole**: personality is **more independent of the past**, open to present experience and to **aspirations, hopes and goals for the future**.
- **Intermediate**: early experience **shapes** personality but **not rigidly or permanently**; later experience can **reinforce or modify** early patterns.

> **Trap.** **Historical determinism** (fixed by about **age 5**) belongs to Issue 3, not Issue 1. A theorist can be a determinist in the Issue 1 sense (behaviour caused by present reinforcement, say) without being a historical determinist. The book labels Issue 3 both "dependent or independent of childhood" and "the importance of the past versus the present". Classic background: the age-5 position is the one later attributed to **Freud** (Chapter 2); **Erikson** (Chapter 6) represents lifelong development.

### Issue 4. Is human nature unique or universal?

- **Uniqueness**: each person's every act and utterance has **no counterpart** in anyone else, which makes **comparing people meaningless** and favours in-depth single-case study (idiographic).
- **Universality**: uniqueness is allowed but interpreted **within overall patterns of behaviour accepted as universal**, at least within a culture; the theorist seeks laws, stages, types or traits that apply to all (nomothetic).

This links back to uniqueness in the definition of personality (1.1) and to the doubt, raised in 1.3 and 1.4, about whether "universal" patterns drawn from White Western samples really are universal.

### Issue 5. Our life goals: satisfaction or growth? Equilibrium versus growth

What ultimately motivates us?

- **Equilibrium (satisfaction)**: we work like **self-regulating machines**, content while an **internal balance** is kept; we act to **satisfy physical needs**, **seek pleasure and avoid pain**, and keep **stress to a minimum**. Some theorists see people as little more than **tension-reducing, pleasure-seeking animals**.
- **Growth**: we are driven mainly by the **need to grow**, realise our potential and reach ever-higher **self-actualisation**, development and fulfilment, seeking challenge rather than mere relief.

> **Trap.** "Satisfaction or growth" (section heading) and "equilibrium versus growth" (summary) are the same issue. "Equilibrium" means a **tension-reduction** (homeostatic) model of motivation, not emotional stability; **self-actualisation** is the **growth** pole.

### Issue 6. Our outlook: optimism or pessimism?

Are people basically **good or evil**, **kind or cruel**, **compassionate or merciless**? This is a **value judgement** about **morality**, supposedly out of place in objective science, yet several theorists have addressed it and it has generated substantial research.

- **Optimism**: people are **humanitarian, altruistic and socially conscious**, and will grow towards goodness under the right conditions.
- **Pessimism**: theorists find **few** such qualities in people, individually or collectively.

### Why the six issues matter

Our image of human nature is the **lens** through which we perceive, judge and deal with others, and through which we **define ourselves**. There are many influences on personality and many ways of explaining it, and **cultural factors such as child-rearing practices** can produce **different images of human nature** (recall the Arab adolescents and the Asian-American students whose well-being came from pleasing their parents). Few people approach the subject without preconceptions, since it is the study of ourselves; the book asks you to record your own answers now and revisit them at the end of the course.

> **Classic background:** **Hjelle and Ziegler** compare theorists on **nine basic assumptions**: freedom-determinism, rationality-irrationality, holism-elementalism, constitutionalism-environmentalism, changeability-unchangeability, subjectivity-objectivity, proactivity-reactivity, homeostasis-heterostasis and knowability-unknowability. For a question naming **Schultz**, the answer is **six** issues. As illustrations of the poles, **Freud** (Chapter 2) is the standard example of determinism, historical determinism and a tension-reduction model with a largely pessimistic outlook; **Maslow and Rogers** (Chapters 9 and 10) of growth and optimism. The full ratings belong to each theorist's chapter.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **One definition, many theories.** The chapter's working definition (an enduring, unique cluster of internal and external characteristics that influences behaviour across situations) is a starting point; each theorist will substitute his or her own account of what lies behind the word "I".
2. **Person and situation together.** The mask-versus-inner-self contrast and the Mischel-Funder debate end in the same place: **interaction**. Keep it in mind when later theories lean towards traits (Allport), environment (Skinner) or their interplay (Bandura).
3. **Context shapes both personality and its measurement.** Gender, race, culture and social media appear first as forces that **shape** personality (1.2 to 1.4), then as forces that **distort assessment** (1.5). Self-enhancement, help-seeking, somatic presentation and test translation are one argument: a theory or test built on White Western men cannot be assumed to fit everyone.
4. **Depth versus rigour.** Projective tests and case studies go deep but are subjective and unreliable; self-report inventories and experiments are objective but miss what people cannot or will not report. "Validity favours the MMPI over the Rorschach" and "the experiment is the most precise method" are the same judgement made twice.
5. **Reliability, validity and correlation are linked.** Reliability and validity are established by correlation; correlation supports prediction but not causation; only the experiment, with manipulation and a control group, shows cause and effect.
6. **Lives and tests.** Every later chapter opens with the theorist's life (theories as partial autobiography, with the warning about late memoirs) and ends with research and evaluation (a theory is only as good as its testable propositions).
7. **The six issues are the comparison grid** applied to every theorist, and culture (for example, the book's reading of karma) can push a whole society towards one pole.

## Worked example

*A viva panel describes a case and asks you to reason it through.* Mrs R, 34, moved two years ago from a village to a large Indian city to live with her husband's joint family. Her physician refers her after months of headaches, body aches and fatigue with no medical cause. She says her heart "feels heavy" but denies sadness, and adds that this is her karma. She studied to class 5 and reads her own language slowly.

**"How would you begin?"** With a **clinical interview**, since it does not depend on reading. I would note appearance, demeanour and attitude, expression, posture and gestures, preoccupations, self-insight and contact with reality, and ask about her life history, family relationships and the referral. Her bodily complaints and "heavy heart" parallel the book's Korean client with a "down heart" (Kershaw, 2013): distress put into physical terms, which may take several sessions of trust before she can name it. Because interview interpretation is **subjective** and shaped by my own orientation, I would supplement it with more objective procedures.

**"Would you give her the MMPI?"** Not first. Self-report inventories may not suit people with **limited reading skills**, and the MMPI-2's **567** items could exhaust her motivation. A translated version normed elsewhere may not be **valid** for her (validation in one Chinese city did not guarantee validity for Chinese elsewhere), and the **true-false format** (Israel, Butcher, 2004) and sexual items (the Arabic MMPI-2) could distort her answers. Any self-report measure should be short, properly translated, read aloud if needed, and interpreted against **appropriate norms**.

**"The Rorschach or TAT, then?"** They avoid the reading problem but are **subjective**, low in **inter-scorer reliability** and validity, and poorly standardised; validity research favours the **MMPI over the Rorschach** (Wood et al., 2002), and Comprehensive System **norms** did not fit Hispanic samples, so they may not fit her either. The **TAT** would be unusable if her family held a religious prohibition on depicting people (Bullard, 2005). At most, a projective test would generate hypotheses to check in interview.

**"How could you see how her symptoms vary across the day?"** **Experience sampling**: she records mood and bodily state at set signals, plus the **context** (alone or with family, what she was doing), by voice note if writing is hard. If headaches cluster in hours spent with her mother-in-law, that reveals what no questionnaire would. Limits: she may forget when busy, and mood may colour her entries (Scollon, Kim-Prieto and Diener, 2009).

**"She says it is her karma."** The book reads karma as a **fatalistic, deterministic** belief that may foster a **passive, resigned** stance, the **determinism** pole of Issue 1 held by a culture; it may explain her delay in seeking help. It is not **historical determinism** (Issue 3). I would respect the belief and look for choices she can make within it.

**"Would gender affect your interpretation?"** Yes. Women are diagnosed with depression and anxiety more often, and the book gives three explanations: a **real difference**, **interpretive bias**, and **therapist bias** (longer therapy, higher doses for women). I would check that my conclusions are not simply following the stereotype.

**"Our adolescent data show r = -.45 between daily social media hours and self-esteem. Does social media lower self-esteem?"** It is a **moderate negative correlation** (more hours, lower self-esteem), within **-1.00 to +1.00**, its strength read from its distance from zero. It does **not** prove causation: as with the book's shyness example, social media may lower self-esteem, low self-esteem may drive adolescents online, or a **third variable** such as parental rejection may cause both. It is still useful for **prediction** (whom to screen). To test causation I would run an **experiment**: randomly assign adolescents to an **experimental group** that cuts social media use and a **control group** that does not, measure self-esteem (the **dependent variable**) **before and after**, and see whether only the experimental group changes, remembering that participants who guess the purpose may try to please me.

## MCQ traps

1. **Personality** < Latin **persona**, the **actor's mask**, about **1500**; not Jung's persona archetype.
2. **Adams (1954)**: personality is what we mean when we say **"I"**.
3. Person-situation debate: **1960s**, started by **Mischel (1968, 1973)**, about **20 years**; **Funder (2001)** called the dichotomy a fake; outcome **interactionism**.
4. Schultz: **unique, relatively enduring internal and external aspects** of character influencing behaviour in different situations; "dynamic organisation ... psychophysical systems" is **Allport (1937)**.
5. **Adjective Check List**: **300** adjectives; Table 1.1 shows **30** (270 more).
6. Online profiles are **as accurate** as face-to-face impressions (**Gosling et al., 2007; Back et al., 2010**, US and Germany); **Blumer and Doring (2012)**: people appear **more emotionally stable** online.
7. **Marriott and Buchanan (2014)**: introverted, neurotic, lonely, awkward people express their true selves **more easily online**.
8. **Lam and Peng (2010)**: Chinese adolescents **13 to 18**; excessive Internet use with **anxiety and depression**.
9. **Gentzler et al. (2011)**: **phone** contact with parents beat **social networking**, which went with loneliness, anxiety, conflict.
10. Australia: **extraverts call**, **neurotic, shy, less conscientious** people **text**. Trolls: **male**, average age **29**, **sadism**.
11. **Alansari (2006)**: **7,000+** students, **16** Islamic nations, women more anxious in **11 of 16**.
12. **McCrae et al. (1998)**: only Chinese immigrants in Canada **10+ years** rose in **extraversion**.
13. **Fischer and Boer (2011)**: **400+ million**, **63 countries**, individualism with **well-being**; **Chiao and Blizinsky (2010)**: collectivism with **lower** anxiety and depression.
14. **Janteloven** is **Nordic**; **Silvera and Seger (2004)**: Americans self-enhanced more than **Norwegians**.
15. **Church et al. (2014)**: **Chinese** most self-effacing (vs US, Mexico, Venezuela); **Heine, Takata and Lehman (2000)**: **Canadians** vs Japanese.
16. **Dwairy et al. (2006)**: authoritarian parenting **did not harm** Arab adolescents.
17. **Reliability** = consistency; **validity** = measuring what is intended; reliable-but-invalid is possible, valid-but-unreliable is not.
18. Projective tests: low **inter-scorer reliability**.
19. **MMPI 1943**; **MMPI-2 1989** (nonsexist); **MMPI-A 1992**, **478** items, adolescents; **MMPI-2-RF 2008**; book's count **567**; **140+** languages; most widely used test.
20. Strictly **567 = MMPI-2**; the original MMPI had **550** distinct items (566 printed).
21. **Schwarz (1999)**: **61.5 percent** chose "think for themselves" from a list, **4.6 percent** unprompted.
22. **Holtgraves (2004)**: faking good made students **more careful and slower**.
23. MMPI-2-RF caught faked **pain** (Crighton et al., 2013) and faked **PTSD** (Mason et al., 2013); patients can learn **online** to hide symptoms (Hartmann and Hartmann, 2014).
24. Online testing: **no score difference** from paper; **more disclosure** online.
25. **Rorschach**: **1921**, **Swiss psychiatrist**, **1884 to 1922**, **Klecksographie/Blotto**, nickname **Klecks**, **10** blots (printing cost), died **9 months** after publication.
26. **Comprehensive System** = **Exner**; validity research favours the **MMPI over the Rorschach** (Wood et al., 2002).
27. **TAT**: **Morgan and Murray (1935)**, **19 pictures + 1 blank** (31 in full set); research scoring for **achievement, affiliation, power**.
28. **Rotter Incomplete Sentence Blank**: sentence completion with **more objective** scoring; word association weighs **response speed**.
29. **Connelly and Ones (2010)**: observers who **know the person better** are more accurate; **Buss and Plomin (1984)**: **mothers** rated **same-sex twins**.
30. Thought sampling: **observer = observed**; experience sampling adds **context**.
31. **Park, Armeli and Tennen (2004)**: **28 days**; negative events mostly **academic**, then **interpersonal**.
32. **Rudolph and Conley (2005)**: **474** children, median age **11**, girls more depressed.
33. US-born Asian Americans **almost twice** as likely to seek treatment (**Meyer et al., 2009**); "**down heart**" (**Kershaw, 2013**).
34. **Arbisi et al. (2002)**: **no** Black-White difference on **any** MMPI scale (VA patients); distrust scores reflect **discrimination**.
35. Hispanics: **MMPI** like Whites, **Rorschach** norms differ; **half** never return after one visit; **1 percent** of US psychologists are Hispanic.
36. **Iran**: **90+ percent** skipped Arabic MMPI-2 sex items; TAT unusable in **Islamic** cultures (**Bullard, 2005**); true-false unfamiliar in **Israel, 1970s**.
37. **Katharina**, **18**, father's seduction attempt at **14**; **Lucy**: hallucinations, rebuffed love for her **employer**.
38. **Independent** = manipulated, **dependent** = measured; **experimental group** gets the treatment.
39. Online research: response rate **~10 percent lower**; **~10 percent** click excessively; introverts **choose online** (Witt et al., 2011).
40. Correlation runs **-1.00 to +1.00**; **-.70 is stronger than +.30**; **Atkinson, Lens and O'Malley (1976)**: need for achievement with grades.
41. Correlation cannot show **causation**; shyness-self-esteem third variables: **physical appearance**, **parental rejection**.
42. **William James**: biography matters more than theories; **Freud**: his most important patient was **himself**.
43. **Historical determinism**: personality fixed by about **age 5** (Issue 3, not Issue 1).
44. Schultz: **six** issues; **nine** assumptions is Hjelle and Ziegler; "satisfaction or growth" = "equilibrium versus growth".

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Personality** | The unique, relatively enduring internal and external aspects of a person's character that influence behaviour across situations |
| **Persona** | Latin word for an actor's mask, root of "personality" |
| **Interactionist approach** | Explaining behaviour by stable traits, the situation and their interaction |
| **Person-situation debate** | Controversy (1960s onwards, about 20 years) over whether traits or situations drive behaviour |
| **Adjective Check List** | Self-description test of 300 adjectives |
| **Cross-cultural psychology** | Study of how cultural forces shape behaviour and personality |
| **Cultural neuroscience** | Field showing cultural differences in brain activity and genetic makeup |
| **Karma** | Hindu and Buddhist belief, read by the book as fatalistic, that past and present actions fix destiny |
| **Fate attribution** | Explaining events by destiny rather than personal action |
| **Individualism** | Cultural emphasis on personal freedom, choice and action |
| **Collectivism** | Cultural emphasis on group norms, roles and constraints |
| **Self-efficacy** | Feeling adequate, efficient and competent in coping and controlling events |
| **Self-enhancement** | Promoting oneself aggressively and conspicuously |
| **Self-effacement** | Playing oneself down; self-criticism |
| **Janteloven** | Nordic code against placing self above community |
| **Narcissism** | Inflated, unrealistic self-concept |
| **Assessment** | Measurement or evaluation of personality |
| **Reliability** | Consistency of response to an assessment device |
| **Inter-scorer reliability** | Agreement between examiners interpreting the same responses |
| **Validity** | Extent to which a device measures what it is intended to measure |
| **Standardisation** | Uniform administration and scoring, with norms from a representative sample |
| **Norms** | Reference-group scores against which an individual score is interpreted |
| **Self-report inventory** | Test in which people answer questions about their own behaviour and feelings |
| **Objective test** | Test scored by fixed key, independent of examiner judgement |
| **MMPI** | Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory; true-false; 1943; most widely used test |
| **MMPI-2** | 1989 revision, contemporary nonsexist language, 567 items, adults |
| **MMPI-A** | 1992 adolescent version, 478 items |
| **MMPI-2-RF** | 2008 Restructured Form of the MMPI-2 |
| **Social desirability** | Tendency to give answers that make one look good |
| **Faking** | Deliberately false answering, to look healthier or more disturbed |
| **Response set** | Answering in a fixed way regardless of content (Classic background) |
| **Projective test** | Test in which people project needs, fears and values onto an ambiguous stimulus |
| **Rorschach Inkblot Technique** | Projective test of 10 inkblots, Hermann Rorschach, 1921 |
| **Klecksographie (Blotto)** | Inkblot game Rorschach played as a child |
| **Comprehensive System** | Exner's standardised Rorschach administration, scoring and interpretation |
| **Thematic Apperception Test** | Storytelling test with ambiguous pictures; Morgan and Murray, 1935 |
| **Word-association test** | Reply to each stimulus word with the first word that comes to mind |
| **Sentence-completion test** | Person completes sentence stems |
| **Rotter Incomplete Sentence Blank** | Sentence-completion test with relatively objective scoring |
| **Clinical interview** | Assessment by conversation about history, relationships and problems, with observation |
| **Behavioural assessment** | Observer evaluates a person's behaviour in a situation |
| **EASI Temperament Survey** | Buss and Plomin's checklist of observable child behaviours, completed by mothers of twins |
| **Thought sampling** | Systematic recording of one's own thoughts over time |
| **Experience sampling method** | Thought sampling plus the social and environmental context |
| **Clinical method** | Research using case studies, tests, interviews and dream analysis |
| **Case study** | Detailed history of an individual from many sources |
| **Experiment** | Controlled technique for determining the effect of variables on behaviour |
| **Independent variable** | Condition the experimenter manipulates |
| **Dependent variable** | Behaviour or response the experimenter measures |
| **Experimental group** | Group exposed to the experimental treatment |
| **Control group** | Group not exposed to the treatment |
| **Random assignment** | Allocating participants to groups by chance |
| **Virtual research** | Research conducted online |
| **Mechanical Turk** | Amazon crowd-sourcing service used to recruit paid participants |
| **Correlational method** | Statistical technique measuring the degree of relationship between two variables |
| **Correlation coefficient** | Index of direction and strength of a relationship, +1.00 to -1.00 |
| **Positive / negative correlation** | High scores go with high scores / high scores go with low scores |
| **Scatterplot** | Graph of each person's scores on two variables (Figure 1.2) |
| **Idiographic / nomothetic** | Study of the individual case / search for general laws (Classic background) |
| **Theory** | Set of principles explaining a class of phenomena; a framework organising data |
| **Autobiographical nature of theories** | A theory may partly reflect its author's life |
| **Image of human nature** | A theorist's basic view of what people are like, on six issues |
| **Free will versus determinism** | Conscious self-direction versus control by forces beyond awareness |
| **Nature versus nurture** | Heredity versus environment as the main influence |
| **Historical determinism** | Personality basically fixed in the early years, changing little thereafter |
| **Uniqueness versus universality** | Each person incomparable versus patterns common to all |
| **Equilibrium versus growth** | Tension reduction and balance versus growth and self-actualisation |
| **Optimism versus pessimism** | People seen as basically good versus lacking such qualities |
