# Chapter 6: Erik Erikson: Identity Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** Every theorist in this book has a view on *when* personality is built, and they disagree sharply. Freud held that the basic structure is laid down by about the age of 5 and that adult life mostly replays it. Jung put the decisive transformation in middle age. Adler and Horney allowed change long after childhood but never mapped it stage by stage. Schultz and Schultz open a new part of the book, **the life-span approach**, with the one theorist who did map it: Erik Erikson, who divided the whole of life, from birth to death, into **eight stages**, and argued that each stage confronts us with a particular **crisis**, a turning point that can be met in a healthy or an unhealthy way. Everything in personality, on this account, can be read as the cumulative record of how those turning points were handled.

Erikson is a strange figure to find in a theory textbook. He never earned a university degree in any subject, yet his books sold like popular non-fiction and his face appeared on the covers of *Newsweek* and the *New York Times Magazine*. He was trained in Vienna by **Anna Freud**, and he always described himself as a Freudian: "Psychoanalysis is always the starting point," he said (quoted in Keniston, 1983). But Anderson and Friedman (1997) put the paradox neatly when they noted that he presented himself as loyal to Freud while moving a long way from orthodox theory. Schultz identifies **three extensions of Freud**, and you should be able to list them without hesitation:

1. **The stages run across the whole life span.** Freud's stages end in adolescence; Erikson elaborated them and added adult stages, so that personality keeps developing until death.
2. **The ego is independent.** For Freud the ego is a servant of the id, built out of id energy to manage its demands. For Erikson the ego is a part of personality in its own right, not subordinate to the id.
3. **Culture and history shape personality.** Innate biological forces working in childhood matter, but they are not the whole explanation. Social, cultural and historical forces also mould who we become.

**How to read this chapter.** Module 6.1 is the life, and with Erikson the life is not decoration: the man who coined the term *identity crisis* spent decades inside one, and almost every concept in the theory can be traced to an episode in his biography. Module 6.2 sets out the machinery (the epigenetic principle, the meaning of "crisis", and why healthy coping includes a dose of the negative pole). Module 6.3 is the core of every exam paper: the eight stages, their ages, their two poles and the basic strength each produces. Module 6.4 covers what goes wrong (maldevelopment, with its confusing labels "maladaptive" and "malignant"). Then come Erikson's position on the six human-nature questions (6.5), his assessment methods (6.6), a long research module (6.7) that runs from his own play-construction study through Marcia's identity statuses to research on generativity, ego integrity, ethnic identity and gender preference identity, and finally the evaluation (6.8). If you can draw Table 6.1 from memory before you start 6.4, the rest of the chapter will sit on a firm frame.

## 6.1 The Life of Erikson (1902 to 1994)

### Personal identity crises

Wallerstein (2014) observed that Erikson experienced the very crises he later theorised about, and was never able to resolve them across his whole life. The story starts before his birth. His mother was Danish and came from a well-off Jewish family. She had married some years earlier, but her husband vanished within hours of the wedding. She later became pregnant by a different man, and to escape the scandal of an illegitimate birth she was sent to Germany, where Erik was born, in Frankfurt, in 1902. For years he begged her to tell him who his father was. She never did.

She stayed in Germany and married Dr Theodore Homburger, the paediatrician who was looking after her baby. For some years Erik did not know that Homburger was not his biological father, and he later said he grew up uncertain both of his name and of who he was psychologically. He carried the surname Homburger until he was **37**, when he took United States citizenship and renamed himself **Erik Homburger Erikson**.

> **Classic background:** The surname he chose is widely read as "son of Erik", that is, a man naming himself as his own father. Whether or not he intended it that way, it is the most compact possible image of a self-constructed identity, which is exactly what his theory says adolescents must build.

School added a second layer of confusion. Although his parentage was Danish he thought of himself as German. To the German boys he was an outsider because his family was Jewish; to the Jewish boys he was an outsider because, tall and fair-haired, he looked like the "Aryan" stereotype. He belonged fully to neither group. His marks were mediocre, but he had a gift for art, and after finishing high school he tried to use that gift as the foundation of an identity.

Notice what this early history gives the theory. A child who does not know who his father is, who is rejected by every group he tries to join, and who cannot get a straight answer about his own origins is living through precisely the problem Erikson would later place at the centre of adolescence: the struggle to integrate what you think you are with what others take you to be.

### Dropping out of society

After school Erikson stepped outside conventional life. He drifted around Europe, reading, keeping a notebook of his thoughts and watching the people around him. He later described himself in that period as morbidly sensitive and neurotic, and at times close to psychotic. One of his daughters, writing many years later (Bloland, 2005), described a father who never got over the feeling that his real father had abandoned him without caring to know him, who fought a tendency to depression all his life, who was full of self-doubt and insecurity, and who constantly needed reassurance and guidance from others.

He enrolled at two art schools and even had his work shown in a gallery in Munich, but each time he abandoned formal training and went back to wandering. Looking back, Erikson acknowledged (1975) that his closest friends would say he had to give the identity crisis a name, and see it in everyone else, before he could deal with it in himself. His biographer Friedman (1999) made the same point from outside: what Erikson observed and felt in his own life served as his "research", in the same way that Freud's analysis of his own dreams and memories had served Freud.

This wandering period matters for two later concepts. It is the template for the **psychological moratorium**, the time-out in which a young person tries on roles before committing to one, and it is also the template for **identity diffusion**, the aimless drifting that Marcia's research would later describe. Critics of the theory (6.8) would also point out that a seven-year moratorium of travel is a luxury most young people cannot afford.

### Becoming a Freudian

At **25** Erikson was offered a teaching job at a small school in Vienna that had been set up for the children of Sigmund Freud's patients and friends. Freud's patients were often wealthy foreigners who moved their whole families to Vienna for the length of their analysis, and their children needed schooling. Erikson later admitted that part of Freud's pull for him was his own search for a father. With this job his professional life began, and he felt for the first time that he had found an identity.

He trained as a psychoanalyst and was analysed by **Anna Freud**, not by Sigmund. The sessions took place almost every day for **3 years**, and the fee was **$7 a month**. Anna Freud, unlike her father, was interested in analysing children. Her influence, together with what he saw while teaching in the classroom, convinced him that social influences shape personality and pushed him towards the study of child development. When he finished his training he became a member of the **Vienna Psychoanalytic Institute**.

> **Classic background:** Anna Freud's *The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence* (1936) helped found **ego psychology**, the branch of psychoanalysis that studies the ego's own adaptive work rather than treating it only as the id's servant. Erikson is usually classed as an ego psychologist, and his independent ego (extension 2 above) is the clearest sign of her influence.

> **Trap.** Erikson was analysed by **Anna** Freud, the daughter, not by Sigmund Freud. Options that say "a disciple analysed by Freud himself" are wrong. The detail that Anna Freud, unlike her father, worked with children is the link examiners expect you to draw to Erikson's child-centred work and play therapy.

### Three marriages and a stable foundation

In **1929**, at a masquerade ball in Vienna, he met **Joan Serson**, an artist and dancer born in Canada, who had herself been analysed by one of Freud's followers. They fell in love, but when she became pregnant Erikson refused to marry her. He gave two reasons: he was afraid of a permanent commitment, and his mother and stepfather would not accept a daughter-in-law who was not Jewish. Friends finally talked him round by pointing out the parallel: if he walked away, he would be doing exactly what the man who fathered him had done, and he would be passing on to his own child the stigma of illegitimacy that had marked his whole life.

When he did marry Joan, he married her three times, in a Jewish ceremony, a Protestant ceremony and a civil one. The Jewish wedding went comically wrong: Joan turned up at the synagogue with a bag that smelled of pork and bacon, both forbidden foods, and Erik forgot the ring. Friedman (1999) read the episode as both a farce and an unconscious mockery of the strict Jewish observance of his mother and stepfather. Joan gave up her own career and became his lifelong intellectual partner and editor. She gave his life the stable social and emotional base it had lacked and helped him build his theory. His half-sister's verdict was that without Joan he would have amounted to nothing (quoted in Friedman, 1999), and Erikson did not disagree.

The episode is a live demonstration of a later stage. Fear of permanent commitment because it threatens a fragile identity is precisely the young-adult problem Erikson called **intimacy versus isolation**: you can only merge your identity with someone else's once you have one secure enough not to be swallowed.

> **Classic background:** Joan Erikson continued the work after his death. In the extended edition of *The Life Cycle Completed* (1997) she proposed a **ninth stage** for people in their late 80s and 90s, in which the earlier crises return with the negative pole now more pressing (for example, failing bodily autonomy). Schultz's 11th edition presents **eight** stages; treat a "ninth stage" as Joan Erikson's later addition, not part of Erik Erikson's eight-stage scheme.

### Coming to America

In **1933**, seeing the Nazi threat grow, the Eriksons left Vienna for **Denmark** and then the **United States**, settling in **Boston**. Erikson opened a private psychoanalytic practice that specialised in children, worked at a guidance centre for emotionally disturbed delinquents, and joined the staff of **Massachusetts General Hospital**.

He started graduate study at **Harvard**, aiming for a PhD in psychology, but failed his first course and left. In **1936**, still without any degree, he was invited to the **Institute of Human Relations at Yale**, where he taught in the medical school and continued analysing children. There he worked with a Yale anthropologist on a study of child-rearing among the **Sioux** of South Dakota. That fieldwork strengthened his conviction that culture shapes childhood. He developed his ideas further at the **Institute of Human Development, University of California at Berkeley**. Unlike most analysts of his time, he deliberately sought the widest possible clinical experience: patients from different cultures, and people he judged normal as well as those who were disturbed.

> **Classic background:** Older textbooks (and Erikson's own *Childhood and Society*, 1950) also describe his work with the **Yurok** of northern California, alongside the Sioux. Schultz mentions only "American Indians in South Dakota and in California".

### Honours and accolades, but no identity

Among American Indians in South Dakota and California, Erikson saw psychological symptoms that orthodox Freudian theory could not explain. They seemed to come from being cut off from cultural traditions, and they showed up as the absence of a clear self-image or self-identity. He first called this condition **identity confusion**. He had seen something very similar in emotionally disturbed veterans returning from the Second World War. His reading of the veterans was a direct break with Freud: they were not suffering from **repressed conflicts**, but from confusion produced by traumatic combat and by having been temporarily torn out of their culture. They no longer knew who or what they were.

> **Trap.** The origin of the identity concept is examinable as a pair: **American Indians** (alienation from cultural tradition) and **WWII veterans** (trauma plus temporary uprooting). The key theoretical move is that Erikson explained their symptoms by *confusion of identity*, not by *repressed* instinctual conflict. A question that attributes the veterans' problems, in Erikson's view, to repressed Oedipal material is wrong.

In **1950** he joined the **Austen Riggs Center** in Stockbridge, Massachusetts, a treatment centre for emotionally disturbed adolescents. Ten years later he went back to **Harvard**, this time as a teacher, giving a graduate seminar and a very popular undergraduate course on the human life cycle, and he retired in **1970**. At **84** he published a book about old age (*Vital Involvement in Old Age*, 1986, discussed under stage 8). He died in 1994.

The last note of the biography is bleak. Despite a lifetime of fame, his daughter reported that he still felt let down by his own achievements, and that being born illegitimate remained a source of shame to him (Bloland, 2005). In the theory's own terms, the man who described ego integrity as accepting one's past never fully accepted his own.

## 6.2 Psychosocial Stages of Personality Development

Erikson split personality growth into eight **psychosocial stages**: eight stages in a fixed order covering the whole life span, at each of which we face a crisis that can be met adaptively or maladaptively. The first four line up with Freud's **oral, anal, phallic and latency** stages. The difference between the two theories is one of emphasis, and it is the difference examiners test: Freud looked at the **biological** (bodily, instinctual) side of each stage, while Erikson looked at its **psychosocial** side, the relationships and social demands that come with it. When Freud talks about the anal stage he is interested in the pleasure of retaining and expelling; when Erikson talks about the same age he is interested in a battle of wills between a toddler and the adults trying to toilet-train him.

### The role of genetics and the environment

Erikson's name for the rule governing development was the **epigenetic principle of maturation**. The prefix *epi* means "upon", and the idea is that development unfolds *upon* a genetic ground plan: the **stages themselves**, their order and their timing, are fixed by inherited factors. Every human being, in every culture, will meet the stages in the same sequence.

But the principle has a second half, and students who learn only the first half get the theory wrong. Whether a genetically scheduled stage is realised well or badly is controlled by the **social and environmental** forces a person is exposed to. Heredity decides *that* a two-year-old will push for independence; parents and culture decide whether that push ends in a confident will or in shame. Personality development is therefore the product of both biological and social factors.

> **Classic background:** Erikson borrowed the term from embryology, where each organ has its own scheduled period of rapid growth on a fixed ground plan; if an organ misses its moment, development of the whole is distorted. The analogy explains why he thought an unresolved stage leaves a lasting mark on everything built after it.

> **Trap.** "Epigenetic" in Erikson does **not** mean what it means in modern molecular biology (environmental switching of gene expression). In this chapter it means a genetically fixed sequence of stages whose *outcome* depends on the environment. If an option says the epigenetic principle makes development "entirely environmental" or "entirely genetic", reject it.

### Conflicts and crises

Development, for Erikson, is a series of **personal conflicts**. The potential for each conflict is present at birth as an innate predisposition, and each one comes to the fore at a particular stage, when the environment starts demanding a particular kind of adaptation. Every such confrontation with the environment is a **crisis**: a turning point that requires a change of perspective, a redirection of our instinctual energy to suit the needs of that stage of the life cycle.

A crisis in Erikson's sense is not a catastrophe. It is a fork in the road. Each stage has its own crisis, and we can respond in one of two ways: **adaptively (positively)** or **maladaptively (negatively)**. Only when a conflict is resolved can personality carry on along its normal sequence with the strength it needs for the next stage. If a conflict stays unresolved, we are less equipped for the problems that follow. Erikson was careful, though, not to make this a sentence of doom: a good outcome remains possible later, it is just harder to reach.

A concrete picture: a child who leaves the school years (stage 4) with a strong sense of inferiority does not automatically fail adolescence, but she enters the identity crisis carrying a belief that she is not good at things, which makes trying on demanding roles feel riskier. A good teacher or a later success can still repair it.

### Adaptive coping

Here is the point most students miss. Erikson did **not** think the ideal outcome of a stage is the positive pole alone. The ego has to take in **both** the adaptive and the maladaptive ways of coping.

Take the first stage. The infant's crisis is helplessness and dependency, and it can be met with a sense of **trust** or of **mistrust**. Trust is plainly the healthier attitude. But someone who trusted completely, who was entirely gullible, would be at the mercy of anyone who wanted to deceive, mislead or manipulate them. So each of us also needs a measure of mistrust as protection.

The ideal at every stage, then, is an ego made up **mainly** of the positive, adaptive attitude, **balanced by some** of the negative one. Only that blend counts as a satisfactory resolution. This balance idea is what makes module 6.4 (maldevelopment) intelligible: if the healthy state is a blend, then an ego with *only* the positive pole is also unhealthy.

> **Trap.** "Successful resolution means the complete elimination of the negative pole" is false in Erikson. Satisfactory resolution means a **favourable ratio**: mostly adaptive, with some maladaptive. Total trust is a form of maldevelopment, not perfect health.

## 6.3 Basic Strengths

Each stage also gives us a chance to develop a **basic strength**, which Erikson also called a **virtue**. Basic strengths are motivating characteristics and beliefs that come out of the satisfactory resolution of a stage's crisis; they appear once the crisis has been resolved well. They are **interdependent**: a strength cannot develop until the strength of the preceding stage has been confirmed. Hope must be in place before will, will before purpose, and so on up the ladder.

| 2 | Muscular-anal | 1 to 3 | Autonomy vs doubt, shame | **Will** | Anal |
| 3 | Locomotor-genital | 3 to 5 | Initiative vs guilt | **Purpose** | Phallic |
| 4 | Latency | 6 to 11 | Industriousness vs inferiority | **Competence** | Latency |
| 5 | Adolescence | 12 to 18 | Identity cohesion vs role confusion | **Fidelity** | (Genital, in classic comparisons) |
| 6 | Young adulthood | 18 to 35 | Intimacy vs isolation | **Love** | None |
| 7 | Adulthood | 35 to 55 | Generativity vs stagnation | **Care** | None |

Schultz pairs only the first four stages with Freud's. **Classic background:** comparisons in other texts often line adolescence up with Freud's genital stage; Freud has no counterpart to Erikson's three adult stages, which is the whole point of extension 1.

A memory aid for the strengths in order: **H**ope, **W**ill, **P**urpose, **C**ompetence, **F**idelity, **L**ove, **C**are, **W**isdom.

> **Trap.** Schultz's labels differ from those in many other textbooks and older Indian keys, and options are often written in the other vocabulary. Stage 2 is usually printed as "autonomy vs **shame and doubt**"; stage 4 as "**industry** vs inferiority"; stage 5 as "**identity vs role confusion**" (Erikson's earlier wording was "identity vs identity diffusion"). Developmental texts also name the first four stages by age (infancy, early childhood, play age, school age) rather than by Freud's zones. The content is the same; do not be thrown by the wording. **Classic background:** the stage names other than Schultz's come from Erikson's own writings and standard developmental texts.

> **Trap.** Ages are a favourite target. Schultz's boundaries are **18 to 35** for young adulthood, **35 to 55** for adulthood and **over 55** for maturity. Many developmental texts use different cut-offs (for example, middle adulthood to about 65 and old age after 65). Answer with the edition the question is based on; for Schultz, use the table above.

### Trust versus mistrust

The **oral-sensory** stage covers the first year of life, when we are at our most helpless. It corresponds to Freud's oral stage, and the infant depends completely on the mother or main caregiver for survival, safety and affection. The mouth matters enormously; Erikson (1959) wrote that the infant lives and loves through the mouth. But the infant's relationship to the world is not just biological, it is social, and it is the quality of the baby's interaction with the mother that decides whether trust or mistrust becomes part of the personality as a stance towards the world.

#### It's up to the mother

If the mother meets the baby's physical needs reliably and gives plenty of affection, love and security, the infant develops a **sense of trust**, an attitude that will colour the growing child's view of both self and others. The child learns to expect what Erikson (1950) called "consistency, continuity, and sameness" from people and situations. Erikson regarded that expectation as the very **beginning of ego identity**: the first sense that the world and I hang together over time.

If the mother is rejecting, inattentive or inconsistent, the infant may develop **mistrust** and become suspicious, fearful and anxious. Erikson went further, and here his views have dated: he held that mistrust can also arise if the mother does not focus **exclusively** on the child, and he argued that a new mother who goes back to work outside the home and leaves the baby with relatives or in day care runs the risk of fostering mistrust.

#### Trust can be lost later in life

Although the trust-mistrust pattern is set in infancy, the issue can come back at later stages. An ideal mother-infant relationship produces high trust, but that trust can be shattered if the mother dies or leaves the family, and mistrust may then take over. The reverse also holds: mistrust from childhood can be changed later through the companionship of a loving, patient teacher or friend. This two-way reversibility is a small instance of Erikson's general optimism (6.5).

#### Hope

The basic strength of the first stage is **hope**: the belief that our desires will be satisfied. Hope is a persistent sense of confidence that survives temporary setbacks and reverses. A clinical picture of its absence: an adult who, when anything goes wrong, concludes at once that nothing will ever work out, and who cannot wait for gratification because he does not believe it will come.

### Autonomy versus doubt and shame

The **muscular-anal** stage covers the **second and third years** and corresponds to Freud's anal stage. Children's physical and mental abilities expand quickly and they can do many things for themselves: communicate better, walk, climb, push, pull, and grasp an object or let it drop. They are proud of these skills and usually want to do as much as possible without help.

Of all these abilities Erikson thought the most important were **holding on and letting go**. He treated them as **prototypes** for how we later handle conflicts in behaviour and attitudes. Holding on can be loving (cherishing, keeping close) or hostile (clinging, hoarding, refusing). Letting go can be an outburst of destructive rage or a relaxed passivity. In other words, the toddler is rehearsing two basic modes of relating that adults go on using.

#### Choice

The central point of this stage is that, for the first time, the child can **choose**, and feels the power of an autonomous will. Still dependent on the parents, the child nevertheless starts to see himself as a person in his own right and wants to use his new strengths. The key question becomes how far society, represented by the parents, will let the child express himself and do what he is capable of.

#### The toilet training crisis

The main clash between parent and child at this age is usually **toilet training**, which Erikson saw as society's first attempt to regulate an instinctual need. The child has to learn to hold on and let go only at the right times and places. Parents can let the child master this at his own pace, or they can become irritated, force the training and react with impatience and anger to mistakes. When parents frustrate the child's attempt at independence in this way, the child develops **self-doubt** and a sense of **shame** in front of others. The anal zone is the focus only because toilet training is the arena; the conflict itself is about independence and social regulation, which is why Erikson calls it psychosocial rather than biological.

#### Will

The basic strength that grows out of autonomy is **will**: the determination to exercise free choice **and** self-restraint despite society's demands. Note both halves. Will is not just stubbornness; it includes the capacity to hold oneself back by one's own decision rather than because one is forced.

### Initiative versus guilt

The **locomotor-genital** stage runs from **3 to 5** and resembles Freud's phallic stage. Motor and mental abilities keep growing, children can accomplish more on their own, and they show a strong desire to take the **initiative** in all sorts of activities.

#### The Oedipal relationship

One form initiative can take is fantasy: the wish to possess the opposite-sex parent and to compete with the same-sex parent. How the parents respond to the child's self-started activities and fantasies decides the outcome. If they punish the child and otherwise squash these displays of initiative, the child develops **persistent guilt** that will hang over self-directed activity for the rest of life.

The Oedipal wish always fails; the child cannot win the parent. But if parents handle the situation with love and understanding, the child learns what behaviour is acceptable and what is not, and the child's initiative can be steered towards realistic, socially approved goals. That prepares the ground for adult responsibility and morality, which in Freud's vocabulary is the **superego**.

> **Trap.** Erikson keeps the Oedipal content of this stage but reframes the outcome: the issue is whether **initiative** survives parental response, not whether castration anxiety is resolved by identification. Guilt here is the maladaptive pole, the result of initiative being punished.

#### Purpose

The basic strength arising from initiative is **purpose**: the courage to imagine goals and go after them. A child who has been allowed to plan, try and sometimes fail without being made to feel wicked for it carries forward the willingness to set goals at all.

### Industriousness versus inferiority

Erikson's **latency** stage runs from **6 to 11** and corresponds to Freud's latency period. The child starts school and meets a new set of social influences. Ideally, at home and at school, the child acquires good habits of work and study, which Erikson called **industriousness**, mainly as a way to earn praise and the satisfaction of finishing a task.

#### Developing new skills

Growing powers of deductive reasoning, and the ability to play games by their rules, lead the child to refine deliberately the skills shown in building and making things. Erikson's examples here reflect the sex stereotypes of his era: in his view boys would build tree houses and model aeroplanes while girls would cook and sew. Whatever the activities, the important thing is that children at this age make **serious attempts to complete a task** through sustained attention, diligence and persistence. Erikson (1959) described this as the period in which the child gets ready to handle the tools and utensils used by adults, so that the basic skills of a culture's technology begin to develop.

Parents' and teachers' attitudes and behaviour largely decide how children judge their own developing skills. Children who are scolded, mocked or rejected are likely to feel **inferior and inadequate**. Praise and reinforcement produce feelings of competence and keep children trying.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Erikson's **inferiority** (the maladaptive pole of stage 4, ages 6 to 11, produced by scolding, ridicule or rejection of a child's efforts) with Adler's **inferiority feelings** (a universal, lifelong motivating force present from infancy) or Adler's **inferiority complex** (an inability to overcome them). Same word, different theorists, different meaning.

#### Competence

The basic strength of the latency stage is **competence**: applying skill and intelligence to pursue and complete tasks.

#### The final four developmental stages

Schultz draws a structural line under stage 4, and it is a frequently examined one. In the **first four** stages, the outcome of each crisis **depends on other people**. Resolution is more a matter of what is done *to* children than of what they can do for themselves. Children become steadily more independent between birth and 11, but their development stays mainly under the influence of parents and teachers, who are usually the most important people in their lives.

In the **last four** stages we gain more and more **control over our environment**. We consciously choose our friends, colleges, careers, spouses and leisure activities. These choices are, of course, shaped by the personality built in the earlier stages. Whether the ego arrives at adolescence dominated by trust, autonomy, initiative and industriousness, or by mistrust, doubt, guilt and inferiority, will steer the rest of life.

### Identity cohesion versus role confusion

**Adolescence**, from **12 to 18**, is the stage at which we must face and resolve the crisis of our basic **ego identity**. Ego identity is the self-image put together during adolescence that integrates our ideas of what we are and what we want to be, including our sense of what other people think of us. When the process goes well, the result is a picture of oneself that is consistent and hangs together.

Building an identity and accepting it are hard, often anxious work. Adolescents try out different roles and ideologies to find which fits best. Erikson described adolescence as a pause between childhood and adulthood, a **psychological moratorium**: a socially permitted time-out in which the young person has the time and energy to play different roles and live with different self-images before committing. The adolescent who spends one year as a devout activist, the next as a musician, and the next preparing for medicine is not necessarily unstable; on Erikson's account she may be doing exactly the trying-on that the stage requires.

> **Trap.** Schultz writes "psychological moratorium"; most other texts (and Marcia) say **psychosocial moratorium**. Same concept. Also do not confuse Erikson's general idea of the moratorium (the normal adolescent time-out) with **Marcia's moratorium status** (6.7), which is one specific identity status: in crisis now, commitments still vague.

#### The identity crisis

Those who come out of adolescence with a strong sense of identity are ready to meet adulthood with certainty and confidence. Those who fail to reach a cohesive identity, who experience what Erikson called an **identity crisis**, show **role confusion**: they are unsure of who they are, of which groups they belong to, and of what direction their lives should take.

Such young people may **withdraw** from the normal life sequence of education, job and marriage, as Erikson himself did for a while, or they may seek a **negative identity**, for example in crime or drugs. Erikson's striking claim is that even a negative identity, as society defines it, is **better than no identity at all**, although it is less satisfying than a positive one. Clinically this explains a familiar pattern: a teenager who is told often enough that he is "the troublemaker" may embrace the label, because being someone definite, even someone bad, is less frightening than being no one.

Erikson also stressed the strong influence of **peer groups** on adolescent identity. He warned that excessive involvement with fanatical groups and cults, or obsessive identification with figures from popular culture, can narrow the developing ego.

> **Trap.** In Schultz the term **identity crisis** is defined as the *failure* to achieve ego identity in adolescence. In everyday use (and in some other texts) "identity crisis" means the normal, universal struggle of adolescence. Read the question: if it asks for Schultz's glossary definition, the answer is "failure to achieve ego identity during adolescence".

#### Fidelity

The basic strength of adolescence is **fidelity**, which comes out of a cohesive ego identity. Fidelity includes sincerity, genuineness and a sense of duty in our relationships. It makes sense that this strength depends on identity: you cannot be reliably true to people or commitments until there is a stable "you" to do the committing.

### Intimacy versus isolation

Erikson saw **young adulthood** as a longer stage than any before it, stretching from the end of adolescence to roughly **35**. In this period we become independent of our parents and of quasi-parental institutions such as college, and start to function as mature, responsible adults. We take up some kind of productive work and form intimate relationships, usually close friendships and sexual partnerships.

#### Caring and commitment

Erikson's **intimacy** is not limited to sex. It includes caring and commitment, feelings that can be shown openly, without retreating behind self-protective defences and without fear of losing one's own identity. The key image is merging without submerging: two people combine their identities without either one disappearing into the other. This is why intimacy has to come *after* identity in the sequence.

People who cannot form such intimacies in young adulthood develop feelings of **isolation**. They avoid social contact, reject others and may even become aggressive towards them. They prefer to be alone because they experience intimacy as a threat to their ego identity. Erikson's own refusal to marry Joan, out of fear of permanent commitment, is the textbook illustration.

#### Love

The basic strength of young adulthood is **love**, which Erikson regarded as the **greatest of all human virtues**. He described it as mutual devotion within a shared identity, the fusing of oneself with another person.

### Generativity versus stagnation

**Adulthood**, roughly **35 to 55**, brings a new need: to take an active part in **teaching and guiding the next generation**. The need is not confined to one's own children or family. Concern becomes wider and longer-range, extending to future generations and the kind of society they will inherit.

Two clarifications are frequently examined. First, you do **not** have to be a parent to be generative. Second, having children does **not** automatically satisfy the need. Erikson held that every kind of institution, business, government, social service or academic, offers outlets for generativity: in whatever organisation or activity we are part of, we can usually find a way to act as a mentor, teacher or guide to younger people, for the good of society as a whole. A childless senior nurse who trains every new intern on the ward is generative; a parent who sees children only as extensions of his own status may not be.

When middle-aged people cannot or will not find an outlet for generativity, Erikson (1968) said they may be overwhelmed by stagnation, boredom and what he called interpersonal impoverishment. Schultz points out that this picture of middle-age distress resembles **Jung's midlife crisis**. Such people may regress to **pseudo-intimacy**, indulging themselves in childlike ways, and may become physical or psychological invalids because they are absorbed in their own needs and comforts.

#### Care

The basic strength that emerges from generativity is **care**, defined as a broad concern for others. Erikson believed it shows itself in the **need to teach**, not only to help others but also to fulfil one's own identity. Teaching, on this view, serves the teacher as much as the taught.

> **Trap.** **Care** (stage 7 strength) and **love** (stage 6 strength) are easily swapped. Love = mutual devotion in a shared identity (young adulthood, intimacy). Care = broad concern for others expressed through the need to teach (adulthood, generativity). And love, not care or wisdom, is the one Erikson called the greatest virtue. Wisdom (stage 8) differs from both: it is a *detached* concern with life as a whole.

### Ego integrity versus despair

In the last stage, **maturity and old age**, we face a choice between **ego integrity** and **despair**, and these attitudes govern how we evaluate our whole life. Our major projects are finished or nearly so. We look back and take life's final measure. If we look back with fulfilment and satisfaction, believing that we have coped with both the victories and the failures, we have ego integrity. Put simply, **ego integrity means accepting one's place and one's past**.

If we review our life with frustration, angry about lost chances and regretting mistakes that can no longer be put right, we feel **despair**. We become disgusted with ourselves, contemptuous of others, and bitter about what might have been.

#### Erikson's own final stage

At **84**, Erikson reported a long-term study of **29 people in their 80s** for whom life-history data had been gathered since **1928**. The resulting book, ***Vital Involvement in Old Age*** (Erikson, Erikson, & Kivnick, 1986), states its prescription in its title. Older people must do more than look back. They must stay **active and vitally involved** in life, looking for challenge and stimulation, through activities such as grandparenting, going back to school, and developing new skills and interests.

As an old man himself, Erikson concluded that **generativity** mattered even more than he had realised when he first built the theory. He suggested that much of the despair of old age is really a continuing sense of stagnation (quoted in Cheng, 2009). In other words, the generativity developed in stage 7 may be the **most important single contributor to ego integrity** in stage 8. An old person who is still mentoring, teaching grandchildren or contributing to a community is in a much better position to look back with acceptance than one who has been stagnating for twenty years.

#### Wisdom

The basic strength of the final stage is **wisdom**. It comes from ego integrity and shows itself as a **detached concern with the whole of life**. It is passed on to later generations as an integration of experience that Erikson summed up in the word **heritage**.

## 6.4 Basic Weaknesses

Just as each stage can yield a basic strength, it can also yield a **basic weakness**: a motivating characteristic that derives from the *unsatisfactory* resolution of a developmental crisis. Recall from 6.2 that the adaptive and maladaptive ways of coping are built into ego identity in a kind of creative balance. The ego should consist mainly of the adaptive attitude, but it will also hold a share of the negative one.

### Maladaptive and malignant conditions

When development is unbalanced, the ego consists of **only one** of the two attitudes, either the adaptive or the maladaptive. Erikson called this condition **maldevelopment**. It comes in two forms, and the labels are counter-intuitive, so learn them carefully:

- When **only the positive (adaptive) tendency** is present, the condition is called **"maladaptive"**. Maladaptions can lead to **neuroses**.
- When **only the negative tendency** is present, the condition is called **"malignant"**. Malignancies can lead to **psychoses**.

Erikson thought both could be corrected through **psychotherapy**. Maladaptions, the milder disturbances, can also be eased by **re-adaptation**, helped along by changes in the environment, supportive relationships, or successful adaptation at a later stage.

The logic is worth spelling out. An ego with nothing but trust is not ideally healthy; it is naive, and in Erikson's table this shows up as "sensory maladjustment". An ego with nothing but mistrust is far worse; it pulls away from the world altogether, which the table calls "withdrawal". The excess of the good pole distorts personality; the absence of the good pole destroys it. That ordering is why maladaptive tendencies are linked to neurosis (milder) and malignant ones to psychosis (severe).

**Table 6.2** Erikson's maldevelopmental tendencies (adapted in Schultz from *Vital Involvement in Old Age*, 1986)

| Stage | Only the adaptive pole present: **maladaptive** tendency | Only the maladaptive pole present: **malignant** tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Oral-sensory (trust / mistrust) | Sensory maladjustment | Withdrawal |
| Muscular-anal (autonomy / doubt, shame) | Shameless wilfulness | Compulsion |
| Locomotor-genital (initiative / guilt) | Ruthlessness | Inhibition |
| Latency (industriousness / inferiority) | Narrow virtuosity | Inertia |
| Adolescence (identity cohesion / role confusion) | Fanaticism | Repudiation |
| Young adulthood (intimacy / isolation) | Promiscuity | Exclusivity |
| Adulthood (generativity / stagnation) | Overextension | Rejectivity |
| Maturity and old age (ego integrity / despair) | Presumption | Disdain |

How to make the table memorable rather than memorised: read each row as "too much of the good pole" on the left and "only the bad pole" on the right. Pure autonomy with no capacity for shame becomes *shameless wilfulness*; pure doubt becomes *compulsion*. Pure initiative with no guilt becomes *ruthlessness*; pure guilt becomes *inhibition*. Industry with no sense of limits becomes *narrow virtuosity* (the child prodigy who can do one thing and nothing else); pure inferiority becomes *inertia*. Identity with no openness becomes *fanaticism*; pure role confusion becomes *repudiation* (rejecting all roles). Intimacy without boundaries becomes *promiscuity*; isolation becomes *exclusivity* (shutting others out). Generativity without limits becomes *overextension*; stagnation becomes *rejectivity*. Integrity without humility becomes *presumption*; despair becomes *disdain*.

> **Trap.** The single most reliable trick question in this chapter: "In Erikson's theory, a **maladaptive** tendency results when the ego contains only the ____ pole." The answer is the **positive (adaptive)** pole. "Malignant" = only the negative pole. Then the second half: maladaptive leads to **neurosis**, malignant to **psychosis**. And the umbrella term for both is **maldevelopment**.

> **Classic background:** Feist and Feist's textbook calls the malignant column the **core pathologies** of each stage (withdrawal, compulsion, inhibition, inertia, role repudiation, exclusivity, rejectivity, disdain), set opposite the basic strengths. If an option says "core pathology of adolescence: repudiation", it is using that vocabulary for the same table.

## 6.5 Questions about Human Nature

Schultz's general rule is that a theorist who maps out basic human *strengths* is painting an optimistic picture, and Erikson is the clearest case so far in the book. Here is his position on each of the six issues, with the reason.

**Optimism versus pessimism: optimistic.** Not everyone attains hope, purpose, wisdom and the other virtues, but all of us have the potential to, and nothing in our nature blocks it. Nor are we condemned to conflict, anxiety and neurosis by instinctual biological forces, which is the direct contrast with Freud. Each stage is built around a crisis, but each also offers the possibility of a positive outcome that both adapts and strengthens us. Even if we fail at one stage and come away with a maladaptive response or a basic weakness, change remains possible at a later stage.

**Free will versus determinism: partly deterministic.** During the first four stages, the experiences we meet through parents, teachers, peer groups and the opportunities available to us are largely out of our control. In the last four stages we have more room for free will, although the attitudes and strengths formed earlier shape our choices. Erikson believed we can consciously direct and shape our growth across life.

**Importance of the past versus the present: both, with the past not final.** We are not solely products of childhood. Childhood influences matter, but events at later stages can counteract unfortunate early experiences. This is the position the Holocaust-survivor study in 6.7 tests directly.

**Nature versus nurture: mainly nurture.** In general Erikson held that personality is shaped more by **learning and experience** than by heredity. Psychosocial experiences, not instinctual biological forces, are the greater determinant. (The epigenetic principle gives heredity the sequence; the outcome of each stage is psychosocial.)

**Uniqueness versus universality:** Schultz does not give Erikson an explicit rating in this edition. The theory itself is clearly **universal in structure**: everyone passes through the same eight stages in the same order under the epigenetic principle. Each person's particular balance of outcomes, and the particular identity they build, makes the result individual.

**Equilibrium versus growth: growth.** Our ultimate and overriding goal is to develop a **positive ego identity that incorporates all the basic strengths**. That is a growth goal, not a tension-reduction goal.

| Issue | Erikson's position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | Partly deterministic; more free will in the last four stages | First four stages largely controlled by others; later stages allow conscious choice |
| Nature vs nurture | Mainly nurture | Learning, experience and psychosocial forces outweigh heredity and instinct |
| Past vs present | Both; past important but not final | Later stages can counteract poor early experience |
| Uniqueness vs universality | Not rated by Schultz; universal stages, individual outcomes | Epigenetic sequence is the same for all; resolutions differ |
| Equilibrium vs growth | Growth | Goal is a positive ego identity incorporating all the basic strengths |
| Optimism vs pessimism | Optimistic | Every crisis can be resolved positively; nothing in human nature prevents the virtues |

> **Trap.** "Erikson presents a pessimistic, biologically determined view similar to Freud's" is the standard wrong option. He is **optimistic**, **partially** deterministic, and emphasises **psychosocial** over **instinctual** forces. Also avoid "fully free will": the first four stages are largely beyond the child's control.

## 6.6 Assessment in Erikson's Theory

Erikson accepted much of Freud's theory but parted company with him over **method**. He doubted both the usefulness and the safety of some standard Freudian techniques, starting with the **couch**. In Erikson's view, asking patients to lie on a couch could open the door to sadistic exploitation, gave a false impression of objectivity, put too much weight on unconscious material, and made the therapist impersonal and aloof. To build a more personal relationship and to make sure patient and therapist saw each other as **equals**, he preferred that they sit **facing each other in comfortable chairs**.

He also relied less than Freud on formal assessment techniques. He used **free association** now and then, but he seldom tried to analyse **dreams**, which he described as a wasteful and harmful technique. His general rule was that assessment methods should be chosen and adapted to fit the needs of the individual patient, rather than applied in the same way to everyone.

> **Trap.** Erikson **rarely** analysed dreams (calling it wasteful and harmful) and used free association only **occasionally**. An option crediting him with heavy reliance on dream analysis, or with keeping the couch, is wrong. Also: his theory was built mainly from **play therapy, anthropological studies and psychohistorical analysis**, not from psychological tests, which he did not use.

### Play therapy

For treating emotionally disturbed children, and for research on normal children and adolescents, Erikson chose **play therapy**. He offered a range of toys and watched how the child used them. The form and intensity of the play revealed aspects of personality that a child might not be able to put into words, because young children's verbal abilities are limited. A five-year-old who cannot say "I am frightened that my parents will separate" may repeatedly build a house and then knock one wall away from the others.

In building the theory itself, Erikson drew mainly on three sources: **play therapy**, **anthropological studies** (the Sioux and other fieldwork from 6.1), and **psychohistorical analysis**.

### Psychohistorical analysis

**Psychohistorical analysis** is Erikson's most unusual technique: the application of his life-span theory, together with psychoanalytic principles, to the study of historical figures. In practice these are biographies. Erikson used his stage framework to describe the crises and the ways of coping of important political, religious and literary figures, including **Gandhi**, **Martin Luther** and **George Bernard Shaw**.

A psychohistory usually centres on one **significant crisis**, an episode that expresses a major theme of the person's life and links past, present and future activity. To understand it, Erikson used what he called **"disciplined subjectivity"**: he deliberately took on the subject's point of view as his own, so as to judge life events as that person would have seen them.

> **Classic background:** The two famous book-length psychohistories are *Young Man Luther* (1958) and *Gandhi's Truth* (1969), the latter a study of the origins of Gandhi's militant non-violence, built around the 1918 Ahmedabad mill strike, which won the Pulitzer Prize and the National Book Award.

> **Trap.** "Disciplined subjectivity" belongs to **psychohistorical analysis**, not to play therapy or to the clinical interview. And psychohistory analyses *historical figures*; it is not a method for analysing whole cultures (that is the anthropological strand).

### Psychological tests

Erikson himself did not use psychological tests, but others later built instruments from his ideas:

| Instrument | Authors (year) | What it measures | Items |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ego-Identity Scale** | Dignan (1965) | Development of ego identity in adolescence | Not stated |
| **Ego Identity Process Questionnaire** | Balistreri, Busch-Rossnagel, & Geisinger (1995) | Two dimensions of adolescent identity: **exploration** and **commitment** | **32** |
| **Loyola Generativity Scale** | McAdams & de St. Aubin (1992) | Level of generativity versus stagnation in adulthood; self-report | **20** |

**Table 6.3** Sample items from a generativity scale. Schultz prints ten items adapted from McAdams and de St. Aubin (1992) and invites you to apply them to middle-aged people you know. They are first-person self-report statements, some worded towards generativity and some reverse-worded towards stagnation. The generative items concern passing on knowledge and skills, being sought out for advice, feeling one has made a difference to others, and keeping long-term commitments; the reverse items concern feeling unneeded, believing nothing one has done will outlast one's death, and reluctance to do charitable work. For instance, a generative respondent would agree with a statement like "younger colleagues often come to me for guidance", and would disagree with one like "nothing I do will matter after I am gone" (paraphrased examples).

> **Trap.** Item counts: **Loyola Generativity Scale = 20 items**; **Ego Identity Process Questionnaire = 32 items**. Exploration and commitment are the EIPQ's dimensions (and the same two dimensions underlie Marcia's statuses in 6.7). Do not attribute the Loyola scale to Erikson himself.

## 6.7 Research on Erikson's Theory

Erikson's own main research method was the **case study**. You already know its weaknesses from earlier chapters: case material is hard to replicate and verify. Erikson's answer was that case histories offer rich insight into personality development and can also help solve the patient's problems. The research in this module runs from his own play-construction study to a large modern literature, much of it built on measures derived from his concepts.

### Play constructions

Erikson used play therapy as a research tool, focusing on what he called **play constructions**: an assessment technique for children in which the structures they assemble from dolls, blocks and other toys are analysed. In one study, boys and girls aged **10 to 12** were asked to build a scene from an imaginary film, using dolls, toy animals, toy cars and wooden blocks.

The two sexes built very different scenes. **Girls** tended to build **static, peaceful** scenes with **low, enclosed** structures; in their scenes, intruders (animals or male figures, but never female figures) tried to force their way into the interiors. **Boys** concentrated on **exteriors, action and height**: tall towers, and cars and people in motion (Figure 6.1).

Look at the four panels. The top-left construction is a cluster of tall block towers; the top-right is an outdoor street with cars and figures moving around a walled building. The bottom-left is a low ring of blocks enclosing a small group of seated figures; the bottom-right is a furnished interior, people sitting at tables and in chairs, with no tall structure at all. The contrast between vertical, outward, moving scenes and low, inward, static ones is exactly what Erikson reported.

Being an orthodox Freudian by training, Erikson interpreted these results in standard psychoanalytic terms. He argued (Erikson, 1968) that the way each sex organised play space mirrored the shape of its genitals: an external, erectable, intrusive organ in the male, and internal organs reached through an entrance in the female. On this reading, the biological difference itself makes girls build low enclosures in which people are walled in and boys build towers.

He has been criticised for this view because it implies that women are victims of their anatomy and that their personalities are shaped by the **absence of a penis**, an echo of Freud's most criticised idea. Erikson conceded that the differences might instead reflect **societal sex-role training**, which encourages girls to be less oriented than boys towards action, aggression and achievement.

> **Trap.** Erikson's *own* interpretation of the play constructions was **anatomical** (genital morphology), not cultural. He only later **admitted** that sex-role training could explain the results. An option saying "Erikson concluded the differences were due to socialisation" misstates his interpretation; one saying "he never acknowledged any alternative" is also wrong.

#### Gender preferences for toys

More than **50 years** after Erikson's study, traditional gender stereotyping in toys and play persists, and most children still prefer gender-typed toys: boys usually play with trucks, soldiers and guns, girls with dolls, jewellery and toy kitchen equipment. Three studies show it at different ages:

- **Wong and Hines (2014)**: among **2- to 4-year-olds**, boys played more with a toy **train** than with a doll, and girls chose the **doll** over the train.
- **Dinella, Devita, and Weisgram (2013)**: **college men** were drawn to a "boy's toy", a model **fighter jet**, but only when it was painted **blue**; painted **pink**, a conventional girls' colour, it was rejected.
- **Joiner (1998)**: **10- to 11-year-olds** choosing software for computer-based problems split by label: boys picked the program labelled "**Pirates**", girls the one labelled "**Princesses**".

#### The role of parents in determining gender preferences for toys

Some parents still teach and encourage these preferences, and since parents buy most young children's toys, their influence is large. They praise children for playing with the "right" toys and discourage play with toys meant for the other sex, and children learn the lesson fast. Martin (1999) described a boy happily playing with a toy racing car until the driver's helmet came off to show long blond hair; on discovering the driver was a woman, he dropped the car at once.

**Fathers** treat sons and daughters more stereotypically than mothers do, so it is mainly fathers who teach and reinforce gender-typed play, along with other gender-typed behaviours and attitudes. Fathers tend to encourage and reward **passive, compliant** behaviour in girls and **assertive, aggressive** behaviour in boys (Quiery, 1998).

### Trust and security

Erikson stressed that an early sense of trust is needed for security and well-being later in life, and this claim has **strong research support** (for example Jacobson & Wille, 1986; Londerville & Main, 1981; Sroufe, Fox, & Pancake, 1983).

In these studies, infants aged **12 to 18 months** who had a strong emotional bond with their mothers, and who were therefore presumed to be high in trust, were functioning at a **higher social and emotional level** when observed **3 years later** than infants whose attachment was less secure. Children with well-developed trust were also more **curious, sociable and popular**, more often leaders in games, and more sensitive to others' needs and feelings. Low-trust children, by contrast, kept to themselves socially and emotionally, held back from playing with others, showed less curiosity and went after their goals with less determination.

> **Key study, Suedfeld et al. (2005): Holocaust survivors.** Survivors of the Holocaust were interviewed **30 to 40 years** after the end of the Second World War (1945). They had coped successfully with **all** of Erikson's stages **except the first**: their view of other people contained significantly more **mistrust than trust**. Yet they had handled the later crises well. Schultz reads this as confirming Erikson's claim that good outcomes at later stages can **counteract or overcome** damaging experiences, even when the damaged stage itself is not repaired.

Note exactly what the finding shows. The *trust* dimension stayed damaged decades later, which fits the 6.3 point that trust can be lost after infancy and does not always come back. But the damage did not block the other strengths: autonomy, identity, intimacy and generativity could still be achieved on top of a mistrustful view of people.

### The psychosocial stages

Several studies test whether the stage sequence shows up in real lives.

- **Ciaccio (1971)**: children aged **4, 8 and 11** made up stories about test pictures, and the stories were coded for which psychosocial stage they reflected. The themes matched Erikson's sequence; for example, the 4-year-olds' stories dealt with **autonomy**, the stage they had just completed, and the older children's stories reflected their own stages.
- **Peterson and Stewart (1990)**: a psychohistorical analysis of the diaries, letters and novels of **Vera Brittain** (1893 to 1970), the British feminist and writer, from age 21 into middle age. Her concerns shifted from **ego identity** to **intimacy** and then to **generativity**, exactly in Erikson's predicted order.

#### Adaptive and maladaptive development

- **Constantinople (1969)** used the **Inventory of Psychosocial Development**, a test of adaptive versus maladaptive development in Erikson's **first six** stages, and found a significant relationship between **happiness** and adaptive development at **each** stage.
- **Reimanis (1974)** found a high correlation between maladaptive development in the first six stages and a sense of **alienation and uprootedness**.
- **Galambos, Barker, and Krahn (2006)**, studying Canadians aged **18 to 25**, found that emerging adulthood was a period of **increasing** psychological well-being.

#### The effects of growing up

- **Waterman, Buebel, and Waterman (1970)** tested Erikson's claim that a good outcome to the identity crisis depends on good outcomes earlier. Adolescents who had developed trust, autonomy, initiative and industriousness in the first four stages showed high **identity cohesion**; those stuck in role confusion had not developed the adaptive ways of coping earlier.
- **Beaumont and Zukanovic (2005)** gave self-report measures of identity, self-worth and psychological distress to three groups of Canadian men aged **19 to 25, 35 to 55 and 65 to 87**. The **youngest** reported the **most** distress and the **oldest** the **least**, consistent with Erikson's view that older people cope better because they have met and resolved more of life's earlier dilemmas.
- **Conway and Holmes (2004)** asked British adults aged **62 to 89** to recall memories from different periods. Memories from the **first decade** centred on trust, autonomy, initiative and industry; memories from the **second decade (11 to 20)** on **identity**; memories from **young adulthood** on **intimacy**. Each period's recollections clustered around the issue Erikson assigned to it.

### Adolescent development

The most extensive research programme on Erikson's adolescent stage is **James Marcia's** (Marcia, 1966, 1980), which identified **five** psychosocial types, or **statuses**, for adolescence: **identity achievement, moratorium, foreclosure, identity diffusion and alienated achievement**.

> **Classic background:** Marcia derived the statuses from two questions asked in an identity interview: has the person gone through a period of **crisis (exploration)** of alternatives, and has the person made a **commitment** to an occupation and an ideology? Crossing the two gives the four classic statuses: crisis + commitment = achievement; crisis now, no commitment yet = moratorium; commitment without crisis = foreclosure; no commitment (with or without crisis) = diffusion.

> **Trap.** Most developmental texts and older Indian keys list **four** Marcia statuses. Schultz's 11th edition lists **five**, adding **alienated achievement**. If a question says "Schultz" or offers five, include alienated achievement; if it asks for "Marcia's four statuses", leave it out.

#### Identity achievement

**Identity achievement** describes adolescents who are **committed** to occupational and ideological choices. Among college students, achieved identity status correlated positively with objective measures of commitment (Streitmatter, 1993), and such students had developed a strong ego identity. They were stable, focused on realistic goals and able to cope with changing demands; they performed better on difficult tasks than adolescents in role confusion; and they tended to choose harder college majors, such as **engineering and the physical sciences** (Marcia & Friedman, 1970).

The benefits extend forward. Teens of both sexes who reached identity achievement **earlier** in adolescence were more likely to have a **stable intimate romantic relationship in their twenties** (Beyers & Seiffge-Krenke, 2010), and were less likely to binge-drink, use illegal drugs or take sexual risks than those who had not reached it (Schwartz et al., 2010). This is the stage 5 to stage 6 dependency (identity before intimacy) showing up in data.

Studies of **American, Chinese and Turkish** high-school and college students found identity achievement strongly correlated with **self-esteem, positive coping, psychological well-being** and a stronger sense of self; it was the most psychologically and socially mature status (Cakir, 2014; Ickes, Park, & Johnson, 2012; Markstrom & Marshall, 2007; Meuss, 2011; Wang, Shi, & Chen, 2010). A large research programme covering **more than 120 studies** found that identity achievement **increased** across late adolescence and young adulthood, as Erikson predicted (Kroger, Martinussen, & Marcia, 2010).

Parenting matters too. Adolescents who thought seriously about their life direction, and so were more likely to reach identity achievement, had parents who gave **direction and control in a loving, caring way**, unlike parents who were either too permissive or too authoritarian (Berzonsky, 2004). The same pattern appeared among **Iranian** college students: those least likely to reach identity achievement had parents who were too authoritarian or too permissive (Mohammadi, 2013).

#### Moratorium

**Moratorium** describes people who are **still in the middle of** their identity crisis. Their occupational and ideological commitments are vague. They are ambivalent towards authority figures, rebelling against them one moment and needing their guidance the next. They can swing between indecision and bursts of energetic, creative activity, and on measures of anxiety they score **high** (Podd, Marcia, & Rubin, 1968; Blustein, Devenis, & Kidney, 1989; Lillevoll, Kroger, & Martinussen, 2013). Bilsker and Marcia (1991) add that people in moratorium are given to daydreaming, are drawn to belief in the supernatural, and take pleasure in childish behaviour.

#### Foreclosure

**Foreclosure** describes adolescents who have **not yet had an identity crisis** but who are nonetheless **committed** to an occupation and an ideology. The catch is that these commitments have usually been decided **for** them by their parents rather than chosen deliberately. Foreclosed teens tend to be **rigid and authoritarian** and to struggle with change (Marcia, 1967). They are achievement-oriented, but direct their energy towards **external** rather than internal goals (Stephen, Fraser, & Marcia, 1992). The student who is in medical college because "everyone in our family is a doctor", and who has never once asked whether she wants to be, is the classic foreclosed case.

#### Identity diffusion

**Identity diffusion** describes people with **no** occupational or ideological commitments who **may not have experienced** an identity crisis. Their lifestyle may actively reject commitment of any kind, and at the extreme it becomes aimless drifting and wandering, like Erikson's in his late teens. These adolescents have **distant relationships** with parents whom they see as **indifferent and rejecting** (Waterman, 1982).

Studies of diffused adolescents in **Greece, Belgium and the United States** found them lower in psychological adjustment and subjective well-being, and higher in unstable self-image and unstable relationships. They were also more prone to impulsive and self-destructive behaviour, an excessive need for attention and grandiose fantasies (Crawford, Cohen, Johnson, Sneed, & Brook, 2004; Luyckx, Goossens, Soenens, Beyers, & Vansteenkiste, 2005; Vleioras & Bosma, 2005). Clinicians will recognise that this profile overlaps with features of emerging personality disturbance.

#### Alienated achievement

The fifth status, **alienated achievement**, describes adolescents who **have experienced** an identity crisis, have **no occupational goal**, and hold firmly to beliefs that are **critical of the social and economic system**. Their commitment to that critique rules out any career that would tie them into the very system they reject. As students they tend to be **cerebral, philosophical and cynical** (Marcia & Friedman, 1970; Orlofsky, Marcia, & Lesser, 1973).

#### Achieving an integrated ego identity

Four of the statuses, in this order, represent **increasingly successful** resolutions of the identity problem:

**identity diffusion → foreclosure → moratorium → identity achievement**

Erikson predicted that people who have reached an integrated ego identity will have more **ego strength** than those further from resolving it, and research on college men supported the prediction (Bourne, 1978a, 1978b).

> **Trap.** The order puts **moratorium above foreclosure**, even though the foreclosed person "has" commitments and the moratorium person does not. The reason: foreclosure skipped the crisis and borrowed its answers, while moratorium is actively working through the crisis, one step from achievement. Alienated achievement is not in the four-step ladder.

| Status (Schultz) | Crisis / exploration? | Commitment? | Typical profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity achievement | Yes, resolved | Yes, self-chosen | Stable, realistic, high self-esteem, harder majors |
| Moratorium | Yes, ongoing | Vague | Anxious, ambivalent to authority, creative or indecisive |
| Foreclosure | No | Yes, set by parents | Rigid, authoritarian, external goals |
| Identity diffusion | Possibly not | No | Drifting, distant parents, poor adjustment |
| Alienated achievement | Yes | No occupation; strong anti-system belief | Cerebral, philosophical, cynical |

#### The effect of other activities on ego identity

High-school students heavily involved in **extracurricular and volunteer activities** scored higher on the ego strength of **fidelity** than uninvolved students (Markstrom, Li, Blackshire, & Wilfong, 2005). Similar results emerged for **physical activity, sport and exercise** among **Japanese and American** students: the more time spent on such activities, the stronger the sense of ego identity (Fukama & Mizuochi, 2011; Jones, Dick, Coyl-Shepherd, & Ogletree, 2014).

### Sex differences in ego identity

Men and women have been found to resolve the identity crisis differently. In one study, men leaned towards **separation and detachment** from others, women towards **connection and attachment** (Mellor, 1989). Later work supported and extended this: male identity tends to centre on **individual competence and knowledge**, female identity on **relationships with others**. So when women build an identity they lean heavily on social relationships, while men focus more on the self and on individual skills and abilities (Curry, 1998). Data from teenagers in the **Netherlands** suggest that girls form an identity at an **earlier age** than boys, though most boys reach some level of identity by the end of the teens (Klimstra, Hale, Raaijmakers, Branje, & Meeus, 2010).

#### A stressful time to search for identity

Adolescence is often turbulent, and three key elements of the stage have been identified:

- **Conflict with parents**: strong resistance to adult authority.
- **Mood disruption**: a volatile emotional life, swings of mood and bouts of depression.
- **Risky behaviour**: reckless, rule-breaking and antisocial acts that may harm the adolescent or others.

> **Key study, Jensen-Campbell and Graziano (2000).** **155 adolescents** kept diaries of their daily interactions for **2 weeks**. **31 percent** of the interactions involved conflict with someone. The teenagers rated conflicts with **parents** as more important and more emotionally intense than conflicts with **peers**.

Longitudinal studies following people from childhood into adolescence add a caution. Many teenagers with depression and other emotional problems had already shown psychological distress as children, which suggests that adolescent difficulties do not necessarily arise *because of* adolescence (Steinberg & Morris, 2001). The stage may expose or intensify problems that were already there.

#### Virtual identity

Computer games and social media give adolescents a new, high-tech way to do exactly what Erikson said this stage demands: try out roles to see which fits best. Role-playing games such as **Second Life (SL)** and **Dungeons and Dragons** let young people adopt fictional personas and act out complex fantasies. In computer vocabulary a "dungeon" means a virtual place, and virtual places that many users share at the same time are called **multi-user dungeons (MUDs)**. In a MUD a player can interact with others and also build a personal virtual world whose imaginary characters interact with other people's.

> **Highlights: Research on childhood and adolescence.** Children with a well-developed sense of trust tend to be socially and emotionally advanced, popular, curious and sensitive to other people's needs and feelings. Schultz lists five adolescent statuses: identity achievement, moratorium, foreclosure, identity diffusion and alienated achievement. Adolescents high in identity achievement tend to have a strong sense of ego identity, realistic goals and high self-esteem, and go on to form mature romantic relationships as young adults.

#### Building a persona online

Online, people can play characters as similar to or as different from their real selves as they like, without revealing who they are. Turkle (1995) captured the appeal: online "you can be whoever you want to be", and you can redefine yourself completely. That is precisely the experimentation with identities Erikson recommended for adolescence.

- **Utz (2003)**: among **German MUD players** with an average age of **25**, interpersonal attraction between players grew the longer they played, and so did the strength of their social identification with the virtual community.
- **Calvert, Strouse, Strong, Huffaker, and Lai (2009)**: identification with a virtual world was thought to be as intense and satisfying as identification with the real world.
- **Linares, Subrahmanyam, Cheng, and Guan (2011)**: an online study of **Second Life** users suggested that people are not so much inventing wholly new "second lives" as carrying parts of their real offline lives into their online self-presentation. Their personas are partly projections of who they actually are.

#### Advantages and disadvantages of an online persona

The online world can help or hinder identity formation.

- **Disadvantages.** Revealing too much personal information online can draw some teenagers into an **extended adolescence**, delaying the construction of a sound ego identity (Jordan-Conde, Menneckem, & Townsend, 2014). Among **seventh to ninth graders**, those who had not yet developed a sense of their true selves used social media **more** than those with a firmer sense of identity (Israelashvili, Kim, & Bukobza, 2012). The risk is that a virtual identity could become so absorbing that it **replaces** the developing real self, though the same can happen offline when someone adopts a different persona. In **Australia**, children and adolescents high in **loneliness and social anxiety** were far more likely to discuss personal and intimate matters online than less lonely, less anxious peers (Bonetti, Campbell, & Gilmore, 2010).
- **Advantages.** A personal home page can play a positive part in identity formation. Schmitt, Dayanim, and Matthias (2008) concluded that children who create home pages feel a strong sense of mastery and use them to express who they are in a way that may feel easier than saying it face to face.

### Gender and ego identity

#### Effects of social forces over time

Erikson argued that social and historical forces shape ego identity, and therefore personality. The **women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s** gave psychologists a natural experiment to test this. The question was whether women who were **adolescents** at the time, and so in the middle of forming an ego identity, were more affected by the movement than **older** women, whose identities were presumed to be already formed.

Two major studies answered yes (Duncan & Agronick, 1995; Helson, Stewart, & Ostrove, 1995). Both studied women who graduated from college between the **1940s and the mid-1960s**, using interviews, questionnaires and self-report personality tests. Women who were in college when the movement began had **higher aspirations**, valued independence more than the older women, and eventually reached **higher education, job status and income**. In middle age they were more **assertive and self-confident** than women who had passed through adolescence before the movement. The timing of a historical event relative to one's stage of identity formation determined how deeply it was absorbed into the self, which is a strong confirmation of Erikson's psychosocial emphasis.

#### Effects of a career orientation

One legacy of the movement was that more adolescent women made a **career orientation** part of their ego identity, and this has consequences for dating and age at marriage. Questionnaire studies of several hundred women college students found that the **career-oriented** women tended to **marry later**, dated less in college and were more wary of committed relationships.

The same study found the **opposite for men**. The stronger a college man's career identity, the **more committed** he was to a dating relationship; in fact men were unlikely to commit to a relationship at all until they felt definitely committed to an occupation. For men in this sample, occupational identity was a precondition for intimacy; for women, it competed with it.

#### Differences in emotional life over time

A longitudinal study followed women who graduated from college in the **1960s** and the men they married, looking at two dimensions of emotional life:

- **Positive emotionality (PEM)**: active, happy involvement in one's work and social world.
- **Negative emotionality (NEM)**: stress, anxiety, anger and other negative feelings.

Measured at various ages from the **late 20s to the mid-50s**, the women scored **higher on NEM** than their husbands in **young adulthood** but **higher on PEM** in **late middle age**. Once child-rearing was over, the women reported more social power, accomplishment and breadth of interest, and less stress and alienation. Helson and Klohnen (1998) read this as evidence that social circumstances mould the emotional dimension of ego identity.

#### Adjustment to changing demands

By **identity consolidation** Erikson meant coming to terms, successfully, with what adult social life actually demands, and adjusting as those demands shift. He thought it usually happens in the **20s**, as people take on adult responsibilities of marriage, family and career.

- **Pals (1999)**: women college graduates assessed at **21 and 27**. Those high in **ego resiliency** who had found an identity in **marriage** were higher in identity consolidation than those who did not meet both criteria.
- **Anthis and LaVoie (2006)**: among women aged **22 to 60**, readiness and willingness to change were positively related to changes in identity commitment at different stages; looking ahead and contemplating change was linked to exploring a different identity later in life.
- **Piot-Ziegler, Sassi, Raffoul, and Delaloye (2010)**: some women must cope with changed physical realities, such as body image after surgery for **breast cancer**. In a study of such women in **Britain**, the altered body image triggered an **identity crisis** that was hard to resolve. Identity, on this evidence, can be reopened in adulthood by bodily change.

#### The identity crisis

Erikson placed the identity crisis between about **12** and **18**. For some people, however, it comes later.

- **Archer (1982)**: up to **30 percent** of the people studied were still searching for an identity at age **24**.
- **Cote and Levine (1988)**: **college** may delay the resolution of the identity crisis and stretch out the period of experimenting with roles and ideologies.
- **Adams and Fitch (1982)**: compared with same-age people in **full-time jobs**, college students reached ego identity **later**; the employed achieved it earlier, and the students stayed longer in the **moratorium** status.
- **McAdams (2001)**: identity construction may even be a **lifelong** process.
- **Janarthanam and Gnanadevan (2014)**: a large-scale study of adolescents in **India** found that **girls** were higher in ego identity than **boys**.

> **Trap.** Two research findings directly qualify Erikson's timing: the crisis may extend **beyond 18** (Archer, 1982: up to 30 percent still searching at 24), and **college delays** resolution (Adams & Fitch, 1982: students stay longer in moratorium than employed peers). An option saying research confirms that everyone resolves identity by 18 is wrong. For an Indian paper, remember that the Indian study (Janarthanam & Gnanadevan, 2014) found **girls higher** in ego identity, consistent with the Dutch finding that girls form identity earlier.

### Generativity

#### Antecedents of generativity

Generativity in middle age seems to be significantly related to having had **warm, affectionate parenting** in childhood (Franz, McClelland, & Weinberger, 1991), and research supports the importance of both mother and father for a child's emotional well-being. Middle-aged adults high in generativity tended to believe in the goodness and worth of human life and to be happier and more satisfied with their own lives than low scorers (McAdams & de St. Aubin, 1992; Van de Water & McAdams, 1989).

> **Key study, McAdams, Diamond, de St. Aubin, and Mansfield (1997).** Men and women who had earlier been scored on the **Loyola Generativity Scale** were asked to describe the major themes of their lives. Four themes kept recurring in the high scorers' life stories: **early good fortune**, an acute **awareness of other people's suffering**, a **settled set of personal beliefs**, and **clear goals**, both personal and for society. None of the four appeared in the low scorers' accounts.

> **Key study, McAdams, Reynolds, Lewis, Patten, and Bowman (2001).** Middle-aged adults wrote about personally meaningful episodes: high points, low points and turning points. High scorers on generativity were far more likely to tell **redemption** stories, in which something bad turned into something good. Low scorers tended to tell **contamination** sequences, in which something good turned bad. (*Redemption* is Schultz's word; *contamination* is McAdams's standard label for the reverse pattern and is Classic background.)

Whitbourne, Sneed, and Sayer (2009) followed college students up **20 and 30 years later**. Most of those who had been **slow to reach ego identity** as young people **caught up by middle age** and reached generativity. A late start on identity is not a life sentence.

#### Correlates of generativity

Generativity in middle age correlates positively with both **power** motivation and **intimacy** motivation (McAdams, Ruetzel, & Foley, 1986). As Erikson's theory predicts, generativity calls on both the need to feel close to others and the need to feel strong in relation to them. Generativity has also been linked to **nurturance** (Van de Water & McAdams, 1989). All of these are qualities a person needs to teach and mentor the next generation, which is the adaptive way of coping at midlife.

#### The benefits of generativity

People high in generativity score higher than low scorers on **extraversion, self-esteem, conscientiousness, altruism, competence, dutifulness and openness to experience** (Cox, Wilt, Olson, & McAdams, 2010; Peterson, Smirles, & Wentworth, 1997; Van Hiel, Mervielde, & De Fruyt, 2006). They are more likely to have satisfying relationships, to feel attached to their community and to be emotionally stable (McAdams, Hart, & Maruna, 1998), and more likely to have **successful marriages**, more **success at work**, more **close friendships** and more altruistic behaviour (Westermeyer, 2004).

There is also a strong positive association between generativity and **psychological well-being**, holding for midlife adults **without children** as well as for parents. Interestingly, the link was **strongest** among people whose satisfaction and success came from **job and career**, not from **parenting** (Clark & Arnold, 2008; Rothrauff & Cooney, 2008). This supports Erikson's point that generativity is not the same thing as parenthood.

#### Generativity in women

- **Vandewater, Ostrove, and Stewart (1997)**: two longitudinal studies of college-educated women tested between **31 and 48**. Those high in generativity at midlife had significantly higher **emotional well-being**.
- **Helson and Srivastava (2001)**: college-educated women who placed a high value on **achievement and social recognition** showed fuller identity development in their **40s**, and scored significantly higher on generativity, than women who did not.
- **Zucker, Ostrove, and Stewart (2002)**: as Erikson predicted, college-educated women's generativity was **higher in their 40s than in their 20s**. But, **contrary to Erikson**, it did **not decline** afterwards; it stayed at the same level into their **60s**.
- **Peterson (2002)**: women high in generativity at **43** kept that level **10 years later**, and gave more care to their **ageing parents**, spouses and children than low scorers.

> **Trap.** Zucker, Ostrove, and Stewart (2002) is a *partial* disconfirmation: the rise from 20s to 40s fits Erikson, but generativity staying high into the 60s does not fit a stage model in which generativity belongs to 35 to 55. If asked which finding was "contrary to Erikson's view", this is it.

#### The effect of technology on generativity

Rapid technological change may lower younger people's regard for their elders and so block mentoring. Research in **Hong Kong** found a marked **drop in generativity** among older people who felt **out of touch with modern technology**: obsolete in relation to the Internet and social media, they felt unable to relate to or mentor the young, came to believe they were not valued or respected, and disengaged from generative goals and behaviour (Cheng, 2009).

### Maturity

#### Reflecting on our lives

Erikson believed that in maturity and old age people spend time recalling and examining their lives, accepting or regretting past choices.

- **Mackavey, Malley, and Stewart (1991)**: most memories of older **psychologists** came from their **college and early adult years**, the period with the largest number of critical decisions that set the course of their lives.
- **Taft and Nehrke (1990)**: elderly people high in **ego integrity** spent much more time **reviewing their lives** to resolve troubling issues and understand their circumstances than those low in integrity.
- **Alfonso, Bueno, Loureiro, and Pereira (2011)**: among people over **65 in Portugal**, reminiscing about their lives brought a feeling of ego integrity and psychological well-being.
- **Torges, Stewart, and Duncan (2008); Torges, Stewart, and Miner-Rubino (2005)**: in adults in their **50s and 60s**, being able to **acknowledge regrets** and missed opportunities was directly related to **life satisfaction and physical health**, for both sexes, as Erikson predicted.
- **Robinson and Stell (2014)**: people aged **60 to 69** showed growing awareness of mortality, a scaling down of goals and activities and, for many, a **continuing struggle** with ego integrity.

#### Concern with ego integrity

- **Van Hiel and Vansteenkiste (2009)**: among **Belgian** adults in their **60s and 70s**, achieving ego integrity was linked to high subjective well-being, positive psychological health, **lower fear of death** and less bitterness and resentment.
- **Sheldon and Kasser (2001)**: in a sample aged **17 to 82**, older people were much more concerned with **generativity and ego integrity** and less concerned with **ego identity** than younger people, supporting Erikson. Age also correlated positively with **subjective well-being**: older people were generally happier.
- **Warburton, McLaughlin, and Pinsker (2006)**: among Australians aged **55 to 93**, continued involvement in **family and community** activities kept feelings of generativity going well into old age, echoing the "vital involvement" prescription.
- **Staudinger (2001a, 2001b)**: younger adults (**25 to 35**) and older adults (**60 to 85**) reflected on their lives **equally often**, but for **different reasons**. Younger people reflected to gain self-insight and solve current problems; older people reflected to evaluate their lives and reach ego integrity.

> **Highlights: Research on ego identity.** An online identity lets you try on different identities, can be as satisfying as a real-world identity, can help or hinder identity formation, and is used more by lonely and socially anxious people. For women, identity formation bore the imprint of the 1960s and 1970s women's movement (for those who were young then), is shaped more by career concerns, goes with readiness to change, and can be shaken by altered body image. People high in generativity tend to be happy and satisfied with life, successful in marriage and career, extraverted, conscientious, open to experience and high in self-esteem. People high in ego integrity spend quality time examining their past, can admit regrets and missed chances, and carry little bitterness or resentment.

#### Gender differences in ageing

Gender differences in ageing may make it harder for women than for men to carry out the calm stock-taking Erikson described. In research on adults in their **60s**, **men** reported much higher **identity, certainty, confidence and power** than women did (Miner-Rubino, Winter, & Stewart, 2004).

Part of the reason is a social **double standard** that treats ageing as worse for women and labels women "old" earlier than men. Schultz's example is film: a 50-year-old male actor may still be offered powerful leading roles, while a 50-year-old actress is often cast as a widow or grandmother, if she is cast at all. Women also tend to **outlive** men, so they are more likely to face illness and incapacity, bereavement, loss of social support and reduced income. This may help explain why women's life reviews are often **less positive** than men's and more likely to end in what Erikson called **despair** rather than integrity (Rainey, 1998).

### Racial and ethnic identity

Erikson himself did not consider one aspect of ego development: **racial identity** and the benefits it brings. Schultz treats the research on it as an extension of his theory.

#### Benefits of racial identity

Research consistently shows that developing and keeping a racial or ethnic identity matters for members of minority groups, and that **denying** one's racial identity can be stressful (for example Franklin-Jackson & Carter, 2007). Across a large number of studies of teenagers who are **Black, Latino or Asian**, living in **Canada, Hong Kong or the United States**, researchers have linked a strong ethnic identity to better psychological well-being and self-esteem, firmer social bonds, greater satisfaction with life and stronger motivation at school (Lee & Lee, 2014; Chae & Foley, 2010; Whittaker & Neville, 2010; Kiang, Witkow, Baldomar, & Fuligni, 2010; Usborne & Taylor, 2010; Lam & Tam, 2011; Yap, Settles, & Pratt-Hyatt, 2011; Smith & Silva, 2011).

Among **Black adolescents** the relationship between racial identity and psychological health is clear, consistent and strong: high scorers on racial identity were also high in subjective well-being, life satisfaction and self-esteem, and less likely to have mental health problems such as anxiety and depression (Constantine, Alleyne, Wallace, & Franklin-Jackson, 2006; Cross, Grant, & Ventunaec, 2012; Pillay, 2005).

Further findings, each worth one line in memory:

- **Bracey, Bamaca, and Umana-Taylor (2004)**: among Black, Asian and biracial teens, self-esteem was **highest among Black** teens, **lowest among Asian** teens, with **biracial** teens significantly in between. Racial identity appeared to be a stronger and more important factor for self-esteem in Black adolescents than in biracial or Asian adolescents.
- **French, Seidman, Allen, and Aber (2006)**: **group esteem** (how people feel about belonging to their racial or ethnic group) **rose** among African-American and Latino-American teenagers across early and middle adolescence, while White students' group esteem stayed **stable and high** throughout.
- **Resnicow, Soler, Braithwaite, Ben Selassie, and Smith (1999)**: Black adolescents high in ethnic identity had **more negative attitudes to drugs** and more positive attitudes to school, linked to better behaviour at school; but those high in **anti-White attitudes** were far more likely to use drugs, dislike school and misbehave. A strong identity helps; an identity built mainly on hostility to another group does not.
- **Bynum, Burton, and Best (2007)**: students who experienced more **racism** reported more stress and poorer psychological functioning.
- **Rogers (2013)**: among Black adolescents, **gender identity** became more important with age alongside ethnic identity, and those high in both scored high in mental health and school adjustment.
- **Santos and Updegraff (2014)**: among US teenagers born in **Mexico**, those with a more traditional, readily recognisable Latino appearance were **higher** in racial identity.

#### Women and racial identity

Among Black and Hispanic women, **identity confusion**, here meaning a conflict between the identity of one's minority culture and that of the majority culture, may lead to **eating disorders**. Identifying with a North American beauty ideal of extreme thinness led some women towards disorders such as **anorexia**; Harris and Kuba (1997) interpreted the disorder as an effort to look like the idealised woman of the dominant White culture.

#### Racial identity and ego identity

Research on adolescents of **Asian-American and Hispanic-American** background shows that ethnicity sits at the **core** of ego identity formation. Teens whose ethnic identity was strong had high self-esteem and better relations with peers and family (Phinney & Chavira, 1992). Some further findings:

- **Umana-Taylor (2004)**: Hispanic-American teens at **predominantly White** schools reported **higher** ethnic identity than those at more ethnically balanced schools. Being a visible minority seems to make ethnicity more salient to the self.
- **Chang & Le (2010); French & Chavez (2010); Torres & Ong (2010)**: a strong ethnic identity commitment among Latinos **buffered stress** and enhanced subjective well-being and academic achievement.
- **Suinn (1999)**: young **Asian Americans** high in ethnic identity resisted **alcohol and marijuana** more strongly than those more assimilated into the majority culture; **Schwartz, Mason, Pantin, and Szapocznik (2008)** found the same in young Hispanic adolescents from immigrant families.
- **Gfellner and Armstrong (2012)**: among **First Nation** adolescents in **Canada**, those who identified more strongly with their own culture scored higher on identity strength than those who saw themselves as **bicultural**.

#### Stages of developing racial identity

**William Cross** proposed a stage account of ethnic identity in African-American adolescents, the **Revised Racial Identity Model**. Cross published the **64-item Cross Racial Identity Scale** to measure the stages of his model, and research has shown it to be a valid test of ethnic identity (Vandiver, Cross, Worrell, & Fhagen-Smith, 2002). The model has **four stages** leading to a psychologically healthy Black identity (Cokley, 2002):

| Stage | Identities within it | What characterises it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Pre-encounter** | **Assimilation**; **miseducation**; **self-hatred** | Assimilation: little racial awareness or identity. Miseducation: negative stereotypes about Black people are taken in as true. Self-hatred: strongly negative views of Black people, producing anti-Black and self-hating attitudes |
| 2. **Encounter** | (single) | An experience of racism or discrimination jolts the adolescent's worldview |
| 3. **Immersion-emersion** | **Intense Black involvement**; **anti-White** | Intense Black involvement celebrates everything Black as good and desirable; anti-White sees everything White as evil and wrong |
| 4. **Internalization** | **Black nationalism**; **multiculturalist inclusive** | Black nationalism holds a pro-Black, Afrocentric view; the multiculturalist inclusive identity embraces Black identity alongside other ethnic, racial and gender identities |

Two studies test the model's developmental claim:

- **Pierre and Mahalik (2005)**: Black men with an average age of **20** in the **pre-encounter** stage reported significantly lower self-esteem, more psychological distress and lower well-being than those in the **internalization** stage.
- **Nghe and Mahalik (2001)**: in Black college students, as racial identity moved from the earliest towards the more mature stages, **defence mechanisms** moved from the least sophisticated, immature defences to more mature ones, as the model would predict.

The importance of such minority identity models, for Schultz, is twofold: they recognise ethnic identity as a vital component of ego identity, and they propose that it develops through a **sequence of stages**, in the same developmental pattern as Erikson's psychosocial stages, even though Erikson never dealt with ethnic identity directly.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition gives the *revised* Cross model **four** stages (pre-encounter, encounter, immersion-emersion, internalization) and a **64-item** scale. **Classic background:** Cross's original "Nigrescence" model (1971) had **five** stages, the fifth being internalization-commitment; older sources may list five. Also keep the sub-identities in the right stage: **miseducation** and **self-hatred** are pre-encounter; **anti-White** is immersion-emersion; **multiculturalist inclusive** is internalization.

### Gender preference identity

Another aspect of ego identity Erikson did not address directly is **gender preference identity**, which may affect overall ego identity and may vary with ethnic identity. In a study of **White, Black and Hispanic** children with an average age of **11**, **Black and Hispanic** children reported far more pressure to **conform to gender roles** than White children did (Corby, Hodges, & Perry, 2007).

#### Stages of identity

Researchers have proposed that **lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBT)** identity develops through a series of stages, in the same way Erikson described ego identity and Cross described ethnic identity. One model gives **four stages** (Frable, 1997):

1. **Sensitisation**: before adolescence, a first sense of being different from same-sex peers.
2. **Identity confusion**: in adolescence, the confusing and possibly frightening realisation that one's feelings and thoughts could be described as homosexual.
3. **Identity assumption**: the person comes to believe he or she is homosexual and begins to accept a gay identity.
4. **Commitment**: the gay identity is fully accepted as a way of life.

> **Trap.** "Identity confusion" appears twice in this chapter with different meanings: Erikson's original label for the identity problems of American Indians and WWII veterans (6.1), and the **second** stage of this gender preference identity model. In the racial-identity research it is also used for minority-majority culture conflict (Harris & Kuba, 1997). **Classic background:** the sensitisation, identity confusion, identity assumption, commitment sequence is usually credited in other texts to **Troiden (1989)**; Schultz cites it via Frable (1997).

#### Consequences of gay identity

Despite greater acceptance of people with transgender identities, they often face serious challenges in daily life. As a group, people whose gender preference identity is **non-traditional** carry a heavier load of **stress and depression**, a higher **suicide** rate, more feelings of **failure and guilt**, and more physical and mental health problems (Liu, Rochlen, & Mohr, 2005; Glicksman, 2013; Budge, Adelson, & Howard, 2013; Blosnich, Brown, Shipherd, Kauth, Piegeri, & Bossarte, 2013).

They are also targets of **bullying, harassment and discrimination**, especially at school, which can damage both emotional well-being and grades. Two things soften the impact: seeing other students **step in** to stop harassment (which also encourages others to intervene), and strong **social support** from family and friends (Wernick, Kulick, & Inglehart, 2014).

**Coming out**, identifying oneself publicly and expressing pride in one's identity with no wish to change or hide it, is associated with **higher** mental and emotional well-being (Bockting, 2014). People who had come out showed **higher self-esteem and less depression** than those who had not (Frable, 1997; Kosciw, Palmen, & Kull, 2014).

A study of male inmates in a **medium-security prison** found that those whose personality included a strong **need for personal relationships** had **less gender role conflict**. The authors suggested that this need for others may outweigh homophobia or the fear of appearing feminine, even in a prison culture that warns against close relationships among inmates. Inmates with less need for personal contact had more gender role conflict about homosexual tendencies (Schwartz, Buboltz, Seemann, & Flye, 2004).

> **Highlights: Research on Erikson's ideas.** Members of ethnic minorities high in ethnic and racial identity tend to score high in subjective well-being and self-esteem, hold less favourable attitudes towards illegal drugs, get on well with family and peers, do better at school and experience less stress. Gender preference research shows that Black and Hispanic children feel strong pressure to conform to gender roles, that conflict over gender preference goes with low self-esteem, guilt and stress, and that people high in gay identity show high self-esteem and no wish to change.

## 6.8 Reflections on Erikson's Theory

### Contributions and criticisms

**Contributions.** Schultz names three substantial contributions: the recognition that **personality keeps developing across the whole life span**; the concept of the **identity crisis** in adolescence; and the building of **cultural, social and historical forces** into a personality theory. All three are the three extensions of Freud from the chapter opening, now judged as achievements.

**Criticisms.** The theory has plenty of critics, and their points fall into five groups.

1. **Vague concepts and weak evidence.** Critics point to **ambiguous terms and concepts**, conclusions drawn **without supporting data**, and a general **lack of precision** (Rosenthal, Gurney, & Moore, 1981; Waterman, 1982). Erikson accepted that these charges were fair and blamed his **artistic temperament** and his lack of formal scientific training. In *Childhood and Society* (1950) he admitted that because he "came to psychology from art", readers would sometimes find him painting contexts and backgrounds where they wanted facts and concepts. Why this criticism holds: terms like "crisis", "basic strength" and "ego identity" were defined loosely enough that it is hard to say what observation would disprove them, and the main method was the case study. What survives: later researchers (Marcia, McAdams, Constantinople) turned the concepts into measurable statuses and scales and found broad support.
2. **An incomplete account of old age, and a too-rosy one.** The description of maturity was thin, which Erikson tried to correct in *Vital Involvement in Old Age* (Erikson et al., 1986). Some psychologists also doubt that development after 55 is as positive as "ego integrity" suggests; for many people this period brings **pain, loss and depression**, even for those who develop wisdom.
3. **Sex differences grounded in anatomy.** His reading of the **play constructions** as biologically based differences flowing from the presence or absence of a penis has been attacked; the differences could equally be **cultural** or the product of **sex-role training**, which Erikson later conceded.
4. **Stages that may not fit women.** The social psychologist **Carol Tavris** (1992), reading Erikson's "stages of man", found the account worrying because she "wasn't having any of my crises in the right order": her identity was still unsettled after her teens, and because she had not married on schedule her intimacy and generativity crises were, as she put it, on hold.
5. **Class bias: the moratorium as a luxury.** The theory may not apply to people in **poor economic circumstances** who cannot afford a moratorium to explore roles. A period of exploration may be available only to those who can attend college or take time off to travel, as Erikson did for **seven years** (Slugoski & Ginsburg, 1989).

Erikson showed little interest in answering his critics. He accepted that there are many ways to describe personality development and that no single account is adequate. His influence spread through his books and through the generations of psychologists, psychiatrists, teachers and counsellors who found his ideas a useful way to describe development from infancy to old age.

> **Trap: "Which is NOT a criticism of Erikson in Schultz?"** Genuine criticisms: ambiguous terms; conclusions without data; lack of precision; incomplete and over-optimistic account of maturity; anatomical interpretation of play-construction sex differences; poor fit to women's lives (Tavris); moratorium available only to the affluent (Slugoski & Ginsburg). **Not** criticisms in Schultz (common distractors): that he ignored the ego (he elevated it); that he ignored culture (he stressed it); that he confined development to childhood (the reverse); that he was a pessimist (he is optimistic); that he relied on dream analysis (he rarely used it).

### Recognition and influence

Erikson was recognised in both professional and popular circles. *Time* magazine called him the "most influential living psychoanalyst" (March 17, **1975**), and *Psychology Today* described him as "an authentic intellectual hero" (Hall, 1983). His concepts are used in **education, social work, vocational and marriage counselling**, and clinical work with **children and adolescents**, and Clark (2010) judged that his work still proves meaningful for contemporary psychology and social thought. The **Erikson Institute for Early Childhood Education** was set up for graduate training in **1966** at Loyola University in Chicago.

Three lasting effects stand out:

- **Life-span developmental psychology**, which has grown enormously in research and theory, owes much of its initial spark to Erikson, as does current interest in the developmental problems of **middle and old age**.
- **Play therapy** has become a standard diagnostic and therapeutic tool for emotionally disturbed and **abused** children. A child who cannot put a physical or sexual assault into words can show what happened through play, using dolls to stand for herself and her abuser.
- **Identity** itself has become a central concept in developmental, clinical and social psychology, as the volume of research in 6.7 shows.

## How the chapter fits together

Seven threads run through the chapter. If you can follow each one from module to module, you understand Erikson rather than just remembering his table.

1. **The theory is the biography, generalised.** Nearly every stage has an episode from Erikson's own life behind it (see the life-to-theory table in 6.1), and he admitted he had to name the identity crisis in others before facing it in himself.
2. **Freud's stages, read psychosocially.** The first four stages keep Freud's zones (mouth, anus, genitals, latency) but move the action from the body to the relationship: feeding becomes trust, toilet training becomes a contest of wills, the Oedipal wish becomes a test of whether initiative survives parental response, latency becomes the school's verdict on a child's effort. The ego is no longer the id's servant but an independent agent.
3. **Balance, not purity.** Healthy resolution is a favourable *ratio* of the positive to the negative pole. This one idea explains adaptive coping (6.2), the counter-intuitive labels of maldevelopment (6.4: only-positive is "maladaptive" and leads to neurosis; only-negative is "malignant" and leads to psychosis), and why some mistrust is protective.
4. **A chain of dependencies.** Each strength needs the previous one. The research shows the chain in data: adaptive coping in stages 1 to 4 predicts identity cohesion (Waterman et al., 1970); early identity achievement predicts stable romance in the twenties (Beyers & Seiffge-Krenke, 2010); generativity is the main route to integrity (*Vital Involvement in Old Age*).
5. **Optimism with repair.** Weakness at one stage can be offset later: Holocaust survivors with persistent mistrust still mastered the later stages (Suedfeld et al., 2005); slow identity achievers caught up by midlife (Whitbourne et al., 2009); a patient teacher can undo early mistrust. That is why Erikson is rated optimistic and only partly deterministic.
6. **Culture and history are inside personality.** Sioux child-rearing, uprooted war veterans, the women's movement imprinting on women who were adolescents at the time, ethnic identity for minority youth, technology cutting older people off from mentoring: the same psychosocial principle at different scales.
7. **Where the data push back.** The identity crisis can run past 18 and college prolongs it; women may form identity earlier and through relationships; generativity in women stays high into the 60s; old age is often less serene than "integrity" implies; the play-construction interpretation is anatomical and culturally naive; the moratorium may be a middle-class luxury.

**Relation to other theorists in the book.** Erikson is the bridge between the neo-psychoanalysts and the developmental and humanistic chapters. Like **Freud** he keeps psychoanalysis as the starting point, unconscious motives and the Oedipal period; unlike Freud he extends development to death, frees the ego and adds culture. Like **Jung** he cares about the second half of life, and his stagnation in middle age resembles Jung's midlife crisis, but he sets out a full eight-stage sequence where Jung stressed one transition. Like **Adler** and **Horney** he holds that social relationships shape personality and that change continues after childhood; Horney's basic anxiety and Erikson's mistrust both start in a child's experience of unreliable care. His emphasis on growth, strengths and conscious direction of one's own development anticipates the **humanistic** theorists later in the book. Through **Anna Freud**, he belongs to ego psychology.

## Worked example

*Rohan, 19, is a second-year engineering student in Pune referred to the college counsellor after failing three papers. He chose engineering because his father and uncle are engineers and "it was always decided". This year he has stopped attending class, spends nights in an online fantasy game where he leads a guild, and has told a friend that he "doesn't know what he is doing with his life". He wants to drop out and become a musician, but says this is "just another fantasy". His parents are strict and controlling; his father calls him "the family failure". As a child he was praised only for marks and mocked when he struggled with mathematics. He has a steady girlfriend but avoids talking about the future with her.*

Work through it with the chapter's concepts.

**Which stage, and what is the crisis?** Rohan is just past Schultz's adolescent range (12 to 18), but Archer (1982) found up to 30 percent still searching at 24, and Adams and Fitch (1982) showed that college students stay in moratorium longer than employed peers. So the crisis is **identity cohesion versus role confusion**, prolonged by college, which is fully consistent with the 11th-edition research.

**What identity status was he in, and where is he now?** Until this year he was in **foreclosure**: committed to an occupation he never explored, chosen for him by his parents. Marcia's foreclosed adolescents are often rigid and oriented to external goals, which fits a career justified only by "it was always decided". The present turmoil, uncertain commitments, anxiety, fantasies of music, ambivalence towards his father's authority, looks like a move into **moratorium**. On the four-step ladder (diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium, achievement) that is *progress*, not collapse, even though it feels worse. The risk is that, without support, he slides into **identity diffusion**, the aimless drifting Erikson himself went through.

**Why is it happening now?** The parenting pattern matters. Berzonsky (2004) and Mohammadi (2013) found that identity achievement is least likely when parents are too authoritarian or too permissive; Rohan's are authoritarian. Stage 4 matters too: being mocked over mathematics sets up **inferiority** rather than competence, and the dependency chain predicts that an adolescent who lacks the adaptive outcomes of stages 1 to 4 will struggle to reach identity cohesion (Waterman et al., 1970).

**What is the risk in the label "family failure"?** Erikson's concept of a **negative identity**: when no positive identity is available, a defined negative one is preferable to none. If "failure" is the only clear role offered, Rohan may adopt it.

**What role does the game play?** Two-sided, as 6.7 shows. Online role-play is a legitimate moratorium laboratory, and leading a guild may give him the competence and recognition his studies do not. But adolescents without a firm sense of self use online life more (Israelashvili et al., 2012), and a virtual persona can come to *replace* the developing real self.

**What about the girlfriend?** Erikson's sequence says intimacy depends on identity: you can merge with another only once there is a stable self that will not be swallowed. His avoidance of the future with her is what the theory predicts while stage 5 is unresolved.

**What would maldevelopment look like?** If he swings to fanatical commitment to one role (music as the only truth, total rejection of everything else), that is **fanaticism**, the maladaptive tendency of adolescence (too much of the positive pole). If he rejects every role, that is **repudiation**, the malignant tendency. Either can be addressed in psychotherapy; Erikson also allows **re-adaptation** through environmental change, supportive relationships, and success at a later stage.

**How would an Eriksonian clinician work with him?** Face to face in chairs, as equals, not on a couch; little or no dream analysis; techniques fitted to the person. The aim is not to choose a career for him but to protect the moratorium: help him explore engineering and music deliberately rather than by default, separate his own commitments from his father's, and build on the competence he shows in the guild. The basic strength the theory expects at the end is **fidelity**: a commitment he can be genuinely true to because he chose it.

## MCQ traps

1. **Erikson was analysed by Anna Freud**, almost daily for **3 years** at **$7 a month**, not by Sigmund Freud.
2. **No university degree**: he failed his first Harvard course and dropped out, yet Yale's Institute of Human Relations invited him in **1936**.
3. Born **Frankfurt, 1902**, to a Danish mother; stepfather **Dr Theodore Homburger** (his paediatrician); became **Erik Homburger Erikson** at **37** on taking US citizenship.
4. Rejected at school by **both** German classmates (Jewish family) **and** Jewish classmates (Aryan looks).
5. Dates: Vienna teaching job at **25**; met **Joan Serson 1929** (three wedding ceremonies); emigrated **1933**; **Austen Riggs 1950**; Harvard ten years later; retired **1970**.
6. **Identity confusion** was first seen in **American Indians** and **WWII veterans**, explained by confusion and cultural uprooting, **not repressed conflicts**.
7. **Three extensions of Freud**: whole life span; independent ego; cultural and historical forces.
8. **Epigenetic principle**: *epi* = "upon"; the **sequence** of stages is genetic, the **outcome** is psychosocial.
9. **Crisis** = turning point, not catastrophe; an unresolved crisis makes later stages harder, **not impossible**.
10. Healthy resolution = **mostly positive with some negative**; total trust is not the ideal.
11. **Maladaptive** = ego holds **only the positive** pole → neurosis; **malignant** = **only the negative** pole → psychosis; both are **maldevelopment**; maladaptions can also yield to **re-adaptation**.
12. Schultz's ages: **0 to 1, 1 to 3, 3 to 5, 6 to 11, 12 to 18, 18 to 35, 35 to 55, 55+**.
13. Strengths in order: **hope, will, purpose, competence, fidelity, love, care, wisdom**; **love** is "the greatest of all human virtues".
14. Schultz's wording vs other texts: **autonomy vs doubt, shame** (shame and doubt); **industriousness** (industry); **identity cohesion vs role confusion** (identity vs role confusion; earlier, identity diffusion); stage names **oral-sensory, muscular-anal, locomotor-genital, latency**.
15. **Holding on and letting go** are the key stage 2 abilities; the crisis is **toilet training**, "more psychosocial than biological".
16. Erikson held that a mother returning to work and using day care **risks mistrust**: a dated view Schultz reports.
17. **First four stages**: outcomes depend on **others**; **last four**: increasing **choice**. Hence **partially** deterministic.
18. **Identity crisis** in Schultz's glossary = **failure** to achieve ego identity; a **negative identity** beats **no** identity.
19. **Generativity** needs no children, and children do not guarantee it; stagnation resembles **Jung's midlife crisis**; regression to **pseudo-intimacy**.
20. ***Vital Involvement in Old Age*** (Erikson, Erikson, & Kivnick, **1986**): **29** people in their **80s**, data since **1928**; generativity is the key to integrity.
21. **Wisdom** = detached concern with the whole of life, passed on as **heritage**; **care** = broad concern for others shown in the **need to teach**.
22. Erikson **rejected the couch**, **rarely** analysed dreams ("wasteful and harmful"), used free association **occasionally**.
23. **Disciplined subjectivity** belongs to **psychohistorical analysis** (Gandhi, Luther, G. B. Shaw).
24. **Loyola Generativity Scale** = **20** items (McAdams & de St. Aubin, **1992**); **EIPQ** = **32** items, exploration and commitment (Balistreri et al., **1995**); **Ego-Identity Scale** = Dignan (**1965**). Erikson used **no** tests himself.
25. **Play constructions**: ages **10 to 12**; girls low, enclosed, static interiors with male or animal intruders (**never female**); boys towers, exteriors, motion. His reading was **anatomical**; he later admitted **sex-role training** could explain it.
26. Toy studies: **Wong & Hines (2014)** 2- to 4-year-olds, train vs doll; **Dinella et al. (2013)** college men, jet blue vs pink; **Joiner (1998)** 10- to 11-year-olds, Pirates vs Princesses; **fathers** stereotype more (Quiery, 1998).
27. **Holocaust survivors** (Suedfeld et al., **2005**), interviewed **30 to 40 years** after 1945: succeeded at every stage **except trust**.
28. **Ciaccio (1971)**: 4-year-olds' stories were about **autonomy**, the stage just completed, not initiative.
29. **Constantinople (1969)**: Inventory of Psychosocial Development, **first six** stages, adaptive development goes with **happiness**; **Reimanis (1974)**: maladaptive development goes with **alienation**.
30. **Marcia**: **five** statuses in Schultz (adds **alienated achievement**); ladder **diffusion → foreclosure → moratorium → achievement**.
31. **Foreclosure** = commitment **without** crisis, set by parents; **moratorium** = crisis **ongoing**, high **anxiety**; **diffusion** = no commitment, parents **indifferent and rejecting**.
32. **Jensen-Campbell & Graziano (2000)**: **155** adolescents, **2-week** diaries, **31 percent** of interactions conflictual; parent conflicts more intense than peer ones.
33. **Archer (1982)**: up to **30 percent** still searching at **24**; **Adams & Fitch (1982)**: students stay in **moratorium** longer than employed peers.
34. **Zucker et al. (2002)**: women's generativity higher in the 40s than the 20s but **level into the 60s**, contrary to Erikson; the generativity and well-being link is strongest when satisfaction comes from **career**, not parenting.
35. **Cross model** (revised): **4** stages, **64-item** scale; miseducation and self-hatred are **pre-encounter** identities. **Frable (1997)**: sensitisation → identity confusion → identity assumption → commitment.
36. *Time* called him the "most influential living psychoanalyst" in **1975**; Erikson Institute for Early Childhood Education founded **1966** in Chicago.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Life-span approach** | The approach to personality that traces development across the whole of life rather than stopping in childhood; Erikson is its representative |
| **Psychosocial stages of development** | Erikson's eight successive stages from birth to death, each posing a crisis to be met adaptively or maladaptively |
| **Epigenetic principle of maturation** | The idea that development follows a sequence of stages fixed by heredity, while the environment shapes how each stage turns out |
| **Crisis** | The turning point faced at each stage, demanding a change of perspective and a choice between adaptive and maladaptive coping |
| **Adaptive vs maladaptive ways of coping** | The positive and negative poles of each stage's crisis (e.g. trust vs mistrust) |
| **Basic strengths (virtues)** | Motivating characteristics and beliefs that come from satisfactorily resolving a stage's crisis; each depends on the previous one |
| **Basic weaknesses** | Motivating characteristics that come from unsatisfactory resolution of a developmental crisis |
| **Maldevelopment** | An unbalanced ego containing only one way of coping with a stage's conflict |
| **Maladaptive tendency** | Maldevelopment in which only the positive pole is present; can lead to neurosis |
| **Malignant tendency** | Maldevelopment in which only the negative pole is present; can lead to psychosis |
| **Re-adaptation** | Easing of maladaptions through environmental change, supportive relationships or later successful adaptation |
| **Oral-sensory stage** | Birth to 1; trust vs mistrust; strength hope |
| **Trust / mistrust** | Expectation that people and the world are reliable / suspicion and fear produced by rejecting or inconsistent care |
| **Hope** | Belief that one's desires will be satisfied, persisting through setbacks |
| **Muscular-anal stage** | Ages 1 to 3; autonomy vs doubt and shame; strength will |
| **Autonomy / doubt and shame** | Sense of exercising one's own will / self-doubt and shame when independence is frustrated, typically in toilet training |
| **Will** | Determination to exercise free choice and self-restraint despite social demands |
| **Locomotor-genital stage** | Ages 3 to 5; initiative vs guilt; strength purpose |
| **Initiative / guilt** | Desire to start activities and fantasies / lasting guilt when initiative is punished |
| **Purpose** | Courage to envision and pursue goals |
| **Latency stage** | Ages 6 to 11; industriousness vs inferiority; strength competence |
| **Industriousness / inferiority** | Good work and study habits / feelings of inadequacy after scolding, ridicule or rejection |
| **Competence** | Applying skill and intelligence to pursue and complete tasks |
| **Ego identity** | The self-image formed in adolescence that integrates what we are and what we want to be |
| **Identity cohesion / role confusion** | A consistent, integrated self-image / not knowing who one is, where one belongs or where one is going |
| **Identity crisis** | In Schultz's glossary, the failure to achieve ego identity during adolescence |
| **Identity confusion** | Erikson's first term for the lack of a clear self-identity seen in American Indians and WWII veterans |
| **Psychological moratorium** | Adolescence as a pause between childhood and adulthood for trying out roles (often "psychosocial moratorium" elsewhere) |
| **Negative identity** | A socially disapproved identity (e.g. crime, drugs), still preferred to having none |
| **Fidelity** | Sincerity, genuineness and sense of duty in relationships, arising from a cohesive identity |
| **Intimacy / isolation** | Caring and committed closeness without loss of identity / avoidance and rejection of others out of fear for one's identity |
| **Love** | Mutual devotion in a shared identity; Erikson's greatest virtue |
| **Generativity / stagnation** | Active involvement in teaching and guiding the next generation / boredom and interpersonal impoverishment when no outlet is found |
| **Pseudo-intimacy** | Childlike self-indulgence into which stagnating adults may regress |
| **Care** | Broad concern for others, shown in the need to teach |
| **Ego integrity / despair** | Accepting one's place and one's past / bitterness and regret over a life that cannot be redone |
| **Wisdom** | Detached concern with the whole of life, passed on as heritage |
| **Vital involvement** | Erikson's prescription for old age: remaining active and challenged rather than only reflecting |
| **Identity consolidation** | Dealing successfully with the social realities of adult life, usually in the 20s |
| **Play therapy** | Observing a child's use of toys to reveal personality the child cannot express in words |
| **Play constructions** | Assessment technique in which structures children build from dolls, blocks and other toys are analysed |
| **Psychohistorical analysis** | Application of Erikson's life-span theory and psychoanalytic principles to the lives of historical figures |
| **Disciplined subjectivity** | Erikson's method in psychohistory of adopting the subject's own viewpoint to assess life events |
| **Ego-Identity Scale** | Dignan's (1965) measure of adolescent ego identity |
| **Ego Identity Process Questionnaire** | 32-item adolescent measure of exploration and commitment (Balistreri et al., 1995) |
| **Loyola Generativity Scale** | 20-item self-report measure of generativity vs stagnation (McAdams & de St. Aubin, 1992) |
| **Inventory of Psychosocial Development** | Test of adaptive and maladaptive development in the first six stages (Constantinople, 1969) |
| **Identity achievement** | Marcia status: committed to self-chosen occupational and ideological choices |
| **Moratorium (status)** | Marcia status: currently in crisis, commitments vague, high anxiety |
| **Foreclosure** | Marcia status: committed without having had a crisis; choices set by parents |
| **Identity diffusion** | Marcia status: no commitments, possibly no crisis; drifting |
| **Alienated achievement** | Schultz's fifth status: crisis experienced, no occupational goal, committed to a critique of the social system |
| **Positive / negative emotionality (PEM / NEM)** | Happy active involvement in work and social life / stress, anxiety, anger (Helson & Klohnen, 1998) |
| **Multi-user dungeons (MUDs)** | Shared virtual places where players interact and build personal virtual worlds |
| **Group esteem** | How people feel about being members of their racial or ethnic group |
| **Revised Racial Identity Model** | Cross's four-stage model of healthy Black identity: pre-encounter, encounter, immersion-emersion, internalization |
| **Cross Racial Identity Scale** | 64-item measure of the stages of Cross's model |
| **Gender preference identity** | One's identity with respect to gender preference, which may affect overall ego identity |
| **Sensitisation / identity assumption / commitment** | Stages 1, 3 and 4 of the four-stage gender preference identity model (Frable, 1997) |
