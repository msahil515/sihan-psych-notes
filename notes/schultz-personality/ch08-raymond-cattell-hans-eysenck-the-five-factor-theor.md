# Chapter 8: Raymond Cattell, Hans Eysenck, the Five-Factor Theory, HEXACO, and the Dark Triad

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 7 gave you Allport, who believed traits were real and who tried to capture them by studying one person at a time. This chapter keeps his belief in traits and throws away his method. Every theory here starts from the same move: collect a very large amount of data on many people, correlate everything with everything, and let a statistical procedure called **factor analysis** tell you how many basic dimensions of personality there are. The answer, frustratingly for students and usefully for examiners, depends on who did the analysis. **Raymond Cattell** found **16 source traits** and built the **16 PF** test to measure them. **Hans Eysenck** found **three** broad dimensions, **extraversion (E), neuroticism (N) and psychoticism (P)**, and tied them to differences in the brain and the autonomic nervous system. **Robert McCrae and Paul Costa** settled on **five** factors, the **Big Five** (neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, conscientiousness), measured by the **NEO** inventory, and this is the model that now dominates research. **Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee** argued for **six**, the **HEXACO** model, whose distinctive addition is **honesty/humility**. **Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams** looked at the dark end of personality and described a **Dark Triad** of narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy. Running under all of it is a second theme that gives this part of the book its name, **The Genetics Approach**: each theorist used twin, sibling and adoption comparisons to argue that a substantial share of personality is inherited, and the chapter closes by weighing how large that share really is.

**How to read this chapter.** Sections 8.1 to 8.11 are Cattell, laid out as Schultz lays out every theorist: his aims and method, his life, his classification of traits, the 16 source traits, motivation, heredity, six developmental stages with ages, his position on the six human-nature questions, his three kinds of data (L, Q and T), his research methods and findings, and an evaluation. Section 8.12 is a short bridge on **behavioural genetics**. Sections 8.13 and 8.14 are Eysenck's life and his three dimensions with their biological explanations and research. Section 8.15 is the long Five-Factor module, which is mostly a catalogue of research findings organised by heading (culture, gender, stability, emotions, behaviour). Sections 8.16 and 8.17 are the newer HEXACO and Dark Triad models, 8.18 looks at how all these traits show up in Internet behaviour, and 8.19 weighs the trait approach as a whole. Hold on to the numbers, because Indian papers love them: 16, 3, 5, 6 and 3 again; one-third versus two-thirds; 80 percent; the stage ages; 35 versus 40 versus 50 versus 55 nations.

## 8.1 Predicting Behaviour

### A different aim from the clinicians

Almost every theorist you have met so far began in the consulting room. Freud, Jung, Adler, Horney and Erikson built their ideas out of people who were unhappy or disturbed and who came to them wanting to change. Cattell began somewhere else. His goal was not to cure anyone; it was to **predict** what a person will do when placed in a particular situation. His research participants were ordinary, normal people, and he wanted to describe their personalities, not treat them. He also argued that trying to change a personality before you understand exactly what you are changing is either impossible or foolish. A clinician who starts reshaping a client before mapping the client's traits is, in his view, working blind.

This aim is the key to everything that follows. Cattell's own definition of personality, the one quoted at the head of the chapter, says it outright: personality is whatever allows us to predict a person's behaviour in a given situation (Cattell, 1950). If you remember that a theory built for **prediction** will value lawfulness, measurement and statistics above insight and empathy, you can work out most of his positions without memorising them.

### A scientific approach

Cattell's approach was relentlessly data-driven. It was common in his studies for a single participant to provide more than **50 different measurements**. Schultz quotes Horn (2001) to the effect that no other personality theory matched Cattell's in its breadth and in its reliance on evidence from empirical research. The contrast with the clinical theorists is deliberate: Freud's evidence was a few dozen patients interpreted by one man; Cattell's evidence was thousands of people measured on dozens of variables.

### Factor analysis

The signature of Cattell's method is what he did with all that data. He fed it into **factor analysis**, a statistical technique that starts from the correlations between every possible pair of measures taken from a group of people and asks whether a smaller number of underlying factors can account for the pattern.

Here is the logic in plain terms. Suppose you give 500 students a battery of tests and find that their scores on an anxiety scale and their scores on an introversion scale rise and fall together very closely: the students who score high on one tend to score high on the other. Cattell would conclude that the two scales are tapping the same, or closely related, aspect of personality. The two measures can then be treated as indicators of a single dimension, a **factor**. Repeat this across dozens of measures and you can reduce a huge table of correlations to a handful of factors, each one a cluster of measures that hang together.

A clinical analogy helps. A psychiatrist who notices that low mood, poor sleep, loss of appetite and hopelessness tend to occur together in the same patients is reasoning informally the way factor analysis reasons mathematically: things that co-vary probably share a common source.

> **Trap.** Factor analysis does not "discover" causes on its own. It shows which measures cluster. Deciding that a cluster is a real trait, and choosing what to call it, are human decisions. This point returns as the main criticism of Cattell in 8.11, and Eysenck used it against him.

### Personality traits

Cattell called the factors he extracted **traits**, and he thought of them as the mental elements out of which personality is built. Because his goal was prediction, traits mattered to him as the information you need before you can forecast anything. On his view you understand a person fully only when you can state, precisely, the whole pattern of traits that marks that person out as an individual.

> **Trap.** Cattell and Allport both used the word "trait", but they meant different things and arrived at them by different routes. Allport (Chapter 7) treated traits as real structures inside one person, best studied idiographically, and stressed what is unique. Cattell derived traits statistically from many people at once by factor analysis and concentrated on the traits everyone shares. Schultz's first review question asks you exactly this: how does Cattell's concept of traits differ from Allport's?

*Classic background:* Cattell did not start from nothing. His raw material for the rating studies was the long list of English trait words compiled by **Allport and Odbert (1936)**, roughly 18,000 terms, which he trimmed and clustered before factor-analysing ratings. This "lexical" starting point, the idea that important individual differences become encoded as words in a language, links Allport, Cattell and later the Big Five researchers.

## 8.2 The Life of Cattell (1905 to 1998)

### An older brother and wounded soldiers

Cattell was born in **1905** in **Staffordshire, England**. His parents set high, demanding standards for how their children behaved, yet left them largely free to spend their leisure as they liked. Cattell, his brothers and their friends lived an adventurous outdoor childhood of boats, swimming, caves and mock warfare, dangerous enough that he later joked about children drowning or tumbling off cliffs.

That carefree boyhood ended at **age 9**, when **England entered World War I**. A large house near his home was turned into a military hospital, and the boy watched train after train bring wounded soldiers back from the battlefields of France. He later wrote that the experience made him unusually serious for his age and left him with a keen sense of how short life is and how much there is to get done while one can. Schultz suggests that his lifelong, almost obsessive commitment to work may have started here.

A second influence was family rivalry. Cattell felt intensely competitive with an **older brother**, and he described the difficulty of protecting his own freedom to develop while living alongside a brother he could not "overcome" (1974a). Adler would have recognised the pattern immediately (Chapter 4): a younger child measuring himself against an elder he cannot beat.

**What this fed in the theory.** The sense of urgency and the dislike of wasted effort show in a theory that values efficient prediction over speculation, and in a working life of extraordinary output.

### Psychology: a bad choice

At **16**, Cattell entered the **University of London** to study **physics and chemistry**, and he graduated **with honours in 3 years**. Living in London sharpened his concern with social problems, and he came to feel that physical science gave him no tools for dealing with them. The human mind, he decided, was the thing to study.

Schultz stresses how risky this was in **1924**. Psychology in England offered few careers; there were only **six academic professorships** in the subject in the whole country, and it was seen as a field for eccentrics. His friends advised against it. He went ahead anyway and began graduate study at the University of London under **Charles E. Spearman**, the psychologist and statistician who had **developed factor analysis**.

The meeting with Spearman is the single most important event in the life for exam purposes. Spearman had used factor analysis to study **mental abilities** (it is the method behind his general intelligence factor). Cattell resolved to take the same technique and apply it to the **structure of personality**.

### A time of hardship

Cattell received his **Ph.D. in 1929**, and his friends turned out to be right: there were almost no jobs. He patched together a living by lecturing at **Exeter University**, writing a book about the **English countryside**, and setting up a **psychology clinic for the schools of Leicester**, while continuing his own research on the side.

These were grim years. Overwork, poor food and a cold basement flat left him with **chronic digestive problems**, and his **wife left him**, unwilling to live with his bleak financial prospects and his complete absorption in work. Yet Cattell later claimed that the hardship did him good. Because he could not afford the luxury of abstract theorising or leisurely experiments, he was pushed to focus on **practical problems**. He described himself as having become as wary and cautious as a squirrel that has survived a hard winter (1974b), with an ascetic streak and no patience for anything irrelevant.

**What this fed in the theory.** The practical, applied cast of Cattell's work, tests for clinics, schools, marriages and employers, makes sense against these years.

### Success in America

The break came **eight years after his doctorate**. The prominent American psychologist **Edward L. Thorndike** invited him to spend a year in Thorndike's laboratory at **Columbia University** in New York. The year after that he took a professorship at **Clark University** in Worcester, Massachusetts, and in **1941** he moved to **Harvard**, where, as he put it, his creativity began to flow. His Harvard colleagues included **Gordon Allport** and **William Sheldon**, who was then working on his theory linking personality to **body type**.

He remarried, this time to a **mathematician** who shared his research interests, and at **age 40** he settled at the **University of Illinois** as a research professor. His output there was enormous: **more than 500 articles and 43 books**. He described the following two decades as a period of smooth, powerful, non-stop work; he was usually the last person to leave the car park at midnight, and he liked to tell the story of arriving at an empty laboratory one day and phoning home, only to learn that his family was sitting down to Thanksgiving dinner (Cattell, 1993). Every day was a working day.

In his **70s** he joined the graduate faculty of the **University of Hawaii**, where he allowed himself one indulgence, a daily swim in the ocean. A colleague remarked that he still worked like a young assistant professor anxious about tenure (Johnson, 1980). He died in **Honolulu at 92**, a year after the American Psychological Association named him for its **Gold Medal Award for Life Achievement in Psychological Science** (1997), whose citation singled out, among other things, his factor-analytic mapping of the domains of personality.

> **Trap.** Classic background: Schultz presents the 1997 Gold Medal as received, but the story did not end there. After objections to Cattell's writings on eugenics, the APA postponed the presentation, and in January 1998 Cattell withdrew his name from consideration; he died the following month. This is the controversy behind Tucker's 2009 book *The Cattell Controversy* in the chapter's reading list. If an option says he was awarded the medal in 1997, that matches the 11th edition; if it asks whether he accepted it at a ceremony, the answer is no.

| Life event | What it fed in the work |
|---|---|
| Wounded soldiers at age 9 (WWI) | Seriousness, urgency, a lifetime of intense work |
| Rivalry with an older brother | A competitive drive to achieve |
| Physics and chemistry degree, honours in 3 years | A natural scientist's standard of measurement and evidence |
| Graduate work under Spearman | Factor analysis, moved from abilities to personality |
| Years of poverty and illness after 1929 | Focus on practical, applied problems; impatience with the irrelevant |
| Thorndike, Clark, Harvard (1941), Illinois at 40 | Resources for massive data collection; 500+ articles, 43 books |
| Hawaii in his 70s; APA Gold Medal (1997) | Recognition as a founder of the trait approach |

> **Trap.** **Spearman** developed factor analysis and used it on **intelligence**; **Cattell** applied it to **personality**. Do not credit Cattell with inventing the technique. Also keep the institutions in order: London (Ph.D. 1929), Columbia with Thorndike, Clark, Harvard (1941), Illinois (at 40), Hawaii (70s).

*Classic background:* Cattell is also the source of the distinction between **fluid intelligence** (the capacity to reason and solve new problems, thought to be more biologically based) and **crystallised intelligence** (knowledge and skills accumulated through experience and culture). Schultz does not discuss it in this chapter, but Indian papers sometimes attach it to Cattell's name in a personality question, and it fits his interest in Factor B (intelligence) and its heritability.

## 8.3 Cattell's Approach to Personality Traits

Cattell defined **traits** as **relatively permanent reaction tendencies** that form the **basic structural units of personality**, and, as the margin definition adds, they are derived by factor analysis. He did not stop at a single list. He sorted traits along four different axes, and much of the examining on Cattell consists of checking that you have not mixed the axes up. Schultz summarises them in Table 8.1, laid out here in my own words.

**Table 8.1** Cattell's ways of classifying traits

| Axis of classification | Trait type | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| How widely shared | **Common traits** | Present in everyone, in varying amounts | Intelligence, extraversion, gregariousness |
| | **Unique traits** | Found in only one person or a few | A passion for genealogy, for a particular sport, for martial arts |
| What the trait does | **Ability traits** | Skills that set how efficiently you work toward a goal | Intelligence |
| | **Temperament traits** | The emotional tone and general style of your behaviour | Being assertive, easy-going, fretful or irritable |
| | **Dynamic traits** | The forces behind behaviour: motives, interests, ambitions | A drive to achieve, an interest in politics |
| How stable, and whether unitary | **Surface traits** | Clusters of correlated behaviours with no single underlying source; less stable | Neuroticism, built from anxiety, indecision and irrational fear |
| | **Source traits** | Single, stable, permanent factors that underlie surface traits | The 16 factors of the 16 PF |
| Where a source trait comes from | **Constitutional traits** | Source traits arising from biological conditions | Careless, talkative, slurred behaviour after heavy drinking |
| | **Environmental-mold traits** | Source traits learned from social and physical surroundings | The bearing of a career army officer versus a jazz musician |

### Common traits and unique traits

A **common trait** is one that every person possesses to some degree. Intelligence, extraversion and gregariousness are Cattell's examples: nobody has none of them, but people differ in how much they have. Why should these traits be universal? Cattell's reason was that human beings share a similar hereditary potential and, within one culture, face similar social pressures, so the same broad dimensions appear in all of them.

People differ in two ways, then. They differ in the **amount** of each common trait they have, and they differ because each has some **unique traits**, features of personality shared with few others. Unique traits show up most clearly in **interests and attitudes**. One of your classmates may be absorbed by family trees and spend weekends in archives; another may know every detail of a particular war, follow a particular cricket team obsessively, or train in a Chinese martial art. Those enthusiasms do not appear as dimensions in a factor analysis of thousands of people, but they are part of who each person is.

### Ability, temperament and dynamic traits

The second axis sorts traits by what they do in behaviour.

**Ability traits** govern **how efficiently** you can work toward a goal. Intelligence is the standard example: two students with the same ambition will pursue it differently if one reasons faster and more accurately than the other.

**Temperament traits** describe the **general style and emotional tone** of behaviour: how assertive, easy-going or irritable a person is. They shape the manner in which you act and react, whatever the goal.

**Dynamic traits** are the **driving forces**. They define motivations, interests and ambitions: why you act at all, and toward what.

A single episode shows all three at once. A trainee clinical psychologist preparing a difficult case presentation is moved by her ambition to qualify (dynamic), copes with the material according to how quickly she can understand it (ability), and goes about it in her characteristic way, calmly or anxiously (temperament).

### Surface traits and source traits

The third axis is the one examiners return to most often, and it is the one closest to Cattell's factor-analytic method.

**Surface traits** are personality characteristics that **correlate with one another** but **do not form a factor**, because no single source determines them. Cattell's example is **neuroticism**: anxiety, indecision and irrational fear tend to occur together and can be lumped under one label, but they come from several different sources. Because a surface trait is a composite of several elements, it is **less stable and less permanent**, and therefore less useful for describing personality. It can strengthen or weaken from one situation to another.

**Source traits** are the important ones. They are **unitary** factors, much more **stable and permanent**, each giving rise to some aspect of behaviour. They are what factor analysis extracts, and several source traits combine to produce each surface trait. A useful image is a set of rooms with several pipes running into each: what you see in a room (the surface trait) depends on several pipes (the source traits) feeding in behind the wall.

> **Trap.** In Cattell's own example, **neuroticism is a surface trait**, a composite with no single source. In Eysenck's model (8.14), neuroticism is one of only three broad **superfactors**, and in McCrae and Costa's model it is one of the five basic factors. The word is the same; its status in each theory is completely different. An option such as "Cattell regarded neuroticism as a source trait" is false on Schultz's text.

### Constitutional traits and environmental-mold traits

The fourth axis applies only to **source traits**, and it sorts them by **origin**.

**Constitutional traits** come from **biological conditions**, but Schultz is careful to say that they are **not necessarily innate**. His example makes the point: drinking too much alcohol or taking drugs can produce carelessness, talkativeness and slurred speech. That pattern has a bodily origin, yet nobody is born with it.

**Environmental-mold traits** are produced by influences in our **social and physical environments**. They are **learned** characteristics that stamp a pattern on personality. A child growing up in a poor, crowded inner-city neighbourhood is shaped differently from a child raised in wealth; a career army officer develops a different pattern of behaviour from a jazz musician. Schultz draws the conclusion that Cattell recognised the **interaction between personal and situational variables**.

> **Trap.** Two traps in one. First, constitutional and environmental-mold are subdivisions of **source** traits, not of surface traits. Second, Schultz describes constitutional traits here as biological in origin **but not necessarily innate**, yet in the Questions about Human Nature section (8.8) he writes that constitutional traits are innate. If an item asks whether constitutional means "inborn", the safer answer follows the definition in this section: **biological origin**, which may or may not be inherited. The trio to keep apart is **constitutional (body), environmental-mold (learning), and the surface/source split (stability and unity)**.

## 8.4 Source Traits: The Basic Factors of Personality

After **more than two decades** of intensive factor-analytic work, **Cattell (1965)** identified **16 source traits** as the basic factors of personality. They are best known in the form most people meet them, as the scales of an objective personality test, the **Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire (16 PF)**. Each factor is bipolar: a person can score toward one end, toward the other, or in the middle. Schultz's Table 8.2 lists the descriptions of low and high scorers; the version below rewords them but keeps each factor's letter and direction.

**Table 8.2** Cattell's 16 source traits (factors) of personality

| Factor | Low scorers tend to be | High scorers tend to be |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Reserved: aloof, keeps people at a distance | Outgoing: warm-hearted, easy to get along with |
| **B** | Lower in intelligence | Higher in intelligence |
| **C** | Low in ego strength: easily upset, emotionally changeable | High in ego strength: calm, emotionally stable |
| **E** | Submissive: meek, docile, unsure of self | Dominant: assertive, forceful |
| **F** | Serious: sober, gloomy, given to worry | Happy-go-lucky: cheerful, enthusiastic |
| **G** | Expedient: weak superego | Conscientious: strong superego |
| **H** | Timid: shy, restrained | Bold: venturesome, adventurous |
| **I** | Tough-minded: self-reliant, demanding | Tender-minded: sensitive, dependent |
| **L** | Trusting: accepting, understanding | Suspicious: jealous, withdrawn |
| **M** | Practical: down-to-earth, attentive to detail | Imaginative: absent-minded |
| **N** | Forthright: naive, unpretentious | Shrewd: worldly, insightful |
| **O** | Self-assured: secure, even complacent | Apprehensive: insecure, self-blaming |
| **Q1** | Conservative: traditional, resists change | Experimenting: radical, liberal, welcomes change |
| **Q2** | Group-dependent: a joiner and follower | Self-sufficient: independent, resourceful |
| **Q3** | Uncontrolled: lax, impulsive | Controlled: exacting, compulsive |
| **Q4** | Relaxed: tranquil, composed | Tense: driven, fretful |

Notice how ordinary the language is. The descriptions are the words you would use about a friend or yourself, and Schultz invites the reader to place themselves on each factor at a glance. That everyday quality is not an accident: the factors were distilled from ratings made with ordinary trait vocabulary.

Some factors are worth tagging for later sections, because research results attach to them:

- **Factor B** is **intelligence**, the one ability factor in the set. Cattell estimated it at about **80 percent genetic** (8.6).
- **Factor H** (timid versus bold) is the other **80 percent genetic** factor in the same estimate.
- **Factors F, I and Q3** were found to be **primarily hereditary**, and **E, G and Q4** **primarily environmental**, in Cattell's 1982 study (8.10).
- **Factor G** (expedient versus conscientious, low versus high **superego**) and **Factor C** (ego strength) show Cattell borrowing Freudian vocabulary for statistically derived dimensions.

*Classic background:* two features of the lettering are commonly asked. The sequence runs A to O but **skips D, J and K**, because those factors, found in early rating studies, did not hold up in adult questionnaire data. And the four **Q factors (Q1 to Q4)** carry a "Q" because they emerged **only in questionnaire (Q-data) research**, not in observers' ratings.

### Additional temperament traits, and source traits as atoms

Cattell later identified further factors that he classed as **temperament traits**, because they concern the general style and emotional tone of behaviour: **excitability, zest, self-discipline, politeness and self-assurance** (Cattell, 1973; Cattell & Kline, 1977).

The governing idea is an analogy from physics. In Cattell's system **source traits are to personality what atoms are to the physical world**: the basic elements. He argued that psychology could not understand personality, or formulate laws about it, until it had described these elements precisely, just as chemistry could not progress until it knew its elements.

> **Trap.** "Sixteen" refers to Cattell's **source traits** and to the **16 PF**; it does not refer to all of his traits. He also described surface traits, dynamic traits and later additional temperament factors. And do not confuse the **16 PF** (16 factors, Cattell) with Eysenck's **3** dimensions, the **Big 5** (McCrae and Costa), or **HEXACO's 6** (Ashton and Lee).

## 8.5 Dynamic Traits: The Motivating Forces

**Dynamic traits** are the traits concerned with **motivation**. Many theories in this book treat motivation as central (Freud's instincts, Adler's striving for superiority, Horney's needs), and Cattell agreed that it cannot be left out. His analogy: a personality theory that ignores the forces driving behaviour is like a description of an engine that never mentions what fuel it runs on. You may know every part of the machine and still have no idea why it moves.

The 11th edition says no more than this. Older editions, and many Indian exam keys, go into the detail of Cattell's motivational system, and Schultz's own review question 8 still asks "What is self-sentiment?", so the older material is worth knowing.

*Classic background:* Cattell divided dynamic traits into two kinds of **dynamic source trait** and one kind of surface expression.

- **Ergs** (from the Greek *ergon*, work or energy) are **constitutional dynamic source traits**: innate drives that supply the basic energy for behaviour. Older editions of Schultz list eleven, including **hunger, sex, gregariousness, curiosity, security (fear), self-assertion, protection (parental care), anger, disgust, appeal and self-submission**. An erg can grow stronger or weaker but cannot disappear from the personality.
- **Sentiments** are **environmental-mold dynamic source traits**: learned patterns of attitudes centred on an important object or area of life, such as a career, a religion, a sport, a parent, a spouse or a political cause.
- **Attitudes** are the most specific and observable level: a particular interest in acting a particular way toward a particular object in a particular situation (Cattell's formula ran roughly "in these circumstances, I want this much to do this with that").
- **Subsidiation** describes how these levels connect: attitudes are subsidiary to (serve) sentiments, and sentiments are subsidiary to ergs. Studying for an exam (attitude) serves a career sentiment, which in turn serves ergs such as security and self-assertion.
- The **dynamic lattice** is Cattell's diagram of this whole network of attitudes, sentiments and ergs in one person, with the ergs at the base and many attitudes feeding into them.
- The **self-sentiment** is the person's **self-concept**, the most important sentiment. It organises and stabilises the others, is reflected in nearly every attitude, and is among the last to reach full development.

> **Trap.** Keep the three motivational terms on their correct sides of the constitutional/environmental-mold line: **ergs = constitutional (innate)**, **sentiments = environmental-mold (learned)**, **attitudes = the specific surface expression** of both. The order of subsidiation runs **attitudes, then sentiments, then ergs**. And remember that this material is absent from the 11th-edition text; if a paper based on the 11th edition asks about it, it is drawing on older editions or Hall and Lindzey.

## 8.6 The Influences of Heredity and Environment

Cattell was keenly interested in how much of personality is inherited and how much is learned, and he went after the question statistically. He compared the degree of similarity between four kinds of pairs:

1. **twins reared together** in the same family,
2. **twins reared apart**,
3. **non-twin siblings reared together**, and
4. **non-twin siblings reared apart**.

The logic is simple once laid out. Twins share more genes than ordinary siblings; children reared together share more environment than children reared apart. By seeing how much similarity rises with shared genes and how much with shared upbringing, he could estimate the share of differences in each trait that comes from **heredity** and the share that comes from **environment**.

*Classic background:* Cattell named this method **Multiple Abstract Variance Analysis (MAVA)**.

The results differed from trait to trait:

- For some traits heredity dominates. Cattell's data indicated that about **80 percent of intelligence (Factor B)** and about **80 percent of timidity versus boldness (Factor H)** could be attributed to genetic factors.
- Taken across personality as a whole, however, he concluded that about **one-third is genetically based** and **two-thirds is determined by social and environmental influences**.

> **Trap.** Two different figures, both genuine: **80 percent** for two specific factors (**B and H**), and **one-third** for personality overall. An option claiming that Cattell found personality "mostly inherited" is wrong; overall he gave the larger share to environment. Contrast Eysenck, who held that heredity is primary (8.14), and the chapter's closing estimate of **up to 50 percent** (8.19).

## 8.7 Stages of Personality Development

Cattell proposed **six stages** of personality development spanning the whole of life, from birth to old age. Unlike Freud's stages, which end in adolescence, Cattell's continue through adulthood; unlike Erikson's, they are not organised around crises with named strengths. They are best read as a descriptive map of what typically changes at each age.

**Table 8.3** Cattell's stages of personality development

| Stage | Age | Main developments |
|---|---|---|
| **Infancy** | Birth to 6 | The major formative period; weaning and toilet training; ego, superego and social attitudes take shape |
| **Childhood** | 6 to 14 | Few psychological problems; growing independence from parents; identification with peers |
| **Adolescence** | 14 to 23 | The most troubled stage; conflicts over independence, self-assertion and sex |
| **Maturity** | 23 to 50 | Productive and satisfying (career, marriage, family); personality less flexible, emotional stability rises |
| **Late maturity** | 50 to 65 | Personality changes in response to physical, social and psychological change; re-examination of values |
| **Old age** | 65 onward | Adjustment to losses of spouse, friends, career and status; loneliness and insecurity |

### Infancy

**Infancy, from birth to 6**, is in Cattell's view **the major formative period** of personality. The child is shaped by parents and siblings and by the experiences of **weaning** and **toilet training**. During these years social attitudes develop together with the **ego** and **superego**, along with feelings of security or insecurity, attitudes toward authority, and perhaps a tendency toward neuroticism.

Cattell was not a Freudian, but here he borrowed two Freudian ideas: that the **early years are crucial** for personality, and that **oral and anal conflicts** can leave a mark on it. Schultz's review question 9 asks precisely which Freudian ideas Cattell took over; these two are the answer.

### Childhood

**Childhood, 6 to 14**, is a quiet stage with **few psychological problems**. Its main movement is outward: the child begins to become **independent of parents** and to **identify increasingly with peers**. (Compare Freud's latency period and Erikson's industry stage, which cover similar years.)

### Adolescence

**Adolescence, 14 to 23**, is more troubled and stressful. **Emotional disorders and delinquency** may appear as young people struggle with conflicts over three drives: for **independence**, for **self-assertion**, and for **sex**. Note the unusually long span: Cattell's adolescence stretches to 23, well past the end of schooling.

### Maturity

**Maturity, roughly 23 to 50**, is generally a **productive and satisfying** period in terms of career, marriage and family. Personality becomes **less flexible** than in earlier stages, and as a result **emotional stability increases**. Cattell found **little change in interests and attitudes** during these years.

### Late maturity

**Late maturity, 50 to 65**, brings personality changes in response to physical, social and psychological change. Health, vigour and physical attractiveness may decline, and the end of life comes into view. People **re-examine their values** and **search for a new self**. Schultz notes that this resembles **Jung's** account of the **midlife** period (Chapter 3), when attention turns inward and the values of the first half of life are questioned.

### Old age

**Old age, from 65 on**, requires adjustment to a series of **losses**: the deaths of spouse, relatives and friends; the loss of a career through retirement; the loss of status in a culture that prizes youth; and a pervasive sense of **loneliness and insecurity**.

> **Trap.** The age boundaries are a favourite target, and they differ from every other stage theory in the book. Cattell: **0 to 6, 6 to 14, 14 to 23, 23 to 50, 50 to 65, 65+**. Adolescence ends at **23**, not 18 or 20; maturity ends at **50**; late maturity is **50 to 65**. The **major formative** stage is **infancy (0 to 6)**, not adolescence. The stage with **few problems** is **childhood**; the **most stressful** is **adolescence**.

## 8.8 Questions about Human Nature

Cattell's definition of personality, "that which permits a prediction of what a person will do in a given situation" (1950, p. 2), is the best clue to his image of human nature. Prediction is only possible if behaviour is **lawful and orderly**. If people acted spontaneously and unpredictably, there would be nothing regular enough to forecast.

Schultz's illustration is a married couple. One spouse can usually predict quite accurately what the other will do in a given situation, because the partner's past behaviour has been consistent. Cattell's view leaves **little room for spontaneity**, because spontaneity would make prediction harder.

**Free will versus determinism.** Cattell falls toward **determinism**. A theory whose whole purpose is prediction must assume that behaviour follows from traits and situations in a regular way.

**Nature versus nurture.** He accepted the influence of **both**. Constitutional traits express the biological side; environmental-mold traits are learned. His own estimate gave about **one-third** of personality to heredity and **two-thirds** to environment (8.6).

**Importance of the past versus the present.** Cattell acknowledged the effect of **early life events** (infancy is the major formative stage, and he borrowed Freud's emphasis on early oral and anal conflicts), but he did **not** believe that childhood forces **fix personality permanently**. Development continues through six stages to old age. So he gives the past real weight without making it all-determining.

**Uniqueness versus universality.** A **moderate** position: **common traits** apply to everyone in a culture, while **unique traits** describe the individual.

**Equilibrium versus growth.** Schultz notes that Cattell proposed **no ultimate or necessary goal** that dominates behaviour: no drive toward self-actualisation pulling us into the future, and no psychosexual conflicts pushing us from the past. He therefore does not take a clear position on this issue; his theory is neither a tension-reduction model like Freud's nor a growth model like Maslow's.

**Optimism versus pessimism.** Here Cattell's **personal** view is clearer than his theoretical one, and it changed over his life. As a younger man he was **optimistic** that people would solve their social problems, gain more understanding and control of their environment, see the general level of intelligence rise, and build what he called a more gracious community of creatively occupied citizens (1974b). Reality disappointed him, and he eventually came to believe that **human nature and society had regressed**.

**How this differs from Freud** (Schultz's review question 7). Both are determinists, and both gave the first six years special weight. But Freud's determinism runs through unconscious instinctual conflict, whereas Cattell's runs through measurable traits and situations; Freud thought adult personality was essentially fixed by about age 5, whereas Cattell thought it kept developing across six stages; Freud's model is driven by tension reduction, whereas Cattell named no overriding goal; and Freud's evidence was clinical, whereas Cattell's was statistical.

| Issue | Cattell's position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | Determinism | Prediction requires lawful, orderly, consistent behaviour; little room for spontaneity |
| Nature vs nurture | Both | Constitutional and environmental-mold traits; roughly one-third heredity, two-thirds environment |
| Past vs present | Past important but not decisive | Infancy is the major formative period, yet childhood does not fix personality permanently |
| Uniqueness vs universality | Moderate (both) | Common traits shared by all in a culture; unique traits distinguish individuals |
| Equilibrium vs growth | No clear position | No ultimate goal: neither a self-actualising pull nor a psychosexual push |
| Optimism vs pessimism | Optimistic early, pessimistic later | Expected social progress when young; later concluded that human nature and society had regressed |

## 8.9 Assessment in Cattell's Theory

Cattell built his theory out of measurement, so his assessment methods are not an afterthought but the foundation. He used three main sources of data, each named with a letter: **L-data** (life records), **Q-data** (questionnaires) and **T-data** (tests).

### Life records (L-data)

**L-data** are **observers' ratings** of specific behaviours that people show in **real-life settings** such as a classroom or an office. An observer might record how often an employee is absent, a student's school grades, how conscientiously a worker carries out job duties, how emotionally steady a player stays during a football match, or how sociable someone is at work.

The defining features are that L-data concern **overt behaviour** that an outsider can see, and that the behaviour occurs in a **naturalistic setting**, not in the artificial conditions of a psychology laboratory. A school counsellor's record of a pupil's attendance, marks and classroom conduct is a good Indian-context example.

### Questionnaires (Q-data)

**Q-data** come from **self-report questionnaires**. The key contrast with L-data is who does the rating: in L-data **observers** rate the person; in Q-data people **rate themselves**.

Cattell was frank about two weaknesses. First, some people have only **superficial self-awareness**, so their answers do not reflect what they are really like. Second, even people who know themselves well may **not want** the researcher to know them, and may deliberately **falsify** their answers. For these reasons he warned that Q-data should never simply be assumed to be accurate. A job applicant filling in a personality questionnaire for a selection panel is the obvious case where both problems arise.

### Personality tests (T-data)

**T-data** come from what Cattell called **"objective" tests**, in which the person responds **without knowing which aspect of behaviour is being assessed**. The aim is to get around the weaknesses of Q-data: if you cannot work out what the tester is looking for, you cannot slant your answers to hide your traits. Shown an inkblot, for example, you would have no way of knowing whether your response would be read as a sign of conservatism, relaxation, adventurousness or apprehension.

On this reasoning Cattell counted the **Rorschach**, the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** and the **word-association test** as objective, because they are **hard to fake**. Schultz adds an important warning: most psychologists find this use of "objective" misleading. They usually call these instruments **subjective** (or projective) tests, because bias enters their **scoring and interpretation**.

> **Trap.** Cattell's meaning of "objective" is **resistant to faking by the test-taker**. The usual meaning is **free of bias in scoring by the tester**. So the Rorschach is "objective" in Cattell's sense and "subjective" in the usual sense. An option saying "Cattell classified the Rorschach as a T-data (objective) technique" is **true**; an option saying "most psychologists regard the Rorschach as objective" is **false**.

| Data type | Who provides it | Setting | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L-data** (life records) | Observers | Real life (school, office, field) | Overt behaviour in natural settings | Depends on the observers' records |
| **Q-data** (questionnaires) | The person (self-rating) | Questionnaire | Direct access to self-view | Shallow self-awareness; deliberate faking |
| **T-data** (tests) | The person's test responses | Test situation | Hard to fake because the aim is hidden | Scoring and interpretation can be biased |

### The 16 PF (Personality Factor) Test

Of the several tests Cattell built, the most important is the **16 PF Test**, which measures the **16 source traits**. Key facts:

- It is meant for people aged **16 and older**.
- It produces a score on **each of the 16 scales**.
- Responses are **scored objectively**, and **computerised** scoring and interpretation are available.
- It is widely used in three settings: **research**, **clinical diagnosis**, and **predicting job success**.
- It has been translated into some **40 languages**.

Schultz illustrates it with a sample profile for a hypothetical **airline pilot** (Figure 8.1).

"Sixteen" in the name refers to the number of **factors**, and the age threshold happens also to be 16, which question-setters exploit. And because the 16 PF is a self-report **questionnaire**, in Cattell's scheme it yields **Q-data**; Schultz calls it an "objective" test because its **scoring** is objective, which is not the same thing as Cattell's faking-resistant T-data.

## 8.10 Research on Cattell's Theory

Cattell distinguished **three ways** of studying personality: the **bivariate**, the **clinical** and the **multivariate** approaches. He judged the first two inadequate and built his own work on the third.

### The bivariate approach

The **bivariate** (two-variable) approach is the standard **laboratory experiment**. The researcher manipulates an **independent variable** and observes its effect on the participants' behaviour, the **dependent variable**. Because only one variable is studied at a time, it has also been called **univariate**.

Cattell granted that bivariate research is scientific, rigorous and quantitative. His objection was that it captures only narrow slices of personality. Real personality is shaped by **many variables interacting at once**, and the artificial laboratory cannot create or reproduce the significant emotional experiences of a life; you cannot, ethically or practically, bring a bereavement or a broken marriage into the lab and vary it. For Cattell, then, the bivariate approach was **too restrictive** to tell us much about traits.

> **Trap.** "Bivariate" and "univariate" are two names for the **same** approach in Schultz's account: two variables (one independent, one dependent), with one variable studied at a time. Do not treat them as opposites.

### The clinical approach

The **clinical** approach includes **case studies, dream analysis, free association** and similar techniques: the methods of Freud and the other clinicians. Cattell rejected it as **highly subjective**, because it does not produce data that can be verified or quantified (Schultz makes the same criticism in Chapters 2 and 5). His verdict on the clinician was memorable: his heart is in the right place, but his thinking is a little fuzzy (1959).

### The multivariate approach

Cattell's choice was the **multivariate** approach, which yields highly specific data through the sophisticated statistical procedure of **factor analysis**. He favoured two forms of it:

- The **R technique** collects a large amount of data from a **group of people** and correlates all the scores to identify personality factors or traits. This is factor analysis across people.
- The **P technique** collects a large amount of data from a **single person** over a **long period**. This is factor analysis within one individual, across occasions.

A clinical example makes the difference concrete. If you gave 2,000 college students the same 50 measures once and factor-analysed the results, you would be using the R technique. If you measured one patient's mood, sleep, stress and social contact every day for six months and factor-analysed that patient's data, you would be using the P technique, and the factors you found would describe how that one person's states hang together.

> **Trap.** **R = many people, once** (group data); **P = one person, many times** (single subject over time). The letters do not stand for "research" and "personality". The P technique is the nearest Cattell came to Allport's idiographic interest in the single case.

| Approach | Method | Cattell's verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Bivariate (univariate)** | Laboratory experiment: one independent variable, one dependent variable | Rigorous and quantitative but too restrictive for personality |
| **Clinical** | Case studies, dream analysis, free association | Subjective; no verifiable or quantifiable data |
| **Multivariate** | Factor analysis (R technique, P technique) | His chosen method; highly specific data |

### A sample of research findings

Cattell and his associates ran **hundreds** of factor-analytic studies. Schultz selects a handful, plus some more recent research using the 16 PF.

**Heredity and environment (Cattell, 1982).** Cattell factor-analysed 16 PF scores from **3,000 males aged 12 to 18**. He concluded that three source traits were determined **primarily by heredity**:

- **Factor F** (serious versus happy-go-lucky),
- **Factor I** (tough-minded versus tender-minded or sensitive), and
- **Factor Q3** (uncontrolled versus controlled).

Three others were determined **primarily by environment**:

- **Factor E** (submissive versus dominant),
- **Factor G** (expedient versus conscientious), and
- **Factor Q4** (relaxed versus tense).

A memory hook: the **heritable** three are about **temperament and self-control** (F, I, Q3); the **environmental** three are about **social position, conscience and tension** (E, G, Q4), things you might expect upbringing and circumstance to shape. Note that the sample was all **male** adolescents, which limits generalisation.

**Marital stability (Cattell & Nesselroade, 1967).** The participants were married couples classified as having either a **stable** or an **unstable** marriage. The criterion for instability was whether the couple had taken **steps toward dissolving the marriage**. Factor analysis showed that marital stability **could be predicted** from 16 PF scores: partners in **stable marriages had similar personality traits**, whereas partners in **unstable marriages had very different traits**. In the old debate about whether "birds of a feather" or "opposites attract", this study comes down on the side of similarity.

**Faking (Simon, 2007).** Like other personality tests, the 16 PF can be **distorted or faked**. College students, prisoners and substance abusers took the test **twice**: first answering honestly, then answering so as to present themselves favourably. The two sets of answers **differed significantly**. This confirms Cattell's own worry about Q-data.

**Translation problems and successes.**

- In **South Africa**, translating the test into **Tshivenda** changed the meanings of the items so much that the responses were **invalid**; as with some other tests and languages, a literal translation proved impossible (**Van Eeden & Mantsha, 2007**).
- A satisfactorily translated **Greek** version clearly distinguished the personality characteristics of Greek **elementary and preschool teachers** from the norms for the Greek population as a whole (**Roussi-Vergou, Angelosopoulou, & Zafiropoulou, 2009; Roussi-Vergou & Zafiropoulou, 2011**).
- A **Turkish** translation was found to be a **valid** measure of personality differences (**Aksu et al., 2010**).

The lesson is that the test travels well when translation captures meaning, and fails when it can only render words.

**Highlights: Research on Cattell's ideas.** Studies show that the 16 PF:

| Finding | Source in this section |
|---|---|
| Predicts marital stability | Cattell & Nesselroade (1967) |
| Can be faked by people who want to look good | Simon (2007) |
| Works across many cultures, though literal translation is impossible in some languages | Tshivenda, Greek, Turkish studies |
| Shows some source traits to be mainly inherited and others mainly environmental | Cattell (1982); the B and H estimates |
| Identifies 16 source traits | Cattell (1965) |
| Serves research, clinical diagnosis and job-success prediction | Uses of the 16 PF |

## 8.11 Reflections on Cattell's Theory

### The criticism: subjectivity inside an "objective" method

Cattell had a legitimate claim that factor analysis is objective and precise. Critics answer that **subjectivity can enter at several points**, because the method requires a series of human decisions:

1. **Data collection.** The researcher must choose which tests to use and which aspects of behaviour to measure. Factors can only emerge from what was measured in the first place.
2. **Analysis.** The researcher must choose which factor-analytic technique to apply and what level of statistical significance to accept.
3. **Naming.** Once factors emerge, someone has to label them. If a label is ambiguous, it may misrepresent what the factor actually is.

Schultz is careful about what this criticism does and does not say. It does **not** identify a flaw inside Cattell's theory itself; it identifies a **potential for subjective error in the factor-analytic approach**. But it may explain a real problem: other researchers have had **difficulty replicating** Cattell's findings and confirming his **16 source traits**. This is exactly the objection Eysenck raised (8.13), and the later finding that only five or so factors replicate robustly (8.15) is the same objection seen from the other side.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a criticism of Cattell?" The genuine criticisms are subjectivity in choosing measures, choosing techniques and significance levels, and naming factors, plus the difficulty of replicating the 16 factors. Lack of empirical data is **not** a criticism of Cattell; if anything his data are massive. Over-reliance on clinical case material is also **not** a criticism of him; he rejected the clinical approach.

### Cattell's isolation and his defence

Cattell knew that he had failed to win most psychologists over, and he defended factor analysis as the **only** worthwhile way to study personality. At **85** he repeated the point, criticising contemporary psychologists for not mastering and using factor analysis and regretting that his work stood apart from mainstream personality theory. He remained sure that his approach would one day predict human behaviour as precisely as **astronomers predict the movements of the planets** (1974a, 1974b, 1990, 1993).

### Contributions

Current opinion has come round to Cattell. He is generally regarded as **the father of the personality trait approach** and as **one of the most influential psychologists of the 20th century** (Denis, 2009; Revelle, 2009; Tucker, 2009). Whatever happens to his specific 16 factors, the trait approach and the study of **genetic influences** on personality, both of which he launched, remain central to research, as the rest of the chapter shows.

| Contributions | Criticisms |
|---|---|
| Rigorous, quantitative, data-heavy approach (50+ measures per person) | Subjective choices in measures, techniques and significance levels |
| The 16 PF: widely used, ~40 languages, research, clinic and workplace | Factor labels can be ambiguous |
| Early twin and sibling estimates of heritability | 16 source traits hard for others to replicate |
| "Father of the trait approach"; APA Gold Medal (1997) | Isolated from mainstream theorising during his lifetime |

## 8.12 Behavioural Genetics

The chapter now widens from Cattell to the general question he helped open. **Behavioural genetics** is the field that studies the **relationship between genetic (hereditary) factors and personality traits**. Schultz's position is that, whatever method is used to assess or investigate personality, a **significant genetic component** has to be taken into account.

**Allport and Cattell** were among the first to propose that inherited factors shape personality and that they rank **in importance alongside environmental factors**. The researchers in the rest of the chapter, Eysenck above all, pursued that causal link between genetic inheritance and personality more forcefully.

The basic designs you will meet repeatedly from here on are:

- **Twin comparisons:** identical (**monozygotic**, MZ) twins share all their genes; fraternal (**dizygotic**, DZ) twins share about half, like ordinary siblings. If MZ twins are more alike than DZ twins on a trait, genes probably contribute.
- **Twins reared apart:** if MZ twins raised in different homes still resemble each other closely, shared upbringing cannot be the explanation.
- **Adoption studies:** if adopted children resemble their **biological** parents more than their **adoptive** parents, genes are doing more work than the home.

> **Trap.** Behavioural genetics does not claim that personality is entirely inherited. It estimates the **share** of differences between people that is associated with genetic differences. Schultz's final verdict (8.19) is that inheritance may account for **as much as 50 percent**, with environment still essential.

## 8.13 Hans Eysenck (1916 to 1997)

### Resigned to psychology

Hans Eysenck was born in **Berlin** in **1916**. In **1934**, after **Adolf Hitler** came to power, he left Germany for **England**. He wanted to study **physics** at the **University of London**, but was told that he lacked the necessary academic background. Disappointed, he asked whether there was any other science he could take. The answer, as he later told it with some irony, was that there was always psychology; he had to ask what that was, was assured he would like it, and enrolled in a subject whose scientific standing was, he thought, shakier than his advisers realised (1980).

More than 40 years later, by then enormously successful, Eysenck was asked whether he had ever regretted the choice. Often, he said, but he had become **resigned** to it. The anecdote matters because it explains the tone of his career: he came into psychology as a would-be physicist and spent his life trying to make it as rigorous as the science he had wanted to study.

### A prolific record

Eysenck's output was extraordinary even by Cattell's standard: **79 books**, some written for the general public, and **1,097 journal articles**. At his death he was the **most frequently cited psychologist in the world** (Farley, 2000). Unfortunately for historians, his **wife destroyed all his personal and professional papers** after he died (Harris, 2011).

He developed several assessment instruments:

- the **Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI)**,
- the **Eysenck Personality Profiler**,
- the **Maudsley Medical Questionnaire**, and
- the **Maudsley Personality Inventory (MPI)**.

His work was pivotal in two ways: in supporting the role of **inheritance** in personality, and in **integrating the scientific study of personality into psychology as a whole** (Corr, 2007; Revelle & Oehlberg, 2008; Rose, 2010).

*Classic background:* Eysenck spent his career at the Maudsley Hospital and Institute of Psychiatry in London, and outside personality theory he is remembered for a provocative **1952** review arguing that there was little evidence that psychotherapy helped neurotic patients more than the passage of time, and for championing **behaviour therapy** in Britain. Indian papers occasionally attach these to his name.

> **Trap.** Eysenck was **German-born** and worked in **London**; Cattell was **English-born** and worked in **America**. Both originally wanted (or trained in) **physics**: Cattell took a physics and chemistry degree; Eysenck was refused entry to physics and took psychology instead.

## 8.14 The Dimensions of Personality

Eysenck spent most of his working life at the **University of London's Maudsley Hospital and Institute of Psychiatry**, researching the measurement of personality. He agreed with Cattell that personality is made up of **traits, or factors, derived by factor analysis**. But he was also a **critic** of factor analysis and of Cattell's research in particular, on the grounds already met in 8.11: the **potential subjectivity** of the technique and the **difficulty of replicating** Cattell's results. His solution was to use factor analysis to uncover traits but to **supplement** it with personality tests and **experimental studies** covering a wide range of variables. Where Cattell let the statistics speak almost alone, Eysenck tied his factors to laboratory experiments and biology.

### A joint effort

Eysenck developed many of his questionnaires together with his second wife, **Sybil Eysenck**, who held a Ph.D. from the University of London (Furnham, Eysenck, & Saklofske, 2010). The **Eysenck Personality Inventory (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1963)** took **12 years** of joint work and **20 factor analyses**. Hans Eysenck wrote that although the inventory carried both their names, it was largely a monument to her skill, patience and endurance (1980). Schultz remarks that few scientists in any field have acknowledged a spouse's research contribution so openly.

### Three dimensions of personality

The outcome of this work was a theory built on **three dimensions**, each defined as a **combination of traits or factors**. Eysenck's dimensions can be thought of as **superfactors** (Eysenck, 1990a, 1990b; Eysenck & Eysenck, 1985):

- **E: extraversion versus introversion**
- **N: neuroticism versus emotional stability**
- **P: psychoticism versus impulse control (or superego functioning)**

Eysenck pointed out that **extraversion and neuroticism** have been recognised as basic elements of personality since the **ancient Greek philosophers**, and he suggested (Eysenck, 1997) that versions of these same dimensions turn up on nearly **every personality assessment device ever constructed**. The **EPI** has been used successfully in translation in nearly **40 countries**, as different as **Italy and Kuwait** (Abdel-Khalek, 2012; Dazzi, 2011).

Each dimension is a bundle of narrower traits. Table 8.4 lists them; a person who scores high on the traits of the E dimension is classified as an extravert, and a person who scores low as an introvert, and similarly for N and P.

**Table 8.4** Traits that make up Eysenck's three dimensions

| Extraversion / introversion (E) | Neuroticism / emotional stability (N) | Psychoticism / impulse control (P) |
|---|---|---|
| Sociable | Anxious | Aggressive |
| Lively | Depressed | Cold |
| Active | Prone to guilt | Egocentric |
| Assertive | Low in self-esteem | Impersonal |
| Seeks sensation | Tense | Impulsive |
| Carefree | Irrational | Antisocial |
| Dominant | Shy | Creative |
| Venturesome | Moody | Tough-minded |

> **Trap.** Two placements in Table 8.4 catch people. **Impulsive** is listed under **psychoticism**, not extraversion (although Schultz's prose does describe extraverts as impulsive), and **shy** is listed under **neuroticism**, not introversion. **Creative** sits under **psychoticism**, which surprises students but matches the research on P below. Also note the name of the low pole of P: **impulse control (superego functioning)**, not "normality" or "sanity".

*Classic background: trait level versus type level.* Eysenck organised personality as a **hierarchy** with four levels. At the bottom are **specific responses** (what a person does on one occasion: chatting to a stranger at a bus stop). Responses that recur in similar situations form **habitual responses** (regularly chatting to strangers). Correlated habitual responses form **traits** (sociability, liveliness, activity). Correlated traits form a **type** or superfactor (extraversion). So in Eysenck's vocabulary, the entries in Table 8.4 are **traits**, and E, N and P are **types** (dimensions). This is why Schultz's review question 11 speaks of "the three personality types proposed by Eysenck" while the text calls them dimensions or superfactors: they are the same thing, at the top level of the hierarchy.

*Classic background: two dimensions first, then three.* Eysenck's early work (for example *Dimensions of Personality*, 1947, and the **Maudsley Personality Inventory**) dealt with only **two** dimensions, **E and N**. The **EPI** also measured **E and N** (with a lie scale). **Psychoticism** was added later, and the questionnaire that measured all three was the **Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ, 1975)**. Older keys that describe "Eysenck's two-factor theory" or "the E and N model" are describing this earlier stage. Crossing the two original dimensions also gave Eysenck a link to the ancient temperaments mentioned above: **unstable introvert = melancholic**, **unstable extravert = choleric**, **stable extravert = sanguine**, **stable introvert = phlegmatic**.

> **Trap.** Eysenck's model is often abbreviated **PEN** (psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism). If an older key offers "two dimensions" for Eysenck, it means **E and N** before P was added; the 11th edition gives **three**. And Eysenck's N is a top-level **dimension**, whereas Cattell treated neuroticism as a **surface trait** (8.3).

### Stability over time

Eysenck's traits and dimensions tend to stay **stable across the life span**, even though people's social and environmental circumstances change. An introverted child tends to remain introverted through adolescence into adulthood (Ganiban, Saudino, Ulbricht, Neiderhiser, & Reiss, 2008). Studies in **England and the Scandinavian countries** confirm this stability, especially for **extraversion and neuroticism** (Billstedt et al., 2014; Gale, Booth, Mottus, Kuh, & Deary, 2013).

### The role of intelligence

Eysenck also researched intelligence extensively. He did **not** list intelligence as a personality dimension, but he regarded it as an important **influence** on personality. A person with a high IQ of **120**, he noted, is likely to have a more complex, many-sided personality than a person with an IQ of **80**. His research suggested that roughly **80 percent of intelligence is inherited**, leaving about **20 percent** to social and environmental forces (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1985).

> **Trap.** Intelligence is a **source trait (Factor B)** for Cattell but **not one of the dimensions** for Eysenck. Both men put the heritability of intelligence at about **80 percent**.

### Extraversion

Most people can describe the typical extravert and introvert without help. **Extraverts** are oriented toward the outside world, prefer the company of others, and tend to be sociable, impulsive, adventurous, assertive and dominant.

**Research findings.**

- High scorers on extraversion on the EPI experience **more pleasant emotions** and are **happier** than low scorers (Fisher & Francis, 2013; Holder & Klassen, 2010; Lucas & Fujita, 2000).
- **Extraverted businessmen** performed **difficult tasks** much better than introverted businessmen (Campbell, Alana, Davalos, McCabe, & Troup, 2011).

**The biological explanation: cortical arousal.** Eysenck wanted to know how extraverts and introverts differ biologically and genetically, and his answer is one of the most examined ideas in the chapter. He found that **extraverts have a lower base level of cortical arousal** than introverts.

Follow the reasoning step by step. Everyone functions best at some moderate level of arousal. An extravert's cortex starts out **under-aroused**, so the extravert **needs and actively seeks** excitement and stimulation (parties, noise, novelty, risk) to bring arousal up to a comfortable level. An introvert's cortex is **already highly aroused**, so extra stimulation pushes it past the comfortable level; the introvert therefore **avoids** excitement and stimulation (Eysenck, 1990b). The behaviour that looks like a social preference is, on this account, a way of regulating arousal.

A prediction follows: introverts should **react more strongly** to sensory stimulation. Studies bear this out: introverts show **greater sensitivity to low-intensity stimuli** and have **lower pain thresholds** than extraverts. Picture two students in a hostel: one studies happily with music playing and friends chatting; the other cannot read a page unless the room is silent. Eysenck's theory says the second student's cortex is already loud enough.

The evidence has a qualification Schultz wants you to note. Later research confirms that extraverts and introverts **respond differently** to sensory stimulation, but the evidence that these differences are **caused by** differences in cortical arousal is **less convincing** (Bullock & Gilliland, 1993; Hagemann & Naumann, 2009; Stelmack, 1997). Nonetheless, as Eysenck predicted, the differences are **genetically based**.

> **Trap.** The direction is what students reverse. **Extraverts = LOW** base cortical arousal, so they **seek** stimulation. **Introverts = HIGH** base cortical arousal, so they **avoid** stimulation, are **more sensitive** to weak stimuli, and have **LOWER pain thresholds** (they feel pain sooner). An option saying "introverts have higher pain thresholds" is wrong.

*Classic background:* in his 1967 book on the biological basis of personality, Eysenck located the arousal differences behind E in the **ascending reticular activating system (ARAS)** and the reactivity behind N in the **limbic system** ("visceral brain"). Schultz's text speaks only of cortical arousal for E and of the sympathetic nervous system for N.

### Neuroticism

**Neurotics**, as Table 8.4 shows, are anxious, depressed, tense, irrational and moody, and may have low self-esteem and be prone to guilt. Eysenck believed **neuroticism is largely inherited**, a product of genetics rather than of learning or experience.

**Research findings.**

- **Scollon and Diener (2006)** followed Americans aged **16 to 70** over **2 years**. Growing satisfaction with **work and social relationships** went with **lower neuroticism** and **higher extraversion**.
- **Smillie, Yeo, Furnham, and Jackson (2006)**, in **Australia**, found that high scorers on neuroticism (EPI) **outperformed** low scorers when the work environment was **fast-paced and stressful**. Neurotics seemed to do best in busy situations that forced them to work harder. This is a counter-intuitive finding and therefore a likely MCQ.
- **Chamorro-Premuzic, Furnham, and Petrides (2006)**, in **England**, found that people high in neuroticism scored **lower in verbal ability**.
- **Crowe, Andel, Pedersen, Fratiglioni, and Gatz (2006)**, in **Sweden**, found that people high in neuroticism in **middle age** were much more likely to show **cognitive impairment** when retested **25 years later**.

**The biological explanation: an overreactive sympathetic nervous system.** People high in neuroticism show **greater activity in the brain areas that control the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system**, the body's alarm system, which responds to stress or danger by speeding breathing and heart rate, increasing blood flow to the muscles, and releasing adrenaline. Eysenck argued that in neurotics this system **overreacts even to mild stressors**, producing **chronic hypersensitivity**. The result is heightened emotionality in almost any difficult situation: a neurotic person may be badly shaken by a minor criticism or a late bus that others shrug off. According to Eysenck these differences in **biological reactivity are innate**: people are **genetically predisposed** toward neuroticism or toward emotional stability.

> **Trap.** Match each dimension to its biology. **E = cortical arousal** (low in extraverts). **N = sympathetic (autonomic) nervous system reactivity** (high in neurotics). **P = speculatively, male hormones** (below). Do not attach the arousal explanation to neuroticism.

### Psychoticism

High scorers on **psychoticism** are **aggressive, antisocial, tough-minded, cold and egocentric**. They have also been found to be **cruel, hostile and insensitive** to the needs and feelings of others. They score low on emotional well-being and have more problems with **alcohol, drug abuse and violent crime** than low scorers (Boduszek, Shevlin, Adamson, & Hyland, 2013; Ciarrochi & Heaven, 2007; Sher, Bartholow, & Wood, 2000).

**Research findings.**

- Paradoxically, high-P scorers can also be **highly creative** (hence "creative" in Table 8.4).
- The evidence suggests a **large genetic component**. Yet **Heaven and Ciarrochi (2006)** found that high scorers had **more authoritarian and controlling parents** than low scorers, which points to a possibly harmful role for the **childhood environment**.
- **Men** generally score higher than women on P, which led Eysenck to suggest that psychoticism may be related to **male hormones**.
- Eysenck speculated that people high on **all three** dimensions may be prone to **criminal behaviour**, but he could cite only **modest** support (Eysenck & Gudjonsson, 1989).
- Research in **China** found a significant positive correlation between **criminal behaviour** and high scores on **both psychoticism and neuroticism** (Huo-Liang, 2006).

**Eysenck's social view.** Eysenck held that society **needs the diversity** that comes from people at all points on all three dimensions, and that an ideal society lets people make the best use of their traits. Some will adapt better than others. A person high in psychoticism, given to hostile and aggressive behaviour, might become emotionally disturbed, or drift into crime, or channel that aggression into a **socially acceptable** outlet such as **coaching college football**.

> **Trap.** Psychoticism in Eysenck's sense is a **personality dimension** found in the normal population, not a diagnosis of psychosis. High P describes a cold, aggressive, impulsive, egocentric and sometimes creative person, closer to antisocial traits than to schizophrenia.

### The primary role of heredity (Eysenck on nature versus nurture)

On the nature-nurture question Eysenck stands much further toward **nature** than Cattell. To Eysenck, traits and dimensions are determined **primarily by heredity**, although the evidence is **stronger for extraversion and neuroticism** than for **psychoticism**. He did not deny environmental and situational influences on personality, such as **family interactions in childhood**, but he believed their effects were **limited** (Eysenck, 1990a).

His evidence came from three lines:

1. **Twin comparisons.** Identical (**monozygotic**) twins are more alike in personality than fraternal (**dizygotic**) twins, **even when the identical twins were raised apart** by different parents in different environments.
2. **Adoption studies.** Adopted children resemble their **biological** parents more than their **adoptive** parents, **even with no contact** with the biological parents.
3. **Cross-cultural consistency.** The three dimensions have been found consistently in **more than 35 nations**, including the United States, England, Australia, Japan, China, Nigeria and Sweden (for example Bouchard, 1985; Eaves, Eysenck, & Martin, 1989; Floderus-Myrhed, Pedersen, & Rasmuson, 1980; Hur, 2009; Martin & Jardine, 1986; Tellegen et al., 1988). If the same three dimensions appear in very different cultures, Eysenck argued, they are unlikely to be cultural products and more likely to be inherited.

> **Trap.** Two country counts sit close together: the **EPI** has been used in translation in **nearly 40 countries**; the **three dimensions** have been **found** in **more than 35 nations**. The Big Five (8.15) were found in **more than 50 nations**, and the gender-difference study covered **55 countries**. Also contrast the nature-nurture positions: **Cattell, one-third heredity overall**; **Eysenck, heredity primary** (strongest for E and N, weakest for P).

### Highlights: research on Eysenck's ideas

| Dimension | Research finding |
|---|---|
| Extraversion | Extraverts experience more pleasant emotions |
| Extraversion | Extraverts have lower base levels of cortical arousal |
| Neuroticism | Neurotics have low self-esteem and strong guilt feelings |
| Neuroticism | Neurotics function well in fast-paced, stressful jobs |
| Neuroticism | Neurotics score lower in verbal ability |
| Psychoticism | High scorers can be cruel, hostile and insensitive |
| Psychoticism | High scorers have more alcohol and drug problems |
| Psychoticism | High scorers are aggressive, antisocial and egocentric |
| All three | Determined primarily by heredity |

## 8.15 Robert McCrae and Paul Costa: The Five-Factor Model

### Why five?

Cattell and Eysenck both used factor analysis, yet one found 16 basic traits and the other 3. Schultz insists this is **not an inherent weakness of the method**; it reflects how each theorist chose to measure personality, which variables went in, and at what level of breadth the factors were extracted. Even so, later researchers were dissatisfied with both answers: **Eysenck had too few dimensions (three)** and **Cattell had too many factors (sixteen)**.

The answer that won out came from **Robert McCrae (born 1949)** and **Paul Costa (born 1942)**, working at the **Gerontology Research Center of the National Institutes of Health** in **Baltimore, Maryland**. From the **1980s** onward they ran an extensive research programme that identified five so-called **robust** or **Big Five** factors (McCrae & Costa, 1985b, 1987):

1. **Neuroticism**
2. **Extraversion**
3. **Openness** (to experience)
4. **Agreeableness**
5. **Conscientiousness**

More than 25 years and hundreds of studies later, McCrae (2011) described the model as marking a turning point in the history of personality psychology, and Schultz agrees that the description is accurate.

**Table 8.5** McCrae and Costa's Big Five factors

| Factor | A high scorer is typically | The opposite pole (low scorer) |
|---|---|---|
| **Neuroticism (N)** | Worrying, insecure, nervous, high-strung | Emotionally stable: calm, secure |
| **Extraversion (E)** | Sociable, talkative, affectionate, fond of fun | Introverted: reserved, quiet |
| **Openness (O)** | Original, creative, independent, daring | Conventional, unadventurous |
| **Agreeableness (A)** | Soft-hearted, good-natured, trusting, courteous | Antagonistic, suspicious, uncooperative |
| **Conscientiousness (C)** | Careful, reliable, organised, hard-working | Careless, unreliable, disorganised |

*Classic background:* the order of factors in English mnemonics is **OCEAN** (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) or **CANOE**. The five factors were first found in **lexical** studies of trait adjectives (Tupes and Christal, 1961; Norman, 1963), and **Lewis Goldberg** popularised the label "Big Five"; McCrae and Costa arrived at the same five through **questionnaires**. In Goldberg's lexical tradition the fifth factor is often called **Intellect**, where McCrae and Costa call it **Openness to experience**. And factor N is frequently reported by its **opposite pole**, **emotional stability**, so a paper may list "emotional stability" as one of the Big Five; it is the same dimension scored in reverse.

> **Trap.** **Neuroticism** and **emotional stability** are the two poles of **one** factor, not two factors. Several studies in this section report results for "emotional stability" (for example Singapore's most valued trait, or the Dutch popularity study); read these as **low neuroticism**. The same reversal is built into Eysenck's N dimension (neuroticism versus emotional stability).

### Measuring the five factors

The factors were confirmed with several different methods: **self-ratings**, **objective tests** and **observers' reports**. McCrae and Costa then built their own instrument, the **NEO Personality Inventory**, whose name is an acronym of the initials of the **first three factors** (**N**euroticism, **E**xtraversion, **O**penness). The test exists in a number of **revised forms**, and a shorter version has been developed for research on the **Internet** (Buchanan, Johnson, & Goldberg, 2005).

Why should the convergence of methods matter? If self-ratings, observers' ratings and tests all yield the same five factors, the factors are less likely to be artefacts of one way of measuring, and can be trusted as genuine distinguishing features of personality.

Other researchers, following McCrae and Costa, devised **adjective checklists** as quicker measures: participants pick the words that best describe themselves. One checklist uses **100 adjectives**; another only **40**.

Despite these alternatives, the **NEO remains the most frequently used** measure of the Big Five. Like all self-report inventories, however, it can be **distorted** by people who deliberately try to appear psychologically well adjusted. A candidate taking the NEO for a hospital job, for example, may push down neuroticism and push up conscientiousness.

*Classic background: NEO-PI versus NEO-PI-R.* The name records the history. McCrae and Costa's first inventory measured only **N, E and O**; **agreeableness and conscientiousness** were added later, which is why the acronym has only three letters. The **NEO-PI** (1985) was followed by the **NEO-PI-R** (Revised, 1992), which has **240 items** and measures each of the five domains through **six facets**, **30 facets** in all (for example, **depression** is a facet of N and **positive emotions** a facet of E, the two facets that appear in the well-being research below). The **NEO-FFI** is a **60-item** short form that gives only the five domain scores. A later revision, the **NEO-PI-3**, also exists.

> **Trap.** "NEO" stands for **Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness**, the first three factors only. An option expanding it to all five, or to "New Experience Orientation", is wrong. The **R** in NEO-PI-R means **Revised**.

### Origin and overlap of factors (the five-factor position on nature versus nurture)

**Where do the factors come from?** Twin studies found that **four of the five** factors have a **stronger hereditary** component: **neuroticism, extraversion, openness and conscientiousness**. **Agreeableness** alone showed a **stronger environmental** component (Bergeman et al., 1993; Pedersen, Plomin, McClearn, & Friberg, 1988). Two further pieces of evidence push the model toward **nature**: the factors appear across very different cultures (next subsection), which Schultz reads as support for a genetic component, and the chapter summary calls them **biologically based**.

So the five-factor position on nature versus nurture is: **mainly nature for N, E, O and C**, **more nurture for A**, with environment still mattering for all.

*Classic background:* in their later **Five-Factor Theory**, McCrae and Costa describe the traits as **basic tendencies** rooted in biology, and they attribute the typical age changes in the traits mainly to **intrinsic maturation** rather than to life experience. This is a stronger nativist claim than the 11th-edition text makes, but it is the logic behind the cross-cultural argument.

**How do the factors overlap with other models?**

- McCrae and Costa's **extraversion and neuroticism** closely resemble **Eysenck's E and N**.
- **Agreeableness and conscientiousness** may represent the **low end of Eysenck's psychoticism** dimension, that is, the **impulse control** pole. (A person low in P is controlled and considerate; a person high in A and C is the same.)
- **Openness** correlates highly and positively with **intelligence**.
- **Agreeableness** correlates with **Adler's social interest** (Chapter 4).

> **Trap.** The heritability pattern is "four strong, one weak". The odd one out is **agreeableness** (more environmental), not openness or conscientiousness. And **A plus C** together map onto the **low** end of Eysenck's P, not onto his N.

### Cross-cultural consistency

The five factors have been found consistently in **Eastern as well as Western** cultures, which also supports a genetic component. McCrae and Costa described them as a **common human structure of personality** that transcends cultural differences (1997, p. 515).

They have been found in **more than 50 nations** (Allik et al., 2009; Heine & Buchtel, 2009; Ispas, Iliescu, Ilie, & Johnson, 2014). Schultz's examples span Asia (**India**, China, Japan, Korea, the Philippines), the Middle East (Israel, Lebanon, Turkey), Western Europe (Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Denmark), Eastern Europe (the Czech Republic, Romania, Russia) and North America (Canada, and the United States, where the factors appear in both native-born and Hispanic residents).

There is an important limit. The countries in which the five factors have been measured tend to be **urban, literate and well-educated** societies. In an extensive study of a **small, isolated, largely illiterate tribal group in a remote part of Bolivia**, **no evidence** of the five-factor structure was found (**Gurven, von Rueden, Massenkoff, Kaplan, & Lero Vie, 2013**). The universality claim, in other words, may really be a claim about modern, schooled populations.

> **Trap.** "The Big Five have been found in every culture studied" is **false** on Schultz's account: the **Bolivian** tribal study (Gurven et al., 2013) found no evidence of them. A "which is NOT true" item will often hinge on this exception.

### The relative value of the factors in different cultures

The same factors appear in virtually all urban cultures, but cultures differ sharply in **how important and desirable** each factor is thought to be:

| Country or region | Factor valued most |
|---|---|
| **Australia** | Extraversion and agreeableness |
| **Japan** | Conscientiousness (above all others, even above emotional stability) |
| **Hong Kong** and **India** | Agreeableness |
| **Singapore** | Emotional stability |
| **Venezuela** | Extraversion |
| Chile, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Turkey, the United States | No single factor stands out |

In Japan, for instance, being conscientious matters more than being extraverted, agreeable, open or even emotionally stable. Overall, **Europeans and Americans** scored **higher in extraversion and openness** and **lower in agreeableness** than **Asians and Africans** (Allik & McCrae, 2004; McCrae & Terracciano, 2005).

> **Trap.** For an Indian paper, remember **India and Hong Kong value agreeableness most**. Japan values **conscientiousness**; Singapore **emotional stability**; Venezuela **extraversion**; Australia **extraversion and agreeableness**. The **United States** is in the "no single factor" list, not the extraversion list.

### Gender differences

The sex differences in the five factors are consistent. A study across **55 countries**, Eastern and Western, found that **women** reported **higher** levels of **neuroticism, extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness** than men (**Schmitt, Realo, Voracek, & Allik, 2008**). The surprising part is where the differences were largest: in **prosperous, egalitarian nations**, where women had **more** opportunity for education and employment. You might have expected equality to shrink the gaps; instead it widened them.

> **Trap.** Women score higher on **four** factors (N, E, A, C), **including extraversion**; openness is the one not listed. The gaps are **largest**, not smallest, in prosperous and egalitarian countries.

### How people view themselves and others

There is a cross-cultural consistency in how people rate their own and others' personalities. People see **themselves** as **more neurotic and more open to experience** than others see them, and they see **others** as **more conscientious** than they see themselves (**Allik, Realo, Mottus, Borkenau, Kuppens, & Hrebickova, 2010**). Put simply: we know our own worries and private enthusiasms from the inside, and we see other people's tidy, dutiful surfaces from the outside.

### Stability of the factors over time

This is the subsection where the findings can look contradictory, and Schultz warns you to check two things in each study: the **country** and, above all, the **age range** and the **time span** covered.

The five factors are found in **children** as well as adults.

| Study | Sample and span | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Costa & McCrae (1988)** | Same people over **6 years** | High stability for **all five** factors; agreeable children tend to stay agreeable as adults |
| **Viken, Rose, Kaprio, & Koskenvuo (1994)** | **Finnish twins aged 18 to 59** | High stability of **E and N** for both sexes across that **40-year age span** |
| **Carmichael & McGue (1994)** | Americans from late adolescence into adulthood | **Modest** but significant stability for **E and N** |
| **McCrae et al. (2002)** | **American and Belgian** adolescents over **4 years** | **E, A and C stable**; **openness increased** in both sexes |
| **Von Dras & Siegler (1997)** | College graduates tested as students and **20 years later** | Significant positive correlation for **extraversion**; extraverts still more sociable at midlife |
| **Hampson & Goldberg (2006)** | US adults measured **40 years apart** | **Extraversion and conscientiousness** the **most stable** |
| **McCrae et al. (2004a, 2004b)** | **Czech Republic and Russia**, adolescence to adulthood | **N, E and O declined**; **A and C rose** |
| **Allemand, Zimprich, & Hertzog (2007)** | **German** adults in their **40s**, then their **60s** | **Neuroticism declined** |
| **Debast et al. (2014)** | Large-scale research review | **N, E and O decrease** by the **60s**; **A and C increase** with age |

Two patterns emerge. First, **rank-order stability** is high: people who are more extraverted than their peers at one age tend to remain so later. Second, there is a typical **direction of change** with age, toward what one might call maturity: **less neuroticism, more agreeableness and more conscientiousness**, with some decline in extraversion and openness after young adulthood. Adolescents' openness, on the other hand, may still be rising (McCrae et al., 2002).

> **Trap.** "Stable" does not mean "unchanging". The same factors can keep their rank order and still shift in average level. The age trend to remember is **A and C up; N (and E and O) down**, especially by the 60s. The **40-year** study (Hampson & Goldberg, 2006) found **E and C** most stable; the **Finnish twin** study's **40-year age span** concerned **E and N**.

### Predicting changes over time

**Graziano, Jensen-Campbell, and Sullivan-Logan (1998)** asked **preschool teachers** to predict what their **3- to 6-year-old** pupils would be like **20 years later**. The teachers' expectations, based on watching the children, **corresponded** with the pupils' later **Big Five scores**. Evidently the teachers assumed that preschool behaviour was closely tied to adult behaviour, and they were right.

Schultz raises a question this study cannot settle: do such expectations lead teachers and parents to **reinforce** certain behaviours, thereby **strengthening** children's genetically based tendencies? Do adults treat extraverted children differently from introverted ones in ways that magnify the difference they were born with? If so, genes and environment are not separate influences but work in the same direction.

### Highlights: research on McCrae and Costa's ideas

- **N, E, O and C** have a strong **hereditary** component.
- **Agreeableness** has a strong **environmental** component.
- All five factors have been found in **diverse cultures**.
- Most factors remain **stable to some degree** over the life span.
- **Women** report higher **N, E, A and C** than men.
- We tend to see **others** as **more conscientious and less neurotic** than ourselves.

### Emotional correlates

#### Emotional well-being and happiness

- **Extraversion** is **positively** related to emotional well-being, and **neuroticism negatively** (for example Heller, Watson, & Hies, 2004; Lischetzke & Eid, 2006). Researchers concluded that people **high in E and low in N** are **genetically predisposed** to emotional stability (Costa & McCrae, 1984; Watson, Clark, McIntyre, & Hamaker, 1992).
- College students high in extraversion **coped with everyday stress** better and were more likely to **seek social support** (Amirkhan, Risinger, & Swickert, 1995).
- At the level of facets, the **depression** facet of neuroticism and the **positive emotions (cheerfulness)** facet of extraversion were the **most consistent predictors** of general life satisfaction and well-being (Schimmack, Oishi, Furr, & Funder, 2004).
- Studies of teenagers in the **United States, Brazil and Italy** confirmed that extraversion goes with **happiness, optimism and life satisfaction**; openness, conscientiousness and low neuroticism also contributed to positive feelings (Bassi et al., 2014; Suldo et al., 2014; Zanon et al., 2014).
- People high in **agreeableness and conscientiousness** showed greater emotional well-being (McCrae & Costa, 1991), and high-agreeableness people were found to be cooperative, helpful, altruistic, honest and selfless (Digman, 1990; John, 1990).
- Among workers in **China**, **extraversion, conscientiousness and low neuroticism** were related to subjective well-being (Zhai et al., 2013).
- **High neuroticism** is associated with **psychological distress** (DeRaad, 2000; Larsen & Kasimatis, 1991; Ormel & Wohlfarth, 1991), and with proneness to **depression, anxiety, substance abuse and self-blame** (Jorm, 1987; Kotov, Gamez, Schmidt, & Watson, 2010; Parkes, 1986).
- A large **German** study found that **high neuroticism and high openness** carried a **greater risk of suicide**, while the **lowest risk** was in people **high in extraversion and conscientiousness** (Bluml et al., 2013).

> **Trap.** Openness is usually a "good" factor in these lists, but in the **German suicide-risk** study it went with **higher** risk, alongside neuroticism. Protective factors there were **E and C**.

#### Popularity and success

- American college students high in **extraversion** enjoyed **higher status and prominence** among peers (Anderson, John, Keltner, & Kring, 2001). Extraverts rated social situations as positive **only if** the situation was pleasant, which suggests that **positive emotion** is central to the factor (Lucas & Diener, 2001).
- Among **fifth- to eighth-graders**, high **conscientiousness** went with **peer acceptance**, more and better friendships, and being **less often the target of aggression** (Jensen-Campbell & Malcolm, 2007).
- Over **4 years** of college, **extraverts** experienced more **positive events** (a good grade, a pay rise, marriage), and high-**neuroticism** students more **negative events** (illness, weight gain, traffic tickets, rejection by graduate school) (**Magnus, Diener, Fujita, & Pavot, 1993**). Personality, it seems, partly selects the events that happen to us.
- American adults aged **25 to 74**: everyday stressors had **much stronger negative emotional effects** on high-**neuroticism** people (Mroczek & Almeida, 2004).
- A **Swedish** study of identical and fraternal twins reared together and apart confirmed, **for women**, that **extraversion and openness** predict more **positive** life events and **neuroticism** more **negative** ones (Saudino, Pedersen, Lichtenstein, McClearn, & Plomin, 1997).
- **Dutch** teenagers high in **extraversion, emotional stability and agreeableness** were judged by classmates to be **more popular and likeable** (Van der Linden et al., 2010).
- In the US, people high in **extraversion and agreeableness** were rated by others as **more physically attractive** (Meier, Robinson, Carter, & Hinsz, 2010).

### Behavioural correlates

#### Personal characteristics

- People high in **openness** have wide intellectual interests and seek challenges; they are more likely to **change jobs**, **try different careers**, and expect varied experiences (McCrae & Costa, 1985a, 1985b). People high in **both openness and extraversion** are more likely to be **self-employed**.
- **Extraverts** are more **active** in retirement and **more satisfied** with it (Lockenhoff, Terracciano, & Costa, 2009; Robinson, Demetre, & Corney, 2010; Shane, Nicolaou, Cherkas, & Spector, 2010).
- High **conscientiousness** goes with being reliable, responsible, punctual, efficient and dependable, and with **better school grades** (Back, Schmukle, & Egloff, 2006; Chowdhury & Amin, 2006; Wagerman & Funder, 2007). **British** university students high in C were more organised, self-disciplined and achievement-oriented in **planning future goals** (Conner & Abraham, 2001).
- **Agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness** all correlated positively with **college academic performance** (Poropat, 2009), and people high in these three were also rated as **better parents** and **better at coping with stress** (Carver & Connor-Smith, 2010; Prinzie, Stams, Dekovic, Reijntjes, & Belsky, 2009).
- **At work**, high-C people set **high goals** and strive for them, initiate desirable work behaviours, and receive **high performance ratings**. Conscientiousness is a **valid predictor of job performance** for **professional, police, managerial, sales and skilled labour** jobs (Barrick & Mount, 1996; Barrick, Mount, & Strauss, 1993; Stewart, Carson, & Cardy, 1996).

*Classic background:* the Barrick and Mount line of research (beginning with a 1991 meta-analysis) is the basis for the common exam statement that **conscientiousness is the most consistent Big Five predictor of job performance across occupations**.

#### Physical health

Conscientiousness dominates the health findings.

- High-C people are likely to be **healthier and live longer** (Friedman & Martin, 2011).
- Conscientious **smokers** were less likely to **smoke at home**, suggesting greater awareness of the risk to themselves and to others in the house (Hampson, Andrews, Barckley, Lichtenstein, & Lee, 2000).
- American college students high in C are more likely to **wear seat belts, exercise, sleep enough, and eat more fruit and vegetables** (Raynor & Levine, 2009).
- Adolescents and young adults with **diabetes** who were higher in C **sought more information** about managing the condition and were **more diligent about self-care** (Skinner, Hampson, & Fife-Schau, 2002).
- More conscientious **British** students were **far less likely to report hypochondriacal complaints** (Ferguson, 2000).
- In a telephone survey of American adults, higher-C people took **greater responsibility** for healthy behaviour (Tucker, Elliott, & Klein, 2006).

Other factors appear too.

- In people with **attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder**, symptoms (hyperactivity, impulsivity, inattention, cognitive and behavioural disorganisation) were significantly **lower** in those **higher in agreeableness** (Nigg et al., 2002).
- Among patients with **chronic renal insufficiency** (a kidney disorder) followed for **4 years**, those higher in **neuroticism** on the NEO had a **mortality rate 37.5 percent higher** than those lower in neuroticism (**Christensen et al., 2002**).
- The **neuroticism** findings are mixed. High N predicted **poorer physical health and well-being** in old age, yet was also associated with **greater longevity**: neurotics may live longer, but with more health problems and less happiness about it (Eysenck, 2009; Friedman, Kern, & Reynolds, 2010). High-N people also appear to show **fewer** of the cognitive declines associated with **dementia** in old age (Williams, Suchy, & Kraybill, 2010).
- Two large American studies linked **higher extraversion** to an **increased risk of stroke**; **high conscientiousness** to a **lower risk of death from stroke and coronary heart disease**; and **high neuroticism** to a greater likelihood of **heart disease** (Jokela, Pulkki-Raback, Elovainio, & Kivimaki, 2014; Lee et al., 2014).
- In **Australia and Finland**, people high in **E, A and C** got **more and better sleep**; high-**N** people reported **worse sleep** (Hintsanen et al., 2014).
- In **Korea**, **overweight-to-obese men** scored **high in openness** and **low in conscientiousness**; **overweight women** scored **low in neuroticism and openness** (Shim et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** Neuroticism's link with cognitive decline is contradictory across studies in this chapter. Eysenck's section reports **Crowe et al. (2006, Sweden)**: high N in middle age predicted **more** cognitive impairment 25 years later. This section reports **Williams, Suchy, and Kraybill (2010)**: high N went with **fewer** dementia-type declines. Similarly, high N predicts **worse health** but **greater longevity**. Name the study before choosing an answer. The renal study's figure is **37.5 percent higher mortality** for high N.

#### Addictive and criminal behaviour

- Adults **low in conscientiousness and agreeableness** were far more likely to be **heavy users of alcohol or illegal drugs** (Walton & Roberts, 2004).
- Among **sexual-minority** adults (gay, lesbian and transgender people), the pattern reversed: **higher extraversion and conscientiousness** went with **heavier** drinking and drug use (Livingston, Oost, Heck, & Cochran, 2014). Note the reversal, since C is usually protective.
- Young adults high in **neuroticism and openness** were more likely to use **prescription drugs not prescribed for them** (Benotsch, Jeffers, Snipes, Martin, & Koester, 2013).
- A **25-year** study in **Finland** found that people high in **agreeableness** reported **less alcoholism** and **less depression**, had **fewer arrests**, and showed **greater career stability** (Laursen, Pulkkinen, & Adams, 2002).
- Among adolescents in **Ireland**, **agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism and extraversion** were all linked to **criminal behaviour** (O'Riordan & O'Connell, 2014).
- Among college students in **Norway**, **neuroticism** was linked to **Internet addiction, exercise addiction, compulsive buying and study addiction**, and **extraversion** to addiction to **Facebook and smartphones**, as well as exercise addiction and compulsive buying (Andreassen et al., 2013).

#### Social relationships

- An **18-month** study of **German** university students from the start of their first year found effects for **three** factors (**Asendorpf & Wilpers, 1998**). High **extraversion**: **more new friends** and more likely to **fall in love**. High **agreeableness**: **less conflict** with acquaintances of the opposite sex. High **conscientiousness**: more likely to **keep in touch with parents and siblings**. **Openness** had **no significant effect** on relationships.
- Among **German** high-school graduates, those who **stayed with their parents** scored **low in openness**; those who moved in with **roommates** showed **increases in openness**; those who moved in with a **romantic partner** showed **increases in conscientiousness** (Jonkmann, Thoemmes, Ludtke, & Trautwein, 2013).
- In **Dutch** couples, **low neuroticism and high extraversion in both partners** went with **marital happiness** (Barelds, 2005).
- **American newlyweds** rated themselves as **rising in agreeableness and conscientiousness** and **falling in neuroticism** over the first **2 years** of marriage. Their **spouses' ratings** told a different story: **decreases** in conscientiousness, agreeableness, extraversion and openness (**Watson & Humrichouse, 2006**). Each partner thought marriage was making them a better person; the other partner disagreed.
- American college students who wanted **higher economic, social and political status** scored **high in extraversion and low in agreeableness** (Roberts & Robins, 2000).
- **Dog people** score **higher in extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness** and **lower in neuroticism and openness** than **cat people** (Gosling, Sandy, & Potter, 2010).

#### Comment

Across this wide range of emotions and behaviour, the five-factor model shows **high predictive value**. Most research uses the five factors as self-contained units rather than the narrower traits that make them up. When researchers compared the two levels, both the broad **factors** and the narrower **traits** predicted well, but the **traits** had **higher** predictive validity (**Paunonen, 1998; Paunonen & Ashton, 2001**). Breadth buys convenience; narrower traits buy precision.

McCrae and Costa's findings have been **replicated** and continue to inspire a great deal of research, offering a well-supported account of personality and of the relative weight of heredity and environment. But not every psychologist accepts that these five are the **only** factors, which leads into HEXACO.

> **Trap.** Paunonen's finding is that **narrow traits** predicted behaviour **better** than the broad Big Five factors, even though most research uses the factors. It is Paunonen and **Ashton**, the same Ashton who co-developed HEXACO.

### Highlights: research on the five factors

| People high in... | tend to... |
|---|---|
| **Extraversion** | be emotionally stable and satisfied with life; cope better with everyday stress; get high grades; enjoy status and prominence in college |
| **Conscientiousness** | be reliable, efficient and punctual; get better grades; be organised and disciplined; set high goals; be accepted by peers and have more friends; be healthier and live longer; wear seat belts, exercise, sleep enough and eat more fruit and vegetables |
| **Conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness and extraversion** | be popular and judged more attractive; get good grades; cope well with stress; be good parents; prefer dogs to cats |

> **Trap.** The Highlights box lumps openness in with "prefer dogs", but the study itself (Gosling, Sandy, & Potter, 2010) found dog people **lower** in openness (and in neuroticism) and higher in E, A and C. If a question asks about the study, go with the study: **cat people are higher in openness**.

## 8.16 Michael Ashton and Kibeom Lee: HEXACO: The Six-Factor Model

**Michael Ashton** of **Brock University** (St. Catharines, Ontario, Canada) and **Kibeom Lee** of the **University of Calgary** (Alberta, Canada) proposed a **six-factor** model of personality (2007, 2009). Its name, **HEXACO**, is an acronym built from its factors (and echoes the Greek *hexa*, six).

How does it relate to the Big Five? Two of its factors, **extraversion and conscientiousness**, are **similar** to their five-factor counterparts. The other four **differ to varying degrees** from the earlier work and are specific to this model (Ashton & Lee, 2008; Shepherd & Belicki, 2008). The genuinely new factor is **honesty/humility**; the chapter summary puts it simply: HEXACO **adds honesty/humility** to the five-factor model.

**Table 8.6** The six factors of the HEXACO model

| Letter | Factor | High pole | Low pole |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H** | **Honesty/humility** | Sincere, honest, faithful | Greedy, pretentious, hypocritical, boastful |
| **E** | **Emotionality** | Emotional, oversensitive, anxious | Brave, tough, self-assured, stable |
| **X** | **Extraversion** | Outgoing, lively, sociable, cheerful | Shy, passive, withdrawn, reserved |
| **A** | **Agreeableness** | Tolerant, peaceful, gentle | Quarrelsome, stubborn, ill-tempered |
| **C** | **Conscientiousness** | Disciplined, diligent, thorough, precise | Reckless, lazy, irresponsible, absent-minded |
| **O** | **Openness to experience** | Creative, innovative, unconventional | Shallow, conventional, unimaginative |

Read the table as a comparison with Table 8.5. **H** has no direct equivalent in the Big Five. **E** in HEXACO is **Emotionality**, not "extraversion" and not quite "neuroticism"; extraversion is carried by **X**. **A**, **C** and **O** keep familiar names, though Schultz notes that A and O differ in degree from their Big Five versions.

*Classic background:* like the Big Five, HEXACO came out of **lexical** studies, but of trait adjectives in **many languages**, where a sixth honesty-related factor kept appearing. In Ashton and Lee's rearrangement, **anger and irritability** belong mainly to the low end of **agreeableness** (in the Big Five they sit largely inside neuroticism), and **emotionality** includes **sentimentality** and attachment along with fearfulness and anxiety.

> **Trap.** In HEXACO the letter **E = Emotionality**, and **X = eXtraversion**. A student who reads "E" as extraversion will get the acronym wrong. The sixth, new factor is **H, honesty/humility**, not "humour", "hope" or "harm-avoidance".

### Assessing the six factors

The six dimensions can be measured with two **self-report** inventories: the **100-item HEXACO Personality Inventory, Revised (HEXACO-PI-R)**, or the shorter **60-item HEXACO-60**. Both have been shown to be **valid and reliable** (Ashton & Lee, 2009). The factors have been documented in many cultures, including **Dutch, French, German, Hungarian, Italian, Korean, Polish, Greek, Croatian, Turkish and Filipino** (Ashton et al., 2004; Ashton et al., 2006; DeRaad & Barelds, 2008). The chapter summary says honesty/humility has been found in **more than a dozen** cultures.

**Research on the six factors** is still building up its behavioural and emotional correlates:

- **Politics, Italy (Chirumbolo & Leone, 2010):** high **conscientiousness** was linked to voting for **right-wing** parties; **left-wing** voters scored higher on **honesty, agreeableness and openness**.
- **Authoritarianism, New Zealand (Sibley et al., 2010):** college students **low in openness** and **low in emotionality** scored high in **right-wing authoritarianism** and **prejudice** toward dissident groups.
- **Work, United States (Johnson, Rowatt, & Petrini, 2011):** employees high in **honesty/humility** received **higher job performance ratings**.
- **Religion and happiness, Egypt (Aghababaei, 2012; Aghababaei & Arji, 2014):** high honesty/humility correlated with **stronger religious feelings** but **not** with **happiness**.
- **Happiness, Portugal (Oliveira, 2013):** among college students, honesty/humility **did** correlate with **happiness**.
- **Crime, England and Australia (Dunlop et al., 2012; Rolison, Hanoch, & Gummerum, 2013):** adult delinquents in England and convicted offenders in Australia scored **low on honesty/humility** and **high on Eysenck's psychoticism**.

> **Trap.** Honesty/humility and happiness: **no** correlation in the **Egypt** studies, **yes** in the **Portugal** study. And the two HEXACO tests are the **100-item HEXACO-PI-R** and the **60-item HEXACO-60**.

**How the trait counts line up.** This is the comparison older Indian keys test most often.

| Model | Author(s) | Number | The factors | Main instrument |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source traits | Raymond Cattell | **16** | Factors A to Q4 (Table 8.2) | **16 PF** |
| Dimensions (superfactors) | Hans Eysenck (with Sybil Eysenck) | **3** | **E**xtraversion, **N**euroticism, **P**sychoticism (PEN) | **EPI** (and Maudsley inventories) |

## 8.17 Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams: The Dark Triad of Personality

**Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams**, of the **University of British Columbia** in Vancouver, introduced a **three-factor** account of the **darker side of personality** (**2002**). Unlike the models above, which try to map the whole of personality, the Dark Triad picks out three socially aversive traits:

- **Narcissism:** extreme selfishness, an inflated view of one's own talents and abilities, and a constant need for **admiration and attention**.
- **Machiavellianism:** a need to **manipulate** others, marked by cunning, deceit and unscrupulous behaviour.
- **Psychopathy:** callous, insensitive, egocentric and antisocial; exploits other people, using considerable **charm** and often **violence**.

A quick way to keep them apart is to ask what each person wants from you. The narcissist wants your **admiration**. The Machiavellian wants to **use** you, and will plan and flatter to do it. The psychopath does not care what happens to you, and may take what he wants by charm or by force.

*Classic background:* Paulhus and Williams described the three as **overlapping but distinct**, and as **subclinical** traits, that is, present in varying degrees in ordinary people who are not patients. Machiavellianism takes its name from **Niccolò Machiavelli**, the Renaissance author of *The Prince*, whose advice to rulers endorsed manipulation and deceit. Later work proposed a **Dark Tetrad** that adds **everyday sadism**, and a 27-item **Short Dark Triad (SD3)** (Jones & Paulhus, 2014) as another brief measure.

> **Trap.** The three Dark Triad traits are **narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy**. Sadism is **not** one of the original three, and neither is borderline traits or neuroticism. The Dark Triad is also **not** Eysenck's psychoticism, although adult offenders who scored low on HEXACO honesty/humility also scored high on P (8.16).

### Assessing the Dark Triad

One quick self-rating measure is the so-called **Dirty Dozen** scale (**Jonason & Webster, 2010**), designed as a fast way of assessing all three traits. It has **12 items** on which people rate themselves. The items fall into three groups of four that correspond to the triad:

- **Machiavellian** items concern manipulating, deceiving, flattering and exploiting others to get one's way (for example, admitting that one tends to use flattery to get what one wants).
- **Psychopathy** items concern lacking remorse, being unconcerned about the morality of one's actions, being callous and being cynical.
- **Narcissism** items concern wanting admiration and attention, seeking prestige or status, and expecting special favours (for example, agreeing that one wants others to pay attention to oneself).

The scale's brevity is its appeal for research; the obvious weakness, shared with every Q-data measure Cattell warned about, is that people who really are manipulative have every reason to rate themselves generously.

### Behavioural correlates

- People high on **all three** traits engaged in **more antisocial activities** and took **greater satisfaction in others' misfortunes** (James, Kavanagh, Jonason, Chonody, & Scrutton, 2014; Porter, Bhanwer, Woodworth, & Black, 2014).
- They were given to strong **self-promotion** and tended to be **cold, duplicitous and aggressive** (Paulhus & Webster, 2010).
- The **verbal content of Facebook updates** validly predicted levels of **psychopathy and narcissism**; such updates tended to be emotionally cold, aggressive and highly self-promoting (Garcia & Sikstrom, 2014).
- People high in **Machiavellianism and psychopathy** scored **low** on the Big Five factors of **conscientiousness, agreeableness and openness**. They also showed **little empathy**, high **aggression**, a **vengeful, unforgiving** attitude and **low emotional stability** (Giammarco & Vernon, 2014; Muris, Meesters, & Timmermans, 2013; Oliveira, 2013).
- In sexual relationships the triad favours **short-term exploitation** over commitment. **Psychopathy** in particular goes with a **strong sex drive** and fantasies with **sadomasochistic** content, and high scorers on **psychopathy and narcissism** reported many **brief** sexual relationships entered **without any plan to commit** (Adams, Luevano, & Jonason, 2014; Baughman, Jonason, Veselka, & Vernon, 2014).

> **Trap.** The Big Five link is to **low** C, A and O for the Machiavellian and psychopathic person, plus **low emotional stability** (that is, **high** neuroticism). The Facebook study predicted **psychopathy and narcissism**, not Machiavellianism.

## 8.19 Reflections on the Trait Approach

Taken together, the theories and research in this chapter suggest that **inheritance may account for as much as 50 percent of personality** (Brody, 1997; Buss, 1988; Stelmack, 1997). The evidence is strongest for **extraversion, neuroticism and psychoticism**, but almost every other dimension personality researchers have studied shows a **strong biological component**.

A second, more surprising conclusion concerns the **shared family environment**, the parts of upbringing that siblings in the same home have in common. In many cases it has only a **minor** influence. Some researchers take a harder line still: they argue that twins, whether **reared together or apart**, will resemble each other in **all facets** of personality regardless of the family in which they grew up, which would mean the genetic effect far outweighs the environmental one.

**Why this matters for the field.** Personality psychologists have traditionally concentrated on the **family** and **early social interactions** as the shapers of personality (think of Freud, Adler, Horney and Erikson). The behavioural-genetic findings imply that future research may have to be **restructured** if personality development is to be fully explained.

**The caution.** Schultz ends by warning against a premature conclusion. Family and other environmental factors cannot be **written off**. Personality remains the product of **both** genetic make-up and life experience, and the task for psychologists is to determine **the relative weight of each**.

| Estimate | Whose | What it refers to |
|---|---|---|
| **One-third** genetic, two-thirds environmental | Cattell | Personality overall |
| **80 percent** genetic | Cattell | Factors B (intelligence) and H (timid versus bold) |
| **80 percent** inherited, 20 percent environmental | Eysenck and Eysenck (1985) | Intelligence |
| **Primarily** heredity | Eysenck | E, N and P (strongest for E and N) |
| **Four of five** factors more hereditary; A more environmental | Twin studies of the Big Five | N, E, O, C versus A |
| **As much as 50 percent** | Brody (1997); Buss (1988); Stelmack (1997) | Personality in general, by the end of the chapter |

*Classic background:* two broader criticisms of trait theories are usually paired with this section in exam answers. **Walter Mischel (1968)** argued that measured traits predict behaviour across situations only weakly (correlations of roughly .30), which began the **person-situation debate**; most researchers now take an **interactionist** view in which traits and situations jointly determine behaviour. And trait models are often said to **describe** personality better than they **explain** it: knowing that someone is high in neuroticism tells you what they are like, not why. Eysenck's arousal and autonomic explanations are the chapter's main attempt to answer this second objection.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a conclusion of Schultz's reflections on the trait approach?" True: heredity may account for up to 50 percent; the evidence is strongest for E, N and P; shared family environment often has minor influence; environment still cannot be discounted. **Not** true: "personality is entirely inherited", "shared family environment is the main determinant", or "the trait approach has shown environment to be irrelevant".

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the chapter. Following them turns five separate models into one story.

1. **Prediction over cure.** Cattell's definition of personality as what lets us predict behaviour sets the agenda for everyone after him. It explains his determinism, his preference for normal participants, his contempt for the "fuzzy" clinical approach, and the way every later model is judged: by how well its factors predict grades, health, marriage, crime, job performance and online behaviour.
2. **Factor analysis as both engine and weak point.** The same technique produced 16 factors (Cattell), 3 (Eysenck), 5 (McCrae and Costa) and 6 (Ashton and Lee). Schultz insists this reflects choices about what to measure, not a defect in the method, yet the subjectivity of those choices (which tests, which technique, what significance level, what names) is the main criticism of Cattell and the reason Eysenck tied his factors to experiments and biology.
3. **Breadth versus precision.** Cattell's 16 narrow source traits, Eysenck's 3 broad superfactors and the Big Five sit at different levels of one hierarchy. Eysenck's own hierarchy (responses, habits, traits, types) and Paunonen's finding that narrow traits out-predict broad factors are the same point: broad factors are convenient, narrow traits are precise.
4. **The march toward heredity.** Allport and Cattell put heredity alongside environment; Cattell's own estimate was one-third genetic. Eysenck made heredity primary, with twin, adoption and 35-nation evidence. The Big Five found four of five factors more hereditary and a cross-cultural structure in 50+ nations. The chapter ends at "as much as 50 percent", with shared family environment surprisingly weak and environment still essential.
5. **Universality and its limits.** The 16 PF in some 40 languages but invalid in Tshivenda; the EPI in nearly 40 countries; the Big Five in more than 50 nations but not in an isolated Bolivian tribe; HEXACO's honesty/humility in more than a dozen cultures. Each claim of universality comes with a counter-example worth remembering.
6. **From the normal range to the dark side.** The early models describe everyone; psychoticism, low honesty/humility and the Dark Triad focus on the aggressive, exploitative and manipulative. The chapter shows these converging: offenders low in honesty/humility score high on psychoticism, and Machiavellian and psychopathic people score low on agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness.

**Relation to other theorists in the book.** The chapter completes **The Genetics Approach** begun by **Allport** (Chapter 7): it keeps his belief that traits are real and partly inherited but replaces his idiographic method with nomothetic factor analysis, and Cattell's common versus unique traits echo Allport's common traits versus personal dispositions. Cattell borrows **Freud's** emphasis on the first six years and on oral and anal conflicts, and his late-maturity stage resembles **Jung's** midlife transition. Five-factor agreeableness correlates with **Adler's** social interest. Eysenck's E and N revive the ancient temperaments that Chapter 7 traced to **Hippocrates**, and his introversion-extraversion dimension gives a biological account of the attitude **Jung** first named. Against the clinicians (Freud, Adler, Horney, Erikson), the whole chapter is a bet that measurement of normal people, not insight into patients, is the road to understanding personality.

## Worked example

*Karan, 27, has applied for a trainee clinical psychologist post at a large city hospital. The selection panel has his 16 PF and NEO profiles, a HEXACO-60, two references, and his records from a previous job. He interviews brilliantly: charming, confident, quick to praise the panel's own publications. His 16 PF shows high scores on Factor A (outgoing), Factor E (dominant) and Factor H (bold), and low scores on Factor G (expedient) and Factor Q3 (uncontrolled). His NEO scores are high on extraversion and low on agreeableness and conscientiousness. On the HEXACO-60 he scores low on honesty/humility. His previous employer's records show frequent absences, two warnings for taking credit for colleagues' work, and repeated clashes with supervisors. A referee writes that he "gets bored easily and needs constant excitement". In the interview he says his father was a harsh, controlling man. One panellist asks whether these results could simply reflect a bad day.*

Reason through it with the chapter's concepts.

**What kinds of data does the panel have, in Cattell's terms?** The previous employer's attendance and disciplinary records are **L-data**: overt behaviour, rated or recorded by others, in a real-life setting. The 16 PF, NEO and HEXACO-60 are **Q-data**: self-ratings. Nothing here is **T-data** in Cattell's sense (a test whose purpose he could not guess). Cattell's warning about Q-data applies directly: Karan could have **limited self-insight**, or could be **faking**; Simon (2007) showed the 16 PF can be faked, and the NEO, like all self-report inventories, can be distorted by someone wishing to look well adjusted. The fact that his self-report is still unflattering on G, Q3, A, C and H, and that it **agrees with the L-data**, makes it more credible, not less. When several kinds of data converge, as in McCrae and Costa's own validation, the conclusion is stronger.

**How would Cattell read the 16 PF?** High **A, E and H** describe a warm-seeming, dominant, venturesome person; low **G** (expedient, weak superego) and low **Q3** (lax, impulsive) describe someone whose conscience and self-control are weak. These are **source traits**, stable and unitary. "Unreliability" as his employer describes it is better seen as a **surface trait**, a cluster (absence, credit-taking, clashes) produced by several source traits together. Note that in Cattell's 1982 data **G** was among the factors **mainly shaped by environment**, while **Q3** was among those **mainly hereditary**, so his profile mixes both origins.

**How would Eysenck describe him?** The referee's comment points to high **E**, and Eysenck's **cortical arousal** account explains it: an under-aroused cortex makes him seek excitement and get bored in routine work. The aggression toward supervisors, egocentric credit-taking and coldness suggest elevated **P** (psychoticism versus impulse control). If he were also high in **N**, Eysenck would note the speculative (and only modestly supported) link between high scores on all three and antisocial behaviour; the Chinese data (Huo-Liang, 2006) tie crime to high **P and N**. Eysenck would also point out that a high-P person can channel aggression into acceptable outlets; his own example was coaching college football.

**How do the Big Five and HEXACO fit?** High **extraversion** with low **agreeableness** matches Roberts and Robins (2000): people seeking status score high in E and low in A. Low **conscientiousness** predicts poorer job performance across professional jobs (Barrick and Mount's line of research) and, with low agreeableness, heavier alcohol and drug use (Walton & Roberts, 2004). In Eysenck's terms, low A plus low C is the **high-P end** of impulse control. Low **honesty/humility** is the most worrying single score: it predicts **lower job performance ratings** (Johnson, Rowatt, & Petrini, 2011), and offenders in England and Australia scored low on it and high on psychoticism.

**Is there a Dark Triad picture?** Flattering the panel, taking credit for others' work and charm combined with conflict fit **Machiavellianism** (manipulation, flattery, exploitation) with features of **narcissism** (wanting admiration and status) and **psychopathy** (callousness, thrill-seeking). Research links Machiavellianism and psychopathy to **low C, A and O**, little empathy and **low emotional stability**. A Dirty Dozen screen would be quick, though, as with all Q-data, a skilled manipulator can rate himself kindly.

**Is it heredity or his father?** The chapter supports **both**. Eysenck would stress heredity: E and P have genetic components, and his dimensions are stable from childhood on. But **Heaven and Ciarrochi (2006)** found that high-P scorers tend to have **more authoritarian and controlling parents**, so Karan's account of his father fits an environmental contribution too. Schultz's closing verdict is the right answer to give the panel: perhaps up to **50 percent** genetic, with environment still a real shaper.

**Answering the panellist ("just a bad day?").** No. Traits in this chapter are by definition **relatively permanent**, and the stability research (Costa & McCrae, 1988; Hampson & Goldberg, 2006, with E and C most stable over 40 years) shows rank order holds over years. A single test session could be distorted, but here **L-data and Q-data agree** across different instruments and different years. The fair caution is the opposite one: he is 27, and the typical age trend is toward **higher agreeableness and conscientiousness and lower neuroticism**, so some maturation is possible.

## MCQ traps

1. **Spearman developed factor analysis** (for intelligence); **Cattell applied it to personality**, after a Ph.D. under Spearman at the University of London in **1929**.
2. **Cattell's life:** born **1905, Staffordshire**; WWI wounded soldiers at **9**; physics and chemistry at **16**, honours in **3 years**; psychology in **1924** (only **6** professorships in England); **Columbia** (Thorndike), **Clark**, **Harvard 1941** (with Allport and Sheldon), **Illinois at 40**, **Hawaii in his 70s**; died at **92**; APA Gold Medal **1997**; **500+ articles, 43 books**.
3. **Cattell's definition:** personality is what permits **prediction** of behaviour in a given situation (**1950**); subjects were **normal** people, to be studied, not treated.
4. **Four classification axes:** common/unique (sharedness); ability/temperament/dynamic (function); surface/source (stability, unity); constitutional/environmental-mold (origin of **source** traits only).
5. **Neuroticism is Cattell's example of a SURFACE trait** (anxiety + indecision + irrational fear) but a top-level **dimension** for Eysenck and a basic **factor** in the Big Five.
6. **Constitutional traits** have **biological origins but are not necessarily innate** (the alcohol example); **environmental-mold** traits are **learned** (army officer versus jazz musician).
7. **16 source traits, Cattell (1965)**, after more than two decades of factor analysis. **B = intelligence, C = ego strength, G = superego, H = timid vs bold, I = tough- vs tender-minded, Q3 = self-control, Q4 = tension.** Letters skip **D, J, K**; **Q1 to Q4** came only from questionnaire data (Classic background).
8. **Later temperament traits** (Cattell, 1973; Cattell & Kline, 1977): excitability, zest, self-discipline, politeness, self-assurance. Source traits are the **atoms** of personality; motivation is the **engine's fuel**.
9. **Ergs = innate (constitutional) dynamic source traits; sentiments = learned (environmental-mold); attitudes = specific expressions; subsidiation runs attitudes, sentiments, ergs; self-sentiment = self-concept.** Classic background, absent from the 11th-ed. text but asked in its review question 8.
10. **Cattell's heredity figures:** **80 percent** for **B and H**; overall **one-third genetic, two-thirds environmental**. Cattell (1982), **3,000 males aged 12 to 18**: **F, I, Q3 hereditary**; **E, G, Q4 environmental**.
11. **Cattell's stages:** infancy **0 to 6** (major formative), childhood **6 to 14** (few problems), adolescence **14 to 23** (most stressful), maturity **23 to 50**, late maturity **50 to 65** (like Jung's midlife), old age **65+**. Freudian borrowings: early years and **oral and anal conflicts**.
12. **Cattell on human nature:** determinism; nature **and** nurture; past important but not permanently determining; moderate on uniqueness; **no ultimate goal**; optimistic when young, later thought society had **regressed**.
13. **L-data = observers in real life; Q-data = self-ratings (shallow self-awareness, faking); T-data = tests resistant to faking.** Cattell called the **Rorschach, TAT and word association "objective"**; most psychologists call them **subjective**.
14. **16 PF:** ages **16+**; 16 scales; objective, computerised scoring; research, clinical diagnosis, job success; about **40 languages**; child, adolescent, marriage-counselling and executive versions. The **pilot** profile: emotionally stable, conscientious, adventurous, tough-minded, practical, self-assured, controlled, relaxed.
15. **Bivariate = univariate** (one IV, one DV); **clinical** = subjective ("fuzzy" head, 1959); **multivariate** = factor analysis. **R technique = group; P technique = one person over time.**
16. **Cattell & Nesselroade (1967):** **similar** traits, **stable** marriage. **Simon (2007):** 16 PF can be faked. **Tshivenda** translation invalid; **Greek** and **Turkish** valid.
17. **Criticisms of Cattell:** subjectivity in choosing tests, techniques, significance levels and **factor names**; **16 traits not replicated**. Lack of data is **not** a criticism. He is the **father of the trait approach**.
18. **Eysenck:** born **Berlin 1916**, England **1934**; refused physics; **79 books, 1,097 articles**; most-cited psychologist at death; wife destroyed his papers; **Maudsley Hospital / Institute of Psychiatry**.
19. **EPI (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1963)** with **Sybil**: **12 years, 20 factor analyses**; used in nearly **40 countries**. Others: Eysenck Personality Profiler, Maudsley Medical Questionnaire, **Maudsley Personality Inventory**.
20. **E** (extraversion vs introversion), **N** (neuroticism vs emotional stability), **P** (psychoticism vs **impulse control / superego functioning**) are **superfactors** (type level); Table 8.4 adjectives are **trait** level. Older keys: **E and N only**, before P (EPQ, 1975; Classic background).
21. **Table 8.4 placements:** **impulsive, creative, tough-minded = P**; **shy = N**.
22. **Cortical arousal:** extraverts **LOW** base arousal, **seek** stimulation; introverts **HIGH**, **avoid** it, more sensitive to weak stimuli, **lower pain thresholds**. Evidence for arousal as the **cause** is weaker; the differences are **genetic**.
23. **N biology = overreactive sympathetic nervous system** (innate), not cortical arousal. **P biology = male hormones** (speculation).
24. **Neuroticism research:** **better** in fast-paced, stressful jobs (Smillie et al., 2006); **lower verbal ability** (Chamorro-Premuzic et al., 2006); midlife N predicts impairment **25 years** later (Crowe et al., 2006).
25. **Psychoticism:** aggressive, cold, egocentric, **creative**; alcohol, drugs, violence; **authoritarian parents** (Heaven & Ciarrochi, 2006); **men** higher; high on all three and crime only **modestly** supported; crime with **P and N** in China (Huo-Liang, 2006).
26. **Eysenck on heredity:** primary, **stronger for E and N than P**; MZ > DZ even reared apart; adoptees resemble **biological** parents; dimensions in **more than 35 nations**; intelligence **80 percent** inherited, not a dimension.
27. **Big Five:** **McCrae (1949) and Costa (1942)**, **NIH Gerontology Research Center, Baltimore**, **1980s**. Eysenck too few (3), Cattell too many (16).
28. **NEO = Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness** only. NEO-PI-R = **Revised** (240 items, 30 facets; Classic background). Adjective checklists of **100** and **40** words. NEO is still the most used; it can be faked.
29. **Big Five heritability:** **N, E, O, C** more hereditary; **agreeableness** more **environmental**. **A + C = low end of Eysenck's P**; **O correlates with intelligence**; **A with Adler's social interest**.
30. **More than 50 nations**, "common human structure" (1997), but **not** in an isolated, illiterate **Bolivian** tribe (Gurven et al., 2013).
31. **Most valued:** **Japan = C**; **India and Hong Kong = A**; **Singapore = emotional stability**; **Venezuela = E**; **Australia = E and A**; **US** and six others: none stands out.
32. **Schmitt et al. (2008), 55 countries:** women higher in **N, E, A, C**; gaps **largest** in prosperous, egalitarian nations. **Allik et al. (2010):** we see ourselves as **more neurotic and open**, others as **more conscientious**.
33. **Stability:** Costa & McCrae (1988), **6 years**, all five; Hampson & Goldberg (2006), **40 years**, **E and C** most stable; with age **A and C rise; N, E, O fall**; adolescents' **openness increased** (McCrae et al., 2002). Teachers predicted **3- to 6-year-olds**' scores **20 years** ahead (Graziano et al., 1998).
34. **High N + high O = higher suicide risk** (Bluml et al., 2013). Renal patients: high N = **37.5 percent** higher mortality (Christensen et al., 2002), yet high N also goes with **greater longevity** and **fewer dementia-type declines**.
35. **Asendorpf & Wilpers (1998):** E = friends and love; A = less conflict; C = family contact; **openness = no effect**. **Newlyweds:** self-ratings improved, **spouse ratings declined**. **Dog people:** lower in N and **O** than cat people.
36. **Paunonen (1998); Paunonen & Ashton (2001):** narrow **traits** predict **better** than broad factors.
37. **HEXACO:** **Ashton (Brock) and Lee (Calgary)**; **E = Emotionality, X = eXtraversion**; new factor **H = honesty/humility**; **E and C** similar to Big Five; **HEXACO-PI-R (100 items)**, **HEXACO-60**.
38. **Honesty/humility:** better **job ratings** (US); religious feelings but **not happiness** (Egypt); **happiness** (Portugal); offenders **low H, high P**.
39. **Dark Triad (Paulhus & Williams, 2002, UBC):** **narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy**; **Dirty Dozen = 12 items** (Jonason & Webster, 2010); Machiavellianism and psychopathy go with **low C, A, O**; Facebook updates predicted **psychopathy and narcissism**.
40. **Internet:** P = Internet as **substitute** for face-to-face (Turkey); **C** = more Facebook friends (**Israel**); **E** = more friends (**Finland**); shopping: **C convenience, O adventure, N avoidance** (Taiwan); sexting = **high N, low A**.
41. **Closing estimate:** inheritance **up to 50 percent** (Brody, 1997; Buss, 1988; Stelmack, 1997); strongest for **E, N, P**; shared family environment often **minor**; environment **not** discounted.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Factor analysis** | A statistical method that uses the correlations among many measures to identify a smaller number of underlying factors |
| **Traits (Cattell)** | Relatively lasting tendencies to react, derived by factor analysis; the basic structural units of personality |
| **Common traits** | Traits everyone has to some degree, such as intelligence or extraversion |
| **Unique traits** | Traits found in only one person or a few, seen mainly in interests and attitudes |
| **Ability traits** | Traits that describe skills and how efficiently a person works toward goals |
| **Temperament traits** | Traits that describe the general style and emotional tone of a person's responses to the environment |
| **Dynamic traits** | Traits that describe motivations, interests and ambitions; the driving forces of behaviour |
| **Surface traits** | Correlated characteristics that do not form a factor because they have no single source; less stable |
| **Source traits** | Stable, permanent, unitary factors derived by factor analysis; the basic elements of personality |
| **Constitutional traits** | Source traits that depend on physiological or biological conditions (not necessarily innate) |
| **Environmental-mold traits** | Source traits learned from social and physical environmental interactions |
| **16 PF (Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire)** | Cattell's objectively scored test of the 16 source traits, for ages 16 and over |
| **Ergs** (Classic background) | Cattell's innate, constitutional dynamic source traits (drives such as hunger, sex, curiosity) |
| **Sentiments** (Classic background) | Learned, environmental-mold dynamic source traits centred on important objects or areas of life |
| **Attitudes** (Classic background) | Specific interests in acting a certain way toward an object in a situation; the observable level of motivation |
| **Subsidiation / dynamic lattice** (Classic background) | The serving relation of attitudes to sentiments to ergs / Cattell's diagram of that motivational network |
| **Self-sentiment** (Classic background) | The self-concept; the sentiment that organises the others |
| **L-data** | Life-record ratings by observers of behaviour in real-life settings such as school or work |
| **Q-data** | Self-report questionnaire ratings of one's own characteristics, attitudes and interests |
| **T-data** | Data from tests designed to be resistant to faking because the person cannot tell what is measured |
| **Bivariate (univariate) approach** | The standard laboratory experiment, manipulating one independent variable to observe one dependent variable |
| **Clinical approach** | Case studies, dream analysis, free association; judged by Cattell too subjective |
| **Multivariate approach** | Cattell's preferred factor-analytic study of many variables at once |
| **R technique** | Factor analysis of large amounts of data collected from a group of people |
| **P technique** | Factor analysis of large amounts of data collected from one person over a long period |
| **Behavioural genetics** | The study of the relationship between genetic or hereditary factors and personality traits |
| **Monozygotic / dizygotic twins** | Identical twins sharing all genes / fraternal twins sharing about half |
| **Superfactor (dimension, type)** | Eysenck's term for a broad combination of traits: E, N or P |
| **Extraversion versus introversion (E)** | Eysenck's dimension of sociability, liveliness, activity and sensation-seeking versus their absence |
| **Neuroticism versus emotional stability (N)** | Eysenck's dimension of anxiety, depression, guilt, tension and moodiness versus calm stability |
| **Psychoticism versus impulse control (P)** | Eysenck's dimension of aggression, coldness, egocentricity and antisocial impulsivity versus superego control |
| **Cortical arousal** | Base level of cortical activation; lower in extraverts (who seek stimulation), higher in introverts (who avoid it) |
| **Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI)** | Hans and Sybil Eysenck's questionnaire (1963), 12 years and 20 factor analyses in the making |
| **Maudsley Personality Inventory / Maudsley Medical Questionnaire** | Earlier Eysenck questionnaires, named after the London hospital where he worked |
| **Five-factor model (Big Five)** | McCrae and Costa's model of neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness and conscientiousness |
| **NEO Personality Inventory** | McCrae and Costa's Big Five test, named from Neuroticism, Extraversion and Openness; revised as NEO-PI-R |
| **Openness to experience** | Big Five factor of originality, creativity, independence and daring; correlates with intelligence |
| **Agreeableness** | Big Five factor of good nature, trust and courtesy; the most environmentally influenced; correlates with social interest |
| **Conscientiousness** | Big Five factor of carefulness, reliability, organisation and hard work; strongest predictor of health and job performance in the chapter |
| **Facet** | A narrower trait within a broad factor (for example, the depression facet of neuroticism) |
| **HEXACO model** | Ashton and Lee's six-factor model: honesty/humility, emotionality, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness |
| **Honesty/humility** | HEXACO's distinctive factor: sincere and faithful versus greedy, pretentious, hypocritical and boastful |
| **Emotionality (HEXACO)** | Emotional, sensitive and anxious versus brave, tough and self-assured |
| **HEXACO-PI-R / HEXACO-60** | The 100-item and 60-item self-report HEXACO inventories |
| **Dark Triad** | Paulhus and Williams's (2002) trio of socially aversive traits: narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy |
| **Narcissism** | Extreme selfishness, inflated self-view, and a constant need for admiration and attention |
| **Machiavellianism** | A need to manipulate others through cunning, deceit and unscrupulous behaviour |
| **Psychopathy** | Callous, egocentric, antisocial exploitation of others through charm and often violence |
| **Dirty Dozen** | Jonason and Webster's (2010) 12-item self-rating measure of the Dark Triad |

## Likely exam points

1. **Cattell's aim and definition:** prediction of behaviour in a given situation (1950); normal subjects; factor analysis of 50+ measures per person.
2. **Cattell's life and its imprint:** WWI wounded soldiers at 9; older-brother rivalry; Spearman and factor analysis; poverty pushing him to practical problems; Harvard with Allport and Sheldon; 500+ articles and 43 books.
3. **Four ways of classifying traits** (Table 8.1) with an example of each, and the rule that constitutional/environmental-mold applies to source traits.
4. **Surface versus source traits**, with neuroticism as Cattell's surface-trait example.
5. **The 16 source traits** (Cattell, 1965) and the meaning of Factors A, B, C, E, G, H, I, Q3 and Q4.
6. **Dynamic traits:** the engine-and-fuel analogy; ergs, sentiments, attitudes, subsidiation, dynamic lattice and self-sentiment from older editions.
7. **Heredity:** 80 percent for B and H; one-third overall; the twin and sibling design; Cattell (1982) F, I, Q3 hereditary and E, G, Q4 environmental.
8. **Six stages with ages:** 0 to 6, 6 to 14, 14 to 23, 23 to 50, 50 to 65, 65+; Freudian borrowings; Jungian parallel in late maturity.
9. **Cattell's human-nature profile** and how it differs from Freud's.
10. **L-, Q- and T-data:** who rates, the setting, Q-data's two weaknesses, and Cattell's special use of "objective".
11. **16 PF facts:** ages 16+, 40 languages, uses, variants, and the airline-pilot profile (Figure 8.1).
12. **Bivariate, clinical and multivariate approaches**; R versus P technique.
13. **Cattell's research:** marital stability (Cattell & Nesselroade, 1967), faking (Simon, 2007), translation (Tshivenda invalid; Greek and Turkish valid).
14. **Evaluation of Cattell:** subjectivity at data collection, analysis and naming; replication failure; "father of the trait approach".
15. **Behavioural genetics:** definition; Allport and Cattell as pioneers; MZ/DZ twin, reared-apart and adoption designs.
16. **Eysenck's life:** Berlin to London (1934); psychology by default; 79 books and 1,097 articles; most cited psychologist; Sybil Eysenck and the EPI (12 years, 20 factor analyses).
17. **E, N and P** as superfactors with their poles (P versus impulse control or superego functioning) and the traits of Table 8.4.
18. **Earlier two-dimension (E, N) model** before P, and Eysenck's four-level trait/type hierarchy (Classic background).
19. **Cortical arousal theory of extraversion**, its predictions (sensitivity, pain thresholds) and the weaker evidence for arousal as the cause.
20. **Neuroticism:** sympathetic nervous system hyper-reactivity; research in the US, Australia, England and Sweden.
21. **Psychoticism:** correlates, creativity, authoritarian parents, male hormones, crime, and Eysenck's view that society needs all trait combinations.
22. **Eysenck on nature versus nurture:** heredity primary (strongest for E and N); twin, adoption and 35-nation evidence; 80 percent heritability of intelligence.
23. **The Big Five:** McCrae and Costa (NIH, Baltimore, 1980s); the five factors; why not 3 or 16; NEO and its acronym; NEO-PI-R.
24. **Five-factor position on nature versus nurture:** N, E, O, C more hereditary; A more environmental; cross-cultural structure as evidence.
25. **Overlaps:** E and N with Eysenck; A and C with low P; O with intelligence; A with Adler's social interest.
26. **Culture:** 50+ nations; the Bolivian exception; which cultures value which factor (India and Hong Kong value agreeableness); Western versus Asian and African profiles.
27. **Gender (Schmitt et al., 2008)** and **self-other perception (Allik et al., 2010)**.
28. **Stability and age trends:** key longitudinal studies; A and C rise, N falls; teacher predictions (Graziano et al., 1998).
29. **Emotional correlates:** E and well-being, N and distress; facets (Schimmack et al., 2004); suicide risk (Bluml et al., 2013).
30. **Behavioural correlates:** conscientiousness and grades, job performance, health and longevity; neuroticism's mixed health findings; addiction and crime; relationships (Asendorpf & Wilpers, 1998; Watson & Humrichouse, 2006).
31. **Factors versus traits** in prediction (Paunonen, 1998; Paunonen & Ashton, 2001).
32. **HEXACO:** authors, six factors with letters, honesty/humility as the added factor, the two inventories, and research on politics, work, religion and crime.
33. **The Dark Triad:** authors and year, the three traits, the Dirty Dozen, and correlates (antisocial behaviour, Facebook updates, low C/A/O, short-term relationships).
34. **Traits and the Internet:** neuroticism and problematic use; extraversion and social media; the Israeli, Taiwanese and sexting findings.
35. **Reflections on the trait approach:** up to 50 percent heritability; weak shared-family effects; implications for research; environment not to be discounted; comparison of 16, 3, 5, 6 and the Dark Triad's 3.
