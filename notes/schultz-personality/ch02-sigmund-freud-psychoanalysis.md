# Chapter 2: Sigmund Freud: Psychoanalysis

**What this chapter is really about.** Schultz and Schultz open their book's first Part, *The Psychoanalytic Approach*, with one man because the whole field starts with him. Psychoanalysis was the earliest formal attempt to explain personality, and every theory that came afterwards either builds on Freud or defines itself by arguing with him. Three claims sit at the centre of his system, and you will meet them in every module: that the forces running a life are mostly **unconscious**; that those forces are **biologically based drives**, chiefly sex and aggression; and that the decisive struggles happen in **early childhood**, in conflicts no child can avoid. Freud's ideas also escaped the clinic and changed how ordinary people talk about themselves: "repressed", "in denial", "anal", "Freudian slip" are all his. When he died in 1939, the *New York Times* called him "the most effective disturber of complacency in our time" (quoted in Bakalar, 2011).

The chapter has a second job that students often miss. It is not only an exposition of the theory but also an **evaluation** of it. Almost a third of the chapter is the module on research: what happened when psychologists who did not share Freud's method tried to test his ideas in the laboratory. Some survived surprisingly well (the unconscious, repression, displacement, projection, the Freudian slip, parts of the oral and anal types); some did not (dreams as wish fulfilment, the Oedipal resolution, women's weaker superego, the psychosexual stages, catharsis); and some could never be tested at all (id, superego, libido, death wish). Exam questions of the "which is NOT supported" kind come straight out of that module.

**How to read this chapter.** Module 2.1 is the life, and it matters more than in most theorists' chapters, because Schultz argue that the theory is partly **autobiographical**: Freud's attachment to his mother, his rivalry with his father, his sexual difficulties and his own neurosis all reappear as concepts. Modules 2.2 to 2.4 build the machine (instincts as the energy, the three levels of awareness, the three structures). 2.5 and 2.6 show what happens when the machine is strained (anxiety, then the defences). 2.7 explains how the machine is assembled in childhood (the psychosexual stages). 2.8 places Freud on Schultz's six human-nature issues. 2.9 covers how the analyst gets at the unconscious (free association, dream analysis). 2.10 is the long research module. 2.11 is Anna Freud's extension of the theory, and 2.12 is the overall verdict. Everything after 2.4 assumes you can say what the id, ego and superego each want, so that module is the hinge.

## 2.1 The Life of Freud (1856 to 1939)

Schultz treat the life as evidence, not decoration. The repeated message of this module is that Freud built a general theory of human nature partly out of the materials of his own childhood, his own dreams and his own sexual conflicts. As you read each event, ask which later concept it fed. A summary table at the end of the module pulls the links together.

### The early years

Sigmund Freud was born in **1856** in **Freiberg, Moravia**, a town now called **Pribor** in the **Czech Republic**. His father was a wool merchant whose business did poorly; when it failed, the family moved first to **Leipzig** in Germany and then, when Sigmund was **4**, to **Vienna**. Freud lived in Vienna for nearly **80 years**, leaving only at the very end of his life.

The family triangle is the first thing to notice. His father was **40** when Sigmund was born, and his mother, the father's **third wife**, was only **20**. The father was stern and authoritarian, and Freud later recalled the anger and hostility he felt towards him; he even remembered feeling superior to his father by about the age of **2**. His mother, by contrast, was attractive, protective and devoted to her first son, and Freud described his feeling for her as passionate, even sexual. Put those two relationships side by side (a young, adoring mother; an older, forbidding father seen as a rival) and you have the raw material of the **Oedipus complex**, which appears in 2.7. Schultz state plainly that much of the theory reflects Freud's own boyhood.

His mother was convinced he would be a great man, and Freud kept, for life, the self-confidence, ambition and hunger for fame that such a conviction breeds. He himself linked the two: in a remark quoted by his biographer Jones (1953), he said that a man who has been his mother's undisputed favourite carries the feeling of a conqueror through life, and that this confidence often produces real success.

The household was crowded. There were **eight children** in the Freud family, including two adult half-brothers who already had children of their own. Sigmund resented his siblings and became jealous each time a new baby arrived to compete for his mother's attention, which is sibling rivalry experienced first-hand long before he theorised about it. The family also organised itself around his studies. His sisters were forbidden to practise the piano in case the noise disturbed him; he had his own room, often ate there to save time, and that room alone had an oil lamp while the rest of the family used candles.

He was academically outstanding. He entered secondary school a year early and was often first in his class. He was fluent in German and Hebrew, learned Latin, Greek, French and English at school, taught himself Italian and Spanish, and read Shakespeare in English as a boy. When he had to choose a career, the professions open to Jews in Vienna were limited, and he chose **medicine**. The motive matters for understanding him: he did not particularly want to treat patients. He saw medicine as a route into **scientific research**, and research as the route to the fame he felt he deserved.

### The cocaine episode

As a medical student Freud experimented with **cocaine**, which was legal at the time. He took it himself and encouraged his fiancée, his sisters and his friends to try it, praising it as a wonder drug that relieved his depression and his chronic indigestion. Markel (2011) describes how it lifted his mood, fuelled romantic letters to his fiancée and fed grandiose fantasies about his career.

In **1884** he published an article on cocaine's benefits, expecting it to make his name. It did the opposite. The article was later judged to have contributed substantially to the **epidemic of cocaine use** that spread through Europe and the United States and lasted into the **1920s**, and Freud was blamed for his part in it. For the rest of his life he tried to bury the episode, even deleting the cocaine papers from his own list of publications. He nevertheless kept using the drug, apparently from **1884 to 1896**, after which he switched to **wine**; in a letter of June 1899 he described wine as becoming an old friend and announced plans to drink plenty of it.

The episode shows two traits that recur in the life: the intense wish for scientific fame, and a readiness to generalise boldly from his own experience.

### Finding the sexual basis of neurosis

Freud's plan of a research career collapsed for financial reasons. His senior professor warned him that a professorship would take many years and would not support him, and Freud had no private income. He was also engaged to **Martha Bernays**, and the engagement lasted **4 years** before they could afford to marry. Private practice was the only realistic option. According to Schultz he set up as a **clinical neurologist in 1881** and began studying the personalities of patients with emotional disorders.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition gives **1881** for the start of Freud's practice. Classic background: most biographies date his MD to 1881 and the opening of his private practice to 1886, after his return from Paris. If an option offers 1881 in a question set from Schultz, accept it; be aware other books say 1886.

Two incidents pushed him towards sex as the hidden cause of neurosis. First, he spent several months in **Paris** studying with the psychiatrist **Jean Martin Charcot**, a pioneer of **hypnosis**. Freud overheard Charcot remark of a patient that in such cases the problem was always genital, "always, always, always" (Charcot quoted in Freud, 1914), and was struck dumb by the vehemence of the remark. Second, back in Vienna, a colleague described a woman whose anxiety he traced to her husband's impotence: in **18 years** of marriage the couple had never had intercourse. The colleague joked that the only real cure was one no doctor could prescribe. These episodes, together with Freud's own sexual conflicts (below), led him to take seriously the idea that emotional disturbance has a sexual basis.

### Childhood sexual abuse: fact or fantasy?

After several years of practice Freud had become convinced that **sexual conflict was the primary cause of all neuroses**. He reported that most of his women patients described traumatic sexual experiences in childhood that resembled seduction, usually by an older male relative and typically the **father**. Today we would call these experiences child sexual abuse, often rape or incest. Freud's first theory, usually called the **seduction theory**, held that these real early traumas caused adult neurosis.

Roughly **a year** after publishing it, he reversed himself. He now said that in most cases the abuse had **never happened**: the patients had been reporting **fantasies**. At first this looked like a disaster for the theory, since an event that never occurred cannot cause anything. Freud rescued the position by arguing that the fantasies were psychologically real to the patients, who believed in them, and that because their content was still sexual, **sex remained the cause** of neurosis. In 1898 he wrote that the most immediate and significant causes of neurotic illness lie in sexual life (quoted in Breger, 2000).

Be precise about what he claimed. Freud never said that every reported abuse was fantasy; he denied that the reports were **always** true, writing that it was hardly credible that perverse acts against children could be so widespread (Freud, 1954).

The modern view complicates this. We now know childhood sexual abuse is far more common than was once believed, so several scholars suggest Freud's **original** interpretation may have been the correct one, and that more of his patients were telling the truth than he was willing to accept (Crewsdon, 1988). Whether he suppressed the truth deliberately (perhaps to make the theory more acceptable) or genuinely believed in the fantasies is unknown. Three further facts tilt the picture:

- About **ten years** after the reversal, Freud admitted in a private letter that such traumatic experiences were often genuine, and a few years later told another friend that he had analysed and cured several cases of real, severe incest (quotes from Kahr, 2010).
- In the **1930s** one of his disciples concluded that abuse was much commoner than Freud admitted publicly, and Freud tried to block publication of those ideas. (Classic background: the disciple is usually identified as Sándor Ferenczi.)
- Krüll (1986) suggested that Freud changed his position because widespread abuse would have made many fathers, possibly **including his own**, suspects.

This matters again in 2.10, where critics argue that Freud may have inferred or even suggested the seduction stories, and where modern research on recovered and implanted memories is reviewed.

### Freud's own sex life

Schultz call it a paradox: the theorist who put sex at the centre of emotional life had an unusually conflicted sex life of his own. Breger (2009) describes him as shy, frightened of women, without contact with the opposite sex through his youth, and a **virgin when he married at 30**. His written attitude to sex was negative. He warned of its dangers even for healthy people, urged people to rise above what he saw as a common animal need, and called the act degrading to mind and body. He apparently **ended his own sexual life at 41**, telling a friend that sexual excitement was no longer of use to someone like him (Freud, 1954). During the marriage he was at times **impotent**, and at times chose **abstinence**, because he disliked **condoms** and **coitus interruptus**, the usual contraception of the day.

He blamed **Martha** for the end of his sex life, and for years had dreams expressing resentment of her: she conceived easily, was often ill in pregnancy, and would not engage in sex beyond what was needed for procreation (Elms, 1994). Schultz add that his impotence may partly have reflected fear of another pregnancy. Notice how closely these private facts match the theory: sexual frustration, abstinence and coitus interruptus are exactly what Freud would soon name as causes of neurosis.

### Freud's neurotic episode

In his **40s** Freud went through a severe **neurotic episode**. He described confused states of mind he could not understand, clouded thoughts and veiled doubts (Freud, 1954). He also had a run of physical symptoms: **migraine headaches**, **urinary problems** and a **spastic colon**. He worried about dying and about his heart, and became anxious about **travel** and **open spaces**.

He diagnosed himself with **anxiety neurosis** and **neurasthenia** (a neurotic condition marked by weakness, worry, and disturbances of digestion and circulation), and traced both to a build-up of **sexual tension**. In his writing he proposed that neurasthenia in men came from **masturbation**, and anxiety neurosis from **abnormal sexual practices** such as **coitus interruptus** and **abstinence**. Krüll (1986) draws the obvious conclusion: Freud was using the theory to interpret and solve his own problems, so that his theory of "actual neurosis" was essentially a theory of his own symptoms.

> **Trap.** Keep the two diagnoses and their supposed causes straight. **Neurasthenia** (in men) → masturbation. **Anxiety neurosis** → coitus interruptus and abstinence. Both were Freud's self-diagnoses during the episode in his 40s.

Despite (or perhaps because of) these conflicts he was drawn to beautiful women; a friend remarked that the number of attractive women among his students looked like more than coincidence (Roazen, 1993).

### Analysing Freud's dreams

Freud **psychoanalysed himself** by studying his own dreams, and kept the practice up for the rest of his life. At the start he told a friend that his chief patient was himself (quoted in Kandel, 2012). This self-analysis was the period of his most creative theoretical work. Through his dreams he realised, for the first time, how much **hostility he felt towards his father**; he recovered childhood **sexual longings for his mother**; and he dreamed of a sexual wish towards his **eldest daughter**. Much of the theory was therefore built around his own neurotic conflicts and childhood experiences as he read them through his dreams. In Gay (1988) he is quoted as saying that his own person was his most important patient.

This is the methodological weak point Schultz return to in 2.10: a theory meant to apply to everyone was partly derived from a sample of one, interpreted by the subject himself.

### Freud's ideas attract attention

As his articles, books and conference papers spread, Freud gathered a circle of disciples who met him **weekly** to learn the new system. (Classic background: this was the Wednesday Psychological Society, founded in 1902, which later became the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society.) The first meeting's topic was, of all things, the psychology of **cigar making**. Opinions of the group differed: one writer dismissed it as a second-rate collection of marginal neurotics (Gardner, 1993), while **Anna Freud** described the early followers more kindly as the unconventional, the doubters, the dreamers and people who knew neurotic suffering from the inside (quoted in Coles, 1998).

Among the disciples were **Carl Jung** and **Alfred Adler**, both of whom later broke away to found their own systems (Chapters 3 and 4). Freud saw them as **traitors** and never forgave them. When he complained at a family dinner about his followers' disloyalty, his aunt told him that his trouble was that he simply did not understand people (quoted in Hilgard, 1987).

At home he ran a disciplined, punctual household: his daughter-in-law recalled that the main midday meal was served at exactly one o'clock, war or no war, and anyone late went without (quoted in Berman, 2008).

### Freud comes to America

In **1909** Freud received formal recognition from American psychology: an invitation to lecture at **Clark University** in **Worcester, Massachusetts**, and an **honorary doctorate**. (Classic background: the invitation came from G. Stanley Hall, Clark's president, and Jung also travelled and lectured.) He was grateful but disliked the country, complaining of its informality, its poor food and its shortage of bathrooms, and although he had had gastrointestinal trouble for years, he blamed America for ruining his digestion (Prochnik, 2006).

America, however, liked him. **Two years** after the visit his American followers founded the **American Psychoanalytic Association** and the **New York Psychoanalytic Society**, and societies soon followed in **Boston, Chicago and Washington DC**. By **1920**, eleven years after the visit, **more than 200 books** about his work had been published in the United States (Abma, 2004). Magazines such as *Time*, *Ladies' Home Journal* and *The New Republic* ran features on him; **Dr Benjamin Spock's** enormously popular child-care books, which shaped how several generations of Americans raised children, drew on Freud's teaching; and his dream theory even turned up in a popular song (Fancher, 2000). The irony Schultz note: the country he said made him ill was also the one that made him world famous.

### The final years

The 1920s and 1930s were the peak of his success and the start of serious decline in his health. He was diagnosed with **cancer of the mouth** in **1923**, and in the **16 years** left to him he had **33 operations**; the disease may well have come from his habit of about **20 cigars a day**. Parts of his palate and upper jaw were removed; he was in almost constant pain and **refused pain medication**; he also had X-ray and radium treatment and even a **vasectomy**, which some doctors then believed might slow the cancer.

When the **Nazis** took power in Germany in **1933**, they publicly **burned his books**, putting him in the company of other writers the regime branded enemies, among them Albert Einstein and Ernest Hemingway. Freud's response was dry: in the Middle Ages, he said, they would have burned him; now they were content to burn his books (quoted in Jones, 1957).

In **1938** the Nazis occupied **Austria**. Freud refused to leave Vienna despite his friends' pleas, even after Nazi gangs had entered his home several times. Only when his daughter **Anna was arrested** (she was later released) did he agree to go to **London**. **Four of his sisters** died in Nazi concentration camps.

He stayed mentally sharp and worked almost to the end. In late **September 1939** he told his physician **Max Schur** that his life had become nothing but pointless torture. Schur had promised not to let him suffer needlessly, and over the next 24 hours gave him **three injections of morphine**, each larger than a sedative dose, ending his life.

### Life events and the ideas they fed

The table below collects the links Schultz draw (or clearly imply) between the biography and the theory. Examiners like to ask which experience "shaped" which concept.

| Event in Freud's life | Concept it fed |
|---|---|
| Young, adoring mother; older, strict father seen as a rival; felt superior to his father by age 2 | **Oedipus complex**: desire for the opposite-sex parent, rivalry with the same-sex parent |
| Father apparently forbade genital play and threatened castration; at about 3 saw that his niece had no penis; ritual circumcision | **Castration anxiety** (see 2.7) |
| Charcot's remark; colleague's patient with an impotent husband; his own sexual conflicts | **Sexual basis of neurosis** |

| Ageing, worsening cancer, the carnage of the First World War, death of a daughter at 26 | **Death instincts** and the **aggressive drive** (see 2.2) |
| Bleak personal judgement of people, darkening with age and illness | **Pessimistic** image of human nature (see 2.8) |

## 2.2 Instincts: The Propelling Forces of the Personality

### What an instinct is

Every machine needs a power source, and in Freud's model of the person the power source is the **instincts**. He treated them as the basic elements of personality: the motivating forces that both **drive** behaviour and **give it direction**. His German word was **Trieb**, which means a driving force or impulse (Bettelheim, 1984); "drive" is arguably the better English rendering, and it is worth knowing both words because questions use either.

The key idea is that an instinct is **physiological energy converted into a psychological form**. It is the bridge between what the body needs and what the mind wants. Follow the sequence step by step:

1. A **bodily need** arises inside the person (hunger, thirst). The stimuli for instincts are always **internal**, not external.
2. The need produces a state of **physiological excitation**, a kind of bodily energy.
3. The **mind transforms** that bodily energy into a **wish**.
4. The wish, the mental representation of the need, is the instinct. It is what pushes the person to act in a way that will satisfy the need: the hungry person goes looking for food.

> **Trap.** The instinct is **not** the bodily state (hunger itself). It is the bodily need **transformed into a mental state, a wish**. The 11th-edition glossary defines instincts as *mental representations* of internal stimuli. An option that equates an instinct with the physiological need is wrong.

While the need persists the person feels **tension**, and the **aim** of every instinct is to satisfy the need and so **reduce the tension**. That makes Freud's a **homeostatic** theory: people are motivated to restore and maintain a state of physiological **equilibrium**, a body free of tension. Two further points follow. First, some tension is always present, so we are always acting to reduce it. Second, unlike an irritating noise outside the window, an internal need cannot be escaped by walking away from it. Instincts therefore influence behaviour continuously, in an endless cycle of need, then reduction of need, then need again.

What makes this a theory of *personality* and not just of physiology is the next step. People take **different routes** to satisfying the same need. The sex drive, Schultz note, can be satisfied through heterosexual, homosexual or autosexual behaviour, or channelled into some quite different activity. Freud believed **psychic energy can be displaced onto substitute objects**, and that this displacement was of primary importance in shaping an individual's personality. Although instincts are the **only source of energy** for behaviour, the energy can be invested in an enormous range of activities, and that is how Freud explains human variety: all our adult interests, tastes and attitudes are, in his view, **displacements of energy** away from the original objects that satisfied our instinctual needs. A person's passion for gardening or chess or politics is, on this account, instinctual energy that has found a new home.

### Two types of instincts

Freud sorted the instincts into two opposing groups.

#### The life instincts

The **life instincts** serve the **survival of the individual and of the species** by satisfying the needs for **food, water, air and sex**. They are oriented towards **growth and development**.

The psychic energy of the life instincts is called **libido**. Libido can be attached to, or invested in, objects (people, things, ideas), and Freud called this investment **cathexis**. Schultz's example: if you like your roommate, Freud would say your libido is cathected to that person.

Of the life instincts, the one Freud thought most important for personality is **sex**, but he defined sex very broadly. He did not mean only the erotic; he meant almost **all pleasurable behaviours and thoughts**, and he described his view as an enlargement of the usual concept of sexuality, taking in affectionate and friendly impulses of the kind ordinary speech calls "love" (Freud, 1925). This broad definition is the key to reading the rest of the chapter: when Freud later says babies are sexual, he means they seek diffuse bodily pleasure, not adult eroticism.

Sex, for Freud, is the **primary motivation** in human life, and its erotic wishes come from three **erogenous zones** of the body: the **mouth**, the **anus** and the **sex organs**. People are, in this picture, predominantly **pleasure-seeking** beings, and much of the theory concerns the necessity of **inhibiting or suppressing** sexual longings.

#### The death instincts

Against the life instincts Freud set the **death instincts**, the destructive forces. His argument was biological: all living things decay and die, returning to an inanimate state, so he proposed that people carry an **unconscious wish to die**.

A key component of the death instincts is the **aggressive drive**, which Freud described as the death wish **turned outward**: instead of being aimed at oneself, it is aimed at other people and things, as an urge to **destroy, conquer and kill**. Over time Freud came to regard aggression as just as compelling a part of human nature as sex.

The timing is part of the lesson. Freud developed the death instincts **late in life**, and Schultz tie the idea to his own experience: the physical and psychological decline of ageing, his worsening **cancer**, the mass slaughter of the **First World War**, and the death of one of his **daughters at 26**, leaving two young children. Death and aggression became central themes in both the theory and his life. In his later years he dreaded his own death and showed marked hostility and aggression towards colleagues and disciples who disagreed with him and left his circle.

The death instincts won only **limited acceptance**, even among loyal followers. One analyst wanted the idea consigned to the dustbin of history (Sulloway, 1979); another suggested that if Freud was a genius, this was a genius having a bad day (Eissler, 1971).

> **Trap.** Older guides and many Indian keys label the two groups **Eros** (life) and **Thanatos** (death). Classic background: Freud did use "Eros" for the life instincts; "Thanatos" was attached to the death instinct by later writers, while Freud's own published term was the death instinct. The 11th edition uses neither Greek label, only **life instincts** and **death instincts**. Also: **libido** is the energy of the **life** instincts only; the book names no equivalent energy for the death instincts.

**Summary: the two instinct groups**

| | Life instincts | Death instincts |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Survival of individual and species | Return to the inanimate state; decay and destruction |
| Needs or expressions | Food, water, air, **sex** | Unconscious wish to die; **aggressive drive** turned outward |
| Energy named | **Libido** (invested through **cathexis**) | Not named in the 11th edition |
| Status | Central to the theory | Developed late; limited acceptance |

## 2.3 The Levels of Personality

Freud's **original** model, before he proposed the id, ego and superego, divided the mind into three **levels of awareness**. Classic background: this is often called the **topographic model**, and the later id/ego/superego scheme the **structural model**; Schultz do not use those labels, but exam papers do.

The **conscious** means what it means in everyday speech: all the sensations and experiences you are aware of at this moment. As you read, you might be conscious of the page, a message you mean to send to a friend, and a neighbour's loud music. Freud thought the conscious was a **limited** part of personality, because only a small fraction of our thoughts, sensations and memories is in awareness at any one time.

He compared the mind to an **iceberg**. The conscious is the small part above the water, the **tip**. The far larger part below the surface, out of sight, is the **unconscious**, and that is what psychoanalysis is about. The unconscious is the home of the **instincts**, the wishes and desires that steer behaviour; it holds the **major driving power behind all behaviour** and is the store of forces we can neither see nor control.

Between the two lies the **preconscious**. This is the store of memories, perceptions and thoughts that are not in awareness right now but **can easily be called into it**. If your mind drifts from this page to what you did last night, you have pulled material from the preconscious into the conscious. Attention moves back and forth across this boundary all day.

The easiest way to keep the three apart is by **retrievability**:

| Level | What it holds | How accessible |
|---|---|---|
| **Conscious** | Present sensations, perceptions, thoughts | In awareness now; the tip of the iceberg |
| **Preconscious** | Memories and thoughts not currently attended to | Easily summoned into awareness at will |
| **Unconscious** | Instincts, wishes, and (as 2.6 will show) repressed material | Not reachable by ordinary effort; the main driving force; the focus of psychoanalysis |

> **Trap.** Preconscious is not "slightly unconscious". The test is whether you can retrieve the material by simply turning your attention to it. Last night's dinner: preconscious. A repressed childhood humiliation: unconscious. Also note the word is **unconscious**, not "subconscious"; Freud's system uses unconscious.

## 2.4 The Structure of Personality

Freud later **revised** the three-level scheme and replaced it with three **structures** in the anatomy of personality: the **id**, the **ego** and the **superego** (Figure 2.1). The two schemes are related but not identical. The id corresponds to the old notion of the unconscious, but the ego and superego also have **unconscious aspects**, so it is wrong to map "ego = conscious" neatly. The figure shows how the two models overlap.

Read the figure in two directions. **Vertically**, the brackets on the right mark the levels: a thin **conscious** layer above the waterline (contact with the outside world), a narrow **preconscious** band just under it (material just below awareness), and a huge **unconscious** mass below (material that is hard to retrieve). **Across** those levels the iceberg is divided into the structures. The **ego**, labelled with the **reality principle** and **secondary-process thinking**, occupies the top and spans the conscious, preconscious and upper unconscious. The **id**, labelled with the **pleasure principle** and **primary-process thinking**, lies entirely below the surface, deep in the unconscious. The **superego**, labelled **moral imperatives**, is a strip that runs from the top of the iceberg down into the depths, so it too operates at every level. The diagram therefore makes the point in the paragraph above visually: only the id is wholly unconscious, while the ego and superego straddle the waterline.

### The id

The **id** is the **reservoir of the instincts and of libido**. Because it supplies **all the energy** for the other two structures, it is the most powerful part of the personality. Being the seat of the instincts, it is tied directly to the satisfaction of bodily needs: need produces tension, and the id pushes for the tension to be discharged.

The id operates on the **pleasure principle**: it seeks to **increase pleasure and avoid pain** through tension reduction. It demands **immediate** satisfaction and tolerates no delay for any reason. It knows only instant gratification and pays no attention to what anyone else wants. Schultz describe it as selfish, primitive, **amoral**, insistent and rash. Most importantly, it has **no awareness of reality**.

The picture to hold in mind is a **newborn baby**: it cries and thrashes when a need is unmet but has no idea how to get what it needs; a hungry infant cannot find food. The id has only two ways to try to satisfy itself:

1. **Reflex action** (the baby's crying, sucking).
2. **Primary-process thought**: wish-fulfilling **hallucination or fantasy**, in which the wanted object is simply imagined. A hungry infant imagining the breast is the textbook case; so is an adult's daydream about a forbidden pleasure.

Primary-process thought feels like satisfaction but changes nothing in the world, which is why a second structure is needed.

### The ego

Children learn, often painfully, that they cannot grab food from other people without consequences, that relief of bowel tension has to wait for the toilet, and that sexual and aggressive urges cannot be expressed anywhere and anyhow. Growing up means learning to deal **intelligently and rationally** with people and the world, and developing the powers of **perception, recognition, judgement and memory** that adults use to meet their needs. Freud called these capacities **secondary-process thought**. Summed up, they are **reason**, and they belong to the second structure, the **ego**, which Schultz call the **rational master** of the personality.

The crucial and frequently mis-stated point: the ego's job is **not to block** the id but to **help the id obtain** the tension reduction it wants. Because the ego, unlike the id, is aware of reality, it decides **when and how** the instincts can best be satisfied, choosing socially acceptable **times, places and objects**. It **postpones, delays or redirects** satisfaction to fit reality; it does not abolish it. Because it perceives and manipulates the environment in a practical way, it operates on the **reality principle**, which stands in opposition to the id's pleasure principle.

Freud's own image is a **rider on a horse**. The horse (id) supplies raw power; the rider (ego) must guide, check and rein it in, or the horse bolts and throws the rider. The analogy also carries a limitation that is easy to miss: the rider has no power of his own. The ego is **never independent of the id**; it is always responsive to the id's demands and **draws its energy from the id**.

The ego therefore serves **two masters**, the **id** and **reality**, and is constantly negotiating compromises between them. It is the ego that keeps you in a job you dislike because the alternative is being unable to feed and house your family; it is the ego that makes you get along with people you cannot stand, because reality says that is the way to get what the id wants. This controlling and postponing work must go on all the time. If it lapses, the id may overpower the ego, and a person ruled by the id can become a danger to society and end up in treatment or in prison. To prevent that, Freud proposed a set of unconscious mechanisms that defend the ego (2.6).

So far the personality is already a battlefield: the ego is simultaneously restraining the id and serving it, perceiving and manipulating reality to relieve the id's tensions, walking a tightrope between instinct and reality.

### The superego

There is a third set of forces: a powerful, largely **unconscious** set of beliefs about **right and wrong**, acquired in childhood. Everyday language calls it the conscience; Freud called it the **superego**.

The superego is usually learned by about **age 5 or 6**, and at first it consists of the rules of conduct laid down by the **parents**. Through praise, punishment and example, the child learns which behaviours the parents consider good or bad. The superego has **two parts**, and the distinction is a favourite question:

- The **conscience** is formed from behaviours for which the child was **punished**. It is the source of guilt.
- The **ego-ideal** is formed from good or correct behaviours for which the child was **praised**. It is the standard of what one should strive towards.

Over time the child **internalises** these teachings, and rewards and punishments become **self-administered**: parental control is replaced by **self-control**. Because of this internalisation we feel **guilt or shame** whenever we act, or even think of acting, against the moral code.

The superego is **relentless**, even cruel, in its pursuit of **moral perfection**. In intensity, irrationality and demand for obedience it resembles the id. Its aim is not just to postpone the id's demands, as the ego does, but to **inhibit them completely**, especially sexual and aggressive ones. It seeks neither pleasure (the id's goal) nor realistic success (the ego's goal), only moral perfection, and, like the id, it admits **no compromise**.

The ego is thus caught between **three masters**: the **id**, **reality** and the **superego**. Paraphrasing Freud, the poor ego has a hard time of it, pressed from three sides and threatened by three dangers. When the strain becomes too great, the result is **anxiety**, which is the subject of the next module.

**Summary: the three structures compared**

| | Id | Ego | Superego |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Biological; reservoir of instincts and libido | Rational master | Moral arm; internalised parental and social standards |
| Operating principle | **Pleasure principle** | **Reality principle** | Moral perfection (no "principle" named in the 11th edition) |
| Type of thought | **Primary process** (reflex, wish-fulfilling fantasy) | **Secondary process** (perception, judgement, memory) | Not specified |
| Wants | Immediate gratification | Realistic, safe, delayed gratification | Complete inhibition of forbidden impulses |
| Level of awareness | Wholly unconscious | Conscious, preconscious and unconscious | Largely unconscious; spans all levels |
| Energy | Source of all energy | Borrowed from the id | Borrowed from the id |
| Compromise? | None | Constant mediator | None |
| When formed | Present from birth (inherited) | Develops from infancy | By about age 5 or 6 |

> **Trap.** Three confusions recur. (1) "The ego suppresses the id": no, it **serves** the id by delaying and redirecting satisfaction; it is the **superego** that wants impulses inhibited **completely**. (2) **Conscience** = punished behaviours; **ego-ideal** = praised behaviours. Students reverse them. (3) Classic background: some older texts call the superego's principle the "morality principle" or the "idealistic principle"; the 11th edition names the pleasure and reality principles only.

## 2.5 Anxiety: A Threat to the Ego

Everyone knows what feeling anxious is like. Freud's technical sense of the word is narrower: anxiety is like **fear**, except that we may not know what we are afraid of. He called it an **objectless fear**, meaning that we cannot point to the particular thing that caused it. He gave anxiety a central place in the theory, arguing that it underlies the development of **all neurotic and psychotic behaviour**.

### The birth trauma: the prototype of all anxiety

Freud proposed that the model, the **prototype**, for all later anxiety is the **birth trauma**. In the womb the fetus lives in the most stable and secure world it will ever know, with every need met at once. Birth throws it into a hostile environment where needs are no longer met automatically, so it must start adapting to reality. Its immature nervous system is flooded with stimulation, and it responds with massive movement, faster breathing and a raised heart rate. That first experience of tension, and of fear that the id's needs will not be met, is our **first experience of anxiety**, and it lays down the pattern of reactions that recurs every time we are threatened later in life.

When anxiety cannot be managed and threatens to overwhelm the person, Freud called it **traumatic**: whatever the person's age, they are reduced to the **helplessness of infancy**. In milder form, that infantile helplessness is re-enacted whenever the ego is under threat.

> **Trap.** Look-alike name. Classic background: **Otto Rank**, one of Freud's circle, later built a whole theory on the birth trauma (1924). In Schultz's Freud chapter the birth trauma is simply Freud's **prototype** of anxiety. If an option says "Freud held that the birth trauma is the prototype of all anxiety", it is correct for this chapter.

Freud distinguished **three types of anxiety**. The quickest way to keep them apart is to ask, for each, **what the ego is caught between**.

### Reality anxiety

**Reality anxiety**, also called **objective anxiety**, is the **first** type and the one from which the other two are **derived**. It is fear of **real dangers in the real world**: fires, hurricanes, earthquakes, a wild animal, a speeding car, a burning building. It is useful: it guides us to escape or protect ourselves from genuine threats, and it **subsides when the threat is gone**. It can, however, be carried to abnormal extremes, as in someone who will not leave the house for fear of being hit by a car, or will not strike a match for fear of fire.

### Neurotic anxiety

The other two types are more consistently troublesome to mental health. **Neurotic anxiety** has its roots in **childhood**, in a conflict between **instinctual gratification and reality**. Children are often punished for openly expressing sexual or aggressive impulses, so the wish to satisfy those id impulses comes to generate anxiety in its own right.

Neurotic anxiety is therefore an **unconscious fear of being punished** for impulsive, id-dominated behaviour. Read the next sentence twice, because it is the point examiners test: the fear is **not of the instincts themselves** but of **what will happen if they are gratified**. The conflict is between the **id and the ego**, and it has some basis in reality (children really were punished).

A clinical illustration: a young man who freezes and feels inexplicable dread whenever he feels angry with his manager, without ever deciding to be afraid. On Freud's account, the dread is not about anger as such but an unconscious expectation, laid down in childhood, that expressing anger brings punishment.

### Moral anxiety

**Moral anxiety** comes from a conflict between the **id and the superego**. In essence it is **fear of one's own conscience**. When you are moved to express an impulse that violates your moral code, the superego retaliates by making you feel **shame or guilt**; in ordinary language you are conscience-stricken.

How much moral anxiety a person suffers depends on **how well developed the superego is**: someone with a strong, inhibiting conscience experiences more conflict than someone with laxer standards. Like neurotic anxiety, moral anxiety has a basis in reality, since children are punished for breaking their parents' moral codes and adults for breaking society's. But the shame and guilt **arise from within**: it is our own conscience that produces the fear, and Freud believed the superego punishes violations of its rules terribly.

**Summary: the three anxieties**

| Type | Source of threat | Conflict | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reality (objective) anxiety** | Real external danger | Ego vs the outside world | Fleeing a burning building; in excess, never lighting a match |
| **Neurotic anxiety** | Fear of punishment for expressing id impulses | **Id vs ego** | Unexplained dread when angry at an authority figure |
| **Moral anxiety** | Fear of one's own conscience | **Id vs superego** | Guilt and shame at merely thinking of an affair |

> **Trap.** Neurotic anxiety = **id vs ego**; moral anxiety = **id vs superego**. Reality anxiety is the **original** form from which the other two derive. A common distractor describes neurotic anxiety as "fear of one's instincts"; Schultz say explicitly that the fear is of the **consequences** of gratifying them.

### The purpose of anxiety

Anxiety is a **warning signal** that something is wrong inside the personality. It creates tension, and so it becomes a **drive**, much like hunger or thirst, which the person is motivated to reduce. It alerts the person that the **ego is under threat** and could be overthrown unless something is done.

The ego has several **rational** options: **run away** from the threatening situation, **inhibit** the impulse that is the source of danger, or **obey** the conscience. If none of these works, the person may fall back on **defence mechanisms**, which are **non-rational** strategies for protecting the ego. That is the subject of 2.6.

## 2.6 Defences against Anxiety

### Why defences are always running

Anxiety signals that danger, a threat to the ego, must be dealt with. The ego has to reduce the conflict between the id's demands and the restrictions of society as carried by the superego. Freud thought this conflict is **permanent**, because instincts never stop pressing for satisfaction and social taboos never stop limiting it.

From this he drew a sweeping conclusion. Just as **all behaviour is motivated by instincts**, **all behaviour is defensive** in the sense of guarding against anxiety. The battle inside the personality changes in intensity but never ends, so defences must, to some degree, **always be operating**. Freud proposed several **defence mechanisms** (Table 2.1) and observed that we seldom rely on just one: we usually use **several at once**, and the mechanisms **overlap**.

Whatever their differences, all defence mechanisms share **two features**:

1. They are **denials or distortions of reality**: necessary ones, but distortions all the same.
2. They operate **unconsciously**. We are not aware of using them, which means that at the conscious level we hold distorted pictures of ourselves and our world.

**Table 2.1** Some Freudian defence mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it does (in brief) | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| **Repression** | Unconsciously denies the existence of something that causes anxiety | A painful memory simply cannot be recalled |
| **Denial** | Denies the existence of an **external** threat or traumatic event | A terminally ill patient insists death is not near |
| **Reaction formation** | Expresses the **opposite** of the impulse that is really driving the person | Sexual longings turned into a crusade against pornography |
| **Projection** | Attributes one's own disturbing impulse to **someone else** | "I don't hate him; he hates me" |
| **Regression** | Retreats to an earlier, less frustrating period of life, with its childish, dependent behaviour | An adult who sulks and demands to be looked after under stress |
| **Rationalisation** | Reinterprets behaviour to make it seem rational, more acceptable and less threatening | A sacked employee decides he never liked the job anyway |
| **Displacement** | Shifts an id impulse from a threatening or unavailable object to an available substitute | Shouting at the dog instead of the boss |
| **Sublimation** | Alters the impulse itself, diverting instinctual energy into socially acceptable activity | Sexual energy channelled into artistic creation |

Now each mechanism in turn, with the reasoning Freud attached to it.

### Repression

**Repression** is the **most fundamental and most frequently used** defence. It is an **involuntary** removal of something from conscious awareness: an unconscious kind of forgetting of something that causes discomfort or pain. It can act on three different targets:

- **Memories** of situations or people.
- **Perception of the present**, so that a person may fail to see a disturbing event happening right in front of them.
- **Physiological functioning**: a man may repress his sex drive so strongly that he becomes **impotent**.

Once repression is in place it is **hard to undo**, and Freud's reason is a neat piece of logic. We repress something because it is dangerous. To give up the repression we would need to discover that the memory is no longer dangerous. But we cannot discover that without first becoming aware of the memory again, which the repression prevents. The defence protects itself. Repression is the basis of much of the theory and, Freud held, is **involved in all neurotic behaviour**.

> **Trap.** Repression vs suppression vs denial. **Repression** is **unconscious and involuntary** forgetting of an **internal** threat (a memory, impulse or idea). Classic background: **suppression** is the **conscious, deliberate** pushing of a thought out of mind ("I won't think about the exam tonight"), so it is not a defence mechanism in Freud's strict sense. **Denial** refuses to acknowledge an **external** threat or event that has actually happened.

### Denial

**Denial** is related to repression but aimed outward: it denies the existence of some **external threat or traumatic event**. A person with a terminal illness may deny that death is close; parents whose child has died may keep the child's bedroom exactly as it was, as though the death had not happened.

### Reaction formation

In **reaction formation** we defend against a disturbing impulse by **actively expressing its opposite**. Someone threatened by their own sexual longings may become a fierce campaigner against pornography; someone disturbed by intense aggressive impulses may become excessively friendly and solicitous. In the unconscious of such a person, lust is turned into virtue and hatred into love. Clinically, the clue is often the **excess**: the opposite attitude is held with a rigidity and intensity out of proportion to the situation.

### Projection

**Projection** defends against disturbing impulses by **locating them in someone else**. Lustful, aggressive or other unacceptable impulses are perceived as belonging to other people, not to oneself. The person in effect says, "I don't hate him; he hates me." A mother may attribute her own sexual desires to her adolescent daughter. The impulse is still expressed, but in a form that feels less threatening.

> **Trap.** Reaction formation vs projection. In **reaction formation** the impulse stays **in the self but is reversed** (hate becomes love). In **projection** the impulse **keeps its content but is moved to another person** (my hate becomes his hate). Ask: "Was the feeling turned upside down, or handed to someone else?"

### Regression

In **regression** the person retreats to an **earlier, more pleasant period of life**, free of the present frustration and anxiety. It usually means a return to one of the **childhood stages** of development (2.7), and the person behaves as they did then, in a childish and dependent way. The link to fixation is worth noticing: Freud's followers often argued that people regress to the stage at which they left the most libido behind.

### Rationalisation

**Rationalisation** gives our own behaviour a reasonable-sounding gloss so that it becomes **easier to accept**. Faced with a thought or act that threatens us, we talk ourselves into believing it had a sensible explanation all along. The person fired from a job decides they never liked it anyway; the person who turned you down suddenly appears to have many faults. The underlying motive is that blaming something or someone else for a failure is less threatening than blaming ourselves.

### Displacement

Sometimes the object that would gratify an id impulse cannot be had, or cannot safely be approached. The impulse is then redirected onto a **substitute** within reach, and this is **displacement**. Children who hate their parents, or adults who hate their bosses, but who fear punishment if they express it, may redirect the aggression to someone **less likely to retaliate**: the child hits a younger brother or sister, the adult shouts at the dog.

The important theoretical point is that the substitute **does not reduce the tension as well as the original object would have**. If a person relies on many displacements, a reservoir of **undischarged tension** builds up, and they are increasingly driven to find new ways of reducing it.

### Sublimation

Displacement changes the **object**; **sublimation** changes the **impulse itself**. Instinctual energy is diverted into channels that society considers **acceptable and even admirable**. Sexual energy, for example, may be sublimated into **artistic creativity**, and Freud believed many human activities, especially artistic ones, are id impulses redirected into socially acceptable outlets.

Two qualifications are examinable. Sublimation is **a form of displacement**, and, like displacement, it is a **compromise**: it never brings total satisfaction, so it too leads to a build-up of undischarged tension.

> **Trap.** Displacement vs sublimation. **Displacement**: same impulse, **new target** (aggression still aggression, aimed at the dog). **Sublimation**: impulse **transformed into a socially valued activity** (aggression becomes competitive sport; sexual energy becomes painting). Schultz call sublimation a **form of** displacement, so "sublimation is a type of displacement" is a correct statement. Classic background: many textbooks call sublimation the most mature or most adaptive defence; Schultz stress instead that it still leaves tension undischarged.

### Lying to ourselves

Pull the module together. Defences are **unconscious denials or distortions of reality**. Using them, we **lie to ourselves without knowing it**, and that unawareness is exactly what makes them work: a lie we knew we were telling would not protect us. When the defences are working well, they keep threatening material out of awareness, which means we may not know the truth about ourselves and may hold a distorted picture of our own needs, fears and desires.

The truth breaks through when the defences **fail**: in times of unusual **stress**, or during **psychoanalysis**, which deliberately dismantles them. Then the person is flooded with **overwhelming anxiety** and feels dismal, worthless and depressed. Unless the old defences are restored or new ones formed, **neurotic or psychotic symptoms** are likely. Freud's conclusion is therefore that defences are **necessary to mental health**: we could not survive long without them.

> **Trap.** Freud does **not** say defences are signs of illness. He says **everyone** uses them **all the time**, that they are **necessary**, and that symptoms appear when they **break down**. An option claiming "only neurotics use defence mechanisms" is false. Classic background: the full catalogue of defences was systematised by **Anna Freud** in *The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense* (1936), which Schultz cover in 2.11.

## 2.7 Psychosexual Stages of Personality Development

The previous modules described forces that are the same in everyone: the same id, the same instincts, the same need for defences. This module explains why people nonetheless turn out **different**. Freud held that while the id is universal, the **ego and superego**, though they perform the same functions in everyone, differ in their **content** from person to person, because they are formed through **experience**, and no two people have exactly the same experiences, not even siblings raised in one house. Part of personality is therefore built from each child's unique relationships with people and objects, and out of them grows each person's own set of **character attributes**: a stable pattern of behaviour that sets them apart from everyone else.

### Growing up is not easy

In Freud's account, each person's distinctive **character type is formed in childhood**, chiefly through **interactions with the parents**. The child keeps trying to maximise pleasure by satisfying the id; the parents, acting for society, keep imposing the demands of reality and morality. He considered these early experiences so decisive that he said the adult personality is **firmly shaped and crystallised by about age 5**.

Two things convinced him. One was his own childhood memories. The other was his patients: lying on the couch, they invariably reached far back into childhood, and Freud came to see adult neurosis as having been formed in the earliest years. He also sensed strong **sexual conflicts** in infants and young children, conflicts that seemed to centre on particular **regions of the body**, with each region becoming the focus of conflict at a different age. From this he derived the **psychosexual stages of development**, each defined by an **erogenous zone** (Table 2.2). Each stage contains a **conflict** that must be resolved before the child can move on.

**Fixation.** Sometimes a person is reluctant or unable to move to the next stage. There are **two opposite routes** to this:

- the stage's conflict was **not resolved** (too much **frustration**), or
- the stage's needs were so thoroughly met by an **indulgent** parent that the child does not want to leave (too much **gratification**).

Either way the person is said to be **fixated** at that stage. In fixation a **portion of libido remains invested** in that stage, leaving **less energy** for the stages that follow. Think of libido as a fixed budget: whatever is left behind at an early stage is unavailable for later development.

> **Trap.** Fixation can result from **excessive frustration or excessive gratification**. Options saying "fixation is caused only by deprivation" are wrong. The 11th-edition glossary definition names both.

**The infant's sex drive.** Central to all of this is the idea that **babies are motivated by sexual impulses**, which shocked both Freud's colleagues and the public. Remember the broad definition from 2.2: Freud meant that the infant seeks a **diffuse bodily pleasure** coming from the **mouth, the anus and the genitals**, the erogenous zones that define the stages in the **first 5 years**.

**Table 2.2** Freud's psychosexual stages of development

| Stage | Ages (11th ed.) | What defines it |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral** | Birth to 1 | The mouth is the main erogenous zone; pleasure comes from sucking; the **id is dominant** |
| **Anal** | 1 to 3 | **Toilet training** (external reality) interferes with the pleasure of defecation |
| **Phallic** | 4 to 5 | Incestuous fantasies; **Oedipus complex**; anxiety; **development of the superego** |
| **Latency** | 5 to puberty | A period in which the sex instinct is **sublimated** |
| **Genital** | Adolescence to adulthood | Development of sex-role identity and adult social relationships |

> **Trap.** Ages differ across books, and examiners mix them. The 11th edition's table gives oral **birth to 1**, anal **1 to 3**, phallic **4 to 5**, latency **5 to puberty**, genital **adolescence to adulthood**. The text adds that the oral stage runs until **some time in the second year** and that toilet training begins at about **18 months**. Classic background: Hall and Lindzey and many Indian keys give phallic **3 to 5 or 3 to 6** and latency **6 to 12**. Answer from the source the paper follows; if it is Schultz, phallic is 4 to 5.

### The oral stage: taking in or spitting out

The **oral stage**, the first, lasts from **birth until some time in the second year**. The infant's main source of pleasure is the **mouth**: sucking, biting and swallowing. The mouth obviously serves survival (food and water), but Freud stressed the **erotic** satisfaction of oral activity.

Because the infant can do nothing for itself, it depends **completely** on the mother or other caregiver, and she becomes the **main target of its libido**. Put in everyday terms, the infant is learning, in a primitive way, to love the mother. How she responds to the infant's demands, which at this point are purely id demands, shapes the baby's small world: from her the infant learns to see the world as **good or bad, satisfying or frustrating, safe or dangerous**.

There are **two modes** of oral behaviour, in a fixed order:

**1. Oral incorporative behaviour (taking in).** This comes **first**: the mouth is pleasurably stimulated by food and by the people who feed and cuddle the baby. Adults fixated here are excessively preoccupied with oral activities: **eating, drinking, smoking, kissing**. If they were **over-gratified** as infants, their adult oral personality is predisposed to **optimism and dependency**. Having been indulged, they go on depending on others and expecting others to meet their needs. They become **gullible**, "swallowing" whatever they are told, and **trust others excessively**. Freud's label for them is the **oral passive** personality type. Notice the pun built into the theory: the adult who will "swallow anything" is the infant who was given everything to swallow.

**2. Oral aggressive or oral sadistic behaviour (biting or spitting out).** This arises during **teething**, a painful and frustrating experience. The infant now comes to view the mother with **hatred as well as love**: from the infant's point of view, everything that has happened to it so far has come from her, so the pain must come from her as well. People fixated at this level are prone to **pessimism, hostility and aggression**. They tend to be argumentative and **sarcastic** (making "biting" remarks), cruel towards others, **envious**, and inclined to **exploit and manipulate** people in order to dominate them.

The oral stage ends at **weaning**, though some libido stays behind if fixation has occurred. The focus then moves to the other end of the digestive tract.

**Summary: the two oral types**

| | Oral incorporative / oral passive | Oral aggressive / oral sadistic |
|---|---|---|
| When | First part of the oral stage | During teething |
| Infant experience | Pleasurable taking in, often over-gratified | Pain and frustration; love mixed with hatred of the mother |
| Adult traits | Optimism, dependency, gullibility, excessive trust; preoccupation with eating, drinking, smoking, kissing | Pessimism, hostility, aggression, sarcasm ("biting" remarks), envy, exploitation |

### The anal stage: letting go or holding back

During roughly the first year, society (in the form of the parents) adjusts to the infant and asks little in return. At about **18 months** this changes dramatically with a new demand: **toilet training**. Freud thought the experience of toilet training in the **anal stage** had a significant effect on personality.

Defecation gives the child erotic pleasure. Toilet training puts the child under pressure to **postpone** that pleasure, and this is the **first time** that the gratification of an instinctual impulse is **interfered with** from outside, as parents try to control when and where the child defecates. It is a time of conflict for everyone. The child also discovers that it has a **weapon**: it controls something the parents want and can choose whether to comply. When training goes badly, because the child finds it hard to learn or the parents push too hard, there are **two** typical reactions.

**1. Defecating when and where the parents do not want it**, defying their attempts at regulation. A child who finds this a satisfying way of reducing frustration, and uses it often, may develop an **anal aggressive personality**. For Freud this was the root of many adult forms of **hostile and sadistic** behaviour: **cruelty, destructiveness, temper tantrums**. Such a person tends to be **disorderly** and to treat other people as **objects to be possessed**.

**2. Holding back (retaining) the faeces.** Freud said this produces erotic pleasure from a full lower intestine, and it is another effective way of **manipulating the parents**, who may grow worried or frantic if the child goes days without a bowel movement. The child has found a new way to secure attention and affection. This is the basis of the **anal retentive personality**: **stubborn, stingy**, inclined to **hoard**, because the person's sense of security depends on what is saved and possessed and on keeping possessions and life in order. Such a person is **rigid, compulsively neat, obstinate and overly conscientious**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition's term for the defiant type is **anal aggressive**. Classic background: many older books and Indian keys call it **anal expulsive**; treat them as the same type. And for the retentive type, the classic shorthand "orderliness, parsimony (stinginess) and obstinacy" (the anal triad) matches Schultz's description (compulsively neat, stingy, obstinate) even though Schultz do not use the phrase.

### The phallic stage

Around the **fourth to fifth year**, the focus of pleasure moves from the anus to the **genitals**, and once again an id impulse meets the demands of society as expressed by the parents.

Children in the **phallic stage** take a lively interest in exploring and handling the genitals, their own and their playmates'. Pleasure comes not only from behaviour such as **masturbation** but also from **fantasies**. Questions appear about where babies come from and about the anatomical difference between boys and girls, and the child may announce a plan to **marry the opposite-sex parent** one day.

Of the pregenital (childhood) stages, the phallic stage comes **last**, and its conflicts are the **most complex** to resolve. They are also the hardest for many people to accept, because they involve **incest**, which is taboo in many cultures. Between incestuous wishes and masturbation, Schultz note, the parents of a typical 4-year-old are likely to feel shock and anger and to clamp down, and reality and morality once more confront the id.

#### The Oedipus complex in boys

The basic conflict of the phallic stage is the child's **unconscious desire for the parent of the opposite sex**, together with an unconscious wish to **replace or destroy the parent of the same sex**. This is the **Oedipus complex**, named after the Greek myth told in **Sophocles'** play ***Oedipus Rex*** (fifth century BC), in which Oedipus kills his father and marries his mother without knowing who they are.

The complex works differently in boys and girls, and Freud developed the **male** version much more fully. Step by step:

1. **Desire.** The mother becomes the boy's love object, and he shows his longing through fantasy and behaviour.
2. **Rivalry.** He sees the father as an obstacle, a rival and a threat, who enjoys a special relationship with the mother from which the boy is excluded. He becomes **jealous and hostile** towards him. Freud drew this directly from his own childhood: he wrote that he had found love of the mother and jealousy of the father in his own case too (Freud, 1954).
3. **Fear.** The boy fears the father will retaliate, and he interprets this fear in genital terms: the father will cut off the offending organ, the penis, the source of his pleasure and longing. This is **castration anxiety**.
4. **Resolution.** The fear is so strong that the boy **represses** his sexual desire for his mother, replaces it with a more acceptable **affection**, and develops a strong **identification with the father**. Through identification he gains some **vicarious sexual satisfaction**, and he tries to become more like the father, adopting his mannerisms, behaviours, attitudes and **superego standards**. This is how the boy's superego is formed.

**The biographical evidence for castration anxiety.** Schultz go out of their way to show that this, too, may be Freud's own childhood:

- Krüll (1986) reports indications that Freud's father told little Sigmund not to play with his genitals and even threatened castration if he did. In later writing on masturbation Freud described such paternal threats as common, and he reported that his adult dreams contained fears of castration by his father.
- At about **age 3**, Freud and his nephew engaged in rough sex play with his **niece** and found she had no penis, which to a 3-year-old could be evidence that penises can be cut off.
- Krüll also points out that for a **Jewish** boy the threat would be especially realistic, because ritual **circumcision** makes the connection with castration easy; Freud later confirmed this in his writing.

#### The Oedipus complex in girls

Freud was **less clear** about the female phallic conflict, which **some of his followers** called the **Electra complex**, after another Sophocles play in which Electra persuades her brother to kill their mother, whom she hates.

> **Trap.** Classic background: the term **Electra complex** was proposed by **Carl Jung**, and **Freud rejected it**, preferring "female Oedipus complex". The 11th edition says only that "some of his followers" used the term, and its heading for this subsection is *The Oedipus Complex in Girls*. An option crediting the term "Electra complex" to Freud himself is the trap.

The sequence for the girl:

1. **First love object = mother**, exactly as for the boy, because the mother is the infant's main source of food, affection and security.
2. **The shift to the father.** In the phallic stage the father becomes the girl's new love object. Why? Freud's answer: her reaction to discovering that boys have a penis and girls do not.
3. **Blame and envy.** She blames the mother for her supposedly inferior condition, loves her less and may even hate her for what she imagines the mother did. She **envies** the father and transfers her love to him because he has the valued organ. This is **penis envy**, the counterpart of the boy's castration anxiety: **the girl thinks the loss has already happened, while the boy fears it is still to come.** Freud (1925) claimed that this envy is the origin of a number of characteristically feminine reactions.
4. **Incomplete resolution.** Freud said the female Oedipus complex can **never be fully resolved**, which he believed led to **poorly developed superegos in women**. An adult woman's love for a man is, he claimed, always tinged with penis envy, for which she can partly compensate by having a **male child**. Eventually the girl identifies with the mother and represses her love for the father, but Freud never specified how this happens.

These are the claims later attacked as Freud's negative view of women (2.12), and two of them were explicitly tested: the weaker superego (not supported) and the idea that women resolve the Oedipal problem by having a child (weak support) (2.10).

**Summary: boys and girls in the phallic stage**

| | Boy | Girl |
|---|---|---|
| First love object | Mother | Mother |
| Phallic-stage love object | Mother (continues) | **Father** (shifts) |
| Rival | Father | Mother |
| Key fear or feeling | **Castration anxiety** (fears he will lose his penis) | **Penis envy** (believes she has lost hers) |
| Name used | **Oedipus complex** | Female Oedipus complex; "Electra complex" (followers' term) |
| Resolution | Represses desire; **identifies with father**; adopts his superego standards | Never fully resolved; identifies with mother, mechanism unclear |
| Freud's claimed outcome | Strong superego | **Poorly developed superego**; partial compensation by bearing a son |

#### The phallic personality

How well the phallic conflicts are resolved largely determines **adult relations with the opposite sex**. Poorly resolved conflicts leave lingering castration anxiety and penis envy. The **phallic character** shows strong **narcissism**. These people keep acting to attract the opposite sex but have trouble forming **mature heterosexual relationships**. They need constant **recognition and admiration** for what they see as their attractive and unique qualities, With a steady supply of admiration they cope; when it dries up, feelings of inadequacy and inferiority take over.

In **men**, Freud said, the type is **brash, vain and self-assured**, proving masculinity through one **sexual conquest** after another. The **female** phallic personality, driven by penis envy, **exaggerates her femininity** and uses her talents and charm to overwhelm and conquer men.

Finally, Freud held that the whole tense drama of the phallic stage is **repressed in everyone**. Its effects keep motivating us unconsciously, but we remember little or nothing of the conflict.

### The latency period

The turmoil of the oral, anal and phallic stages supplies most of the material from which the adult personality is built. By about **age 5** the three structures (id, ego, superego) are formed and their relationships are setting.

The next **5 or 6 years** are, fortunately for children and parents, calm. The **latency period** is **not a psychosexual stage**. Freud believed the sex instinct is **dormant** during this time and is temporarily **sublimated** in **school activities, hobbies and sports**, and in **friendships with members of the same sex**.

> **Trap.** Latency is a **period**, not a stage, because no new erogenous zone emerges. The 11th-edition glossary defines the psychosexual stages as **oral, anal, phallic and genital**: four, with latency outside the list, even though Table 2.2 prints it as a row. If asked how many psychosexual stages Freud proposed, the strict answer is four, plus the latency period.

### The genital stage

The **genital stage**, the last, begins at **puberty**. The body is maturing physically, and if no major fixation has occurred earlier, the person may be able to lead a **normal** life. Freud thought the conflict of this stage is **less intense** than in earlier stages. Adolescents must conform to social rules about sexual expression, but the conflict is reduced through **sublimation**: the sexual energy pressing for release in the teenage years can be partly satisfied through socially acceptable substitutes and later through a **committed adult relationship**.

The mature **genital personality** finds its satisfactions in **love and work**, work being an acceptable outlet for the sublimation of id impulses. Classic background: this is the source of the phrase usually attributed to Freud that maturity means the capacity "to love and to work" (*lieben und arbeiten*).

### The importance of childhood

Schultz close the module by underlining Freud's position. The **first 5 years are the crucial ones**. The theory gives less attention to later childhood and adolescence, and Freud was little interested in personality development in **adulthood**. In his view, how we behave, think and feel as adults is determined by conflicts we meet and must manage **before many of us can even read**. This is the claim that later research (2.10) and later theorists (Erikson in particular, Chapter 6) challenge most directly.

## 2.8 Questions about Human Nature

Schultz rate every theorist on the same six issues, so this module is effectively a compressed summary of the whole theory seen from above. For Freud the verdict is blunt: his image of human nature is neither flattering nor hopeful.

**Optimism vs pessimism: pessimistic.** Freud pictured each person as a dark cellar of conflict in which a battle never stops. We are locked in a struggle with our own inner forces that we are nearly always destined to lose; we are condemned to anxiety, to the frustration of at least some of our drives, and to continuing tension, endlessly defending ourselves against an id that is always ready to topple us. Schultz trace this gloom partly to the man: his view of humanity darkened with age and illness, and his private judgement of people was harsh. In a letter he wrote that he had found little good in human beings and that most of them, in his experience, were "trash" (Freud, 1963). That severity, Schultz suggest, is visible in the theory itself.

**Equilibrium vs growth: equilibrium.** In Freud's system there is **only one ultimate goal in life: to reduce tension**. This follows from the homeostatic view of instincts in 2.2: the organism seeks a tension-free state of balance, not growth or self-actualisation. (Contrast Jung, Maslow and Rogers later in the book, who all make growth the master motive.)

**Nature vs nurture: a middle ground.** The **id**, the most powerful part of personality, is **inherited** and physiologically based, and so is the sequence of **psychosexual stages**. But other parts of personality are **learned** in early childhood through **parent-child interactions**. Freud therefore gives weight to both, though the inherited id supplies all the energy.

**Uniqueness vs universality: both.** There is **universality** in that everyone passes through the same psychosexual stages and is driven by the same id forces. But part of personality is **unique**: the ego and superego do the same jobs in everyone, yet their **content** differs because each is formed from personal experience, and different **character types** (oral, anal, phallic) can emerge from the stages.

**Free will vs determinism: determinism, with one opening.** Freud's view is **deterministic**. Almost everything we do, think and even dream is fixed in advance by the life and death instincts, forces inside us that we cannot see or reach. The adult personality is determined by interactions that took place **before age 5**, when we had little control, and those experiences keep their grip for life. However, Freud also argued that people who undergo **psychoanalysis** can gain **more free will** and take responsibility for their choices: the more a person makes conscious what was unconscious, the more they can take charge of their own life (Solnit, 1992). Psychoanalysis is therefore presented as potentially **liberating** people from determinism.

**Importance of the past vs the present: the past.** The decisive events are in the first 5 years; adult behaviour is the working out of childhood conflicts. This is also the basis of a criticism in 2.12: that Freud ignored the pull of the future, of goals and aspirations.

**Freud on the six issues**

| Issue | Freud's position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Determinism** (psychoanalysis can increase free will) | Instincts and pre-5 experience fix behaviour; making the unconscious conscious frees the person |
| Nature vs nurture | **Both** (middle ground) | Id and stages inherited; ego and superego content learned from parents |
| Past vs present | **Past** | Personality crystallised by about age 5 |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Both** | Same id and stages for all; unique ego, superego and character types |
| Equilibrium vs growth | **Equilibrium** | The one goal of life is tension reduction (homeostasis) |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Pessimism** | Endless, mostly losing battle with the id; doomed to anxiety and conflict |

> **Trap.** "Freud was a strict determinist with no room for free will" is close to but not exactly the 11th-edition position. Schultz add that **psychoanalysis can increase free will**. On nature-nurture, the answer is **both**, not "nature". On uniqueness, the answer is **both**, not "universality".

## 2.9 Assessment in Freud's Theory

For Freud the **unconscious** is the main motivating force in life, the store of all the childhood conflicts that have been **repressed** out of awareness. The whole goal of psychoanalysis is to bring those repressed memories, fears and thoughts **back into consciousness**. The obvious problem is how anyone can examine something that, by definition, is hidden from the person who has it. Over years of work with patients Freud developed **two methods of assessment**: **free association** and **dream analysis**.

### Free association

**Origins: Breuer and catharsis.** The technique owes much to **Josef Breuer**, a Viennese physician who befriended Freud in his early years of practice. Breuer was treating a young woman with symptoms of **hysteria** and found that **hypnotising** her allowed her to remember **repressed events**. Recalling them, actually reliving the experiences, brought **relief from the symptoms**. (Classic background: this patient is the famous "Anna O.", whose case appeared in Breuer and Freud's *Studies on Hysteria*, 1895; she called the method the "talking cure".)

Freud adopted hypnosis with some success and called the process **catharsis**, from the **Greek word for purification**: the expression of emotion that is expected to reduce disturbing symptoms. He later **abandoned hypnosis**, for two reasons: he had trouble hypnotising some patients, and some patients who revealed disturbing events under hypnosis could not remember them afterwards when questioned.

**The couch.** Looking for another route to repressed material, Freud had patients **lie on a couch** while he sat **behind it, out of sight**. His reasons were partly personal and partly professional. He said he could not stand being stared at for eight or more hours a day. He also explained that while listening he let his own unconscious thoughts run, and did not want his facial expressions to give patients material to interpret or to influence what they said (quoted in Lingiardi & De Bei, 2011).

**The method.** Patients were encouraged to relax, concentrate on the past and engage in a kind of **daydreaming out loud**, saying **whatever came to mind**. They were to report every idea and image spontaneously and exactly as it occurred, however **trivial, embarrassing or painful**, without leaving anything out, rearranging it or tidying it up.

The theoretical assumption behind the method is the most testable part: Freud believed **nothing in free association is random**, and that what emerges is **not under the patient's conscious choice**. The material is **predetermined**, forced out by the nature of the patient's conflict. This is psychic determinism (2.8) applied to the consulting room.

### Free association is not always so free

Freud found that the method did not always flow. Some memories were evidently too painful to talk about, and the patient balked at disclosing them. He called these moments **resistances**. Rather than treating them as obstacles, he treated them as **signposts**: a resistance means the analysis is getting **close to the source** of the patient's problem, that treatment is on the right track, and that the analyst should keep probing in that area. Part of the analyst's job is to **break down or overcome** resistances so the patient can face the repressed experience.

A clinical picture: a patient who talks fluently about work and childhood holidays, but who falls silent, changes the subject, forgets the next appointment or jokes whenever her father's drinking comes up. For the analyst, that pattern is itself data pointing to where the conflict lies.

> **Trap.** **Resistance** is a phenomenon **within free association** (blocking or refusing to disclose painful memories), and Freud read it as a **good sign** that the analysis is near the core conflict. It is not the same as a defence mechanism in Table 2.1, though it is driven by the same need to avoid anxiety.

### Dream analysis

Freud believed dreams express, in **symbolic** form, **repressed desires, fears and conflicts**. These have been repressed so thoroughly that they can surface only in **disguise**, during sleep. (Classic background: Freud called the interpretation of dreams the "royal road" to the unconscious.)

He distinguished **two aspects** of a dream:

- The **manifest content**: the actual events of the dream as the dreamer experiences and reports them.
- The **latent content**: the **hidden, symbolic meaning** of those events.

The analyst's task is to work from the manifest content to the latent content. Over the years Freud found **recurrent symbols** in patients' dreams, events that seemed to mean the same thing for nearly everyone (Table 2.3).

**Table 2.3** Dream symbols and their latent psychoanalytic meaning (as Freud read them)

| Dream symbol or event | Latent meaning |
|---|---|
| House with a smooth front | Male body |
| House with ledges or balconies | Female body |
| King and queen | The parents |
| Small animals | Children |
| Children | Genital organs |
| Playing with children | Masturbation |
| Going bald; having a tooth pulled | Castration |
| Long objects (tree trunks, umbrellas, ties, snakes, candles) | Male genitals |
| Enclosed spaces (boxes, ovens, cupboards, caves, pockets) | Female genitals |
| Climbing stairs or ladders, driving a car, riding a horse, crossing a bridge | Sexual intercourse |
| Bathing | Birth |
| Setting out on a journey | Dying |
| Being naked in a crowd | Wanting to be noticed |
| Flying | Wanting to be admired |
| Falling | Wanting to return to a satisfied, protected state such as childhood |

Freud attached an important caution to this list: despite the apparent universality of the symbols, **many symbols are specific to the individual** and could mean something different for someone else. The list is a starting point, not a dictionary.

Several further features of dreams matter for interpretation:

- Dreams present conflicts in **condensed, intensified** form.
- Dream events rarely have a single cause; **any element can have many sources**. (Classic background: Freud called the compressing of several latent ideas into one image **condensation**, and the shifting of emotional weight onto a trivial element **displacement**; together with symbolisation and secondary revision they make up the "dream work". Schultz mention only the condensed character of dreams.)
- Dreams can have **mundane origins**. External physical stimuli, such as the room temperature or contact with a bed partner, can trigger a dream, and so can internal ones, such as a fever or an upset stomach.

Schultz add two modern studies here. In **Hong Kong**, people who slept **prone** (flat on the stomach) were more likely to dream of **sexual activity**, of being **smothered**, locked up, tied, or unable to move than people who slept in other positions (**Yu, 2012**), a neat case of a bodily stimulus shaping dream content. A large **German** study found the **five most typical dreams** were **flying, trying something again and again, being chased, sexual experiences, and school-related matters** (**Goritz, 2014**).

Finally, a pointed irony. Of the **more than 40** of his own dreams that Freud described in ***The Interpretation of Dreams*** (classic background: published 1900), **only a few had sexual content**, despite his conviction that dreams usually involve an infantile sexual wish. The dominant theme in his reported dreams was **ambition**, a trait he strongly **denied** having. A Freudian could hardly ask for a clearer example of a defence at work.

### Uncovering the conflicts

Both techniques yield a great deal of repressed material, but always in **disguised or symbolic form**. The therapist therefore has to **interpret**, translating it for the patient. Freud compared this to the work of an **archaeologist** reconstructing a community destroyed and buried for centuries: just as the archaeologist rebuilds a structure from broken fragments, the analyst rebuilds an experience from buried, fragmented memories.

The consequence is important for the evaluation that follows: assessment in Freud's system, the uncovering of a patient's unconscious conflicts, **depends on the skill, training and experience of the analyst**. There is no standard scoring, no norm, no second rater; the interpretation is only as good as the interpreter. That is exactly the weakness the next module begins with.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition names **two** Freudian assessment methods: **free association** and **dream analysis**. Classic background: older guides also list analysis of **slips of the tongue** (parapraxes) and of **transference** (the patient redirecting feelings about parents onto the analyst; the analyst's own emotional reactions to the patient are **countertransference**). Schultz discuss the Freudian slip only as a research topic in 2.10. If a question asks for Freud's two assessment techniques in Schultz, answer free association and dream analysis.

## 2.10 Criticisms of Freud's Research

This is the longest module in the chapter and the one most likely to be examined in "supported / not supported" form. It has two halves. The first asks how good Freud's **own evidence** was. The second reviews what happened when later researchers **tested his concepts** with experiments and surveys. Keep the two apart: a concept can rest on weak original evidence and still receive modern support (repression, the unconscious), or rest on a confident clinical claim and fail (dreams as wish fulfilment).

### The case study and its limits

Freud's main research method was the **case study**: a detailed history of one individual, built from many sources. Chapter 1 of the book has already listed its limitations, and they apply in full here:

- It does **not rely on objective observation**.
- Its data are **not gathered systematically**.
- The situation (the analytic session) **cannot be duplicated** or independently **verified**.
- We cannot **vary the conditions of childhood** in which patients grow up, nor recreate a person's home in a laboratory.

So, unlike a controlled experiment, clinical observations **cannot be repeated**.

**The data themselves.** A more fundamental objection concerns what Freud actually recorded. He kept **no verbatim records** of sessions and advised analysts **not to take notes** during them, believing it would distract attention from the patient's words. He wrote his notes **several hours later**, from memory, in the evening after work (Grubrich-Simitis, 1998). Three problems follow:

1. The data may be **incomplete**, consisting only of what he remembered hours afterwards.
2. His memory may have been **selective**: he may have recorded mainly material that fitted the theory, or interpreted material so that it did.
3. Even a perfect record would not establish whether the **patients' reports were true**. Freud made few attempts to check their stories, for instance by asking friends and relatives about the events described.

Schultz's conclusion: the first step of Freud's research, **data collection**, must be judged **incomplete and possibly inaccurate**. His notes might have been accurate, but we cannot know, because we cannot compare his reports with what patients actually said.

**The seduction stories revisited.** Some critics go further and argue that Freud's patients did not reveal childhood sexual experiences at all, because in most cases none had occurred; Freud **inferred** the seduction stories from their **symptoms**. Although he claimed that nearly all his women patients reported seduction by their fathers, his **case notes do not show any patient making that claim** (**Kihlstrom, 1994**). Others agree that Freud supplied the seduction accounts without hearing them, because he had already decided that seduction caused neurosis. Still others charge that he may have used **suggestion** to **implant** memories of seductions that never happened (**McCullough, 2001**); Webster (1995) says that where patients did not volunteer sexual material, Freud did not hesitate to steer them towards it.

> **Trap.** Notice how this criticism cuts against 2.1. The biography presents the abandoned seduction theory as possibly **true** (abuse is common, and Freud privately admitted real cases). The research critics argue that the stories may have been **inferred or suggested by Freud**. Both views appear in the 11th edition; a question can be built on either.

**The sample.** Freud's research rests on a **small, unrepresentative sample**: himself, plus the people who came to him for analysis. Only about **a dozen** cases are described in detail in his writings, and most were **young, unmarried, upper-class, well-educated women**. Generalising from that group to humanity is hard.

**Notes versus published cases: the Rat Man.** There may also be gaps between Freud's session notes and the case histories he published from them. Researchers who compared his notes with the published case of the **Rat Man**, one of his most famous patients, found **three discrepancies**: the **length of the analysis was extended**, the **sequence of events** the patient disclosed was **wrong**, and the claim that the analysis **cured** the patient was **unsupported** (**Eagle, 1988; Mahoney, 1986**). Whether Freud made these changes deliberately (to strengthen the theory, or his ego) or unconsciously cannot be known, nor whether other cases were similarly altered, because Freud **destroyed most of his patient files** soon after writing them up.

**The cases as evidence.** Finally, it has been argued that **none** of Freud's handful of published cases offers compelling support for the theory. His biographer Sulloway (1992) remarked that some present such dubious evidence that one wonders why Freud published them at all.

**What the critique does and does not mean.** Schultz add a balanced caution. These criticisms apply equally to **later theorists** who relied on case studies and patients' reports, and they do **not** automatically mean the work is worthless: Freud and others provided a wealth of insight into personality. Anyone who accepts their ideas, however, has to accept them for reasons **other than experimental proof**.

### Freud's negative views on experimental research

Freud knew the experimental method but had little faith in it; Chiesa (2010) summarises his view as being that scientific research and psychoanalysis are inherently incompatible. When an American psychologist sent him reports of experiments designed to validate Freudian concepts, Freud reportedly threw the reprints across the table impatiently and replied that he did not put much value on such confirmation (**Rosenzweig, 1985**). (Classic background: the psychologist was Saul Rosenzweig, writing in 1934 about his experimental work on repression.) Freud saw no need for such studies because, as Holzman (1994) puts it, they told him nothing he had not already learned from patients.

He insisted that his work **was** scientific, that he had ample proof, and that **only psychoanalysts who used his techniques** were qualified to judge it. Psychoanalysis, he wrote, rested on countless observations, and only someone who had repeated those observations on himself and others could form a judgement of it (Freud, 1940).

The difficulty is circular. The observations Freud says must be repeated **cannot be repeated**, because nobody knows exactly what he did when collecting his data and turning observations into hypotheses.

> **Trap.** Freud did **not** say psychoanalysis was unscientific. He claimed it was scientific but that only trained analysts could judge it, and he dismissed **laboratory** confirmation as unnecessary. "Freud welcomed experimental tests of his theory" is false.

### Scientific testing of Freudian concepts

Since Freud's death in 1939 many of his ideas have been tested. The landmark review is **Fisher and Greenberg (1977, 1996)**, who evaluated about **2,500 studies**. They **excluded case histories** and restricted themselves to data with a high degree of objectivity. Their findings fall into three groups, and this three-way split is one of the most examinable lists in the chapter.

**Fisher and Greenberg's verdicts**

| Verdict | Concepts |
|---|---|
| **Could not be tested** experimentally | **Id, ego, superego, death wish, libido, anxiety** |
| Testable and **supported**, however slightly | **Oral and anal character types**; the basic **Oedipal triangle**; **castration anxiety**; the idea that **females resolve the Oedipal dilemma by having a child** to compensate for lacking a penis |
| Testable and **not supported** | **Dreams** as the disguised voice of repressed wishes; the boy's Oedipal resolution through **identification with the father** and adoption of his superego **out of fear**; **women's inadequately developed superegos**; the **psychosexual stages**; any link between **Oedipal variables and later sexual problems** |

> **Trap.** Two things trip students. (1) The basic Oedipal **triangle** and castration anxiety got slight support, but the boy's **resolution through fear-driven identification** did **not**. Same complex, different verdicts for different parts. (2) The **ego** is on Fisher and Greenberg's untestable list, yet the next section reports research on **ego control and ego resiliency**. The resolution: later researchers operationalised two specific ego functions; they did not test the ego as Freud defined it.

The rest of the module walks through the research areas one at a time.

### Research on the unconscious

That **unconscious forces influence conscious thought and behaviour** is now **well established**, and current research suggests unconscious influence may be **even more pervasive** than Freud thought (Custers & Aarts, 2010; Scott & Dienes, 2010; Gafner, 2012). Pervin (2003) summarises the consensus that much psychological functioning happens without conscious choice and that some behaviour actually runs against what we consciously want. Much of the information processing in cognition is unconscious (Armstrong & Dienes, 2014), and some researchers propose that the causal mechanisms behind **all** thought and behaviour may be unconscious (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; Wegner & Wheatley, 1999).

The modern unconscious is, however, a different creature from Freud's. Bornstein and Masling (1998) describe it as "smarter" than first believed: capable of processing complex verbal and visual information, anticipating and planning for future events, and contributing to problem solving, hypothesis testing and creativity, rather than being merely a store of drives and impulses.

> **Trap.** Research supports **unconscious influence**, not Freud's particular **content** for the unconscious. The cognitive unconscious of modern research is intelligent and adaptive; Freud's is a cauldron of sexual and aggressive wishes. An option saying "modern research confirms Freud's view that the unconscious is primarily sexual" is wrong.

#### Subliminal perception

Much of this work uses **subliminal perception**: presenting stimuli **below the threshold of conscious awareness** (from Latin *sub*, below, and *limen*, threshold). People cannot report seeing the stimuli, yet their thoughts and behaviour are affected by them.

- **Shevrin (1977).** Participants were shown words and pictures too briefly to perceive consciously, then asked to **free-associate**. Their associations reflected the unseen stimuli: after a picture of a **bee**, for example, the associations included **"sting"** and **"honey"**. Many such studies support the view that cognitive activity is influenced by the unconscious (Westen, 1998).

#### Effects on behaviour

- **Bargh, Gollwitzer, Lee-Chai, Barndollar and Troetschel (2001).** In experiments on college students in the **United States and Germany**, **goals** were activated outside awareness, and the students then acted to satisfy those goals without knowing it. A subliminally primed goal of **performing well** improved task performance; a primed goal of **cooperation** produced cooperative behaviour. The authors concluded that behavioural goals can be activated with no conscious choice involved.
- **Winkielman, Berridge and Wilbarger (2005).** American college students shown **happy faces** subliminally **drank more** of an available beverage than students shown **angry faces**, said they were willing to **pay more** for it, and **wanted more** of it. None consciously saw the faces.

#### Effects on emotional processes

- **Silverman and Weinberger (1985).** The words **"Mommy and I are one"** were flashed for **4 milliseconds** together with a picture of a man and a woman joined at the shoulders. **Male schizophrenic patients** who received this subliminal message **improved more** than controls. **Female** schizophrenic patients did **not** improve with that message but **did** improve with **"Daddy and I are one"**. Note the Oedipal logic: the comforting fusion message works when it names the **opposite-sex** parent.
- **Weinberger and Silverman (1990).** In other studies the "Mommy and I are one" message helped various participants **stop smoking and drinking**, become **more assertive**, eat **more healthily** and **reduce fears**, so a subliminal message has been shown to have **therapeutic value**.
- **Hunt, Keogh and French (2006).** Adults in **England** high in **anxiety sensitivity** were far more likely than low scorers to detect **anxiety-related words** presented below the level of awareness; their sensitivity made them more vigilant.
- **Hansel and von Kanel (2013).** Subliminally presented **fearful faces** raised participants' **physiological stress levels**, although they never consciously saw the faces.

### Research on the ego: ego control and ego resiliency

Freud's ego mediates between reality and the id, controlling and postponing the id's demands. Psychoanalytic researchers have identified **two components** of the ego.

**Ego control** is closest to Freud's original idea: the **amount of control** a person exerts over impulses and feelings. It runs from **under-controlled** (unable to restrain any impulses) to **over-controlled** (tightly inhibiting their expression), and **both extremes are maladaptive**.

**Ego resiliency** is the **flexibility** to adjust one's usual level of ego control to meet daily changes in the environment. People low in it are called **ego brittle**: they cannot change their level of control to meet challenges. People high in it can tighten or loosen control as the situation demands.

An example makes the distinction concrete. A student who is always tightly controlled, studying rigidly and never relaxing even at a friend's wedding, is **over-controlled** and also **ego brittle**. A student who is disciplined during exams but can let go at the wedding has a moderate ego control and **high ego resiliency**.

- **Paris and Helson (2002).** **Mothers aged 21 to 27** who rated their mothering experience as positive and satisfying had **high ego resiliency**; those who rated it negatively had **lower** ego resiliency. The researchers suggested that difficult experiences, setbacks and failures tend to **lower** ego resiliency.

#### Ego control among children

- Children **low in ego control** are rated by teachers as more **aggressive** and less **compliant and orderly**. Teachers see highly **ego-resilient** children as **handling stress better**, **less anxious**, and **needing less reassurance**.
- **Smeekens, Riksen-Walraven and van Bakel (2007).** **Five-year-olds in the Netherlands** with **low ego resiliency** showed more **physiological signs of stress** during negative situations, usually interactions with their **parents**; high-resiliency children did not.
- High ego resiliency correlates positively with **intelligence, school grades, popularity with peers, life satisfaction and social functioning**. **Adolescent drug abuse** is predicted by **low ego control** in both sexes and by **low ego resiliency in girls** (Block & Block, 1980; Chung, 2008; Hofer, Eisenberg, & Reiser, 2010; Shiner, 1988).
- **Letzring, Block and Funder (2005).** College students **low** in ego control were **unpredictable, assertive, rebellious, moody and self-indulgent**; those **very high** in ego control were **bland, consistent, dependable and calm**; those **high in ego resilience** were **assertive, poised, socially skilled and cheerful**.
- **Block and Block (2006).** A **longitudinal** study of Americans assessed periodically from **age 3 to 23** found both ego control and ego resiliency were generally **stronger later in life** than in childhood, and individual differences in ego control could be seen early.
- **Cross-cultural and gender differences.** In **Italy**, ego resilience was markedly **stable from 16 to 20** in both sexes. In **Sweden**, **boys became less** ego resilient in adolescence while **girls became more** resilient, suggesting cultural and gender differences (Chuang, Lamb, & Hwang, 2006; Vecchione, Alessandri, Barbaranelli, & Gerbino, 2010).
- **Mental health.** Across the **United States, Italy, Spain, Portugal, South Korea and Pakistan**, with samples from college students to the elderly to **amputee war veterans**, high ego resiliency went with high **subjective well-being, extraversion and agreeableness** (Alessandri, Vecchione, Caprara, & Letzring, 2012; Gunsung, 2013; Seaton, 2014; Zeb, Naqvi, & Zonash, 2013).

> **Trap.** Ego **control** = how much you restrain impulses (a level, under to over). Ego **resiliency** = how flexibly you can shift that level (low = "ego brittle"). Over-control is **not** the healthy end: both extremes of ego control are maladaptive; it is **resiliency** that tracks good outcomes.

### Catharsis

For Freud, **catharsis** meant the physical expression of an emotion through recalling a traumatic event, which often relieved the symptom. In popular culture the word has drifted to mean **venting** emotion to reduce anger and aggression; self-help books advise hitting a pillow or punching bag or breaking a dish. Does venting work? The research answer is **no**.

- **Bushman, Baumeister and Stack (1999).** College students read a message that either **supported or disputed** the idea that venting relieves anger. Some were then **provoked**: an essay they had written was harshly criticised and described as one of the worst ever. Provoked students who had read the **pro-catharsis** message were significantly more likely to **hit a punching bag**. In a **second experiment**, pro-catharsis students not only hit the bag but were also **more aggressive towards the person who had criticised them**, and even towards **innocent people** who had nothing to do with their anger. Hitting the bag did not dissipate the anger and may have **increased** it.
- Further studies confirmed that venting **increases** the likelihood of more anger and does **not reduce** negative emotion (Bushman, 2002; Lohr, Olatunji, Baumeister, & Bushman, 2007).
- **Bushman and Whitaker (2010).** People who believed venting was good for them were more attracted to **violent video games**.

> **Trap.** The research tested **popular "venting" catharsis**, not Breuer and Freud's original cathartic recall of trauma, but the 11th edition's chapter summary lists **catharsis** among concepts **not** validated. "Research shows that venting anger on a punching bag reduces aggression" is false: it **increases** later aggression, including towards innocent people.

### Displacement

**Displacement** shifts id impulses from an unavailable or threatening object to a substitute. It is one of the better-supported mechanisms.

- **Marcus-Newhall, Pedersen, Miller and Carlson (2000).** A **meta-analysis of 97 studies** concluded that **displaced aggression** is a **real and reliable** phenomenon, and that the more **negative and stressful** the setting, the **more intense** the displacement.
- **Bushman, Bonacci, Pedersen, Vasquez and Miller (2005).** College students were provoked to anger; one group then spent **25 minutes** focusing on their angry thoughts and feelings (**rumination**). They were far more likely to show **displaced aggression** than provoked students who did not brood. Dwelling on anger keeps it alive and makes outward aggression more likely, which fits neatly with the catharsis findings above.

### Repression

- **Glucksberg and King (1967).** Participants memorised **two lists of words** flashed on a screen; some words across the lists were **conceptually related** (for example, cats and dogs are both animals). Some words on the **first list** were paired with **electric shock**; none on the second list were. On testing, participants **forgot the shocked words** but remembered unshocked ones, and they also failed to recall words on the **second list** that were **conceptually similar** to the shocked words. The researchers concluded that the threatening words had been **pushed out of awareness**, and that the forgetting spread to related material.

#### Repressors and non-repressors

Research has also compared people who **habitually repress** unpleasant experiences (**repressors**) with those who do not.

- Repressors are **not simply forgetful**. They have trouble recalling only **specific negative, stressful, fearful and traumatic** experiences (**Saunders, Worth, Vallath, & Fernandes, 2014**).
- They tend to be **low in anxiety and high in defensiveness**, and recall **fewer negative childhood memories** (Davis, 1987; Myers & Derakshan, 2004).
- They are far more likely to **avoid romantic attachments** (Davis, 1987; Vetere & Myers, 2002).
- They are **less likely to smoke and drink**, yet score **higher** on the belief that heavy drinking would not harm them personally (Shirachi & Spirrison, 2006).
- They are much more likely to **deny having traits they find emotionally threatening** (selfishness, laziness, rudeness, dishonesty), and they have **fewer unpleasant emotional memories available** for retrieval, because those have been repressed (Newman, Duff, & Baumeister, 1997; Newman & McKinney, 2002; Schimmack & Hartmann, 1997).
- **Davis (1987).** Shown neutral and embarrassing or threatening pictures, repressors **avoided looking** at the threatening ones. Asked to free-associate to **sexual or aggressive phrases**, repressors were **highly aroused physiologically** but their **words showed no sign** of anger or sexual arousal; non-repressors' words did show it. This dissociation between body and speech is close to a laboratory picture of repression itself.
- **Boden and Baumeister (1997).** After watching a **grisly film** about animal mutation and lingering death from **nuclear testing**, repressors asked to recall a happy experience retrieved **more pleasant memories** than non-repressors. They coped with the film by **accessing positive memories**, and so experienced the distressing emotions less often and less intensely. They were not pretending; the repression was genuinely successful.
- **Phipps and Steele (2002).** Among American children, those with **cancer or other chronic debilitating illness** were **more likely to be repressors** and **less likely to express anger** than healthy children.

### Projection

- **Rucker and Pratkanis (2001).** Accusing another person of **lying and cheating** in a game **increased the blame** placed on that person and **reduced the blame** participants placed on **themselves** for the same behaviour.
- **Cramer (2009).** Adults who grew up in a **higher social class** are **more likely** to use projection than those raised in lower-class families.
- **Schul and Vinokur (2000).** **Unemployed job seekers** projected their own **depression** about unemployment onto their **partners** when making everyday judgements about them, and the **more alike** the partners actually were in depression, the **stronger** the projection. The authors observed that people assume their spouses resemble them most when the spouses really do. In this case, projection was **accurate**.

### A hierarchy of defence mechanisms

Several studies suggest the defences form a **developmental hierarchy**: **simpler** mechanisms are used **earlier** in life and **more complex** ones appear with age.

- **Tallandini and Caudek (2010).** **Denial**, a simple low-level defence, is used mostly by **young children** and less by adolescents; **identification**, more complex, is used much more by **adolescents**. Denial is used more by **boys**; **girls** lean more on three more complex defences: **regression, displacement and reaction formation**.
- **Porcerelli, Thomas, Hibbard and Cogan (1998).** Students in **grades 2, 5, 8 and 11 and first-year college** responded to **Thematic Apperception Test** pictures. Use of **denial and projection decreased** with age while **identification increased**.
- **Cramer (2007).** A longitudinal study of **150 students aged 11 to 18** found **projection and identification** were used more than **denial**, and their use **increased** from early to late adolescence.
- **Cramer and Block (1998).** People tested in **nursery school** and again at **age 23**. Denial usually declines with age, but the **men** still using denial at 23 had shown psychological problems in nursery school: **emotional immaturity and unworthiness**, and **low personal competence and ego resiliency**. No such link appeared for **women**; the authors suggested boys may be **more vulnerable to stress**.
- **Diehl, Chui, Hay, Lumley, Gruhn and Labouvie-Vief (2014).** In American adults, use of **displacement and regression decreased** from adolescence to early old age in the **mid-60s**; people **older** than that **reverted** to the more maladaptive defences of their youth.
- **Canada (Arokach, 2006; Couturier & Lock, 2006).** **Denial** was far commoner among teenage girls with **anorexia nervosa**, and among older women **abused by their husbands**, than among comparison groups, apparently to minimise or distance themselves from their situations.
- **Mahalik, Cournoyer, DeFrank, Cherry and Napolitano (1998).** Men who guarded against feelings of weakness by being **powerful and competitive** and **avoiding emotional expression** used more **immature** defences; men with less need to dominate and freer emotional expression used more **mature** ones.
- **Cramer and Kelly (2010).** Parents who **abuse their children** tend to use the immature defence of **denial**.

> **Trap.** **Identification** appears in this research as a relatively **mature** defence that **increases** with age, although it is **not** one of the eight mechanisms in Table 2.1. **Denial** is the low-level, early, **decreasing** defence (more in boys). If asked which defence increases from childhood through adolescence: identification (and, in Cramer 2007, projection within adolescence); which decreases: denial.

### Defence mechanisms in Asian cultures

The idea of defences was developed in **Europe** from studies of **White middle-class** patients, and most later research used American or European participants.

- **Tori and Bilmes (2002).** Comparing Americans with **Asian Buddhists in Thailand**, they found strong **similarity** between the cultures in the use of **regression, reaction formation, projection, repression, denial and compensation**.
- **Xiang, Li and Shen (2010).** Chinese college students diagnosed with **borderline personality disorder** used more **immature** defences than students without the disorder.

### Dreams

The headline verdict comes first. Early dream research **confirmed** Freud's idea that dreams reflect **emotional concerns**, in disguised or symbolic form. It does **not** support his idea that dreams are the **fulfilment of wishes**. His dream theory has nonetheless prompted a very large research literature, grouped by Schultz into five themes.

#### Effect of traumatic experiences on dreams

Dreams are often full of highly emotional material from the dreamer's own life, past and present.

- **Schredl and Piel (2006).** Large-scale surveys of **Germans** between **1956 and 2000** showed that people old enough to have been directly affected by the **Second World War (1939 to 1945)** were still having highly emotional **war-related dreams more than 50 years** after it ended.
- **Barrett, Sogolow, Oh, Panton, Grayson and Justiniano (2014).** **British soldiers** who had been **prisoners of the Germans** in the Second World War dreamed of **battle, imprisonment, escape attempts and hunger**.
- **Phelps and Forbes (2012).** Survivors of the 21st-century wars in **Iraq and Afghanistan** had dreams marked by **flashbacks** to traumatic battle scenes and other combat stress.
- **Valli, Revonsuo, Palkas and Punamaki (2006).** **Kurdish and Palestinian children** exposed to physical danger in daily life dreamed of **threatening and traumatic** situations far more than children raised in more peaceful surroundings.

#### More ordinary dream content

Much research confirms that dreams also reflect **mundane, everyday experience**, and that the **emotional intensity** of that experience and the person's **mood** shape the dream story (Schredl, 2006; Schredl, Funkhouser, & Arn, 2006). A bad day tends to bring bad dreams. Even smells matter:

- **Hutson (2010).** In a German sleep laboratory, just **10 seconds** of the smell of **roses** during sleep produced **more pleasant dreams** than the smell of **rotten eggs**.
- **Sex differences (Blume-Marcovici, 2010; Schredl, 2010a).** In American and German adults, **women recall their dreams far more** than men, perhaps because they wake more often in the night. **Men dream more about male characters**, while women dream about both sexes roughly equally. **Men have many more aggressive dreams**.
- **Pesant and Zadra (2006).** Canadian adults who kept long-term **dream diaries**: those with **lower psychological well-being** reported more dreams of **aggression towards others, negative emotions, and failure and misfortune**; those with **higher** well-being dreamed of **friendly interactions, positive emotions, and success and good fortune**.
- **Rainville and Rush (2009).** College students often dream about **sex**; **male** students dream more about **bonding with friends**, **female** students more about **pregnancy, weddings and shopping**.

#### Effects of electronic media on dreams

- **Gackenbach, Kuruvilla and Dopko (2009).** Mobile phones, computers, DVDs, video games and interactive internet use all influence the **content and frequency** of dreams. Heavy **video-game players** have more **bizarre** dreams containing **dead and imaginary characters** (also **Schneider & Domhoff, 2006**).
- **British children (Lambrecht, Schredl, Henley-Einion, & Blagrove, 2013; Stephan, Schredl, Henley-Einion, & Blagrove, 2012).** What children **watched on television** and what they **read** affected their dreams, and the **more time** spent on an activity, the **more** it shaped dream content.
- **Schwitzgebel, Huang and Zhou (2006).** **Chinese college students** who had grown up watching **black-and-white** television and films said they dreamed in **black and white**; those with more exposure to **colour** media dreamed in **colour**.
- **Gackenbach, Darlington, Ferguson and Boyes (2013).** Among students who had experienced **trauma**, heavy video-game players were **less troubled by nightmares**, but the protective effect held **only for men**, not women.

#### Cultural differences in dreams

- **Kracke (2010).** The **Parintintin** people of **Brazil's Amazon rain forest** treat dreams as a way of **telling the future** and take their own and their relatives' dreams seriously.
- **Xian-Li and Guang-Xing (2006).** **Chinese** students reported **more familiar people** and **fewer aggressive situations** in their dreams than **American** students.
- **Yu (2010).** Chinese dreams contained far more of what Freud would call **sex symbols** (**knives, swords and daggers**) than American dreams.
- **Fiske and Pillemer (2006).** **White** American students were much more likely than **Asian-American** students to have **told their parents about their dreams** in childhood, to share dreams with friends as they grew older, and to **value** their dreams; Asian-American students were more **secretive** and reluctant to discuss them.
- **Mazandarani, Aguilar-Vafaie and Domhoff (2013).** **Iranian** students were far more likely than **American** students to dream of **people they know**, of events **indoors**, and to feel **more positive emotions** in dreams.

#### Interpreting our dreams

People in **both Eastern and Western cultures** tend to believe dreams may contain **hidden personal truths** that tell them something useful about themselves and their world (**Morewedge & Norton, 2009**), so interest in dream interpretation is widespread.

- **Salem, Ragab and Abdel (2009); Schredl (2010b).** Among Islamic students in the **United Arab Emirates**, about **two-thirds of the women** and **one-third of the men** expressed great interest in interpreting their dreams. **Salem (2014)** found UAE Muslims far more interested in dream interpretation than a same-age **Canadian** sample.
- **Horikawa, Tamaki, Miyawaki and Kamitani (2013).** **Japanese** researchers woke sleeping participants **every 6 minutes** and asked what they had just been seeing, then trained a computer program on brain-activity patterns recorded by **MRI** during waking and sleep. The computer learned to decode dream images with an average accuracy of **66 percent**, which the authors present as a first step towards machine interpretation of dreams.

> **Trap.** "Research supports Freud's view that dreams are disguised wish fulfilments" is **false**. What is supported is that dreams reflect **emotional concerns**, current mood and daily experience, including trauma. Fisher and Greenberg also listed dreams-as-disguised-wishes as **not supported**.

### The Oedipus complex

Most research concludes there is **no validity at all** to the Oedipus complex as Freud described it (**Kupfersmid, 1995**). But a number of studies have looked at a key ingredient: children's behaviour and attitudes towards the same-sex and opposite-sex parent.

- **Watson and Getz (1990).** Parents of children aged **3 to 6** recorded the affectionate and the aggressive or hostile behaviours their children directed at them. **Affection towards the opposite-sex parent** and **aggression towards the same-sex parent** were much more frequent than the reverse. These Oedipal-type behaviours **peaked around age 4** and began to **decline by age 5**, which fits Schultz's 4-to-5 dating of the phallic stage.
- **Hall and Van de Castle (1965).** A classic dream study: dreams with **castration-anxiety** themes were reported significantly more often by **men**, while dreams expressing **castration wishes or penis envy** came significantly more often from **women**.
- **R. L. Jones (1994).** Freud proposed that penis envy turns the girl towards her father and that this wish is later replaced by the wish for a baby. College-age women exposed **subliminally** to **pregnancy themes** later gave **inkblot** responses with significantly **more phallic imagery** than control women or men exposed to the same stimuli. The researchers took this as support for Freud's idea that **pregnancy has phallic significance** for women.
- **Maio, Fincham and Lycett (2000).** In **Wales**, children aged **12 to 14** who felt **ambivalent** towards their **fathers** (a mix of love and hatred) showed **less secure attachment** to other people than children without that ambivalence, which the researchers read as support for Freud's emphasis on the father's role in shaping later relationships.

### Oral and anal personality types

- **Masling, Rabie and Blondheim (1967).** A strong relationship between an **oral orientation**, identified with the **Rorschach**, and **obesity**, consistent with Freud's claim that oral types are preoccupied with eating and drinking.
- **Tribich and Messer (1974).** **Oral** types **conformed** more to an authority figure's suggestions than **anal** types. This fits the theory: oral personalities should be **dependent and submissive**, anal personalities **hostile** and resistant to conformity.
- **O'Neill and Bornstein (1990).** Freud claimed **women were more orally dependent** than men; this study found **no sex difference**.
- Overall there is **some** research support for both the **oral and anal** types (**Westen, 1998**), but **little evidence for the phallic** type.
- **Haslam (2011).** The **anal character** keeps resurfacing in psychology under new names: **conscientiousness**, **orderliness**, **obstinacy**, and **obsessive-compulsive personality disorder**.

### Age and personality development

Freud held that personality was formed by **about age 5** and changed little afterwards. The evidence disagrees.

- **Kagan, Kearsley and Zelazo (1978).** Follow-up studies over **6 to 7 years** showed that preschool children's personality characteristics **changed dramatically**.
- Other studies suggest that **middle childhood (ages 7 to 12)** may matter **more** than early childhood for adult personality patterns.
- **Kagan (1999).** Reviewing the literature, **Jerome Kagan** concluded that personality depends more on **temperament** and on **experiences in later childhood** than on early parent-child interactions.

The first 5 years clearly do affect personality, but personality **continues to develop** well beyond them.

### The Freudian slip

Freud claimed that apparently ordinary forgetting or a casual **slip of the tongue** actually reflects **unconscious motives or anxieties**.

- **Motley (1987).** Two groups of **men** saw the same **word pairs** on a computer screen and, when a **buzzer** sounded, said them aloud. One group had **electrodes** attached and was told it would receive a **painful electric shock**, an experimental way of inducing anxiety. In the other group the experimenter was an **attractive, provocatively dressed woman**, and participants took a **test of sexual anxiety**. Men anxious about shock produced slips like **"damn shock"** when shown **"sham dock"**; men in the sexual condition produced slips like **"nude breasts"** from **"brood nests"**, and those scoring **highest on sexual anxiety** made the **most** sex-related slips. A **control group**, shown the same words without either anxiety condition, made **no** such slips.

Not every speech error is a Freudian slip, but the research suggests **some** are what Freud said: hidden anxieties surfacing in embarrassing ways. Schultz add two public examples (Pincott, 2013): an American president who told teachers he would like to "spank" them, plainly meaning "thank", and a politician who referred to "the breast" when he meant "the best and the brightest".

### Repressed memories of childhood sexual abuse

In the **late 1980s** the question of repressed memories returned through sensational **court cases**. People who claimed to have suddenly recalled abuse from years earlier brought charges: women accused fathers, uncles and family friends, and men accused priests, coaches and teachers. Some defendants went to prison on the strength of memories of events said to date back as far as **20 years**, and such cases continue.

The evidence cuts both ways, and Schultz present both sides.

**Evidence that abuse can be forgotten and later recalled**

- **Delmonte (2000).** There is ample evidence that real abuse can be **forgotten for many years** before being recalled.
- **McNally, Clancy, Schacter and Pitman (2000).** Women who had **repressed**, **recovered**, or **never forgotten** abuse memories were compared. Those reporting **recovered** memories scored higher on **fantasy proneness** and **dissociation** (a splitting of mental processes into separate streams of awareness). Schultz note these states could themselves be **results** of childhood trauma.

**Evidence that false memories can be implanted**

Research also shows how easily **false memories** can be planted and real recollections distorted, until something that never happened feels conscious and genuine (Loftus & Ketcham, 1994; Ofshe & Watters, 1994).

- **Ceci and Bruck (1993).** Young children were interviewed **4 years** after spending **5 minutes** playing with a man who sat across a table and **never touched them**. Interviewers created an **accusatory climate**, telling the children they would be asked about an important event and pressing them with questions implying something bad had happened and that telling would make them feel better. **One-third** agreed with the suggestion that the man had **hugged and kissed** them; **two** children agreed they had been **photographed in the bathroom**; **one** agreed the man had **bathed** her.
- **Mazzoni, Lombardo, Malvagia and Loftus (1999).** College students in **Italy** had said weeks earlier, on a questionnaire, that **no traumatic events** had happened in their childhood. Half then had their dreams interpreted by a psychologist who was also a **popular radio personality**, who told them their dreams showed **repressed memories** of childhood trauma such as being **abandoned by their parents** or **lost in an unfamiliar place**. The other half received no such interpretation. Questioned **10 to 15 days** later, the **majority** of the experimental group now agreed the trauma had happened and that they had repressed it for years. Dream interpretation itself can implant memories.
- **Elizabeth Loftus**, a pioneer in this area, concluded with Davis that there is **little support** for the idea that trauma is commonly banished from awareness and later reliably recovered by processes beyond ordinary forgetting and remembering, and that false memories of impossible or highly improbable traumatic events have clearly been created, especially by **suggestive recovery procedures** (Loftus & Davis, 2006).

**The balancing point.** Childhood sexual abuse **does occur**, far more widely than Freud imagined, and its effects can be debilitating: people abused as children show strong tendencies towards **anxiety, depression, self-destructiveness, low self-esteem and suicide** (McNally, Perlman, Ristuccia, & Clancy, 2006; Pilkington & Lenaghan, 1998; Westen, 1998). The scientific conclusion is that **some recovered memories may be real and others implanted or distorted**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition does **not** say all recovered memories are false, nor that repression explains them all. It says abuse can be forgotten and recalled (Delmonte, 2000), that false memories can be implanted by suggestion (Ceci & Bruck, 1993; Mazzoni et al., 1999), and that abuse is real and harmful. Mazzoni et al. used **dream interpretation** as the implanting technique; Ceci and Bruck used **accusatory interviewing** of children.

### Highlights: research on Freud's ideas

Schultz's Highlights box condenses the module. In outline:

- **Low ego control** goes with being **aggressive and non-compliant**, **unpredictable and assertive**, **moody and self-indulgent**.
- **Low ego resiliency** goes with **stress during negative interactions with parents**, being **anxious and needing reassurance**, and being **unassertive, sad and lacking social skills**.
- People who **vent anger** (popular catharsis) end up **angrier**, and are **more attracted to violent video games**.
- Research **supports** seven Freudian ideas: **repression**, **denial**, **projection** and **displacement** among the defences; the influence of the **unconscious**; the **Freudian slip**; and the view that **dreams mirror emotional concerns**.

### The research scorecard

Pulling Fisher and Greenberg, the later studies and the chapter summary together:

| Supported (at least partly) | Not supported or not validated | Untestable (Fisher and Greenberg) |

> **Trap.** The chapter summary lists the concepts **not scientifically validated** as the **id, superego, death wish, libido, catharsis and anxiety**, while Fisher and Greenberg's **untestable** list is **id, ego, superego, death wish, libido and anxiety**. The difference: the summary drops the **ego** (because of the ego-control and resiliency work) and adds **catharsis** (because of the venting research). Both lists can appear in questions.

## 2.11 Extensions of Freudian Theory

Later chapters of the book cover theorists who built their positions **in opposition** to Freud (Jung, Adler, Horney and others). A second group stayed loyal to Freud's basic assumptions but tried to **expand, extend or elaborate** them, correcting what they saw as weaknesses or omissions. The most important member of this second group was his daughter **Anna**.

### Ego psychology: Anna Freud

#### Her life

**Anna Freud (1895 to 1982)** liked to say she would never have been born if her parents had had a safer contraceptive. She became the **only one of Freud's six children** to follow him into psychoanalysis (Young-Bruehl, 1988). Her childhood was unhappy: she was jealous of an older sister whom her mother favoured, and was ignored by her other siblings; she later recalled feeling that she merely bored them, and was herself bored and lonely (Appignanesi & Forrester, 1992).

Her father did not ignore her. She became his **favourite**, and by **age 14** she was attending meetings of his psychoanalytic group, following the case discussions closely. At **22** she began **4 years of psychoanalysis with her father**, a decision for which Freud was later sharply criticised. One historian called it an impossible, incestuous treatment, an Oedipal drama acted out on both sides of the couch (Mahoney, 1992); another argued that no one else would have taken the case on, because analysing Anna would inevitably have meant questioning Freud's role as her father (Donaldson, 1996). Analysing one's own child **broke Freud's own rules** of practice, and the arrangement was **kept secret** for many years.

In the analysis Anna reported **violent dreams** of shooting, killing and dying, and of **defending her father** against his enemies. She also shared her **sexual fantasies and masturbation**, and came out of the analysis grateful to her father and more devoted to him than ever (Edmundson, 2007).

She joined the **Vienna Psychoanalytic Society** with a paper titled ***Beating Fantasies and Daydreams***. Although she presented it as the case of a patient, she was actually describing **her own** fantasies: an incestuous father-daughter love, a physical beating, and sexual gratification through masturbation.

She devoted her life to caring for her father and to his system. Several years after his death she described a series of dreams about him in which, she noted, the leading role was played not by her longing for him but by his longing for her (Zaretsky, 2004). Near her own death, some **40 years** after his, she would sit in her wheelchair wearing his **old wool coat**, which she had kept all that time (Webster, 1995).

#### Anna Freud's approach to psychoanalysis

The contrast with her father is the first examinable point. **Sigmund Freud worked only with adults**, reconstructing their childhoods from their recollections, fantasies and dreams. **Anna Freud worked only with children**. She set up a **clinic** and a **training centre** for analysts in the building **next to her father's London home**. (Classic background: the Hampstead Child Therapy Course and Clinic, renamed the Anna Freud Centre after her death.) In **1927** she published ***Four Lectures on Child Analysis***. Her father approved, saying her views on child analysis were independent of his, developed from her own experience, though he shared them (quoted in Viner, 1996).

Her major theoretical move was to **greatly expand the role of the ego**: in her account the ego is **not a servant of the id but works independently of it**. Schultz call this a fundamental and radical change to the Freudian system. Recall from 2.4 that for Sigmund the ego is **never independent of the id** and borrows all its energy from it; Anna's ego has functions and a standing of its own. She set out these refinements in ***The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense*** (**1936**, published while her father was still alive), which clarified how the defence mechanisms work. The book was widely praised and is considered a **basic work of ego psychology**. Schultz credit her with the **full development and articulation** of the standard defence mechanisms covered in 2.6, and stress that this is only one of her significant contributions. (Classic background: later ego psychologists, notably Heinz Hartmann, pushed the idea of an autonomous ego further.)

> **Trap.** Two dates, two books. ***Four Lectures on Child Analysis*** = **1927**. ***The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense*** = **1936**. And the central idea is the **ego's independence from the id**, which directly contradicts Sigmund Freud's view that the ego is never independent of the id. Look-alike names: Anna Freud (ego psychology, child analysis, defences) is not Melanie Klein (object relations, play technique), who is outside this chapter.

## 2.12 Reflections on Freud's Theory

### Contributions

Freud's system has had an extraordinary effect on theory and practice in **psychology and psychiatry**, on our image of **human nature**, and on our understanding of **personality**. It also reached the general culture through popular books, magazines and newspapers; one article called him an "inescapable force" still felt **65 years after his death** (see Adler, 2006).

Schultz list specific debts:

- Psychoanalysis fed the growing interest of American psychologists in **personality** from the **1930s**.
- In the **1940s and 1950s** its ideas shaped the emerging psychological study of **motivation**.
- Contemporary psychology has absorbed many Freudian concepts: the role of the **unconscious**, the importance of **childhood experience** in shaping adult behaviour, and the operation of the **defence mechanisms**, all of which still generate research.
- Many later theorists either **built on** Freud or **defined themselves against** him. Great ideas, Schultz observe, inspire not only by being judged correct but also by being judged wrong, because opposition stimulates new viewpoints.

### The decline of Freudian psychotherapy

A distinction worth keeping: Freud's **theory of personality** remains **more influential** than his **therapy**. Research on his ideas continues to be plentiful, but psychoanalysis as a treatment has **declined in popularity**, especially in the **United States**.

The picture is not uniform:

- In **China**, psychoanalytic therapy has become **more popular**, and American analysts have trained a growing number of Chinese analysts over **Skype** (Osnos, 2011; Wan, 2010).
- In some **European** countries, traditional psychoanalytic sessions conducted **over the internet** have grown rapidly, with encouraging early reports (Migone, 2013).
- Some research suggests **social robots** ("companion robots") can make psychoanalysis more effective, especially among the **elderly**, helping with physical and cognitive impairments and improving well-being (Costescu, Vanderborght, & David, 2014).

Why the decline? More people than ever seek help for behavioural and emotional problems, but fewer choose Freud's **expensive, long-term** method. **Brief therapy** of **1 to 15 sessions** has become the norm, alongside growing use of **psychotherapeutic drugs**. In the United States the trend is reinforced by **managed care**: it is much cheaper for insurers to approve a drug prescription than an analysis that may last years, and managed care requires **empirical evidence of effectiveness** before reimbursing treatment, while the evidence for the effectiveness of psychoanalytic psychotherapy is **weak** (see, for example, Mayes & Horwitz, 2005).

### Criticisms of psychoanalysis

Beyond the methodological problems of the case study (2.10), raised mostly by **experimental psychologists**, other **personality theorists** have raised a set of substantive objections. Each one maps onto a later chapter's theory, which is why examiners like to ask which criticism came from which direction.

| Criticism | What the critics say | Where it leads in the book |
|---|---|---|
| Too much **biology** | Freud overstated instinctual biological forces as determinants of personality | Social and cultural theories (Adler, Horney, Erikson) |
| Too much **sex and aggression** | We are shaped more by **social** experiences than sexual ones | Adler, Horney, Erikson |
| Too much **determinism** | We have more **free will** than Freud allowed; we can choose, grow spontaneously and partly control our fate | Humanistic theorists (Maslow, Rogers) |
| Too much **past** | Freud ignored **goals, hopes and plans**; the future influences us as much as or more than events before age 5 | Adler, Jung, Allport |
| Too much **pathology** | He studied the emotionally disturbed and neglected the **healthy and mature**; why not study the best and healthiest people and positive qualities as well as negative ones? | Maslow, Rogers |
| Views on **women** | **Penis envy**, women's **poorly developed superegos**, and women's supposed **inferiority feelings about their bodies** | Horney (Chapter 5) |
| **Ambiguous concepts** | Terms such as **id, ego and superego** are confused and contradictory: are they physical brain structures or fluid processes? Freud acknowledged the difficulty in later writing, but the questions remain | General scientific critique |

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a criticism of Freud made in the 11th edition?" Look for an option that criticises him for neglecting **biology** or **childhood**, or for being too **optimistic** or too focused on **free will**: those are the reverse of the real criticisms. The genuine list is: too biological, too sexual and aggressive, too deterministic, too past-focused, too centred on disturbed people, negative towards women, ambiguous definitions, plus the case-study problems (unrepresentative sample, no verbatim records, no verification, unreplicable, data possibly altered).

### Freud's lasting influence

Schultz end with a judgement about foundations. The book is a history of modern insights into personality, and just as individuals are never free of their past (nor should want to be), the field is not free of Freud. Later theorists built on his work, and if psychoanalysis had done nothing except inspire others and supply a framework within which to develop new ideas, his place in the history of ideas would still be secure. Every structure depends on the soundness of its foundation, and Freud gave personality theory a highly **original, stimulating and challenging** base to build on, or to push against.

## How the chapter fits together

Six through-lines connect the twelve modules. Tracing each one is the quickest way to answer the essay-style "explain" and "evaluate" questions.

1. **The theory is partly autobiography.** The adoring young mother and the stern older father become the Oedipus complex; the father's threats, the niece and circumcision become castration anxiety; abstinence and coitus interruptus become the causes of anxiety neurosis; the self-analysis becomes dream analysis; war, cancer and a daughter's death become the death instincts; a darkening view of people becomes a pessimistic image of human nature. Schultz's chapter summary opens with exactly this claim.
2. **Energy and its fate.** Everything runs on instinctual energy (libido) that seeks discharge. The id holds it, the ego borrows it, cathexis invests it, displacement and sublimation redirect it, fixation leaves some of it behind at an early stage, and undischarged tension accumulates when substitutes are imperfect. The one goal of life is tension reduction.
3. **Conflict is permanent.** The ego serves three masters (id, reality, superego). Strain produces three anxieties (reality, neurotic, moral). Anxiety calls up defences, which are unconscious distortions that everyone uses all the time and that we cannot live without. Symptoms appear when the defences fail.
4. **The decisive events happen before age 5.** Personality is crystallised in the oral, anal and phallic stages; latency is a pause; the genital stage completes what childhood set up. This is the claim most directly undermined by later research (Kagan) and later theorists (Erikson's life span).
5. **Hidden material, indirect access.** Because the important material is unconscious, it can only be reached indirectly (free association, resistance, manifest to latent content) and must be interpreted, which makes assessment depend on the analyst's skill and makes the theory hard to test.
6. **Mixed verdict from research.** Freud's own evidence is weak (case studies, no verbatim notes, a dozen unrepresentative cases, altered Rat Man record, destroyed files), yet several concepts survive testing (unconscious influence, repression, displacement, projection, denial, the slip, parts of the oral and anal types), while others fail (dreams as wishes, Oedipal resolution, weaker female superego, the stages, venting catharsis) or cannot be tested (id, superego, libido, death wish).

**How Freud relates to the rest of the book.** Almost every later chapter can be read as a reply. **Jung** and **Adler** were disciples who broke away (2.1): Jung widened libido beyond sex, Adler replaced sex with social striving and inferiority. **Horney** attacked penis envy and the view of women. **Erikson** kept the stage idea but extended it across the whole life span and made the ego and society central, which is also the direction **Anna Freud** pointed in by freeing the ego from the id. The humanists (**Maslow**, **Rogers**) reversed Freud's pessimism, determinism and focus on the disturbed by studying healthy, growing people. **Allport** stressed the present and conscious motives over the unconscious past. The behavioural and social-learning theorists (**Skinner**, **Bandura**) rejected unobservable inner structures altogether and insisted on the experimental testing Freud dismissed. Freud is, in Schultz's metaphor, the foundation that all of them build on or push against.

## Worked example

*A 34-year-old man, a mid-level bank officer, is referred for "anger problems at home". At work he is described as unfailingly polite to a demanding, belittling branch manager. At home he shouts at his wife and young son over small things, then feels intensely guilty. He insists his manager is "actually a decent man" and that he himself "never gets angry at work, there's no reason to". He is meticulously tidy, keeps detailed accounts of every rupee spent, and becomes upset when anyone moves his things. A friend has advised him to "let it all out" by hitting a punching bag after work. In early sessions he talks easily about work but goes quiet and changes the subject when asked about his father, a strict disciplinarian. He reports a recurrent dream of climbing a staircase that keeps collapsing beneath him. A relative has suggested that his problems must come from "some childhood abuse he has blocked out".*

Work through it with the chapter's machinery.

**Which defences are visible?** The clearest is **displacement**: the aggressive impulse belongs to the manager, an object too threatening to attack (he could lose his job), so it is redirected to his wife and son, who are less able to retaliate. Freud's theory also predicts what he describes: displacement discharges tension **less completely** than the original target would, so tension accumulates and the outbursts recur. His insistence that the manager is "a decent man" and his excessive politeness at work suggest **reaction formation** (hostility expressed as its opposite, with the tell-tale excess). "There's no reason to be angry at work" may be **rationalisation**. All of these, Freud would stress, operate **unconsciously**: he is not lying to the therapist, he is lying to himself without knowing it.

**Which anxieties?** Fear of losing his job if he confronts the manager is partly **reality anxiety**. The dread attached to his own anger, an unconscious expectation of punishment for expressing aggression, laid down by a punitive father, is **neurotic anxiety** (id versus ego). The intense guilt after shouting at home is **moral anxiety** (id versus superego): his conscience, built from punished behaviours, retaliates.

**What does the structural model say is happening?** The **id** presses for aggressive discharge; **reality** forbids discharging it on the manager; the **superego** condemns discharging it at all. The **ego**, serving three masters, finds a compromise (aim it at home) that satisfies none of them fully, and the strain shows up as anxiety and guilt.

**Is there a character type?** His tidiness, stinginess with money, rigidity and distress when his things are moved fit Freud's **anal retentive** personality (in classic shorthand, orderliness, parsimony and obstinacy), which Freud would trace to a conflicted toilet-training period with demanding parents. The research section allows a hedged statement: there is **some** support for the oral and anal types, and the anal pattern resurfaces today as conscientiousness, orderliness and obsessive-compulsive personality traits (Haslam, 2011). The claim that the pattern was **fixed by age 5** is the weak part; Kagan's work suggests later childhood and temperament matter more.

**How would a Freudian assess him?** Through **free association**, watching especially for **resistance**: the silence and subject-changing around his father is, for Freud, a sign that the analysis is near the core conflict, so the analyst would keep gently probing there. Through **dream analysis**: the **manifest content** is a collapsing staircase; Freud's symbol list reads climbing stairs as **sexual intercourse**, but Freud himself warned that many symbols are **individual**, so the latent meaning must come from the patient's own associations (for example, fear of falling from a precarious position at work). Interpretation depends on the analyst's skill.

**What would you not do?**

- **Do not endorse the punching bag.** The friend's advice is popular catharsis. Bushman, Baumeister and Stack (1999) showed that venting after provocation **increases** later aggression, including towards **innocent people**, which is exactly his problem at home. Bushman et al. (2005) add that **brooding** on anger increases displaced aggression.
- **Do not hunt for a "blocked-out" abuse memory.** There is no report of abuse. Suggestive questioning and confident interpretation of dreams as signs of repressed trauma can **implant** false memories (Ceci & Bruck, 1993; Mazzoni et al., 1999); Loftus and Davis (2006) found little support for routine banishing and reliable recovery of trauma. Real abuse does occur and must be taken seriously if reported, but the clinician must not create it.

**What would the evaluation say?** A Freudian formulation organises this case coherently, and several of its pieces (displacement, the cost of venting, the unconscious) have research support. But its developmental claims rest on retrospective reconstruction, its interpretations cannot be independently verified, and a modern clinician would also look at present-day maintaining factors (the manager, rumination) that the theory's focus on the first five years tends to neglect.

## MCQ traps

1. **Born 1856, Freiberg, Moravia (now Pribor, Czech Republic); died September 1939, London.** Moved to Vienna at **4** and stayed nearly **80 years**.
2. **Father 40, mother 20** (his **third** wife) at Freud's birth; **eight** children in the family, including two adult half-brothers. Felt superior to his father by age **2**.
3. **Cocaine article: 1884**; used cocaine **1884 to 1896**, then wine; blamed for contributing to a cocaine epidemic lasting into the 1920s.
4. **Practice as clinical neurologist: 1881 (Schultz)**; other books give 1886. Engagement to **Martha Bernays: 4 years**.
5. **Charcot** (Paris, hypnosis) alerted Freud to the sexual basis of neurosis; colleague's patient whose husband had been impotent for **18 years**.

11. **Libido = energy of the life instincts**; **cathexis = investment** of psychic energy in an object or person. Life instincts serve food, water, air, sex.

13. **Erogenous zones: mouth, anus, sex organs** (three).
14. **Preconscious = easily retrievable**; unconscious = not retrievable by ordinary effort; **iceberg** metaphor, conscious = tip.
15. **Only the id is wholly unconscious**; ego and superego have unconscious parts (Fig 2.1).
16. **Id: pleasure principle, primary-process thought** (reflex action and wish-fulfilling hallucination). **Ego: reality principle, secondary-process thought**. Superego: moral perfection.
17. **Ego serves the id**, delaying and redirecting; it is **never independent of the id** and borrows its energy (rider and horse). Anna Freud later made the ego **independent**.
18. **Superego formed by age 5 or 6**. **Conscience = punished** behaviours; **ego-ideal = praised** behaviours.
19. **Prototype of anxiety = birth trauma**; overwhelming anxiety = **traumatic** (infantile helplessness).
20. **Reality (objective) anxiety** is the original form; **neurotic = id vs ego** (fear of punishment for gratifying impulses, not of the impulses); **moral = id vs superego** (fear of conscience).
21. **All defences share two features**: they distort or deny reality, and they operate unconsciously. Everyone uses several at once; they are **necessary**.
22. **Repression = most fundamental**, involuntary, can act on memory, present perception, and physiology (impotence). **Denial = external** threat (terminal illness, child's room kept unchanged).
23. **Reaction formation reverses** the impulse; **projection relocates** it ("I don't hate him; he hates me").
24. **Sublimation is a form of displacement** and still leaves undischarged tension. Displacement changes the **object**, sublimation changes the **impulse**.
25. **Ages (Table 2.2): oral birth to 1, anal 1 to 3, phallic 4 to 5, latency 5 to puberty, genital adolescence to adulthood.** Toilet training begins about **18 months**.
26. **Latency is not a stage**; the glossary lists four stages (oral, anal, phallic, genital).
27. **Fixation from frustration or over-gratification**; a portion of libido stays invested.
28. **Oral incorporative comes first** (over-gratified → optimistic, dependent, gullible = **oral passive**); **oral aggressive/sadistic** arises with **teething** (pessimistic, sarcastic, envious, exploitative).
29. **Anal aggressive** (Schultz's term; older term anal expulsive): cruel, destructive, disorderly. **Anal retentive**: stingy, stubborn, rigid, compulsively neat, obstinate.
30. **Oedipus Rex by Sophocles, fifth century BC**. "**Electra complex**" was a term used by Freud's **followers** (classic background: Jung); Freud preferred female Oedipus complex. **Penis envy** is the counterpart of **castration anxiety**.

32. **Phallic personality = narcissism**; male brash, vain, self-assured; female exaggerates femininity. **Genital personality = love and work**.
33. **Two assessment methods in Schultz: free association and dream analysis.** **Breuer** (hysteria, hypnosis) → **catharsis** (Greek, purification); Freud abandoned hypnosis (some unhypnotisable; recall failed afterwards).
34. **Resistance = a good sign** that analysis is close to the core conflict.
35. **Manifest = actual dream events; latent = hidden meaning.** Of **40+** dreams in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, few were sexual; the dominant theme was **ambition**.
36. **Fisher and Greenberg (1977, 1996): about 2,500 studies**, case histories excluded. **Untestable: id, ego, superego, death wish, libido, anxiety.**
37. **Rat Man** (Eagle, 1988; Mahoney, 1986): analysis lengthened, sequence wrong, cure unsupported. **Kihlstrom (1994)**: case notes show no patient claiming paternal seduction.
38. **Shevrin (1977)**: bee → sting, honey. **Silverman and Weinberger (1985)**: "Mommy and I are one", **4 ms**, helped **male** schizophrenics; "Daddy and I are one" helped females.
39. **Ego control** (under to over, both extremes bad) vs **ego resiliency** (flexibility; low = "ego brittle"). **Paris and Helson (2002)**: mothers aged **21 to 27**.
40. **Bushman, Baumeister and Stack (1999)**: venting on a punching bag **increased** aggression, even towards innocent people.
41. **Marcus-Newhall et al. (2000)**: **97 studies**, displaced aggression reliable. **Bushman et al. (2005)**: **25 minutes** of brooding increased displaced aggression.
42. **Glucksberg and King (1967)**: shocked words forgotten, and conceptually similar words on the second list also forgotten.
43. **Denial decreases, identification increases** with age (Tallandini & Caudek, 2010; Porcerelli et al., 1998 with TAT, grades 2 to college). **Cramer (2007)**: 150 students aged 11 to 18.

47. **Anna Freud (1895 to 1982)**: only one of **six** children to follow him; analysed by her father from age **22** for **4 years**; ***Four Lectures on Child Analysis* (1927)**; ***The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense* (1936)**.
48. **Brief therapy of 1 to 15 sessions** is now the norm; psychoanalysis declining in the US but growing in **China** (via Skype).

## Likely exam points

1. **Part opener framing**: psychoanalysis is the earliest formal personality theory, built on unconscious forces, biological drives of sex and aggression, and unavoidable early-childhood conflicts.
2. **Autobiographical roots**: mother-father triangle → Oedipus complex; father's threats, niece, circumcision → castration anxiety; sexual frustration → anxiety neurosis and neurasthenia; self-analysis → dream analysis; war, cancer, daughter's death → death instincts.
3. **Seduction theory**: proposed, reversed about a year later (fantasy), sex kept as the cause; later private admissions of real cases; Krüll's suggestion about fathers; modern view that abuse is common.
4. **Key life dates**: 1856 birth; 1884 cocaine article; 1881 practice (Schultz); 1909 Clark University; 1923 onward 33 cancer operations; 1933 books burned; 1938 to London; 1939 death.
5. **Instinct as a wish**, the mental representation of a bodily need; homeostasis; displacement of energy explains the variety of adult interests.
6. **Life vs death instincts**, libido and cathexis; aggressive drive as the death wish turned outward; limited acceptance (Sulloway, Eissler).
7. **Three levels** (conscious, preconscious, unconscious) and the iceberg; replaced by the **three structures**; Fig 2.1 shows ego and superego straddling all levels while the id is wholly unconscious.
8. **Id, ego, superego** with their principles and thought processes; ego as rider, serving three masters; conscience vs ego-ideal; superego by 5 or 6.
9. **Anxiety**: objectless fear; birth trauma prototype; traumatic anxiety; reality, neurotic (id-ego) and moral (id-superego) anxiety; anxiety as a drive and warning signal.
10. **Defence mechanisms**: two common features; always operating; several at once; necessary for mental health; breakdown produces symptoms.
11. **Table 2.1 eight defences** with an example of each; repression as most fundamental; sublimation as a form of displacement.
12. **Psychosexual stages and ages** (Table 2.2), personality crystallised by 5, fixation via frustration or over-gratification.
13. **Oral incorporative vs oral aggressive**; **anal aggressive vs anal retentive** with adult traits.

17. **Free association**: Breuer, hypnosis, catharsis, couch, why Freud sat out of sight, non-randomness of associations.
18. **Resistance** as a sign of nearing the core conflict.
19. **Dream analysis**: manifest vs latent content; Table 2.3 symbols and the warning that symbols can be individual; Yu (2012) prone sleepers; Goritz (2014) five typical dreams; Freud's own dreams about ambition.
20. **Case-study criticisms**: no verbatim records, notes from memory, no verification, small upper-class sample (about a dozen cases), Rat Man discrepancies, destroyed files, Kihlstrom (1994), suggestion (McCullough, Webster).
21. **Freud's rejection of experiments** (Rosenzweig, 1985) and claim that only analysts could judge psychoanalysis.
22. **Fisher and Greenberg (1977, 1996)**: 2,500 studies; untestable, supported and unsupported lists.
23. **Unconscious research**: Shevrin (1977); Bargh et al. (2001); Winkielman et al. (2005); Silverman and Weinberger (1985); Hunt et al. (2006); Hansel and von Kanel (2013); the "smarter" unconscious.
24. **Ego control and ego resiliency** with their correlates in children, students and adults, and cultural and gender differences.
25. **Catharsis research** (Bushman et al., 1999): venting increases aggression.
26. **Displacement** (97-study meta-analysis; 25-minute rumination study) and **repression** (Glucksberg & King, 1967; repressor studies: Davis, 1987; Boden & Baumeister, 1997; Phipps & Steele, 2002).
27. **Projection** (Rucker & Pratkanis; Cramer 2009 social class; Schul & Vinokur unemployed couples).
28. **Hierarchy of defences**: denial early and declining, identification rising; boys more denial; Cramer and Block (1998) men still denying at 23; Diehl et al. (2014) reversion after the mid-60s.
29. **Dream research**: supports emotional concerns, not wish fulfilment; trauma, everyday mood, sex differences, media, culture, interpretation, Horikawa et al. (2013) 66 percent decoding.
30. **Oedipal, oral and anal research**: Watson and Getz (1990), Hall and Van de Castle (1965), R. L. Jones (1994), Maio et al. (2000), Masling et al. (1967), Tribich and Messer (1974), O'Neill and Bornstein (1990), Haslam (2011).
31. **Age 5 challenged**: Kagan, Kearsley and Zelazo (1978); Kagan (1999); middle childhood 7 to 12.
32. **Freudian slip**: Motley (1987).
33. **Recovered vs implanted memories**: Delmonte; McNally et al. (2000); Ceci and Bruck (1993); Mazzoni et al. (1999); Loftus and Davis (2006); abuse is real and harmful.
34. **Anna Freud**: life, analysis by her father, child analysis, 1927 and 1936 books, ego independent of the id, ego psychology.
35. **Reflections**: contributions; decline of psychoanalytic therapy (brief therapy, drugs, managed care, weak evidence) with growth in China and online; seven substantive criticisms.
