# Chapter 3: Carl Jung: Analytical Psychology

**What this chapter is really about.** This chapter opens the second part of the book, **the neo-psychoanalytic approach**: theorists who started inside Freud's camp and then walked out. Jung and Alfred Adler knew Freud personally and worked with him before rebelling; Karen Horney never had a personal tie to Freud but began as an orthodox Freudian; Erik Erikson built his theory out of psychoanalysis. They disagree with one another on much, but all four reject Freud's claim that **instincts are the primary motivators** of behaviour and his **determinism**, and all four offer a more hopeful, more flattering picture of human nature. The authors also want you to notice the speed of it: within about ten years of personality theory formally beginning, the field had split into rival schools.

Jung is the strangest member of the group because he is the only dissenter who went *further* into the unconscious than Freud instead of retreating from it. Beneath a personal unconscious he placed a **collective unconscious** holding the inherited experience of the species, organised into universal patterns called **archetypes**. He offered the first serious theory of **adult** development, with **middle age** rather than early childhood as the decisive period and **individuation** as its goal. And three of his clinical ideas entered ordinary psychology and stayed there: **introversion and extraversion**, the **complex**, and the **word-association test**. His typology later became the **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator**, the most widely used personality test ever produced.

**How to read this chapter.** 3.1 is the life, and almost every major idea can be traced to an event in it. 3.2 sets up the energy system; its first principle, **opposites**, is the key to the whole theory, because Jung builds nearly every structure as a pair of opposing tendencies. 3.3 is the anatomy of the psyche (ego, attitudes, functions, types, personal unconscious and complexes, collective unconscious and archetypes). 3.4 is development, ending in individuation; 3.5 rates Jung on Schultz's six questions about human nature; 3.6 covers assessment, 3.7 research (mostly with the MBTI) and 3.8 the evaluation.

## Opening: Three Places Where Jung Parted from Freud

Freud once named Jung his spiritual son and heir. Jung instead built a system so unlike orthodox psychoanalysis that it needed its own name, **analytical psychology**. The book frames the difference as three disagreements, which examiners like to ask for as a set.

**1. The role of sexuality.** Freud's **libido** is essentially sexual. Jung widened it into a general **psychic energy** that includes sex but is not limited to it. The drive to create, understand, worship or achieve is not, for Jung, disguised sexuality; it is energy flowing into those channels.

**2. The direction of the forces that shape us.** Freud's people are prisoners of their early history. Jung's obituary in the *New York Times* (7 June 1961) summed up the opposite view: people are not doomed to be pushed around forever by traumas they could not control. We are shaped by our **future as well as our past**, by what we aim to become as much as by what happened to us as children.

**3. The unconscious.** The other neo-psychoanalytic dissenters (Adler, Horney, Erikson) all shrink the unconscious. Jung gave it **even more weight than Freud did** and added a new layer: the inherited experience of the human species and its **prehuman** ancestors. Freud had granted that inherited primal experiences might matter a little; Jung made them the core of his system, drawing on history, mythology, anthropology and religion.

| Issue | Freud (psychoanalysis) | Jung (analytical psychology) |
|---|---|---|
| Libido | Sexual energy | General psychic or life energy; sex is one outlet among many |
| What shapes personality | The past, above all childhood | The past **and** the future (aims and aspirations) |
| The unconscious | Personal, built from repressed material | Even more important; adds an inherited **collective** layer |

> **Trap.** "Like the other neo-Freudians, Jung reduced the importance of the unconscious" is false. Jung is the exception: he placed *greater* emphasis on the unconscious than Freud. Adler, Horney and Erikson are the ones who downplayed it.

## 3.1 The Life of Jung (1875 to 1961)

Jung himself described his theory as a kind of personal confession, and in a lecture at the age of 50 he acknowledged that his life events had shaped it. Read each subsection with one question in mind: which part of the theory did this feed?

### An Unhappy Childhood

Jung was born in **Switzerland** in 1875 into a family thick with clergy: his father and eight of his uncles, **nine clergymen** in all. Religion and the classics came early, and so did religious doubt. The childhood the book describes is bleak: deaths and funerals, two neurotic parents in a failing marriage, bizarre dreams and visions, and for company a wooden doll.

His **father** was kind and tolerant, and the boy felt close to him, but he saw him as weak, moody and irritable, never the strong authority he needed. His **mother** was the more powerful parent but emotionally unstable: she could switch in a moment from cheerful to mumbling incoherently and staring into space. The boy came to experience her as **two people living in one body**, which disturbed him deeply. Ellenberger (1978) suggested that insanity seemed to run through her whole side of the family.

Two consequences matter for the theory. Jung grew **wary of women**, a suspicion that took years to fade. And because he remembered his mother as fat and unattractive, the book suggests this may explain why he **flatly rejected Freud's Oedipus complex**: the claim that every boy sexually desires his mother matched nothing in his own childhood.

> **Trap.** Jung rejected the Oedipal longing for the mother, and the book links this to his own mother. Classic background: Jung also coined the term **Electra complex** (1913) for the girl's version of the Oedipal situation, a label Freud rejected. "Freud's Electra complex" is a wrong attribution; the term is Jung's.

To escape the household tension, the boy spent hours alone in the **attic**, carving a small wooden figure to whom he confided his secret thoughts and fears. A sister born when he was **9** did nothing to ease his loneliness.

### Strange Dreams and Fantasies

Distrusting his mother and disappointed in his father, Jung felt cut off from the outer world of conscious reality and turned inward to dreams, visions and fantasies, where he felt safer. It became a lifelong habit: faced with any problem, he looked for the answer in his dreams.

His theory grew the same way. At about **three** he dreamed he was inside a **cavern**; later he dreamed of digging below the earth's surface and unearthing the **bones of prehistoric animals**. He read these dreams as pointing him towards the unconscious, the region beneath the surface of behaviour. The second one neatly anticipates the collective unconscious: a buried layer of the mind holding the species' prehistoric past.

He trusted such messages so fully that he titled his autobiography ***Memories, Dreams, Reflections*** (1961). His theory, like Freud's, is intensely **autobiographical**.

He was also socially isolated. Jung avoided other children and they avoided him; Bair (2003) notes that village parents kept their children away from the odd boy with the peculiar parents. Jung later wrote that he had been a solitary then and still was (Jung, 1961). The book draws the link: his theory is about the **inner growth of the individual**, not relationships with others, whereas Freud's theory is crowded with other people, perhaps because Freud's childhood was not so lonely and inward.

### The Study of Medicine

School bored and annoyed Jung; he preferred reading religion and philosophy on his own. A series of **fainting spells** kept him at home for **6 months**, to his delight. When he returned, his fainting so distracted the class that the teachers sent him home again (Bair, 2003).

Then he overheard his father asking anxiously how the boy would ever earn a living (Jung, 1961). The fainting stopped, and he went back and worked harder than before. He later said the episode taught him what **neurosis** is: he had **unconsciously arranged** the illness to get what he wanted, and the realisation left him angry and ashamed. Notice the forward-looking reading of a symptom here: it served a goal. That fits Jung's later insistence that aims, not only past events, shape behaviour.

He studied **medicine at the University of Basel** and, to his professors' disappointment, chose **psychiatry**, then a low-status field, because it promised room for his real interests: dreams, the supernatural and the occult. From **1900** he worked at a Zurich mental hospital under **Eugen Bleuler**, who coined the term **schizophrenia** (classic background: the hospital was the Burghölzli). After marrying the second-richest heiress in Switzerland, he gave up the hospital post, drove the countryside in a red Chrysler convertible, lectured at the **University of Zurich** and built a private practice.

### The Years with Freud

Jung was already well known when he became associated with Freud in **1907**. Their first meeting ran to **13 hours** of talk, and a close friendship followed. It was openly cast as father and son: Freud wrote that he had adopted Jung as eldest son and anointed him "crown prince" (Freud & Jung, 1974, p. 218), and Jung asked for a friendship of father and son rather than of equals. The book notes the Oedipal flavour, including the son's wish to topple the father.

The book also suggests an earlier wound. At **18**, Jung said, an older family friend who had been a father figure to him made sexual advances. Jung, repelled, broke off the relationship. When Freud, nearly **20 years older**, later tried to make Jung his son and heir, Jung may have felt the same intrusion and the same disappointment, and been unable to stay close.

For some years, though, they remained close, meeting periodically and writing constantly. In **1909** Jung travelled with Freud to lecture at **Clark University** in the United States. Freud was grooming him to lead the **International Psychoanalytic Association**, partly so that a non-Jew would head the movement and psychoanalysis would not be branded a "Jewish science" (the label the Nazis later used). (Classic background: Jung became its first president in 1910.) But Jung was no uncritical disciple, and once he voiced his own ideas the split was inevitable. They **broke in 1913**.

> **Trap.** **1907**: association begins (the 13-hour meeting). **1909**: Clark University. **1913**: the break, and the start of Jung's neurotic episode.

### Jung's Neurotic Episode

In **1913**, at **38**, Jung fell into a severe **neurotic episode lasting 3 years**. Fearing he was losing touch with reality, he resigned his University of Zurich lectureship and at times contemplated suicide; Noll (1994) reports a revolver kept by his bed. He could not do scientific work, yet he went on seeing patients.

His dreams and visions were violent: ice covering the earth, rivers of blood, the end of civilisation (see Elms, 2010), and nightmarish personal scenes that Corbett (2009) lists, from wandering the land of the dead to being crushed by a giant serpent. Jung recorded them in calligraphy and painted images across about **200 pages** of a journal bound in red leather, ***The Red Book***. Locked in a Swiss bank vault, it was not published until **2009**, nearly 50 years after his death (see Harrison, 2009), and became an international sensation.

The book stresses the parallel with Freud, who also had a neurotic episode at about the same age and resolved it by analysing his dreams. Jung likewise recovered by **confronting his unconscious** through dreams and fantasies, less systematically than Freud but in a similar way. He later called those years of pursuing his inner images the most important of his life (Jung, 1961). Two features of the theory come straight from the crisis:

1. **Middle age, not childhood, is the crucial period.** Freud put the decisive years in early childhood; Jung, whose crisis came at 38, put it at midlife.
2. **The method of individuation.** What Jung did to heal himself (attend to dreams, give fantasies shape in pictures and writing, let unconscious material in without being swamped) is what he later prescribes as the first step of individuation (3.4).

Like Freud, he started from intuition and personal experience and then refined the ideas using patient data. Nearly **two-thirds of his patients were middle-aged** and struggling with what he had struggled with, which naturally confirmed his view (and is part of the sampling criticism in 3.7).

### Jung's Sex Life

The contrast with Freud is almost comic. Jung gave sex a small role in his theory but enjoyed a vigorous, anxiety-free sex life, with several affairs, one lasting many years with his wife's knowledge. Women patients and disciples routinely fell in love with him, and Noll (1997) reports that Jung often told them at the start of treatment that this would happen. Freud, by contrast, was troubled about sex and had given up sexual relations while building a theory that made sex the root of neurosis. Schultz (1990) draws the conclusion: the satisfied Jung saw sex as a minor motive, the frustrated Freud saw it as the central one. A theorist's own needs leak into the theory.

### Fame and Weird Behaviour

Jung's later life was successful, though his habits were odd. He wished his pots and his coffee pot good morning (Bair, 2003). Although wealthy, he worried about money, hid cash in books and in jars buried in the garden, and then forgot the codes he had invented to find it; the family recovered the book money, but the garden cash is probably still there. With their **three daughters**, he and his wife were cold and formal, with handshakes instead of hugs (Bair, 2003).

He remained productive for most of his **86 years**, and his following grew. His ideas reached the English-speaking world, especially the United States, largely through the money of the **Rockefeller, McCormick and Mellon** families, several of whose members were in analysis with him and paid for English translations. Noll (1997) suggests that without them his work might have stayed confined to German readers.

| Life event | What it fed in the theory |
|---|---|
| Unstable mother, unattractive to him | Wariness of women; rejection of the Oedipus complex |
| Lonely, introverted childhood | A theory of inner growth rather than relationships |
| Dreams of a cavern and of prehistoric bones | Exploring the unconscious beneath behaviour; a buried ancestral layer |
| Habit of seeking answers in dreams | Dreams as the central assessment tool |
| Fainting spells that kept him from school | Neurosis as unconsciously purposeful |
| Freud as father figure, then the 1913 break | Analytical psychology as an independent system |
| Breakdown at 38, cured by confronting the unconscious | Middle age as the crucial period; the method of individuation |
| Free and satisfied sex life | Sex given only a minor role in motivation |

## 3.2 Psychic Energy: The Basis of Jung's System

### Libido redefined

The first thing Jung disputed with Freud was the nature of **libido**. Jung did not accept that libido was mainly sexual. To him it was a **broad, undifferentiated life energy**, a general force that can flow into any activity at all.

He actually used the word in two senses, and examiners sometimes test the distinction:

1. **The wide sense:** libido as a diffuse, general **life energy**, the vitality of the living organism as a whole.
2. **The narrow sense,** closer to Freud's usage: libido as the **psychic energy** that powers the work of the personality. Jung's word for the total personality is the **psyche**. Psychic energy is what makes psychological activity happen at all: perceiving, thinking, feeling and wishing all run on it.

When a person invests a great deal of psychic energy in an idea or feeling, it has high **psychic value** (the chapter summary simply calls it a **value**) and a strong grip on that person's life. The book's example is power: someone strongly motivated to gain power spends most of their energy devising ways to get it. An entrance-exam aspirant for whom one institute's admission has enormous value is the same: it organises her days, worries and even dreams, while low-value things barely register.

> **Trap.** "Jung, like Freud, treated libido as sexual energy" is wrong. For Jung libido is generalised psychic or life energy; sex is only one of its possible channels. In the narrow sense he used it for the energy that fuels the psyche, but even then it is not sexual by definition.

### Principles of Psychic Energy

Jung borrowed from **physics** to describe how psychic energy behaves. He proposed **three principles: opposites, equivalence and entropy** (Jung, 1928).

#### The principle of opposites

The **principle of opposites** (also called the **opposition principle**) runs through the whole of Jung's system, and once you understand it, much of the rest of the theory becomes predictable. Jung pointed out that physical energy in the universe shows up in polarities: heat and cold, height and depth, creation and decay. He argued that psychic energy works the same way. **Every wish or feeling has its opposite.** Love comes paired with hate, the wish to be close with the wish to be free, ambition with the longing to rest.

The crucial step is what he made of this. The conflict between the two poles is not a defect; it is the **primary motivator of behaviour and the generator of psychic energy**. And the relationship is graded: **the sharper the conflict between the opposites, the more energy is produced.** A student who is only mildly torn between studying medicine and studying music has a small store of energy around the question; one who is passionately torn feels driven, restless and energised by it.

Watch how often the principle reappears: introversion against extraversion, thinking against feeling, sensing against intuiting, conscious against unconscious, persona against shadow, anima against animus. Jung's structures are almost always pairs.

#### The principle of equivalence

For the **principle of equivalence**, Jung took the physical law of the **conservation of energy** (classic background: the first law of thermodynamics) and applied it to the mind. Energy used to bring about some condition is **not lost**; it is moved to another part of the personality. If the psychic value attached to one area weakens or disappears, that energy is transferred somewhere else in the psyche.

The book's examples are everyday ones. If you lose interest in a person, a hobby or a subject of study, the energy you used to invest there shifts into something new. The energy that runs your conscious activity while you are awake shifts into **dreams** while you sleep.

The word *equivalence* adds a condition: the new target should carry **equal psychic value**, being as attractive or absorbing as the old one. Where no such outlet exists, the surplus **flows into the unconscious**. Either way, energy is **continually redistributed**, never destroyed. A retired cricketer who finds coaching as gripping as playing moves his energy there; if nothing grips him, it sinks into the unconscious and may surface as restlessness, odd dreams or symptoms.

#### The principle of entropy

In physics, **entropy** refers to the evening-out of energy differences. Put a hot object against a cold one and heat flows from the hotter to the colder until both sit at the same temperature. Energy has been exchanged and the two objects have reached a kind of homeostatic balance.

Jung's psychological version is the **principle of entropy**: the personality **tends towards balance**. When one desire or belief is held far more strongly than another, energy drains **from the stronger towards the weaker**. A person who has lived entirely for career, for instance, will feel energy leaking towards the neglected side of life (family, play, inner reflection).

The ideal is a personality in which psychic energy is spread **equally** over all its aspects. But that ideal is **never reached**, and Jung saw why it could not be: if perfect equilibrium were achieved, there would be no conflict between opposites, and since the principle of opposites says that conflict is what produces energy, the psyche would have **no energy at all**. A perfectly balanced psyche would be a dead one.

| Principle | Physics source | What it says about the psyche |
|---|---|---|
| **Opposites (opposition principle)** | Polarities in nature: heat/cold, height/depth, creation/decay | Every wish has its opposite; their conflict generates energy; sharper conflict, more energy |
| **Equivalence** | Conservation of energy | Energy is shifted, never lost; the new target should have equal value, or the surplus goes to the unconscious |
| **Entropy** | Equalisation of energy differences (hot and cold objects reach one temperature) | Energy flows from stronger to weaker structures towards equilibrium; perfect balance is never reached |

> **Trap.** Equivalence and entropy are easy to swap. **Equivalence = conservation** (energy is *moved*, not lost). **Entropy = equalisation** (energy *evens out* between strong and weak). A question describing "energy withdrawn from one interest reappears in another" is equivalence; "energy flows from the strongly held belief to the weakly held one, tending to balance" is entropy.

> **Trap.** The entropy principle's ideal (equal distribution of energy) is **never achieved**, and its achievement would be self-defeating because it would remove the conflict that the opposition principle needs to produce energy. If an option says the healthy psyche reaches complete equilibrium, it is wrong.

## 3.3 Aspects of Personality

Jung saw the total personality, the **psyche**, as made up of several separate systems or aspects that **influence one another**. Before the details, here is the overall map, because exam questions often depend on knowing which level something lives in.

| Level | What it contains | How accessible | Rough Freudian analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ego** (consciousness) | Conscious perceiving, thinking, feeling, remembering; the dominant attitude and function | Fully conscious | The conscious mind |
| **Personal unconscious** | Forgotten or suppressed personal material, organised into **complexes**; the non-dominant attitude and functions | Fairly easy to retrieve | The **preconscious** |
| **Collective unconscious** | Inherited experience of the human and prehuman species, expressed through **archetypes** | Deepest and least accessible | No real equivalent (Freud's nearest idea was inherited primal experiences) |

### The Ego

The **ego** is the **centre of consciousness**. Its business is conscious **perception, thought, feeling and memory**; it is your sense of being yourself, and it handles the routine work of waking life, from catching a bus to answering an examiner.

The ego is **selective**, admitting to awareness only a fraction of the stimuli that reach us. In a crowded canteen you screen out dozens of conversations yet instantly catch your own name from another table; that filtering is the ego at work.

> **Trap.** Jung's **ego is the conscious part of the personality**, full stop. Freud's ego is different: it is partly unconscious and works as the mediator between id, superego and reality. And for Jung the ego is the centre of *consciousness*, not the centre of the *whole* personality; that role belongs, eventually, to the **self** archetype (see below).

### The Attitudes: Extraversion and Introversion

Much of how we consciously perceive our surroundings, and how we respond to them, depends on two opposing **attitudes**: **extraversion** and **introversion**. The idea is about the direction in which psychic energy flows. Jung held that libido can be channelled **outward**, towards the external world, or **inward**, towards the self.

- **Extraverts** are open, sociable and socially assertive. Their orientation is towards other people and the outer world; they draw their energy from it and direct it there.
- **Introverts** are withdrawn and often shy. Their orientation is towards themselves, their own thoughts and feelings.

Everyone has the capacity for **both**, but **only one becomes dominant** and steers behaviour and consciousness. The other does not vanish: it drops into the **personal unconscious** and still exerts influence. So an introvert may act extraverted in certain situations, wish she were more outgoing, or be drawn to an extravert. This is the principle of opposites again: one pole rules consciousness while the other works from below.

> **Trap.** The non-dominant attitude goes into the **personal** unconscious, not the collective unconscious. And it remains influential; options saying it is "lost" or "inactive" are wrong.

> **Trap.** Introversion in Jung's sense is about the **direction of psychic energy**, inward rather than outward. Shyness is a common feature of introverts in the book's description, but it is not the definition, and introversion is not social anxiety. A confident scholar who simply prefers her own thoughts to company is an introvert in Jung's sense without being anxious at all.

Classic background: Jung set out the attitudes in *Psychological Types* (German original 1921) and spelled the word "extravert"; "extrovert" is the popular form. They are the ancestors of the extraversion dimension in Eysenck and the Big Five (Chapter 8).

### Psychological Functions

Jung soon saw that two introverts could be utterly unlike each other, so he added a second set of distinctions, the **psychological functions**: opposing ways of perceiving and handling both the outer world and the inner, subjective one. There are **four**, **sensing, intuiting, thinking and feeling** (Jung, 1927), in two pairs.

**The nonrational pair: sensing and intuiting.** These are called **nonrational** because they do **not use reasoning**. They simply take in experience; they do not evaluate it.

- **Sensing** reproduces an experience through the senses, much as a photograph copies an object. It registers what is actually there: the colour of the wall, the smell of the room, the exact words someone used.
- **Intuiting** does **not** come directly from an external stimulus. It is a hunch, a sense of what lies behind or beyond the facts. The book's example: you are in a dark room and feel sure someone else is there, although you have not seen or heard anyone. Your belief rests on intuition, not on sensory evidence.

**The rational pair: thinking and feeling.** These are called **rational** because they involve **making judgements and evaluations** about experience. They are opposites, but both are in the business of organising and categorising what we take in.

- **Thinking** is a conscious judgement of whether something is **true or false**.
- **Feeling** judges in terms of **like or dislike**, pleasant or unpleasant, stimulating or dull.

Four friends viewing a flat make the four concrete. The **sensing** friend notices the damp patch and the traffic noise; the **intuiting** friend imagines what the place could become and suspects the landlord is hiding something; the **thinking** friend weighs rent against commute; the **feeling** friend registers whether it feels like home. The first two take information in; the last two judge it.

| Function | Pair | What it does | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensing** | Nonrational | Registers experience directly through the senses, like a photograph | What is actually here? |
| **Intuiting** | Nonrational | Perceives through hunches, beyond the sensory evidence | What might be behind this, or what could it become? |
| **Thinking** | Rational | Judges by logic: true or false | Is it correct? |
| **Feeling** | Rational | Judges by value: liked or disliked, pleasant or unpleasant | Is it good, do I like it? |

The dominance rules mirror those for the attitudes. Everyone possesses **all four** functions, yet **just one leads**, and the rest sink into the **personal unconscious**. Put precisely, **one pair** leads (rational or nonrational), and inside it **one member** leads. Since each pair is made of **opposites**, thinking and feeling can never rule together, and neither can sensing and intuiting.

> **Trap.** **Feeling is a rational function** in Jung's system. Students assume "feeling" means emotion and therefore must be irrational. It does not: Jung's feeling is an act of *evaluation* (like or dislike), and evaluation is judgement. The nonrational functions are **sensing and intuiting**, which perceive without judging.

> **Trap (older books and keys).** Schultz and Schultz call sensing and intuiting **nonrational** (they slip once into "irrational"). Jung's own translated term, used in Hall and Lindzey and many older Indian texts, is **irrational functions**, meaning "outside reason", not "against reason". Treat *nonrational* and *irrational* as the same category in an exam.

Classic background: Jungian writers call the dominant function the **superior function**, its opposite within the same pair the **inferior function** (the most unconscious and least developed), and a helper from the other pair the **auxiliary function**. So a thinking type's inferior function is feeling. Schultz and Schultz do not use these labels, but some older Indian question papers do.

### Psychological Types

Combine the **two attitudes** with the **four functions** and you get Jung's **eight psychological types**. Each type is named by its dominant attitude plus its dominant function. The book summarises them in a table, and then describes each in a paragraph; the descriptions are reproduced below in my own wording, with the key features an MCQ would use.

**Table 3.1** Jung's eight psychological types

| Type | Core features | Further detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraverted thinking** | Logical, objective, dogmatic | Live strictly by society's rules; repress feelings and emotion; can seem rigid and cold; make good **scientists** because they study the external world with logical rules |
| **Extraverted feeling** | Emotional, sensitive, sociable | Repress thinking; conform to the traditional values and moral codes they were taught; very sensitive to others' opinions and expectations; make friends easily, effervescent; Jung thought this type **more common in women than men** |
| **Extraverted sensing** | Outgoing, pleasure-seeking, adaptable | Pursue pleasure, happiness and new experiences; strongly oriented to the real world; adapt to different people and changing situations; not introspective; great capacity for enjoying life |
| **Extraverted intuiting** | Creative, motivating, opportunity-seizing | Succeed in **business and politics** by spotting and exploiting opportunities; drawn to new ideas; inspire others to achieve; changeable, jumping from one venture to the next; decide on hunches rather than reflection, yet their decisions tend to be **correct** |
| **Introverted thinking** | More interested in ideas than in people | Get on poorly with others and struggle to communicate their ideas; poor practical judgement; intensely private; prefer abstractions and theories; seek to understand themselves rather than others; seen as stubborn, aloof, arrogant and inconsiderate |
| **Introverted feeling** | Reserved and undemonstrative, yet capable of deep emotion | Repress rational thought; feel deeply but avoid showing it; seem mysterious and inaccessible; quiet, modest, childish; show little consideration for others' feelings and thoughts; appear withdrawn, cold and self-assured |
| **Introverted sensing** | Outwardly detached; express themselves through aesthetic pursuits | Passive, calm, detached from the everyday world; look on human activity with benevolence and amusement; aesthetically sensitive, expressing themselves in **art or music**; repress intuition |
| **Introverted intuiting** | Concerned with the unconscious more than everyday reality | So absorbed in intuition that they lose touch with reality; **visionaries and daydreamers**; aloof and unconcerned with practical matters; poorly understood, seen as odd and eccentric; struggle with everyday life and with planning for the future |

Two entries cut against intuition and make good distractors: **extraverted intuiting** types decide by hunch, yet their decisions are **likely to be correct**; and **introverted feeling** types feel deeply yet show **little consideration for others' feelings**, because "feeling" here means private evaluation, not empathy.

> **Trap.** Jung proposed **eight** types (2 attitudes × 4 functions). The **MBTI** yields **sixteen**. Classic background: Briggs and Myers added a fourth scale, **judging versus perceiving**, which is not one of Jung's attitudes, so their scheme is E or I, S or N, T or F, J or P. An option that credits Jung with sixteen types, or with the judging-perceiving dimension, is wrong.

> **Trap.** The claim that the **extraverted feeling** type is found more often in **women** is Jung's own claim as reported in Table 3.1. It is a standard one-liner MCQ.

### The Personal Unconscious

Below the ego lies the **personal unconscious**. The book compares it directly to Freud's **preconscious**: it is a store of material that **was once conscious** but has since been **forgotten or suppressed**, either because it was trivial or because it was disturbing.

What makes it preconscious-like is the ease of movement: there is **constant two-way traffic** with the ego. Your mind drifts from this page to something you did yesterday and back again. The book's image is a **filing cabinet**: a file can be pulled out with little effort, looked at, and put back until next needed. The personal unconscious also houses the **non-dominant attitude and functions**, so it is not merely an archive; it holds the side of you that consciousness does not express.

> **Trap.** Jung's personal unconscious is likened to Freud's **preconscious**, not to Freud's unconscious. If an option pairs "personal unconscious" with "Freud's id" or with "Freud's repressed unconscious", it is the wrong analogy as the book gives it.

### Complexes

As more and more experiences are filed in the personal unconscious, they start to cluster. Jung called these clusters **complexes**. A complex is a **core or pattern of emotions, memories, perceptions and wishes organised around a common theme**.

The everyday phrase "she has a complex about it" is Jung's idea: preoccupation with a theme (power, status, appearance, money) strong enough to shape behaviour. With the book's example, a power or status complex, a person might seek power directly by standing for election, or borrow it by identifying with powerful things such as a big motorcycle or a fast car. By steering thought and action, the complex ends up **determining how the person perceives the world**; every meeting becomes a contest for status.

Four properties of complexes are tested:

1. **They may be conscious or unconscious.** The unconscious ones are the troublesome kind, because complexes outside conscious control can **intrude on and interfere with consciousness**.
2. **The person usually cannot see the complex, but others can.** The person with a complex is generally unaware of its influence, though its effects may be obvious to everyone around them. Think of a colleague who bristles at every instruction from a senior and does not notice that the pattern repeats with every boss he has had.
3. **They can be harmful or useful.** A **perfection or achievement complex** may push a person to work hard at developing particular talents or skills, which is an asset.
4. **Their origins are not only personal.** Complexes grow out of childhood and adult experience, but Jung also believed they draw on **ancestral experience**, the heritage of the species stored in the **collective unconscious**. This is the bridge between the two unconscious layers.

In practice Jung detected complexes with his **word-association test** (3.6): a stimulus word that touches a complex produces a tell-tale disturbance in the response.

> **Trap.** "Complexes are always unconscious" is false: the book says they may be conscious or unconscious. And "complexes are always harmful" is false: the achievement complex is the book's example of a useful one.

### The Collective Unconscious

The **collective unconscious** is the **deepest and least accessible** level of the psyche, and the book calls it the most unusual and controversial part of Jung's whole system.

The idea is the personal unconscious scaled up to the species. As each individual files personal experience away, **humankind** has stored the experience of all its **human and prehuman ancestors** in the collective unconscious, and this inheritance passes to every new generation. Whatever is **universal**, repeated in every generation, becomes part of our personality, so our primitive past forms the base of the psyche and steers present behaviour. Jung saw this store as **powerful and controlling**, and it ties each personality to the past twice over: to personal childhood, as Freud said, and to the **history of the species**.

The vital qualification: **we do not inherit the experiences themselves, only the potential for them.** We are not born afraid of snakes; we are born **predisposed** to fear them. Whether a predisposition becomes reality depends on what life actually brings.

The experiences universal enough to leave this imprint are ones every generation has met: a **mother** figure, **birth** and **death**, terrors in the dark, worship of **power** or a god-like figure, fear of an **evil being**. Jung wrote that the shape of the world awaiting a person is "already inborn in him, as a virtual image" (Jung, 1953, p. 188). So a baby arrives ready to perceive a mother in a certain way; if the real mother nurtures and supports as mothers typically do, inborn expectation and reality match.

**Why did Jung believe in it?** Because the concept is so strange, the book stresses the reasons and the evidence:

1. **Cross-cultural symbols.** In studying ancient cultures, both mythical and real, Jung found what he took to be **the same themes and symbols** appearing in widely separated parts of the world, in cultures that, as far as he could tell, had **never passed these ideas to one another** by speech or writing.
2. **Patients' dreams and fantasies.** His patients described in their dreams and fantasies the same kinds of symbols he had found in those ancient cultures, though they had no knowledge of them.

He could think of no other way to explain shared symbols across such vast distances of place and time than that each person carries them in the unconscious mind. Notice the structure of the argument: it is an argument from the absence of any other explanation. That is exactly where critics push back (3.8), because similar symbols could arise from similar human circumstances, or could have travelled by routes Jung did not detect, and because the claim cannot be put to experimental test.

> **Trap.** Personal versus collective unconscious. **Personal**: individual, once conscious, forgotten or suppressed, easily retrieved, organised into **complexes**, like Freud's preconscious. **Collective**: species-wide, **never conscious** in the individual's lifetime, inherited, the deepest and least accessible level, expressed through **archetypes**. Complexes belong to the personal unconscious (though they may draw on the collective); archetypes belong to the collective.

> **Trap (older books and keys).** Older textbooks and some older Indian keys call the collective unconscious the **racial unconscious**, and Hall and Lindzey also use **transpersonal unconscious**. The 11th edition uses only "collective unconscious". Treat all three as the same concept. Also, we inherit **predispositions**, not memories or images in finished form; an option saying "we inherit our ancestors' fear of snakes" is wrong, while "we inherit the potential to fear snakes" is right.

### Archetypes

The ancient experiences stored in the collective unconscious show themselves as **recurring themes or patterns**, which Jung called **archetypes** (Jung, 1947). He also called them **primordial images**. There are as many archetypes as there are common human experiences. Because they have been repeated in the lives of generation after generation, they have been **imprinted** in our psyches, and they find expression in our **dreams and fantasies**.

Jung named many archetypes, including **the hero, the mother, the child, God, death, power and the wise old man**. You meet them constantly in myth and story: the hero who leaves home, faces trials and returns transformed; the wise old guide who appears at the moment of crisis; the all-nourishing and sometimes devouring mother. A Jungian would say these figures recur across cultures because they are drawn from the same inherited templates.

A few archetypes are **more fully developed** than the rest and influence the psyche more **consistently**. These are the major archetypes: the **persona**, the **anima and animus**, the **shadow** and the **self**.

#### The Persona

**Persona** was the stage mask through which an actor showed the audience a role. Jung's **persona archetype** is the same thing in daily life: a public face that presents us as someone other than who we really are. It is **necessary**, because school, work and dealing with many kinds of people each demand a different face (the polite trainee, the confident presenter, the dutiful son-in-law).

The danger is **believing** the mask. Then we stop playing the role and **become** it, and the rest of the personality cannot develop. When the ego identifies with the persona instead of the person's real nature, Jung called it **inflation of the persona**. Picture a senior surgeon who is "the surgeon" even at the dinner table, unable to be uncertain or playful with his own children: the role has swallowed the man.

Either way, Jung said, there is **deception**. If you merely play the role, you deceive **others**. If you come to believe the role, you deceive **yourself**.

#### The Anima and Animus

The **anima** and **animus** archetypes express Jung's view that human beings are **essentially bisexual**, in the psychological sense of containing qualities of both sexes. He gave two grounds:

- **Biologically**, each sex secretes the hormones of the other sex as well as its own.
- **Psychologically**, each sex has taken on characteristics, temperaments and attitudes of the other sex through **centuries of living together**.

So the psyche of a **woman** contains masculine aspects, the **animus** archetype, and the psyche of a **man** contains feminine aspects, the **anima** archetype. The book illustrates the idea with the Yin-Yang symbol.

The circle is split by an S-shaped curve into two interlocking halves. In the book's reading the **dark (right) half is the feminine side, the anima**, and the **light (left) half the masculine side, the animus**. The key detail is the **dot of the opposite colour** inside each half: each side carries a seed of the other, just as a man's psyche holds a feminine element and a woman's a masculine one. Together the halves form one whole, which previews the self as a union of opposites.

Jung's reason for these archetypes is adaptive: they aid the **adjustment and survival of the species** by letting each sex **understand the other**, and they predispose us to be attracted by certain opposite-sex qualities and guide our behaviour towards the other sex.

He **insisted that both must be expressed**. Unexpressed, these sides stay dormant and the personality becomes **one-sided**; a photo caption in the book notes that the fully developed personality shows behaviour usually thought typical of the other sex. A father comforting a frightened child with real tenderness, or a woman asserting herself firmly in a negotiation, is expressing the anima or the animus.

> **Trap.** **Anima = the feminine side of a man. Animus = the masculine side of a woman.** A simple anchor: *anima* ends in *-a* like many feminine Latin nouns, *animus* in *-us* like many masculine ones. In Figure 3.1 the **dark** half is the **anima** (feminine) and the **light** half is the **animus** (masculine).

> **Trap.** Jung said the anima and animus must be **expressed**, not suppressed (this is one of the book's review questions). And in individuation, accepting them is described as the **most difficult step** (3.4).

#### The Shadow

The **shadow** is the **most powerful archetype**. It holds the **primitive animal instincts**, which gives it the **deepest roots** of all, and it is where behaviour society calls evil and immoral resides. This dark side must be tamed for people to live together; we have to keep restraining and defending against its impulses, or society will punish us.

The dilemma is that the shadow is **also the source of vitality, spontaneity, creativity and emotion**. **Suppress it completely** and the psyche turns **dull and lifeless**. The **ego's task** is therefore a balance: hold the instincts down enough to be civilised, let enough through for creativity and vigour.

Total suppression has a second cost. The instincts do not disappear; they lie dormant until a **crisis** or a **weak moment of the ego**, then **revolt** and take over, leaving the person dominated by the unconscious. Picture a man known for years as rigidly correct, who has never admitted any aggression in himself, turning uncharacteristically violent after a business collapse: in Jungian terms, the denied shadow has seized a weakened ego.

> **Trap.** Shadow versus Freud's **id** (another review question). **Similar:** both hold primitive, animal instincts that society condemns and that must be restrained. **Different:** the shadow is an **archetype** in the **collective** unconscious with roots in the species' prehuman past, and it is also the source of **creativity, spontaneity and vitality**, so total suppression is harmful; Jung's ideal is acknowledgement and integration, not just control.

> **Trap.** Persona versus shadow. The **persona** is the face we **show**; the **shadow** is what we **hide**, even from ourselves. In the first half of life, the persona is used to screen the shadow from others and from ourselves (3.4). And the "most powerful" archetype in the book is the **shadow**, not the self.

#### The Self

The **self** archetype stands for the whole personality brought into **unity, integration and harmony**, and striving towards that wholeness is, for Jung, **the ultimate goal of life**. It draws all the other parts together and balances them. Here the psyche's great opposition, **conscious versus unconscious**, must be assimilated, so that the **centre of the personality moves from the ego** to a **point of balance midway between conscious and unconscious**, and unconscious material gains more influence than before.

Three further features matter:

1. **It is a goal for the future.** Full realisation is **rarely achieved**; the self **pulls us from ahead**, whereas past experience **pushes from behind**. This is Jung's forward-looking view at its clearest.
2. **It emerges late.** It cannot appear until all the other systems have developed, around **middle age**.
3. **It is life's hardest task.** It needs plans for the future, an accurate view of one's abilities and, above all, **self-knowledge**, so Jung called it the most difficult process we face, demanding persistence, perceptiveness and wisdom.

Classic background: Jung took the **mandala**, the circular design of Hindu and Buddhist tradition, as a symbol of the self, the circle standing for wholeness.

> **Trap.** **Ego** versus **self**. The ego is the centre of **consciousness**; the self is the centre of the **whole personality**, located (once realised) **midway between conscious and unconscious**. The ego is present from childhood; the self emerges only around **middle age** and is rarely fully achieved.

**Summary of the major archetypes**

| Archetype | What it is | Healthy use | Danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Persona** | The public mask or role | Lets us play necessary social roles | **Inflation of the persona**: the ego identifies with the role and the rest of the personality stops growing |
| **Anima** | Feminine aspects in the male psyche | Understanding women; tenderness, emotional range | Left unexpressed: one-sided personality |
| **Animus** | Masculine aspects in the female psyche | Understanding men; assertiveness | Left unexpressed: one-sided personality |
| **Shadow** | Primitive animal instincts; the dark side; the most powerful archetype with the deepest roots | Source of vitality, spontaneity, creativity, emotion | Fully suppressed: flat, lifeless psyche, and the shadow may revolt in a crisis |
| **Self** | Unity, integration and harmony of the whole personality | The ultimate goal of life; pulls us from ahead | Rarely achieved; impossible without self-knowledge |

## 3.4 The Development of the Personality

Jung's developmental theory starts from the second of his three breaks with Freud. Personality is shaped not only by what we were and what happened to us, but also by **what we hope to become**. He criticised Freud for treating past events as the only shapers of personality and leaving out the future. In Jung's view we **keep developing at every age**, always moving towards a fuller level of self-realisation.

Jung also took a much **longer view** than Freud. Freud concentrated on the earliest years and expected little real development **after the age of 5**. Jung did not lay out a detailed sequence of stages in the way Freud did, but he wrote of **two general periods** in the developmental process (Jung, 1930): the first half of life, which is about adapting to the outer world, and the second half, which is about turning inward. The book's table divides the first half into childhood and puberty-to-young-adulthood, which gives three rows.

**Table 3.2** Jung's developmental stages

| Stage | What happens | Direction of focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Childhood** | The ego begins to develop once the child can tell **self apart from others** | Personality largely a reflection of the parents |
| **Puberty to young adulthood** | The adolescent must adapt to the mounting demands of reality: education, career, family. **Consciousness is dominant**. Puberty is **psychic birth** | External; mainly **extraversion** |
| **Middle age** | A **period of transition**: the personality's focus shifts from external to internal, trying to **balance the unconscious with the conscious** | Internal; shift towards **introversion** |

> **Trap.** The text says Jung described **two general periods** of development (Jung, 1930); Table 3.2 lists **three** stages because it splits the first period. Classic background: some older textbooks describe **four** stages (childhood, youth, middle life and old age), drawing on Jung's essay "The Stages of Life", in which he compared a lifetime to the sun's course across the sky, rising in the morning of life and turning inward in the afternoon. The 11th edition does not use old age as a stage; if a question is set from Schultz, middle age is the last stage named.

### Childhood to Young Adulthood

The **ego** begins to form in early childhood, but at first only in a primitive way, because the young child has no distinct identity yet. What we might call a young child's personality is, at this point, **little more than a reflection of the parents' personalities**. It follows that parents have a large influence on the child's personality, and they can help or hinder its development by how they treat the child.

Parents can get in the way in two opposite ways. They may **impose their own personalities**, wanting the child as an extension of themselves (a driven lawyer mother treating her son's dreaminess as a defect). Or they may push the child towards a personality **quite unlike their own**, to make up vicariously for their **own shortcomings** (a father who never got to study medicine steering his daughter into it).

The ego takes real shape only when children can **distinguish themselves from other people and objects**. Put simply, **consciousness forms when the child is able to say "I"**.

The psyche does not take on a **definite form and content** until **puberty**. Jung called this period **psychic birth**. It is a time of difficulty and of the need to adapt: childhood fantasies have to give way as the adolescent meets the demands of reality.

From the teenage years through **young adulthood**, life is taken up with **preparatory activities**: finishing education, starting a career, marrying and beginning a family. During these years:

- our **focus is external**;
- our **conscious is dominant**;
- our main conscious attitude is, in general, **extraversion**.

The aim of this half of life is to reach our goals and build a secure, successful place for ourselves in the world. Young adulthood, in Jung's picture, ought to be exciting and demanding, full of new horizons and achievements.

> **Trap.** **Psychic birth** is Jung's name for **puberty**, when the psyche takes on definite form and content. It is not physical birth, not the moment the child first says "I" (that is the beginning of consciousness), and not age 5.

### Middle Age

For Jung, the **big personality changes come between 35 and 40**, the age band in which he and many of his patients went through crisis.

His starting puzzle: by about 40 the adaptation problems of youth are usually solved, and the typical 40-year-old is settled in career, marriage and community. Why, then, are so many overcome by **despair and worthlessness** just when success has arrived? His patients said the same thing: they felt **empty**; adventure and zest had gone and **life had lost its meaning**. Jung became convinced these changes are **inevitable and universal**, a **natural transition** in which the personality is *meant* to change, **necessary and beneficial** rather than a breakdown.

The irony is that success causes the crisis. Enormous energy went into the preparatory tasks of the first half; by 40 they are done, but the energy remains with **nowhere to go** and must be **rechannelled** into new interests. This is the **principle of equivalence** (3.2) at work: the energy that built the career is not destroyed once the career is built; without an outlet of equal value it sinks into the unconscious and is felt as emptiness.

What should the new direction be? Jung's answer is the reversal of everything that worked in the first half:

| First half of life | Second half of life |
|---|---|
| Focus on the **objective** world of reality: education, career, family | Focus on the **inner, subjective** world that has been neglected |
| Attitude: **extraversion** | Attitude shifts towards **introversion** |
| **Consciousness** dominant | Consciousness tempered by awareness of the **unconscious** |
| Interests **physical and material** | Interests **spiritual, philosophical and intuitive** |
| Exclusive focus on consciousness | **Balance** among all aspects of the personality |

At middle age, then, we have to begin the work of **realising, or actualising, the self**. If we succeed in **integrating the unconscious with the conscious**, we can reach a new level of positive psychological health that Jung called **individuation**.

> **Trap.** In Jung's view the midlife crisis is **normal, inevitable, universal and beneficial**, a necessary transition, not a pathology. And the shift is from **extraversion to introversion**, from **conscious to unconscious**, from **material to spiritual**; a question reversing any of these directions is a trap.

### Individuation: How to Reach Fulfilment

**Individuation** means, simply, becoming an individual: fulfilling one's capacities and developing one's self. More formally it is the state of psychological health that results when **all the conscious and unconscious facets of the personality are integrated**.

The tendency towards individuation is **innate and inevitable**, but the environment can **help or hinder** it. The book names educational and economic opportunities and the quality of the **parent-child relationship** as examples. Reaching Jung's ideal of self-fulfilment involves several stages or steps, which the book sets out under five headings.

#### Confront the Unconscious

The middle-aged person must **drop the behaviours and values that ran the first half of life** and **confront the unconscious**, bringing it into awareness and accepting its guidance. In practice that means **attending to dreams**, **following fantasies**, and giving them form through **creative imagination** (writing, painting or another medium). The guide is no longer the calculating reason of earlier years; Jung wanted the **unconscious to be allowed to flow spontaneously**, since that alone reveals the true self. (This is how Jung handled his own crisis, with *The Red Book* as its record.)

His caution is a favourite exam point: **admitting unconscious forces does not mean being ruled by them.** The material must be **assimilated and balanced** with consciousness, and at this stage **no single aspect should dominate**, neither consciousness nor the unconscious, nor any one attitude, function or archetype. Individuation means all of them in **harmonious balance**.

#### Dethrone the Persona

A central part of the midlife process is a change in how the archetypes operate, and the first change is **dethroning the persona**. We still need to play social roles to function in the real world and to get along with different people, but we must recognise that our **public personality may not represent our true nature**, and we must come to accept the **genuine self** that the persona has been covering. The surgeon from 3.3 does not stop being a surgeon at work, but he stops being only the surgeon.

#### Accept Our Dark Sides

The second archetypal shift concerns the **shadow**: we have to recognise its destructive pull and own this dark side, including primitive impulses such as **selfishness**. Acknowledging is not surrendering: we do not give in to these impulses or let them run us; we simply accept that they exist.

In the first half of life, the **persona** is used to shield the dark side, because we want others to see only our good qualities. But in hiding the shadow from others we **also hide it from ourselves**. That has to change as part of coming to know ourselves. A fuller awareness of both the **destructive and the constructive** sides of the shadow gives the personality a deeper, richer dimension, because the shadow's tendencies bring **zest, spontaneity and vitality** to life.

This step shows the central theme of the whole individuation process: every aspect of the personality must be brought into harmony with every other. Knowing only the good side of our nature produces a **one-sided** personality. As with all the opposing components of personality, **both sides must be expressed** before individuation can be achieved.

#### Accept Our Anima and Animus

We must also come to terms with our **psychological bisexuality**. A man must be able to express his **anima**, traditionally feminine qualities such as **tenderness**; a woman must express her **animus**, traditionally masculine qualities such as **assertiveness**.

Jung considered this the **most difficult step** in individuation, because it means the **greatest change in our self-image**. The rewards are correspondingly large: accepting the emotional qualities of both sexes opens **new sources of creativity** and acts as the **final release from parental influences**.

#### Transcend

Once the structures of the psyche have been individuated and acknowledged, the next stage of development can happen. Jung called it **transcendence**: an **innate tendency towards unity or wholeness** in the personality, which unites all the opposing aspects of the psyche. Environmental factors can block it. The book's examples are an **unsatisfactory marriage** and a **frustrating job**, either of which can hold back transcendence and prevent the full achievement of the self.

> **Trap.** **Individuation** versus **transcendence**. Individuation is the overall process and the resulting state of health: integrating all conscious and unconscious facets and fulfilling one's capacities. Transcendence is the **final stage**, the innate drive towards **unity** that joins the opposites once the structures have been individuated. Also remember which steps carry superlatives: accepting the **anima/animus** is the most difficult **step** of individuation; developing the **self** is the most difficult **process** in life.

> **Trap.** The tendency towards individuation is **innate and inevitable**, yet it can be **helped or hindered** by the environment (education, economic opportunity, the parent-child relationship; for transcendence, a bad marriage or a frustrating job). Options claiming it is "entirely learned" or "unaffected by environment" are both wrong.

## 3.5 Questions about Human Nature

On Schultz's six issues Jung differs sharply from Freud, almost always in the hopeful direction.

**Free will versus determinism.** Less deterministic than Freud, Jung still allowed that childhood experience and the archetypes **partly determine** personality. Beyond that his system leaves ample room for **free will** and for **spontaneity**, which springs from the **shadow**.

**Nature versus nurture.** A **mixed** position: the drive towards individuation and transcendence is **inborn**, but **learning and experience** can help or block it, just as an inherited predisposition (fear of snakes) is realised only through experience.

**Equilibrium versus growth.** Life's ultimate and necessary aim is **realising the self**; few reach it, but we are **always driven** towards it, which puts Jung on the **growth** side.

> **Trap.** Do not let the **entropy principle** mislead you. Entropy describes a tendency of psychic energy to flow towards balance, and individuation involves balancing opposites, but Jung's answer on the equilibrium-versus-growth issue is **growth**: the goal is self-realisation, and a perfectly balanced, energy-free psyche is something he said could never and should never be reached.

**Importance of childhood.** Childhood matters, but it does **not finish shaping personality by age 5**, as Freud held. **Midlife experience** and our **hopes for the future** weigh more.

**Uniqueness versus universality.** The answer depends on the half of life. We are **unique only in the first half**. Once individuation gets under way in midlife, a **universal personality** develops in which **no single aspect dominates**; **uniqueness then disappears**, and the person no longer fits any one psychological type.

> **Trap.** "Individuation" sounds as if it should mean becoming more unique. In Schultz's account of Jung it means the opposite on this issue: individuation produces a **universal** personality in which no attitude, function or archetype dominates, so the person is **no longer a particular type** and **uniqueness disappears**. Uniqueness belongs to the **first** half of life.

**Optimism versus pessimism.** Jung is **more hopeful** than Freud. We are driven to grow and improve, development continues **throughout life** rather than stopping in childhood, and becoming better is always possible. Even the **species keeps improving**: today's generations are well ahead of our primitive ancestors. But the optimism came with a warning about a **sickness of dissociation** in **Western culture**: by overvaluing **materialism, reason and empirical science**, the West risks losing contact with the unconscious and its **archetypes**. His hope was, in the book's phrase, of a **watchful, warning kind**.

| Issue | Jung's position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Free will vs determinism** | Mainly **free will**, partly determined | Childhood and archetypes have some influence, but there is ample room for choice and for spontaneity from the shadow |
| **Nature vs nurture** | **Both** | The drive to individuation and transcendence is innate; learning and experience help or thwart it |
| **Past vs present (and future)** | **Present and future** weigh more | Childhood matters but does not fix personality by 5; midlife experience and future hopes matter more |
| **Uniqueness vs universality** | **Unique in the first half of life**, **universal** after individuation | Once no single aspect dominates, the person is no longer a type |
| **Equilibrium vs growth** | **Growth** | The ultimate goal is realisation of the self, rarely reached but always pursued |
| **Optimism vs pessimism** | **Optimistic**, with a warning | Growth continues for life and the species improves; but the West risks a **sickness of dissociation** |

## 3.6 Assessment in Jung's Theory

Jung assessed the psyche with tools drawn from both **science and the supernatural**, so his approach was partly **objective** and partly **mystical**. On one side he mined the symbols, myths, religions and rituals of many cultures and eras, his patients' dreams and fantasies and his own, and his reading in **ancient languages, alchemy and astrology**. On the other, what first made American psychologists notice him was **empirical and physiological** work: timing responses and recording bodily reactions in word association. An unorthodox **blend of opposites**, fittingly, for a theory built on opposition.

### Jung's Assessments of His Patients

Jung's sessions were unconventional and often chaotic. **There was no couch**; he said he had no wish to put patients to bed. Usually the two sat in comfortable **chairs facing each other**, sometimes with Jung gazing out of a window at the nearby lake, and occasionally the session moved onto his **sailboat**.

A former patient quoted by Bair (2003) recalled him pacing and gesturing as he moved between her problem, a dream, a memory, a parable or a joke, and then abruptly turning quiet, sitting uncomfortably close and delivering an interpretation sharp enough that its painful truth could not be dodged.

He could be **rude**. A patient arriving punctually was sent home to cure herself because Jung could not face another case that day (quoted in Brome, 1981). Another who started on her **mother**, exactly what Freud would have welcomed, was cut short with "Don't waste your time" (Bair, 2003). For Jung, more interested in present and future, a long tour of childhood grievances could be a detour.

He also **took patients' fantasies at face value**, as real for them. On first meeting **Marie-Louise von Franz** (1915 to 1998), later a lifelong disciple, he described a patient who lived on the moon; when von Franz suggested he meant the woman acted as if she did, Jung insisted she really did, and von Franz concluded that one of them must be crazy (obituary, *New York Times*, 23 March 1998).

Beyond these informal encounters, Jung used three **more formal techniques**: the **word-association test**, **symptom analysis** and **dream analysis**. A fourth instrument, the **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator**, was developed later by other people to measure Jung's psychological types.

| Feature | Freud | Jung |
|---|---|---|
| Seating | Patient on the couch, analyst out of sight | Face-to-face in chairs; sometimes on a sailboat |
| Style | Neutral, reserved | Animated, personal, sometimes rude |
| Childhood and the mother | Central material | Could be dismissed as a waste of time |
| Patients' fantasies | Disguised expressions of wishes | Accepted at face value, real to the patient |

### Word Association

In the **word-association test**, a person hears a **stimulus word** and responds with **whatever word comes immediately to mind**. The technique has since become a standard tool in both laboratory and clinical psychology.

In the **early 1900s** Jung used a list of **100 words** chosen because he believed they could stir up emotion. He measured two things:

1. **Reaction time**: how long the patient took to respond to each word.
2. **Physiological reactions**: bodily changes that showed the emotional effect of the stimulus word. (Classic background: these included changes in breathing and in the skin's electrical conductance, which is why the method fed into later lie-detection techniques.)

The point of the test was to **uncover complexes**. A word that touches a complex disturbs the smooth flow of response, and the disturbance shows up in characteristic ways. The book lists these **complex indicators**:

| Complex indicator | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Physiological response | A change in bodily arousal to a particular word |
| Delay | An unusually long reaction time |
| Perseveration of the same response | Giving the same reply to different stimulus words |
| Slip of the tongue | Saying something other than intended |
| Stammering | Stumbling over the response |
| More than one word | Answering with a phrase or sentence instead of a single word |
| Made-up words | Producing a word that does not exist |
| No response | Failing to answer at all |

**Table 3.3** Normal and neurotic responses to Jung's word-association test. The book reproduces a short list of stimulus words from Jung's 1909 paper on the association method, each paired with the reply typical of a normal person and the reply actually given by a neurotic patient. The normal replies tend to be simple, single, matter-of-fact words. The neurotic replies show the complex indicators in action. To take two examples in paraphrase: for a stimulus word meaning "rich", the patient answered with a money word and then added an uncertain "I don't know"; for a word about sinning, the patient produced a whole sentence firmly disowning the idea altogether. A long, defensive reply to a single emotionally loaded word is precisely the kind of disturbance that points the clinician towards a complex.

How it would work today in a clinic: a 30-year-old man referred for unexplained anxiety answers most words in under two seconds, but pauses for seven seconds at "father", repeats the word "work" to three unrelated stimuli, and gives a rambling sentence to "failure". Each is a complex indicator; together they suggest a complex organised around his father, work and failure, which the clinician can then explore.

> **Trap (look-alike names).** Classic background: **Francis Galton** was the first to experiment with word association (around 1879, testing himself), and it was later used in Wundt's laboratory. **Jung**, working with his colleague Franz Riklin at the Burghölzli, turned it into a **clinical** instrument by adding reaction-time and physiological measurement and using the disturbances to detect **complexes**. If a question asks who used word association to uncover complexes, the answer is Jung; if it asks who first experimented with free word association, it is Galton.

> **Trap.** The book's glossary classes the word-association test as a **projective technique**. The **MBTI** is not projective; it is a **self-report** questionnaire. And the word-association test is the contribution the book credits with inspiring the **Rorschach inkblot test** and **lie-detection** methods (3.8).

### Symptom Analysis

**Symptom analysis** focuses on the **symptoms the patient reports** and works from the patient's **free associations to those symptoms**. The book likens it to Freud's **cathartic method** (the "talking cure" from the Breuer period). Often the combination of the patient's associations and the analyst's interpretation is enough to **ease the symptoms or remove them**.

For example, a woman with recurrent stomach pain before family gatherings might be asked to associate freely to the pain itself: what it feels like, when it began, what comes to mind as she describes it. Her associations might lead to a long-standing conflict with her mother-in-law, and once the meaning of the symptom is brought into the open and interpreted, the pain may ease.

### Dream Analysis

Jung agreed with Freud that dreams are the **"royal road" to the unconscious**. But he went beyond Freud in two ways: he was interested in more than the **causes** of dreams, and he believed dreams were **more than unconscious wishes**. He gave dreams two forward-looking and balancing functions:

1. **Dreams are prospective.** They help us **prepare for experiences and events we expect** to happen. A student who dreams repeatedly of walking into an interview room and calmly answering questions is, on this view, rehearsing.
2. **Dreams are compensatory.** They restore **balance between the psyche's opposites**, offsetting whichever structure has become **overdeveloped**. A meek clerk who is never assertive at work may dream of commanding an army; a rigidly rational engineer may dream in floods of emotion. The dream supplies what the waking personality leaves out, in line with the principle of opposites.

Jung also changed the method of working with dreams:

- **A series, not a single dream.** Freud interpreted each dream on its own. Jung worked with a **series of dreams** reported by the patient **over a period of time**, which let him find recurring themes, issues and problems persisting in the patient's unconscious.
- **Amplification instead of free association.** In Freud's free association, the patient starts from one dream element and builds a **chain** of associations away from it, reporting related memories and events. In **amplification**, Jung held the patient to the **one dream element**, asking for association after association to **that same image** until a theme showed itself. Free association travels away from the image; amplification circles around it.
- **No manifest-latent split.** Jung did **not** try to separate the **manifest** content of a dream from a hidden **latent** content, as Freud did. For Jung the dream means what it shows, if one can learn its symbolic language.

| Aspect | Freud | Jung |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose of dreams | Disguised fulfilment of unconscious wishes | **Prospective** (prepare for the future) and **compensatory** (balance the psyche) |
| Unit of analysis | Each dream separately | A **series** of dreams over time |
| Method | **Free association**: a chain moving away from the element | **Amplification**: repeated associations to the **same** element |
| Manifest vs latent content | Central distinction | **Not** used |
| Shared ground | Dreams are the royal road to the unconscious | Same |

> **Trap.** Jung **did not** distinguish manifest from latent content. If an option says Jung's dream analysis decoded the latent content behind the manifest dream, it is describing Freud.

> **Trap.** **Amplification** is not free association under another name. Free association moves outward along a chain; amplification keeps returning to the one element. And "prospective" (preparing for what is coming) and "compensatory" (balancing what is over-developed) are the two functions Jung added to Freud's wish-fulfilment view.

### The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator

The **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)** is an assessment instrument based on Jung's psychological types and the attitudes of introversion and extraversion. It was developed in the **1920s** by **Katharine Cook Briggs** and her daughter **Isabel Briggs Myers** (Briggs & Myers, 1943, 1976). Today it is the **most popular and most frequently used personality test ever devised**, taken by **more than two million people every year**, often for **hiring and promotion** decisions (Cunningham, 2012). Anyone entering the corporate world is quite likely to meet it.

**Table 3.4** Sample items from the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator. The book shows five forced-choice items. Each asks which of two answers comes closer to how the respondent usually feels or acts, and the two answers are designed to pull towards opposite poles of a type preference. Two examples in paraphrase: whether, on a day out, you would rather plan what you will do and when or simply set off and see what happens (a planning-versus-spontaneity preference); and whether you tend to have a handful of deep friendships or a wide circle of broader ones (an introversion-versus-extraversion preference). The other items follow the same two-choice format.

**How the test came about.** The MBTI's origin story is told in some detail, and it is a family drama. The test was developed in **Washington, DC**, by **Katharine Briggs**, who adored her teenage daughter Isabel. Katharine had home-schooled Isabel and wrote a book-length manuscript about her, calling her a genius and even a "little Shakespeare". When Isabel went to **Swarthmore** College near Philadelphia, mother and daughter wrote to each other almost every day. Then Isabel brought home a law student, **Clarence Myers**.

The clash was one of temperament. Gladwell (2004) describes Katharine and Isabel as bold, imaginative and intuitive, and Clarence as practical, logical and detail-minded. Startled by how unlike them her future son-in-law was, Katharine set about teaching herself psychology to make sense of him. Her answer arrived in **1923**, when she came across Jung's ***Psychological Types***: here was a scheme for sorting people and accounting for their differences. With no research grant, no university affiliation and no graduate students, she developed, with Isabel's help, a test to measure those differences.

**Institutional history:**

- **1975**: Isabel Briggs Myers joined **Mary McCaulley**, a **University of Florida** psychology professor, in founding the **Center for Applications of Psychological Type** to train MBTI users and support research.
- **1979**: founding of the **Association for Psychological Type**.
- Research on the test's uses appeared in **two journals** devoted to it.

The book calls the MBTI the **most visible practical outgrowth** of Jung's work on personality.

Classic background: the MBTI scores four bipolar preferences, **Extraversion-Introversion, Sensing-iNtuition, Thinking-Feeling and Judging-Perceiving**, and combines them into a four-letter code (for example ESTJ or INFP), giving **16 types**. The **judging-perceiving** scale was Briggs and Myers's addition. The best-known criticisms are that many people receive a **different type when retested** after a short interval, and that scores on each scale are spread **continuously** rather than falling into two clear groups, so the type boundaries are artificial. Keep these as background; the 11th edition's own criticism is simply that not all research supports Jung's types (Pittenger, 2005).

> **Trap.** The MBTI was developed by **Katharine Cook Briggs** (mother) and **Isabel Briggs Myers** (daughter), **not by Jung**. It was developed in the **1920s**; the book's citations are dated **1943** and **1976**. Katharine read *Psychological Types* in **1923**. Clarence Myers was Isabel's husband-to-be, not a co-author. The 1975 centre was co-founded with **Mary McCaulley**.

## 3.7 Research on Jung's Theory

### Jung's own method: life-history reconstruction

Like Freud, Jung relied on the **case study**. His version, **life-history reconstruction**, meant gathering an extensive account of a person's past in order to trace the **developmental patterns** Jung believed had produced the **current neurosis**.

The book turns every criticism of Freud's data on Jung, and "which is NOT a criticism" questions draw on the list:

1. **Not objective, not controlled.** The data were not gathered by objective observation or in a **controlled, systematic** way, and the clinical interviews could not be **duplicated, verified or quantified**.
2. **No verbatim records, no checks.** Jung kept no word-for-word notes and did not test whether patients' reports were **accurate**.
3. **Small, unrepresentative sample** (nearly two-thirds middle-aged), so generalising is hard.
4. **Subjective, unexplained analysis.** Jung **never described his procedures**, and his interpretations, filtered through his reading across cultures and disciplines and his **own dreams and fantasies**, are among the most unusual in personality theory.
5. **Possible slanting and borrowed visions.** He may have bent conclusions to fit the theory, and Noll (1993, 1994) claims the visions of his midlife crisis can be **traced to things he had read**; if so, the "spontaneous" archetypal imagery may have come from his library.
6. **Untestable propositions.** Many of his observations, like Freud's, **cannot be put to experimental test**.

Jung did not care. He once said that anyone who wants to learn about the human mind will learn "nothing, or almost nothing, from experimental psychology" (quoted in Ellenberger, 1970, p. 694).

> **Trap.** **Life-history reconstruction** is Jung's name for his case-study method. It is not a projective test and not a questionnaire. And note that the criticisms of Jung's data are the **same** as those made of Freud's; the book does not claim Jung was more scientific.

### Research on Psychological Types

In spite of Jung's dismissal of experimental psychology, researchers have managed to test some parts of his theory experimentally, and the results **support some of his propositions**. Most of this work uses the **MBTI** and concentrates on the attitudes of **introversion and extraversion**. Not all research supports Jung's account of the psychological types, however (**Pittenger, 2005**).

#### Studies of College Students

**Job interests (Stricker & Ross, 1962).** In a study of college students, job interests were closely related to Jungian attitudes and types. **Introverts** showed strong interest in work **without much personal interaction**, such as **technical and scientific** jobs. **Extraverts** preferred jobs with **a lot of social interaction**, such as **sales and public relations**. This is exactly what the definition of the attitudes predicts: energy directed inward suits solitary, object-focused work; energy directed outward suits people-focused work.

**Grades (DiRienzo, Das, Synn, Kitts, & McGrath, 2010).** **Introverted, feeling and judging** types had **higher grade point averages** than the other types.

**Professions (Hanewitz, 1978).** A large MBTI sample covered four groups: **police officers**, **schoolteachers**, and students of **social work** and of **dentistry**. Teachers and social work students came out high on **intuiting and feeling**. Police officers and dental students, whose dealings with people take a different form, came out high on **extraversion, sensing and thinking**.

**Creative versus conventional interests (Apostal, 1991).** College students high in **intuiting** leaned towards more **creative** vocational interests; those high in **sensing** preferred more **conventional** ones. Again this fits the functions: intuition perceives possibilities, sensing perceives what is already there.

**Who graduates (Murray & Johnson, 2001).** Among **women admitted to the U.S. Naval Academy** who took the MBTI, the **extraverted-sensing-thinking-judging** types were the most likely to **graduate**. The women most likely to **drop out** scored higher in **feeling and perceiving**.

**Who drops out (Barrineau, 2005).** A **10-year** research programme on students at **liberal arts colleges** also found that those most likely to leave before graduating scored high in **perceiving**.

**Medical specialties (Stilwell, Wallick, Thal, & Burleson, 2000).** Among medical students who took the MBTI, those who became **primary care physicians** had scored high in **feeling and introversion**, while those who became **surgeons** had been classed as **extraverted and thinking** types.

**Well-being (Harrington & Loffredo, 2001).** In another sample of college students, **extraverts** scored higher than introverts in **psychological well-being and general life satisfaction**.

#### Studies of Cognitive Functioning

Jungian types seem to differ in how they process information.

- **Memory for kinds of stimuli (Carlson & Levy, 1973).** **Introverted thinking** types remember **neutral or impersonal** stimuli, such as **numbers**, better. **Extraverted feeling** types remember **human stimuli with emotional overtones**, such as **facial expressions**, better.
- **Brain waves (Gram, Dunn, & Ellis, 2005).** **EEG** activity differed for each of the psychological types as assessed by the MBTI.
- **Autobiographical memory (Carlson, 1980).** Participants were asked to recall their most vivid experiences of emotions such as **joy, anger and shame**. **Extraverted feeling** types most often reported memories that **involved other people** and recalled **highly emotional details**. **Introverted thinking** types more often recalled events that happened when they were **alone**, and remembered more **emotionally neutral, factual** experiences.

The logic of these studies is worth seeing. Introverted thinking and extraverted feeling are opposite on **both** dimensions (attitude and function), so if Jung's types mean anything, these two should differ most sharply in what they attend to and remember. They do.

#### Studies of Diverse Cultures

The MBTI has been translated for use in many countries, including **Turkey, Syria and other Arab countries** (Atay, 2012; Ayoubui & Ustwani, 2014). Cross-cultural findings include:

- **Muslims in England (Francis & Datoo, 2012):** within a Muslim context, **religious participation** was associated with **extraversion** rather than introversion and with **thinking** rather than feeling.
- **Greek Orthodox churchgoers in London (Lewis, Varvatsoulias, & George, 2012):** they scored high in **introversion, sensing, thinking and judging**.
- **Women college students in Israel (Francis, Yablon, & Robbins, 2013):** **extraverts and feeling** types scored higher on **happiness** than introverts and thinking types.
- **College students in Singapore (Goby, 2006):** **extraverts** preferred to communicate **in person**, **introverts** preferred **online** contact.
- **Students in Bulgaria aged 13 to 16 (Papazova & Pencheva, 2008):** those high on **intuiting and sensing** had **greater self-esteem**; those **low on sensing and judging** had **lower self-esteem**.
- **Junior and mid-level managers in India (Mathew & Bhatewara, 2006):** managers scoring very high on **thinking** tended to be **collaborative** in managing **conflict**, while those high in **feeling** tended to **avoid** conflict. **Men** scored higher on **thinking**, **women** higher on **feeling**.
- **Managers in China (Huifang & Shuming, 2004):** **no significant differences** between men and women in MBTI scores, in contrast to the Indian finding.
- **More Chinese managers (Yang & Zhao, 2009):** their MBTI scores were **much more similar to those of managers in the United States** than to the general population of China. Occupation, in other words, may shape (or select) type more than nationality does.
- **Australians and Canadians (Ashton, Lee, & Paunonen, 2002):** for people high in extraversion, the **fundamental motivating feature** was the **social attention** their behaviour brought them.

> **Trap.** The **Indian** study is **Mathew and Bhatewara (2006)**: high **thinking** went with **collaborative** conflict management, high **feeling** with **avoiding** conflict, and men scored higher on thinking while women scored higher on feeling. Do not mix it up with the **Chinese** managers study (Huifang & Shuming, 2004), which found **no sex differences**.

### Research on Dreams

To look for archetypes in dreams, **Cann and Donderi (1986)** collected three kinds of dream report from each participant across **3 weeks**: the **latest** dream, the **most vivid** one and the **earliest** one remembered. Participants also completed the **MBTI** and a second personality test. The findings:

- **Introverts** were more likely than extraverts to recall **everyday dreams** with no relation to archetypes.
- **Intuiting** types recalled **more archetypal dreams** than **sensing** types did.
- People high in **neuroticism** recalled **fewer** archetypal dreams than people low in neuroticism.

The researchers concluded that these results agreed with predictions derived from Jung's theory. The intuiting finding is the cleanest fit: the function that perceives beyond the sensory surface is the one most open to material from the collective unconscious.

### Individuation

**Executives (Lyons, 2002).** An intensive study of men and women aged **37 to 55** in **senior executive** positions found behaviour that supports Jung's idea of individuation. The design used several sources: **interviews** with the executives, their **colleagues** and their **family members**; **observation** of their behaviour at work; and three tests, the **TAT, the MBTI and the Adjective Check List**. The researcher concluded that these executives looked inward for direction and energy, questioned the values they had inherited, gave up outdated parts of themselves, revealed new sides of who they were, and allowed themselves more playfulness and spontaneity. They also acted on their **own wishes and desires** rather than merely reacting to outside pressures. These are the behavioural and emotional signs Jung described in individuation: confronting the unconscious, dethroning the persona, accepting the neglected sides.

#### The Midlife Crisis in Women

Jung placed the onset of middle age at about **40**, the time of crisis for him and many of his patients. Jung, and others who studied the **midlife crisis**, first thought of it as far more likely to strike **men** than women. More recently it has been recognised that **women undergo a similar crisis**.

- **National survey (Barrett, 2005).** Compared with men, American women reported **poorer health**, **little or no say over how their marriages went**, and **fewer chances of employment**.
- **Stressful change (Darling, Coccia, & Senatore, 2012).** Women at midlife who went through **more stressful changes** reported **lower life satisfaction**.
- **Sexual orientation and race (Brown, Matthews, & Bromberger, 2005; Howell & Beth, 2004).** **Lesbian** women reported **less emotional turmoil** at midlife than heterosexual women. **Black** women had **more positive self-perceptions** in middle age than **White** women.
- **The hardest period (Helson, 1992).** Women in their **50s**, studied at intervals since their **senior year of college**, were asked to describe the most difficult period of their lives since graduating. A range of self-ratings showed that the **early 40s** were the time of **greatest conflict**, as Jung would predict.
- **Career versus home (Levinson, 1996).** Many women in midlife go through an intense period of **self-evaluation**, reviewing their lives and judging their success or failure. Women who had **actively built careers** found the transition **easier** than women who had stayed home to concentrate on marriage and family. Many in the home-centred group concluded that their marriages had been a **partial or complete failure**, and their regret led them to consider drastic changes. The career women felt much **less need for major change** at midlife.

#### Making Life Changes

Research on **two samples of college-educated women** confirmed this rather gloomy picture. The women were studied first as students and again in their **late 30s or early 40s**.

- The **majority** went through a period of **life re-evaluation around age 40**, as Jung predicted.
- About **two-thirds** went on to make **major changes in their lives between 37 and 43**, prompted directly by that self-evaluation.
- Asked at **37** whether they would make the same life choices again, **34 percent** of the sample from a **wealthy private college** said **no**, and so did **61 percent** of the sample from a **big state university**. Given a second chance, they would pursue **education and career** rather than family goals.

A sense of dissatisfaction at midlife pushed many women to change, but not all of them could return to study or find work that used their abilities. Those who managed to make **satisfying life changes by age 43** reported significantly **greater general well-being** than those who could not. The group that changed gained a **stronger sense of personal identity** and a **broader view of their own capabilities**. Regret about earlier choices, in other words, had led to positive change (**Stewart & Ostrove, 1998; Stewart & Vandewater, 1999**).

A separate midlife sample pointed the same way: **two-thirds** of the women judged their own lives **less successful than those of their grown daughters with jobs outside the home** (**Carr, 2004**).

#### Personal Growth or Stagnation?

**Three prototypes (John, Pals, & Westenberg, 1998).** A **longitudinal** study followed women for **20 years after they graduated from college**. Based on ratings by **independent judges**, the women's personalities at midlife fell into **three levels or prototypes**:

| Prototype | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Conflicted** | Lowest | Beset by inner conflict and psychological problems; anxious, hostile, rigid, with poor relationships; judged **psychologically immature** |
| **Traditional** | Middle | Duty-bound and guilt-prone, living for others and for social approval at the cost of their own growth and self-expression; capable, responsible citizens centred on **marriage rather than career**, but short on maturity and self-insight |
| **Individuated** | Highest | Matches Jung's **individuation**, the ideal resolution of midlife: autonomous, creative, warm and empathic, tolerant, resilient, achievement-oriented and self-actualising, with intellectual and cultural breadth |

**Growth in midlife (Arnold, 2005; Leonard & Burns, 2006).** Studies of women in the **United States and Australia** confirmed that for some, middle age brings **increasing personal growth**: moving in new directions, shedding old problems and feeling free to be themselves. They had reached a higher level of psychological maturity, which supports Jung's view of individuation as the **highest state of psychological health and self-development**.

> **Trap.** The order of the John, Pals and Westenberg (1998) prototypes is **conflicted (lowest), traditional (middle), individuated (highest)**. The **traditional** women were competent and responsible but not psychologically mature; "traditional" is not the healthiest level.

> **Trap.** The two-sample midlife study: **34 percent (private college) versus 61 percent (state university)** would not repeat their life choices when asked at **37**; about **two-thirds** made major changes between **37 and 43**; satisfying change by **43** meant greater well-being. The "two-thirds less successful than their working daughters" finding is a **different** study (**Carr, 2004**).

### Highlights: Research on Jung's Ideas

The book closes the research section with a Highlights box. In summary form:

**On psychological types:**
- Introverts are drawn to **technical and scientific** jobs.
- Extraverts prefer jobs with plenty of **social interaction**.
- **Introverted, feeling and judging** types tend to earn **high grades**.
- Extraverts score higher on **happiness and life satisfaction**.
- Introverts prefer **online** to face-to-face social contact.

**On the midlife crisis in women:**
- **Lesbian** women experience **fewer emotional difficulties** at midlife than heterosexual women.
- In middle age, **Black** women are **more satisfied with themselves** than White women.

## 3.8 Reflections on Jung's Theory

### Contributions

Jung's complicated and unusual approach to personality has had a **broad influence outside psychology**, notably in **psychiatry, sociology, economics, political science, philosophy and religion**. The wider intellectual world recognised him: he received **honorary degrees from Harvard and Oxford**, and many scholars have acknowledged him as a strong influence on their work.

Within psychology, the book lists several important and lasting contributions (see, for example, Summerville, 2010):

1. **The word-association test** became a **standard projective technique**, and it inspired the development of the **Rorschach inkblot test** and of so-called **lie-detection** techniques.
2. **Complexes** and the distinction between **introverted and extraverted** personalities are widely accepted in psychology today.
3. **Personality scales measuring introversion and extraversion** are standard tools for **diagnosis and selection**, and a large amount of research continues on the introversion-extraversion dimension (taken up in **Chapter 8**, with Eysenck and the five-factor model).
4. **Influence on later theorists:**
   - Jung's **individuation**, a form of **self-actualisation**, anticipated the work of **Abraham Maslow**.
   - Jung was the **first to emphasise the role of the future** in determining behaviour, an idea taken up by **Alfred Adler**.
   - **Maslow, Erik Erikson and Raymond Cattell** accepted Jung's proposal that **middle age** is a time of crucial personality change.
5. **The midlife crisis.** Many now regard the midlife crisis as a **necessary stage of personality development**, and the idea has been supported by considerable research (3.7).

> **Trap.** "Jung was the first to emphasise the role of the future in determining behaviour" is a contribution the book states directly, and it was **adopted by Adler** (whose fictional finalism and goal-directed striving appear in Chapter 4). "Jung anticipated Maslow" refers to **individuation and self-actualisation**. "Erikson, Maslow and Cattell" are the three named as accepting **midlife** as crucial.

### Criticisms

For all these contributions, the **bulk** of Jung's theory was **not warmly received** by psychologists. The book gives three main reasons.

**1. It is hard to understand.** Freud, Adler and others wrote clearly enough for their books to be read with ease. Jung did not write for the general public, and reading him can be **frustrating** because his books contain **many inconsistencies and contradictions**. Jung once described his thoughts as erupting out of him like a **geyser**, and said that those who came after him would have to put them in order (quoted in Jaffé, 1971). One Jungian scholar judged one of Jung's major books only partly intelligible, with unclear connections between one thought and the next and many internal contradictions (Noll, 1994). The same can be said of much of his writing: it is difficult, and it **lacks internal consistency and systematisation**.

**2. The occult and the supernatural.** Most of the hostility to Jung's theory probably stems from his openness to the **occult and the supernatural**. Evidence drawn from **mythology and religion** carries little weight in an age that treats **reason and science** as the most legitimate routes to knowledge. Critics charge that Jung accepted as **scientific evidence** the mythical and mystical experiences his patients reported. (Recall the patient who "really did" live on the moon.)

**3. Weak data and untestable claims** (detailed in 3.7). The support that exists is concentrated on the **types and attitudes** (mostly via the MBTI) and on **midlife**; the collective unconscious and the archetypes remain **untestable**.

Classic background: two further objections appear often in other textbooks. The **collective unconscious** seems to require that experiences acquired by ancestors be biologically inherited, which sits uneasily with modern genetics; and the **anima and animus** are built on traditional stereotypes of what counts as masculine (assertiveness) and feminine (tenderness), which modern critics see as dated. Neither is a point the 11th edition makes, but neither contradicts it.

**The revival.** Even so, interest in Jung has **surged since the late 1980s** and has not faded. You can train as a **Jungian analyst** in **New York, Chicago, Boston, San Francisco, Los Angeles** and other North American cities, as well as at institutes in **South America, Europe and Israel**. The ***Journal of Analytical Psychology*** is the publication of the **Society of Analytical Psychology**, which dates from **1947**.

**"Which is NOT a criticism of Jung?"** These questions turn on whether you can separate Jung's real weaknesses from Freud's.

| A valid criticism of Jung | NOT a criticism of Jung (it fits Freud, or Jung did the opposite) |
|---|---|
| Writing is obscure, inconsistent, contradictory, unsystematic | Overemphasis on sexuality |
| Embrace of the occult, supernatural, mythology, alchemy, astrology | Neglect of the unconscious |
| Accepted mystical patient reports as evidence | Ignoring development after childhood |
| Unsystematic, uncontrolled, unrepeatable case data (life-history reconstruction) | A wholly deterministic, pessimistic view |
| No verbatim records; procedures never explained; small, unrepresentative sample | Ignoring the future in favour of the past |
| Visions possibly traced to his reading (Noll, 1993, 1994) | Making personality fixed by age 5 |
| Many concepts (collective unconscious, archetypes) untestable | Lack of any research support at all (types, attitudes and midlife do have support) |

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Everything is a pair of opposites.** The principle of opposites is not one item in a list; it is the organising logic. Introversion against extraversion, thinking against feeling, sensing against intuiting, conscious against unconscious, persona against shadow, anima against animus, and even Jung's assessment methods (science against mysticism). Conflict between the poles makes energy; the dominant pole rules consciousness while the other works from the personal unconscious; dreams compensate for whatever is over-developed; and health is the balanced union of the poles in the self.
2. **The future pulls as well as the past pushes.** This is Jung's second break with Freud, and it reappears in the purposeful fainting spells, the self that pulls from ahead, prospective dreams, the growth answer on Schultz's questions, and the contribution that Adler adopted.
3. **Two halves of life.** The first half is outward, conscious, extraverted and preparatory; the second is inward, balancing the unconscious, introverted and spiritual. The switch happens between 35 and 40, which is when Jung's own crisis came (38) and when two-thirds of his patients were. Individuation, the self archetype, the universal personality and the research on executives and midlife women all belong to the second half.
4. **Three layers, two kinds of content.** The ego (conscious), the personal unconscious (complexes, the non-dominant attitude and functions; like Freud's preconscious) and the collective unconscious (archetypes; inherited predispositions, not memories). Complexes are personal but draw on ancestral experience; archetypes are collective but are realised through personal experience.
5. **The theory is autobiography.** The lonely childhood gave a theory of inner growth; the dreams gave a method; the 1913 breakdown gave the midlife focus and the technique of confronting the unconscious; the easy sex life gave a minor role to sex. This is the same lesson the book drew from Freud, and it reappears with Adler and Horney.
6. **What survived and what did not.** Introversion-extraversion, the complex, the word-association test, the midlife transition and the idea of self-actualisation entered mainstream psychology and have research support. The collective unconscious and the archetypes, along with the mystical evidence for them, did not.

**How Jung relates to the other theorists in the book.** Against **Freud** (Chapter 2), Jung broadened libido, added the future, and deepened the unconscious, while keeping dreams as the royal road. With **Adler, Horney and Erikson** he forms the neo-psychoanalytic group that rejects instinct-driven determinism, but unlike them he did not shrink the unconscious. Jung's emphasis on the future was adopted by **Adler** (Chapter 4). His view of midlife as a turning point was taken up by **Erikson** (Chapter 6), **Maslow** (Chapter 9) and **Cattell** (Chapter 8). His individuation anticipates **Maslow's self-actualisation**. His introversion and extraversion become a measured trait dimension in **Eysenck** and the five-factor model (Chapter 8).

## Worked example

*Mr R. is a 43-year-old chartered accountant, a partner in a Mumbai firm, married, with two teenage children. He is referred for "low mood". For about eighteen months he has felt that his life is "a balance sheet with nothing on it", although nothing has gone wrong: the firm is doing well and his family is healthy. Colleagues describe him as precise, logical, rule-bound and unemotional; they call him "the auditor", and he is the same at family weddings. Recently he has started painting late at night and hides the canvases because the hobby embarrasses him. His wife reports that he has become irritable and last month smashed a plate during a minor argument, which she says is "not him at all". Over several months he has had a recurring dream: the walls of his office dissolve into a flooded forest, and an unknown woman is calling him from the water. On a word-association test he takes a long time over "mother" and "weak", gives the same reply ("numbers") to "home" and "future", and answers "cry" with a full sentence insisting that he never does.*

Reason it through with Jung's concepts.

**What is happening developmentally?** He is in the **midlife transition** that Jung placed between **35 and 40** and saw in most of his own patients. The preparatory tasks of the first half of life (education, career, marriage, family) are complete. By the **principle of equivalence**, the energy that went into them has not disappeared; with no outlet of equal value it has flowed into the **unconscious**, and he experiences the result as emptiness and loss of meaning. Jung would treat this as **normal, inevitable and potentially beneficial**, a signal to turn inward, not as an illness to be removed.

**What type is he?** Everything in the description points to an **extraverted thinking** type: logical, objective, rule-following, emotion repressed. By the dominance rules, the opposite function in the rational pair, **feeling**, is submerged in his **personal unconscious**, and so is the **introverted** attitude. The **compensatory** pressure of these neglected opposites is what the dreams and the painting are expressing.

**What is the persona doing?** "The auditor" at work, at home and at weddings is a mask that has become the man: **inflation of the persona**. The ego has identified with the role, and the rest of the personality has not been allowed to develop. In Jung's terms he has been deceiving himself, not just others.

**What is the broken plate?** The **shadow**. His primitive impulses (anger, here) have been fully suppressed rather than acknowledged, so they wait for a moment of **ego weakness** and break through. The same shadow is also the source of the **creativity** now showing up as painting; total suppression is what has made his life feel flat.

**What is the woman in the dream?** The most natural Jungian reading is the **anima**, the feminine side of his psyche (tenderness, emotional range) calling him towards the inner, emotional world (the water and the forest). The dream is recurring, so Jung would work with it as part of a **series**; it is **compensatory** (a flood of feeling against an over-rational waking life) and **prospective** (preparing him for the inward turn of the second half of life). Jung would use **amplification**, returning again and again to the woman and the water until a theme emerged, rather than following a chain of free associations away from them, and he would **not** look for a hidden latent sexual wish behind the manifest dream.

**What does the word-association test show?** Long delays, the same reply to different words, and a defensive full-sentence reply are all **complex indicators**. They suggest a **complex** organised around weakness, emotion and home, sitting in the personal unconscious, possibly linked to his mother. Jung would note it, but he would not spend months excavating the childhood; recall his "don't waste your time".

**What would Jung do in session?** Chairs facing each other, an active and sometimes blunt therapist, attention to the dream series and the paintings, and the steps of **individuation**: **confront the unconscious** (record dreams, keep painting, take the fantasies seriously as creative imagination); **dethrone the persona** (he can remain a meticulous accountant at work without being "the auditor" at home); **accept his dark side** (acknowledge his anger and selfishness without acting them out); and **accept his anima** (allow tenderness, especially with his teenage children), which Jung would warn is the **most difficult step** because it changes his self-image most. **Transcendence**, the unifying of these opposites, can be blocked by environmental factors, so a frustrating partnership or a strained marriage would also need attention.

**What would you not conclude?** That he should abandon his career, or swing over to become a pure introverted feeling type. Jung's goal is **balance**: no single attitude, function or archetype should dominate. If he progresses, Jung would say he is moving away from being a particular type at all, towards the **universal** personality of the individuated person. The research fits the case: Lyons (2002) found the same pattern of looking inward, questioning inherited values and becoming more playful in executives aged 37 to 55.

## MCQ traps

1. **Analytical psychology** is Jung's theory; **psychoanalysis** is Freud's; **individual psychology** is Adler's. Do not swap the labels.
2. **Three breaks with Freud:** libido broadened to general psychic energy; personality shaped by the **future** as well as the past; **more** emphasis on the unconscious (adding the collective layer), not less.
3. **University of Basel** for medicine; from **1900** in Zurich under **Eugen Bleuler**, who coined **schizophrenia**.
4. **1907** association with Freud (a **13-hour** first conversation); **1909** Clark University; **1913** break. Freud was nearly **20 years** older.
5. Neurotic episode: **1913**, age **38**, lasting **3 years**; recorded in ***The Red Book*** (about **200 pages**, red leather, published **2009**).
6. Autobiography: ***Memories, Dreams, Reflections*** (**1961**). The book that inspired the MBTI: ***Psychological Types*** (read by Katharine Briggs in **1923**).
7. **Three energy principles (Jung, 1928)**: **opposites** (conflict generates energy), **equivalence** (energy conserved and shifted), **entropy** (energy evens out towards balance). Equivalence is conservation; entropy is equalisation.
8. Jung's **ego** = centre of **consciousness**, wholly conscious; not the centre of the whole personality (that is the **self**).
9. The **non-dominant attitude and functions** sit in the **personal** unconscious, not the collective.
10. **Rational** functions = **thinking and feeling** (they judge). **Nonrational** (older texts: **irrational**) = **sensing and intuiting** (they perceive). Feeling is rational.
11. Only **one pair** (rational or nonrational) and **one function within it** can dominate; thinking and feeling cannot both rule.
12. **Eight** types in Jung (2 × 4); **sixteen** in the MBTI, which adds **judging-perceiving**.
13. **Extraverted feeling** type is, by Jung's claim, **more common in women**; **extraverted thinking** suits scientists; **extraverted intuiting** suits business and politics; **introverted sensing** expresses itself through art and music.
14. **Personal unconscious** is likened to Freud's **preconscious**; it is a "filing cabinet" with two-way traffic to the ego.
15. **Complexes** can be **conscious or unconscious**, **harmful or useful** (achievement complex), and originate partly in **ancestral** experience.
16. **Collective unconscious**: deepest, least accessible, most controversial; older name **racial unconscious**. We inherit **predispositions** (the potential to fear snakes), not the fears themselves.
17. **Archetypes** = **primordial images** (Jung, 1947). Major four: **persona, anima/animus, shadow, self**.
18. **Inflation of the persona** = the ego identifies with the mask. Playing the role deceives **others**; believing it deceives **yourself**.
19. **Anima** = feminine side of a **man**; **animus** = masculine side of a **woman**. In Fig 3.1 the **dark** half is the anima, the **light** half the animus.
20. **Shadow** = the **most powerful** archetype, with the **deepest roots**; also the source of **creativity and vitality**; full suppression gives a lifeless psyche and risks revolt.
21. **Self** = unity and harmony; centre shifts from the ego to a point **midway between conscious and unconscious**; emerges at **middle age**; pulls from **ahead**; rarely achieved.
22. **Psychic birth** = **puberty**. Consciousness begins when the child can say **"I"**.
23. Major midlife changes between **35 and 40**; the crisis is **normal, inevitable, universal and beneficial**. The shift is **extraversion to introversion**, material to spiritual.
24. Jung wrote of **two general periods** of life (Jung, 1930); Table 3.2 has **three** rows (childhood; puberty to young adulthood; middle age).
25. Individuation steps in order: **confront the unconscious, dethrone the persona, accept the shadow, accept the anima/animus, transcend**. The **most difficult step** = accepting the **anima/animus**.
26. **Uniqueness** exists only in the **first half of life**; individuation yields a **universal** personality, no longer a type.
27. **Sickness of dissociation** = the West's overemphasis on materialism, reason and science at the expense of the unconscious.
28. **Word-association test**: **100 words**, **early 1900s**, measured **reaction time** and **physiological reactions**, used to detect **complexes**; classed as **projective**. **Galton** experimented with word association first; Jung made it clinical.
29. **Symptom analysis** resembles Freud's **cathartic method**.
30. Dreams are **prospective** and **compensatory**; Jung used a **series** of dreams and **amplification**, and did **not** separate manifest from latent content.
31. **MBTI**: **Katharine Cook Briggs** and **Isabel Briggs Myers**, developed in the **1920s** (citations 1943, 1976); **2 million+** takers a year (Cunningham, 2012); **1975** Center for Applications of Psychological Type with **Mary McCaulley** (University of Florida); **1979** Association for Psychological Type.
32. **Life-history reconstruction** = Jung's case-study method.
33. **Mathew & Bhatewara (2006)**, Indian managers: thinking = **collaborative** conflict handling, feeling = **avoidance**; men higher on thinking, women on feeling. **Huifang & Shuming (2004)**, Chinese managers: **no** sex differences.
34. **Cann & Donderi (1986)**: **intuiting** types recall more archetypal dreams; **introverts** more everyday dreams; high **neuroticism**, fewer archetypal dreams.
35. **John, Pals & Westenberg (1998)**: **conflicted < traditional < individuated**.
36. **34 percent** (private college) versus **61 percent** (state university) would not repeat their choices at age **37**; about **two-thirds** changed between **37 and 43**.
37. Contributions: the word-association test inspired the **Rorschach** and **lie detection**; the future emphasis was adopted by **Adler**; individuation anticipated **Maslow**; **Maslow, Erikson and Cattell** accepted midlife as crucial. Honorary degrees: **Harvard and Oxford**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Neo-psychoanalytic approach** | Theorists who began within or from Freud's psychoanalysis (Jung, Adler, Horney, Erikson) but rejected instinct-driven determinism and offered a more hopeful view |
| **Analytical psychology** | Jung's name for his theory of personality |
| **Libido** | For Jung, a broad, general psychic or life energy, of which sexual energy is only one form |
| **Psychic energy** | The energy that fuels the psyche and makes perceiving, thinking, feeling and wishing possible |
| **Psyche** | Jung's term for the whole personality |
| **Psychic value** | The amount of energy invested in an idea or feeling; high value means strong influence on behaviour |
| **Opposition principle (principle of opposites)** | Conflict between opposing tendencies is what generates psychic energy; the sharper the conflict, the more energy |
| **Equivalence principle** | Energy is never lost but is redistributed; energy withdrawn from one interest moves elsewhere, ideally to something of equal value, otherwise into the unconscious |
| **Entropy principle** | A tendency for energy to flow from stronger to weaker structures towards balance; the ideal of equal distribution is never reached |
| **Ego** | For Jung, the conscious part of the personality, the centre of consciousness, selective in what it admits |
| **Attitudes** | The two opposite orientations of psychic energy: extraversion and introversion |
| **Extraversion** | An attitude in which psychic energy is directed outward, towards other people and the external world |
| **Introversion** | An attitude in which psychic energy is directed inward, towards one's own thoughts and feelings |
| **Psychological functions** | Four opposing ways of perceiving and dealing with the outer and inner worlds: sensing, intuiting, thinking, feeling |
| **Rational functions** | Thinking and feeling, which make judgements and evaluations of experience |
| **Nonrational (irrational) functions** | Sensing and intuiting, which take in experience without evaluating it |
| **Sensing** | Perceiving experience directly through the senses, like a photograph |
| **Intuiting** | Perceiving through hunches rather than direct sensory evidence |
| **Thinking** | Judging experience as true or false |
| **Feeling** | Judging experience as liked or disliked, pleasant or unpleasant |
| **Psychological types** | Jung's eight types formed by combining the two attitudes with the four functions |
| **Personal unconscious** | The store of material that was once conscious but has been forgotten or suppressed; likened to Freud's preconscious |
| **Complex** | A cluster of emotions, memories, perceptions and wishes in the personal unconscious organised around a theme such as power or status |
| **Collective unconscious** | The deepest level of the psyche, holding the inherited experience of the human and prehuman species (older term: racial unconscious) |
| **Archetypes** | Universal patterns or images in the collective unconscious, expressed in dreams, fantasies and myths |
| **Primordial images** | Jung's alternative name for archetypes |
| **Persona archetype** | The public mask or role a person presents to others |
| **Inflation of the persona** | The condition in which the ego identifies with the persona rather than with the person's true nature |
| **Anima archetype** | The feminine aspects of the male psyche |
| **Animus archetype** | The masculine aspects of the female psyche |
| **Shadow archetype** | The dark side of the personality, holding primitive animal instincts; also a source of vitality and creativity |
| **Self archetype** | The archetype of unity, integration and harmony of the whole personality; the ultimate goal of life |
| **Psychic birth** | Jung's term for puberty, when the psyche takes on definite form and content |
| **Midlife crisis** | The transition around ages 35 to 40 in which success leaves energy without an outlet and the personality must turn inward |
| **Individuation** | Psychological health achieved by integrating all conscious and unconscious aspects of the personality and fulfilling one's capacities |
| **Transcendence** | The innate tendency towards unity that joins all the opposing aspects of the psyche once they are individuated |
| **Universal personality** | The post-individuation personality in which no single attitude, function or archetype dominates |
| **Sickness of dissociation** | Jung's warning that Western overemphasis on materialism, reason and science cuts people off from the unconscious |
| **Word-association test** | A projective technique in which the person answers each stimulus word with the first word that comes to mind; used by Jung to detect complexes |
| **Complex indicators** | Signs in word association that a word has touched a complex: delays, physiological reactions, repeated responses, slips, stammering, multi-word replies, made-up words, no reply |
| **Symptom analysis** | A technique, akin to catharsis, that interprets the patient's free associations to his or her reported symptoms |
| **Dream analysis** | Interpreting dreams to uncover unconscious conflicts; for Jung, attending to their prospective and compensatory functions |
| **Prospective dreams** | Dreams that help prepare the dreamer for anticipated events |
| **Compensatory dreams** | Dreams that balance the psyche by making up for the overdevelopment of one structure |
| **Amplification** | Jung's method of having the patient associate repeatedly to the same dream element until a theme emerges |
| **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)** | A self-report test, developed by Katharine Cook Briggs and Isabel Briggs Myers, that measures Jung's types and the introversion-extraversion attitudes |
| **Life-history reconstruction** | Jung's case-study method: reconstructing a person's past to find the developmental patterns behind a present neurosis |
| **Conflicted / traditional / individuated prototypes** | The three levels of midlife personality found in women by John, Pals and Westenberg (1998), from least to most mature |

## Likely exam points

1. **Neo-psychoanalytic approach:** Jung and Adler (former associates), Horney (orthodox Freudian, no personal tie), Erikson (built on Freud); all reject instinct-driven determinism and are more optimistic.
2. **Three differences from psychoanalysis:** generalised libido; the future as well as the past; a deeper unconscious with a collective layer.
3. **Life into theory:** lonely childhood and inner growth; the two-sided mother and rejection of the Oedipus complex; cavern and prehistoric-bones dreams; purposeful fainting spells; the 1913 breakdown at 38 and the midlife focus.
4. **Chronology:** 1900 Bleuler in Zurich; 1907 Freud (13 hours); 1909 Clark University; 1913 break; *The Red Book* (about 200 pages) published 2009.
5. **Sex lives of Jung and Freud** as personal bias shaping theory (Schultz, 1990); spread of Jung's work via the Rockefeller, McCormick and Mellon families (Noll, 1997).
6. **Libido in two senses** and psychic value; the **three principles** (Jung, 1928) and why perfect entropy is impossible.
7. **Structure of the psyche:** ego; personal unconscious with complexes; collective unconscious with archetypes.
8. **Attitudes and functions:** one attitude dominant, the other active in the personal unconscious; rational (thinking, feeling) versus nonrational (sensing, intuiting); one pair and one function dominant.
9. **Eight types** (Table 3.1), including extraverted feeling as more common in women.
10. **Personal unconscious and complexes:** preconscious analogy, filing cabinet, conscious or unconscious, useful or harmful, ancestral roots.
11. **Collective unconscious:** inherited predispositions not memories; universal experiences; evidence from cross-cultural symbols and patients' dreams.
12. **Archetypes:** primordial images; the major four with Fig 3.1 for anima and animus; inflation of the persona; the shadow as most powerful and creative; the self midway between conscious and unconscious.
13. **Review-question contrasts:** shadow versus Freud's id; persona versus self; personal versus collective unconscious.
14. **Development:** two periods (Table 3.2 has three rows); psychic birth at puberty; extraversion and preparation in the first half.
15. **Midlife (35 to 40):** emptiness after success, rechannelled energy, the turn to introversion and the inner world; normal and beneficial.
16. **Individuation:** definition; innate but environmentally influenced; the five steps in order; the most difficult step; transcendence and what blocks it.
17. **Human nature table:** free will with partial determinism; nature and nurture; future over childhood; unique then universal; growth; optimism with the sickness-of-dissociation warning.
18. **Jung as therapist:** chairs not couch, sailboat, bluntness, fantasies taken at face value (von Franz and the moon patient).
19. **Word-association test:** 100 words, reaction time and physiological measures, complex indicators, normal versus neurotic replies (Table 3.3); projective; Galton versus Jung.
20. **Symptom analysis** as a cathartic-method analogue; **dream analysis** with prospective and compensatory functions, dream series, amplification, no manifest-latent split.
21. **MBTI:** Briggs and Myers, 1920s; the Clarence Myers origin story; 1923 reading of *Psychological Types*; over 2 million takers a year; 1975 centre, 1979 association.
22. **Life-history reconstruction** and the methodological criticisms (Noll on the visions; Jung's dismissal of experimental psychology).
23. **Type research on students and careers:** Stricker and Ross (1962), Hanewitz (1978), Apostal (1991), Murray and Johnson (2001), Barrineau (2005), Stilwell et al. (2000), Harrington and Loffredo (2001), DiRienzo et al. (2010).
24. **Cognitive research:** Carlson and Levy (1973), Carlson (1980), Gram, Dunn and Ellis (2005).
25. **Cross-cultural research,** especially Mathew and Bhatewara (2006) on Indian managers, set against the Chinese studies.
26. **Dreams and individuation:** Cann and Donderi (1986); Lyons (2002) on executives aged 37 to 55.
27. **Midlife women:** Barrett (2005), Helson (1992), Levinson (1996), Stewart and colleagues (34 versus 61 percent; two-thirds changed between 37 and 43), Carr (2004), John, Pals and Westenberg (1998), and the Highlights box.
28. **Contributions:** word association leading to the Rorschach and lie detection; complexes; introversion-extraversion scales; the future emphasis taken up by Adler; individuation anticipating Maslow; midlife accepted by Maslow, Erikson and Cattell; Harvard and Oxford degrees.
29. **Criticisms:** obscure and contradictory writing (the geyser remark; Noll, 1994); occultism and mystical evidence; weak, untestable data; the post-1980s revival and the Society of Analytical Psychology (1947).
