# Chapter 13: Albert Bandura: Modeling Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** The previous chapter left you with Skinner's picture of a person: an "empty organism" whose behaviour is shaped, response by response, by the reinforcements the environment hands out. Bandura accepts half of that picture and throws away the other half. He agrees that personality is mostly learned behaviour, that it should be studied in the laboratory, and that reinforcement matters. He rejects the idea that a person has to *do* something and *be reinforced* for it before learning can happen. Most of what we know, he argues, we picked up by **watching other people** and noticing what happened to them. And he refuses to leave the organism empty: between what we see and what we do sit **cognitive processes** (attention, memory, rehearsal, expectation, self-evaluation) that decide whether an observed act is ever performed. That combination, learning by observation plus thinking in the middle, is why Schultz files him under a separate heading, **the social-learning approach**, rather than under behaviourism proper.

Three ideas carry the chapter and carry the exam. The first is **observational learning** through **vicarious reinforcement**, demonstrated in the Bobo doll studies and governed by four processes in a fixed order (attention, retention, production, incentive and motivation). The second is **self-efficacy**, our belief that we can cope and succeed, which Bandura came to regard as the single most important factor in a person's success or failure across the whole life span; it has four sources of information, of which one is clearly the strongest. The third is **reciprocal determinism**, his answer to the free will question: the person shapes the environment as much as the environment shapes the person. Around these sit the applications that made Bandura famous outside psychology departments: modelling therapy for phobias, guided participation, covert modelling, and the long research line linking violent television, films and video games to aggression.

**How to read this chapter.** The opening module folds in Schultz's part opener, *The Social-Learning Approach*, and the chapter's short introduction on how Bandura departs from Skinner; learn those contrasts first, because examiners love "Skinner vs Bandura" items. Module 13.1 is the life, which is shorter than Freud's or Jung's but still feeds the theory directly. 13.2 and 13.3 are the core theory of modelling and must be known cold, including the three groups of factors that affect modelling and Table 13.1. 13.4 and 13.5 build the self-system (self-reinforcement and self-efficacy) and its development from infancy to old age. 13.6 is the clinical payoff (behaviour modification). 13.7 and 13.8 are short in the 11th edition, so the human-nature table here combines the one paragraph the book gives with what its chapter summary states. 13.9 is a long research module with many named studies, and 13.10 is the evaluation.

## Opening: The Social-Learning Approach and the Break with Skinner

Schultz places Bandura in a part of the book of his own, **The Social-Learning Approach**, immediately after Skinner's *Behavioral Approach* and immediately before *The Limited-Domain Approach* (Rotter, Zuckerman and Seligman). The placement tells you how to think about him: he grew out of behaviourism and he rebelled against it at the same time.

What he kept from Skinner:

- The focus on **overt behaviour**, the things a person actually does, instead of inner needs, traits, drives or defence mechanisms.
- The conviction that behaviour is **learned**, and that **reinforcement** is important to learning.
- **Laboratory research** rather than clinical case material as the route to understanding personality. Like Skinner, he did not build his theory from patients on a couch; yet, like Skinner's, his principles ended up being used widely in clinics through **behaviour-modification** techniques.

What he changed:

- He allowed **internal cognitive variables** to stand between stimulus and response. Schultz sums this up in one line: for Bandura, the organism is not empty.
- He studied **human beings in social settings**. Skinner worked mostly with individual animals (rats and pigeons) tested alone.
- He accepted that reinforcement is vital but **reinterpreted what reinforcement is and how it works**: it can be seen happening to someone else, and it can be given to oneself.

Because cognition is central to his account, Bandura's work both reflected and strengthened the **cognitive movement** in psychology, and his approach is also described as **cognitive-behavioural**. You will meet several labels for the same body of work: social-learning theory, observational-learning theory, modelling theory (Schultz's chapter title), and social-cognitive theory (a phrase the chapter itself uses once, when discussing behaviour modification).

> **Trap.** Classic background: Bandura himself renamed his approach **social cognitive theory** in his 1986 book *Social Foundations of Thought and Action*, to stress the role of thought. Older Indian keys and Hall and Lindzey usually say "social learning theory"; newer sources say "social cognitive theory". They refer to the same theorist and the same core ideas. Do not treat them as rival theories.

Bandura's own criticism of Skinner is worth stating exactly, because it is the logic of the whole chapter. Data gathered from an animal working alone in a box involve **no social interaction**, and almost nobody lives in social isolation, so such data cannot be expected to tell us much about everyday human life. A theory of personality, on this view, has to study behaviour **as it is formed and changed in a social context**.

The chapter's epigraph states the central claim in Bandura's own words: "Virtually every phenomenon that occurs by direct experience can occur vicariously as well, by observing other people and the consequences for them."

### Vicarious reinforcement

Bandura did not deny that a great deal of learning comes from reinforcement. His point was that **almost any kind of behaviour can also be learned without the learner ever being reinforced directly**. Think of a child who watches an older sister get praised for setting the table, and the next evening sets the table herself without ever having been praised for it. She has not been reinforced; her sister has. Learning of this kind is **observational learning**: acquiring new responses by watching how other people behave.

The mechanism that makes it work is **vicarious reinforcement**: we learn, or strengthen, a behaviour by watching someone else perform it and seeing what follows, instead of experiencing the reward or punishment ourselves. Learning by example, rather than always by direct reinforcement, is the **distinctive feature** of Bandura's theory, and it is the first thing to say in any answer that compares him with Skinner.

### The role of cognitive processes

The second distinctive feature is the place Bandura gives to **thinking**. Skinner had a direct line from stimulus to response and from behaviour to reinforcer. Bandura breaks that line. In his account we do **not imitate automatically** whatever we see. We decide, consciously and deliberately, whether to copy a behaviour, and to learn from example at all we must be able to **anticipate** the consequences of the acts we observe and **appreciate** what they would mean for us.

That capacity lets us steer our behaviour by **imagining outcomes we have never experienced**. A teenager who has never been caught cheating can still picture the scene in the principal's office after watching it happen to a classmate, and that imagined consequence regulates his own behaviour. So, for Bandura, cognitive processes **mediate** between stimulus and response; there is no direct connection between them of the kind Skinner proposed.

### A less extreme form of behaviourism

Put the two features together and you have what Schultz calls a **less extreme behaviourism**. Learning comes largely from **observing others**, and it is **mediated by cognition**. The research base is also different from the two traditions that preceded it: careful laboratory work with **normal people interacting socially**, rather than (in Schultz's memorable contrast) a rat in a cage or a neurotic patient on a couch.

| Issue | Skinner (Ch 12) | Bandura (Ch 13) |
|---|---|---|
| Name of approach | Behavioural (radical behaviourism) | Social-learning (also cognitive-behavioural, social-cognitive) |
| How learning happens | Direct reinforcement of one's own responses | Observation of models and vicarious reinforcement, as well as direct reinforcement |
| Inner processes | Not useful for science; the "empty organism" | Cognitive processes mediate between stimulus and response |
| Who controls behaviour | Reinforcers | Models (and the person's own cognitions) |
| Subjects studied | Individual animals, alone | Human beings in social interaction |
| Sources of reinforcement | External | External, vicarious and self-administered |
| Shared ground | Overt behaviour, learning, laboratory method, behaviour modification in the clinic | Same |

> **Trap.** Bandura does **not** reject reinforcement. A statement such as "Bandura held that reinforcement plays no role in learning" is false. His position is that reinforcement **helps** learning but is **not necessary** for it, and that reinforcement can be **vicarious** or **self-administered** as well as direct.

## 13.1 The Life of Bandura (1925 to 2021)

The book gives only his birth year in this heading, with an open-ended date, because Bandura was alive when the 11th edition was published. Classic background: he died on 26 July 2021, aged 95, at Stanford, California.

### Get drunk or go to school

Bandura grew up in the province of **Alberta, Canada**, in a town so small that its high school had only **two teachers and 20 students**. Classic background: the town was **Mundare**, a farming village in northern Alberta, and he was the youngest of six children and the only son. His parents were **immigrants from Poland**, and they put a high value on education. His mother put the choice to him starkly when he was young: he could stay, labour in the fields and end up drinking in the local beer parlour, or he could get himself an education (the remark is quoted in Foster, 2007, and gives the subsection its title). He took the second road.

Classic background: Bandura later recalled that a school with so few teachers and almost no resources left students to take charge of their own learning, a habit of self-direction he valued all his life. Whether or not one reads too much into it, the link to his later ideas is easy to see: a theory in which people set their own standards, reward themselves for meeting them and believe they can master what they set out to do is a theory of **self-regulation**.

After finishing high school he spent a summer on a construction crew in the **Yukon Territory**, filling holes on the **Alaska Highway**. His workmates were an odd lot, many of them men who had run away from debts, alimony payments or probation officers. Bandura later described the experience (Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award citation, 1981) as giving him an early, sharp appreciation of the psychopathology of ordinary life, which seemed to flourish out on the bare northern tundra. For a clever, curious young man it was a crash course in how varied, and how socially shaped, human behaviour can be.

### Finding psychology

At the **University of British Columbia** in Vancouver he came to psychology almost by accident. He commuted every day in a car pool with engineering and pre-medical students whose classes all began early in the morning. A psychology course happened to be offered in the same early slot, so he signed up because the timing was convenient, not because he was interested. He was soon captivated, and he went on to a **PhD at the University of Iowa in 1952**.

Notice the irony an examiner might use: the man who built a theory around how chance encounters and other people's examples shape our paths found his own career through a car pool timetable.

Classic background: Iowa was then a stronghold of **Hull-Spence learning theory** (Kenneth Spence chaired the department), so Bandura was trained in rigorous experimental learning research, the tradition he would later extend to social behaviour. In the same period he was influenced by **Neal Miller and John Dollard's *Social Learning and Imitation* (1941)**, the first major attempt to explain imitation in learning-theory terms. Miller and Dollard treated imitation as a response that the learner must actually make and be reinforced for. Bandura's departure, that people can learn a whole new pattern of behaviour simply by watching, without making the response or being reinforced for it, is easiest to understand as an argument with that earlier account.

After a year at the **Wichita Guidance Center** in Kansas, he joined the faculty of **Stanford University**, where he began the new line of research. Classic background: at Stanford he was drawn to **Robert Sears's** work on how family relationships and identification with parents shape children's social behaviour, and his first doctoral student, **Richard Walters**, became his collaborator on studies of aggressive adolescents and their families (*Adolescent Aggression*, 1959) and on *Social Learning and Personality Development* (1963). You will meet Bandura and Walters (1963) in the next module.

Taking on the dominant position in the field was a risk for a young unknown. Writing in 2011, Bandura said that when he started out, behaviourism "had a stranglehold" on psychology, and that he launched his research on observational learning in that unwelcoming climate (Bandura, 2011). The gamble paid off quickly:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1952 | PhD, University of Iowa |
| 1973 | President of the American Psychological Association, only 21 years after his PhD |
| 1980 | APA Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award |
| 2006 | American Psychological Foundation Gold Medal Award for Life Achievement |

Schultz adds a story about his absent-mindedness that he enjoyed telling against himself. Asked whether he walked or drove to his office, he answered that he did both, sometimes on the same day: he would drive in, become absorbed in an idea, and walk home, forgetting the car in the university car park.

### From the life to the theory

The links are less dramatic than Adler's rickets or Erikson's identity crisis, but they are real and examinable:

- A **tiny rural school with two teachers** and parents who pressed education on him: a background in which a child must direct his own learning, which fits a theory built on **self-regulation, self-reinforcement and self-efficacy**.
- The **Alaska Highway crew**: early exposure to how people's behaviour reflects their social circumstances and the company they keep, the raw material of a **social** learning theory.
- **Iowa's experimental learning tradition** (classic background): the rigorous, laboratory, learning-theory method he never abandoned, which is why Schultz praises the theory's empirical base.
- **Miller and Dollard, and Sears** (classic background): an existing social-learning tradition about imitation and identification that he extended, by showing that **observation alone** can produce learning.
- A career started **against behaviourism's stranglehold**: the reason his central claims are framed as corrections of Skinner.

## 13.2 Modelling: The Basis of Observational Learning

Bandura's basic claim is simple to state: we can learn **by observation or example**, not only by being reinforced for our own responses. He never denied that direct reinforcement shapes behaviour. What he denied is that it is the **only** way behaviour can be acquired or changed.

His argument against relying on direct reinforcement alone is practical. Operant conditioning works by trial and error: the learner keeps emitting responses until one of them happens to be reinforced. For many skills that would be absurdly slow, and for some it would be lethal. Imagine learning to **swim** or to **drive** purely by trial and error, waiting for the correct sequence of actions to turn up by chance and be rewarded. You would drown or crash long before you got there. In reality, people learn these skills largely from **examples**, and most of the enormous range of behaviour we show in daily life is acquired the same way, sometimes on purpose (a trainee copying a senior nurse's way of calming a frightened patient) and sometimes without intending to (a child picking up a parent's turn of phrase) (Gaskins & Paradise, 2010; Oates, 2012).

**Modelling** is the name for this process: watching what a **model** does and then repeating it ourselves. Through modelling we can acquire responses we have **never performed before**, and we can also **strengthen or weaken** responses we already have.

> **Trap.** Schultz's margin glossary defines **modelling** narrowly, as a **behaviour-modification technique** in which a person watches models and **participates with them** in performing the desired behaviour. In the body of the chapter the word is used in the wider everyday sense of learning by copying a model. If a question quotes the glossary definition ("a behaviour-modification technique that involves observing others and participating with them"), the answer is modelling, not observational learning or disinhibition.

### Bobo the inflatable doll

Bandura's best-known demonstration of modelling uses the **Bobo doll**, an inflatable plastic figure roughly **4 feet tall** that bounces back up when knocked down (**Bandura, Ross, & Ross, 1963**).

**Who and what.** The participants were **preschool children**. Each child watched an **adult model** attack Bobo, hitting and kicking it and shouting aggressive phrases as it did so (one was "Sock him in the nose!").

**The test.** Afterwards the children were left **alone with the doll**, and their behaviour was observed.

**The comparison.** Their behaviour was compared with a **control group** of children who had **not** seen the model attack Bobo.

**The finding.** Children who had watched the aggressive model were **twice as aggressive** as the controls. Left alone with the doll, they patterned what they did on the example they had just seen.

**The medium.** The intensity of the aggression in the experimental children was **the same whether the model had been seen live, on television, or as a cartoon character**. In all three media, watching the model drew out aggression that children who had not seen a model did not show to the same degree.

**What it shows.** New behaviour can be **acquired without the child ever having performed it or been reinforced for it**. That is the core of observational learning, and the study became the classic piece of evidence against a purely Skinnerian account of how children learn aggression.

> **Trap.** The book makes two statements about the medium that look contradictory. In this section it says the **intensity** of aggression was the **same** for live, televised and cartoon models. In the later section on characteristics of the model it says the **extent** of imitation **fell as the model became less similar** to the child (live child model, then filmed child, then filmed cartoon), though even the cartoon produced significantly more imitation than no model. If a question asks what the Bobo doll study showed about filmed and cartoon models, the safe answer is that **all three produced substantially more aggression than no model**. If it asks about similarity, the answer is that **more similar models are imitated more**.

> **Trap.** Classic background, because Indian papers ask about "the Bobo doll experiment" as if it were one study. There were several. **Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961)** used a **live** adult model with nursery-school children at Stanford and compared an aggressive model, a non-aggressive model and no model. **Bandura, Ross and Ross (1963)**, the study Schultz cites, compared **live, filmed and cartoon** models. **Bandura (1965)**, described later in this module, varied the **consequences** to the model (rewarded, punished, no consequence). If a question gives the year, match it: 1961 = live model, 1963 = medium of presentation, 1965 = vicarious reinforcement and punishment.

### Other modelling studies

**Parents as models (Bandura & Walters, 1963).** In some of his early work Bandura compared the parents of two groups of children: **highly aggressive** children and markedly **inhibited** children. If children learn by modelling, their behaviour should mirror their parents'. It did. The parents of the inhibited children were themselves inhibited, and the parents of the aggressive children were themselves aggressive. The children had patterned themselves on the examples available at home.

Notice what this design can and cannot show. It is correlational: aggressive parents might also pass on temperament genetically, or might reinforce aggression directly. Bandura read it as modelling, consistent with his laboratory results, but on its own it is the weakest of the modelling studies.

**Verbal modelling.** A model does not have to be seen. **Verbal modelling**, describing a behaviour in words, can also induce it, provided the activity is **explained fully and adequately**. Most instruction is verbal modelling: the manual that tells you how to change a tyre, or a driving instructor's explanation of how to reverse into a parking space. In practice verbal instructions are usually **combined with demonstration**: the driving instructor tells you what to do and also does it, serving as a behavioural model.

### Disinhibition

Most of us hold back from many behaviours we are capable of. Research shows that such **suppressed or inhibited behaviours are performed more readily when a model performs them first** (Bandura, 1973, 1986). Bandura called this **disinhibition**: the weakening of an inhibition or restraint as a result of watching a model.

The everyday example is a crowd. Individuals who would never smash a window or scream abuse when alone may join a riot once those around them start doing it. Seeing others act aggressively lowers their own restraint.

**Disinhibition of sexual responses (Walters, Bowen, & Parke, 1963).** This experiment showed that modelling can change even what we look at.

- **Participants:** male undergraduate students.
- **Procedure:** they watched a film containing erotic pictures of nude men and women. They were told that a **moving spot of light** on the film showed the eye movements of an earlier participant, indicating which parts of the pictures that person had looked at. This supposed previous viewer was the **model**.
- **Manipulation:** for half the participants the spot of light dwelt on the **breasts and genitals**; for the other half it stayed in the **background**, as though the model had avoided looking at the bodies.
- **Test:** the students were then shown still pictures from the film while their own **eye movements were recorded**.
- **Result:** those whose model had looked directly at the erotic regions (the uninhibited model) did the same. Those whose model had avoided the nudes spent significantly more time looking at the **backgrounds**.
- **Conclusion:** modelling influenced the participants' **perceptual** responses. It determined not only what they did but **what they looked at and perceived**.

#### Trolling

Posting comments online gives the same **anonymity** as being lost in a large crowd, and the comments of others act as models. The result is an online form of disinhibition known as **trolling**: posting inflammatory, derogatory or hateful messages about a person or a group. The consequences can be grave; some adolescents have been driven to suicide by cruel, widely shared comments naming them (Zhuo, 2010).

- **Who trolls (Thacker & Griffiths, 2012).** Among frequent video gamers, those most involved in trolling were **younger and male**. Their stated motives varied: some trolled as **deliberate revenge** on someone, others out of **boredom**, and others simply for **amusement** with no further aim.
- **Age (Vaidya, Latzman, Markon, & Watson, 2010).** In young Americans aged **18 to 25**, disinhibition was **strong at 18 to 19** but **greatly reduced at 22 to 25**. Schultz presents this as good news: the disinhibition effect seems to weaken with age.

> **Trap.** **Disinhibition** is not the same as acquiring a new behaviour. The rioter already knew how to throw a stone; the model removed the restraint. Observational learning proper is the **acquisition** of a response not previously in the repertoire. Classic background: Bandura's earlier writing distinguished three effects of models: an **observational-learning (modelling) effect** (new responses acquired), an **inhibitory or disinhibitory effect** (existing responses restrained or released, depending on the consequences seen) and a **response-facilitation effect** (an already acceptable behaviour cued, as when one person starts clapping and others follow).

### The effects of society's models

From all this research Bandura concluded that **much of our behaviour, good and bad, normal and abnormal, is learned by imitating others**. From infancy we respond to the models our society provides. Parents come first: from them we learn our language and absorb the customs and accepted behaviours of our culture.

People who **deviate** from cultural norms learned their behaviour in **exactly the same way** as everyone else. The only difference is that they followed models the rest of society considers undesirable. This is an important theoretical move: deviance needs no special pathology; it needs only a different set of models.

#### Bad models

Bandura was an outspoken critic of societies that offer children the wrong models, especially the **violence that is routine on television, in films and in video games**. If we become what we watch, then the step from watching a violent cartoon character to committing violence oneself is short.

Modelling also transmits **non-rational fears**. A child who sees that a parent is frightened during thunderstorms, or tense with strangers, readily takes on the same anxieties and may carry them into adulthood with little idea where they came from. A client who says "I have always been scared of thunder, I have no idea why" may simply have watched her mother hide under the stairs during storms. The same route carries **positive** qualities too: strength, courage and optimism are also learned from parents and other models.

Schultz condenses the contrast with Skinner into one line worth memorising: in Skinner's system, **reinforcers** control behaviour; for Bandura, **models** do.

### Characteristics of the modelling situation

Not every model is copied, and not every observer copies. Bandura and his associates (Bandura, 1977, 1986) identified **three groups of factors** that determine how much modelling occurs:

1. the **characteristics of the models**,
2. the **characteristics of the observers**,
3. the **reward consequences** associated with the behaviour.

#### Characteristics of the models

**Models who are like us.** We are more influenced by people who seem **similar** to us than by people who differ from us in obvious and important ways. In the laboratory, children imitated a child model in the same room, a child shown on film and a filmed cartoon character, but **the amount of imitation fell as the similarity between model and child fell**. A live model was copied more than an animated one, yet even the cartoon produced significantly more imitation than the no-model control group.

**Age and sex.** We are more likely to imitate a model of **our own sex** than of the other sex, and a model of **our own age**. **Peers who seem to have solved the problems we are now facing** are especially powerful models: a first-year student copies the study habits of a second-year student who has just passed the same exams, not those of a professor.

**Status.** **Status and prestige** increase imitation. Pedestrians are much more likely to cross against a red light if they see a **well-dressed** person do it than a poorly dressed one. Advertisers exploit this constantly by using **athletes and celebrities** to endorse products, in the expectation that consumers will copy them and buy.

**Type of behaviour.** What the model does matters. **Highly complex behaviours** are imitated less quickly and readily than **simple** ones. **Hostile and aggressive behaviours** are imitated strongly, especially by children.

- **Infants and tools (Esseilly, Rat-Fischer, O'Regan, & Fagard, 2013).** Infants as young as **16 months** learned to copy a model using a tool (a **rake** to pull in an out-of-reach toy), but **only if they had first been shown what the tool was for**. Imitation of even a simple act depends on grasping its purpose.
- **Relationships (Kuhn & Kinsky, 2013).** American college students who had observed a **positive relationship between a caregiver and a child** successfully carried that pattern into their own current romantic relationships.

**Size and weight.** Even a model's body size can influence behaviour. In a study of **9th- and 10th-grade students in Canada**, those attending schools where the older students tended to be **overweight or obese gained more weight** than students at schools where older students were not overweight (Leatherdale & Papadakis, 2011).

#### Characteristics of the observers

**Age of observers.** In **infancy**, modelling is limited to **immediate imitation**. Infants have not yet developed the cognitive capacities needed to reproduce a behaviour some time after seeing it, namely the **imaginal and verbal representational systems** described in the next module. To be learned, the behaviour also has to be **repeated several times** after the infant's first attempt to copy it, and it must lie **within the infant's range of sensorimotor development**. By about **age 2**, children have enough attentional, retention and production capacity to imitate **some time after** observing, not only on the spot.

What we find reinforcing, and therefore choose to imitate, also **changes with age**:

1. **Younger children** are reinforced mainly by **physical stimuli**: food, affection, punishment.
2. **Older children** come to associate pleasant physical reinforcers with **signs of approval** from significant models, and unpleasant ones with **signs of disapproval**.
3. Eventually these rewards and punishments become **self-administered** (the bridge to self-reinforcement in 13.4).

**Attributes of the observers.** People **low in self-confidence and self-esteem** are much more likely to imitate a model than people high in them. And a person who has been **reinforced for imitating** in the past, such as a child praised for acting like an older brother or sister, becomes **more susceptible** to models than a child who has not been reinforced for imitating.

> **Trap.** It is the **less** confident, **lower** self-esteem observer who imitates more. Options that say "high self-esteem observers are more influenced by models" are reversed.

#### The reward consequences of the behaviour

The consequences attached to a behaviour can shape modelling so strongly that they **override** the characteristics of both model and observer. A prestigious model may get us to try something, but if the rewards it brings are not meaningful to us, we will drop the behaviour and pay less attention to that model in future.

**Seeing the model rewarded or punished (Bandura, 1965).** In another Bobo doll study, one group of children watched the model who hit Bobo receive **praise and a soda and candy**. Another group saw the model **scolded and physically punished** for the same aggression. The children who had watched the **punishment** were **significantly less aggressive** towards Bobo than those who had seen the model **rewarded**. This is **vicarious reinforcement** (and vicarious punishment) in its cleanest experimental form.

> **Trap.** Classic background that older keys test: in the 1965 study, when the children were afterwards **offered attractive incentives** to reproduce what the model had done, the differences between groups largely disappeared; the children who had seen the model punished could reproduce the aggression perfectly well. Vicarious consequences therefore affected **performance**, not **acquisition**. This is the **learning versus performance distinction**, and it is the experimental basis for the claim in 13.3 that reinforcement is not necessary for learning.

## 13.3 The Processes of Observational Learning

Seeing a model is not enough to guarantee learning. Bandura broke observational learning down into **four related processes**, and the order matters because each depends on the one before:

1. **Attentional processes**
2. **Retention processes**
3. **Production processes**
4. **Incentive and motivational processes**

A useful way to remember them is to follow a single learner through all four. Schultz uses learning to drive, and so will this module.

### Attentional processes

Nothing is learned from a model the observer does not attend to. **Mere exposure** is not enough: the observer may miss the relevant cues, or may perceive the situation inaccurately. The model must be perceived **accurately enough** for the observer to pick up the information needed to reproduce the behaviour. A learner driver who spends the demonstration drive looking at his phone has been exposed to a model but has not attended to one.

Several variables decide how closely we attend:

- **Characteristics of the model**, the same ones listed in 13.2: age, status, sex and similarity to the observer. In addition, **celebrities, experts, and models who seem confident and attractive** draw more attention and more imitation than models without those qualities. Some of the most effective models today appear on **television, YouTube and other online sites**, and viewers often watch them closely even when no reinforcement is involved.
- **The observer's cognitive and perceptual skills.** The more developed our cognitive abilities, and the more we already know about the behaviour being modelled, the more carefully we attend and the more we perceive. An experienced cook watching a chef notices details a beginner never sees.
- **The value and relevance of the behaviour.** We pay more attention when we expect to **do the thing ourselves** than when it has no personal relevance. A person who has just bought a car watches parking manoeuvres in a way a non-driver does not.
- **The consequences shown.** We attend more closely to behaviour that leads to **positive or negative** consequences than to behaviour with **neutral** outcomes.

### Retention processes

To reproduce a behaviour later, we must **remember** its important features. To retain what we attended to, we must **encode** it and **represent it symbolically**. Bandura described **two representational systems**:

- The **imaginal system.** While watching, we form **vivid images** that are easy to call up later, the same capacity that lets you picture the person you went out with last week or a place you visited last summer. In observational learning we form a **mental picture of the model's behaviour** and use it later as the template for imitation.
- The **verbal system.** We **code the behaviour in words**, for example by describing to ourselves what the model is doing ("mirror, signal, then check the blind spot"). Such verbal codes can be **rehearsed silently**, without performing the behaviour openly. We can talk ourselves through the steps of a complex skill and mentally run through the sequence we will perform later; when the time comes, the verbal code supplies **hints, reminders and cues**.

Together, **images and verbal symbols** are the means by which we store what we have observed and rehearse it for later performance. This is also why infants are limited to immediate imitation (13.2): without these systems, the behaviour cannot be held in memory until a later occasion.

### Production processes

Attending and remembering still do not guarantee that we can **do** what we saw. The **production processes**, which Schultz says can be called more simply **practice**, translate the imaginal and verbal representations into **overt behaviour**. Difficulty is most likely with **highly skilled actions** made up of many component behaviours.

Return to the learner driver. From watching a model he has the basic movements stored, and he may have gone over them in his head many times. Yet his first attempts at turning those symbols into driving are clumsy: he brakes too early or too late, or **over-corrects the steering**. Observation alone did not give him smooth, skilled performance. What produces it is **practice of the actual physical movements plus feedback on their accuracy**.

### Incentive and motivational processes

However well we attend, remember and can perform a behaviour, **we will not perform it without incentive or motivation**. When incentives are present, observation is **turned into action more quickly**. Incentives also act backwards on the earlier stages: without a reason to watch, we pay less attention, and what we do not attend to we cannot retain.

#### Anticipation of reinforcement

Our incentive to learn depends on what we **expect** to happen if we do. Seeing that a model's behaviour **earns a reward or avoids a punishment** is a strong incentive to attend to it, remember it and perform it correctly. The reinforcement is experienced **vicariously** while we watch the model; afterwards we **expect** that doing the same thing will bring us the same consequences.

#### Reinforcement is not always necessary

Bandura was careful to add that although reinforcement **can facilitate** learning, it is **not always required** for learning to occur. Many things besides reward determine what we attend to, retain and rehearse: **loud sounds, bright lights and exciting videos** grab attention without our ever having been reinforced for noticing them. His research showed that children who watch a model **on television or in a video game** imitate the model **whether or not they have been promised a reward**.

So reinforcement **helps** modelling but is **not essential** to it. And when reinforcement does occur, it can come from **three sources**: it can be **given by another person**, **experienced vicariously**, or **administered by oneself**.

**Table 13.1** The four processes of observational learning

| Process (in order) | What it involves | What it needs | Driving example (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attentional** | Noticing the model and perceiving the behaviour accurately enough to copy it | Developed cognitive and perceptual skills; a model worth watching (similar, high-status, expert, attractive); personal relevance; visible consequences | Actually paying attention in the driver-education class instead of dozing |
| **Retention** | Storing the observed behaviour so it can be reproduced later | Encoding in the imaginal system (mental pictures) and the verbal system (self-descriptions), plus silent rehearsal | Making notes on the class or on a video of someone driving |
| **Production** | Turning the stored images and verbal codes into one's own overt behaviour | Physical practice of the movements and feedback on their accuracy | Going out with an instructor to practise changing gears and steering round cones in an empty car park |
| **Incentive and motivational** | Expecting that performing the behaviour will bring the consequences the model received | Anticipated reinforcement (direct, vicarious or self-administered) | Expecting that once you have mastered driving you will pass the test and get a licence |

> **Trap.** The order is fixed: **attention, retention, production, motivation**. Two common errors: putting motivation first ("you must be motivated before you attend"; Bandura does say incentives affect attention, but motivation is still the fourth process), and confusing retention with production. Retention is **memory** (images and words); production is **practice** (turning memory into movement). Older textbooks and Indian keys often call the third process **motor reproduction** and the fourth simply **motivational**; Schultz's 11th edition says **production processes** and **incentive and motivational processes**. Same processes, different labels.

> **Trap.** "Without reinforcement there is no observational learning" is **false** for Bandura. Reinforcement facilitates learning and governs **performance**, but learning can take place without it.

## 13.4 Self-Reinforcement and Self-Efficacy

Bandura's "self" is not what Freud, Jung or Rogers meant by the word. It is **not a psychic agent** that sits inside the person and causes behaviour. It is a **set of cognitive processes and structures concerned with thought and perception**. Two aspects of this self matter most: **self-reinforcement** and **self-efficacy**.

> **Trap.** For Bandura the self is **not** an agent, an inner homunculus or a structure like the ego that "decides" behaviour. It is a set of **cognitive processes and structures** for thinking and perceiving. Contrast Rogers (Ch 10), for whom the self is the organised core of experience that the person strives to actualise.

### Self-reinforcement

**Self-reinforcement** means rewarding or punishing ourselves according to whether our behaviour meets, exceeds or falls short of **our own standards**. For older children and adults it is **as important as reinforcement given by other people**. We set personal standards of conduct and achievement, reward ourselves for reaching or surpassing them, and punish ourselves when we fail.

Self-administered rewards can be **tangible** (treating yourself to new running shoes, or even a car) or **emotional** (pride, the satisfaction of having done a job well). Self-administered punishment typically takes the form of **shame, guilt or depression** about not having behaved as we wanted to.

> **Trap.** Self-reinforcement **looks like** what other theorists call the **conscience** or **superego**, but **Bandura denied that it is the same thing**. An option saying "Bandura's self-reinforcement is his term for Freud's superego" is wrong. The superego is an unconscious structure built from internalised parental prohibitions and fuelled by instinctual energy; self-reinforcement is a conscious, learned process of evaluating one's behaviour against standards that can be revised.

A continuing cycle of self-reinforcement regulates much of what we do. It depends on **internal standards of performance**: subjective criteria, or reference points, against which we judge our own behaviour. These standards are not fixed.

- Our **past performance** can become the reference point for judging present performance, and an incentive to do better next time.
- When we reach a given level of achievement, it may **no longer challenge, motivate or satisfy** us, so we **raise the standard** and demand more of ourselves. A runner who has broken 50 minutes for 10 km starts aiming for 45.
- Repeated **failure** may lead us to **lower the standard** to something more realistic.

#### Unrealistic performance standards

Trouble comes when the standards are unrealistic, for example when they were learned from **unusually talented and successful models**. Such people keep trying to meet expectations that are too high, despite failing again and again. Emotionally they punish themselves with feelings of **worthlessness and depression**, and those self-produced feelings can lead to **self-destructive behaviour** such as **alcohol and drug abuse** or a **retreat into fantasy**.

Picture a medical student whose father and grandfather both topped their medical college. Measured against those models, coming twentieth in her class feels like failure. If she keeps applying the family standard, every result confirms her sense of inadequacy, and she may start drinking to escape it. A Bandurian therapist would not dig for unconscious conflict; he would look at the **standard** and at the **models it came from**.

Our **first** internal standards, realistic or not, are learned from **models**, usually **parents and teachers**. Increasingly, however, standards are being learned from **online sources** such as celebrity blogs and social media. Once we adopt a style of behaviour, we begin a lifelong process of **comparing our behaviour with that of our models**.

### Self-efficacy, or "believing you can"

How well we meet our own standards determines our **self-efficacy**. In Bandura's system self-efficacy means our **feeling of adequacy, efficiency and competence in coping with life**. Meeting and maintaining our standards **raises** self-efficacy; failing to meet and maintain them **lowers** it (Bandura, 2012, 2013).

Bandura also described self-efficacy as our **perception of the control we have over our lives**. In his account (Bandura, 1995), people try to exert control over the events that affect them; where they can exercise influence, they are better able to bring about the futures they want and prevent the ones they do not, and this striving for control runs through nearly everything people do.

Another psychologist, Maddux, put it more simply, calling self-efficacy "the power of believing you can" and arguing that believing you can achieve what you want is one of the most important ingredients of success (Maddux, 2002). The belief that you have the ability to succeed is itself a powerful asset in striving to achieve.

#### Low and high self-efficacy

| People **low** in self-efficacy | People **high** in self-efficacy |
|---|---|
| Feel **helpless**, unable to control life events | Believe they can deal effectively with events and situations |
| Believe any effort they make is **futile** | Expect to overcome obstacles, so they **persevere** and often perform at a high level |
| **Give up quickly** if the first attempt fails | Have greater confidence in their abilities and show **little self-doubt** |
| At the extreme, **do not even try**, convinced nothing they do will make a difference | See difficulties as **challenges rather than threats**, and actively **seek novel situations** |
| Low self-efficacy can **destroy motivation, lower aspirations, interfere with cognitive abilities and harm physical health** | High self-efficacy **reduces fear of failure, raises aspirations, and improves problem solving and analytical thinking** |

Classic background: Bandura's original 1977 paper on self-efficacy separated an **efficacy expectation** (the belief that I can carry out the behaviour) from an **outcome expectation** (the belief that the behaviour, if carried out, will lead to a given result). A smoker may fully believe that quitting would protect his lungs (high outcome expectation) and still believe he cannot quit (low efficacy expectation). Self-efficacy is the first of these.

#### Sources of information about self-efficacy

We judge our own efficacy from **four sources of information**:

1. **Performance attainment**
2. **Vicarious experiences**
3. **Verbal persuasion**
4. **Physiological and emotional arousal**

**1. Performance attainment: the most influential source.** Our own previous **successes** are the most direct evidence of how competent we are. Past achievements demonstrate our capabilities and strengthen self-efficacy; past **failures**, especially **repeated failures in childhood**, weaken it. An important part of performance attainment is **feedback** on how we did, on a work assignment or a classroom quiz, for example.

- **Feedback (Elliot, Faler, McGregor, Campbell, Sedikides, & Harackiewicz, 2000).** College students working on complicated puzzles who were given **positive feedback** reported **higher perceived competence** at the task than those given negative feedback.
- **Tai Chi (Li, McAuley, Harmer, Duncan, & Chaumeton, 2001).** **Older adults** who completed a **six-month** Tai Chi training programme reported **significantly greater self-efficacy** than those who did not take the training.
- **Self-defence (Weitlauf, Cervone, Smith, & Wright, 2001).** **Female college students** who completed a **16-hour physical self-defence course** showed significantly higher self-efficacy across several domains: **physical competence, general coping skills and interpersonal assertiveness**. A control group without the course showed **no change**. Mastery in one domain spread to others.

The principle is cumulative: every achievement adds to our belief in what we can still achieve, and to our sense of being competent and in charge.

**2. Vicarious experiences.** Watching **other people succeed** strengthens our self-efficacy, particularly when those people are **similar to us in ability**. The silent reasoning is "if they managed it, so can I". Seeing others **fail** can lower it in the same way: "if they could not, neither can I". Effective models therefore matter a great deal to our sense of competence, and they also show us **strategies** for handling difficult situations. A diabetic teenager who meets another teenager managing her insulin confidently gains both the belief and the method.

**3. Verbal persuasion.** Simply **telling people they have the ability** to achieve what they want can raise self-efficacy. This **may be the most common** of the four sources: parents, teachers, spouses, coaches, friends and therapists all do it whenever they say, in effect, "you can do this". To work, though, persuasion must be **realistic**. Schultz's example: urging someone who is 5 feet tall to aim at professional basketball is poor advice when another sport, such as a martial art, would suit far better. Unrealistic encouragement sets up the failures that then lower self-efficacy.

**4. Physiological and emotional arousal.** How **fearful or calm** we feel in a stressful situation is information we use to judge whether we can cope. We are more likely to believe we will master a problem if we are **not agitated, tense or bothered by headaches**. The relationship is **inverse**: the calmer and more composed we feel, the **higher** our self-efficacy; the more **fear, anxiety or tension** we experience, the **lower** it is. A student whose heart pounds and hands shake outside the viva room reads those sensations as evidence that she is not up to it.

> **Trap.** Two "most" questions. **Performance attainment** is the **most influential** source (the book says so explicitly). **Verbal persuasion** is **possibly the most common** source (the book says "may be"). Do not swap them. And arousal works **inversely**: high arousal lowers self-efficacy.

> **Trap.** Names differ across editions and authors. Classic background: Bandura's 1977 paper called the sources **performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion and emotional arousal**; his 1997 book calls them **enactive mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological and affective states**. Schultz's 11th edition uses **performance attainment, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional arousal**. All three lists are the same four sources in the same order, and the first is always the strongest.

#### Ways of increasing self-efficacy

Bandura drew four practical conditions from these sources, one for each:

| Source | How to strengthen it |
|---|---|
| Performance attainment | Give people **success experiences** by setting **reachable goals** |
| Vicarious experiences | Expose people to **appropriate models who perform successfully** |
| Verbal persuasion | Use persuasion to encourage people to **believe they have the ability** to succeed |
| Physiological arousal | Improve physical condition through **proper diet, stress reduction and exercise**, which build strength, stamina and the ability to cope |

> **Trap.** The book's wording for the fourth condition is "strengthening physiological arousal", which seems to clash with the rule that high arousal lowers self-efficacy. The point is improving the body's **physical condition and stress tolerance** (diet, stress reduction, exercise), so that the person feels strong and calm rather than tense. Do not conclude that Bandura recommends making people more aroused.

Bandura applied these conditions in many settings. He helped people learn to **play musical instruments**, relate better to members of the **opposite sex**, master **computer skills**, give up **cigarette smoking**, and overcome **phobias and physical pain**.

## 13.5 Developmental Stages of Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy is not handed to us at birth; it **develops gradually**, and at each stage of life different people and experiences supply the four sources of information from 13.4. Bandura's stages are **childhood, adolescence, adulthood (young adulthood and the middle years) and old age**.

### Childhood

Self-efficacy begins in **infancy**, as babies try to exert more and more influence over their **physical and social environment**. They learn what their own abilities can do: their **physical prowess, social skills and language competence**. These abilities are in almost constant use acting on the world, and mostly they act on it **through their effects on the parents**: a cry brings a parent, a smile brings a smile back, a word gets the cup refilled. Ideally, parents **respond** to the growing child's activities and attempts to communicate, and provide **stimulating surroundings** with the **freedom to grow and explore**. An infant whose signals reliably produce results is collecting her first performance attainments.

These early efficacy-building experiences are **centred on the parents**, and the parental behaviour that builds high self-efficacy appears to **differ for boys and girls** (**Schneewind, 1995**):

- **High self-efficacy men** tended, as boys, to have had **warm relationships with their fathers**, while their **mothers were the more demanding** parent, expecting higher performance and achievement.
- **High self-efficacy women** had experienced, as girls, **pressure from their fathers for high achievement**.

> **Trap.** In Schneewind (1995) the **father** figures in both patterns, but differently: **warmth** from the father for sons (with the mother more demanding), **achievement pressure** from the father for daughters. Questions swap these.

#### Reduction of parental influence

As the child's world widens, parents become **less significant** and other models enter: **siblings, peers and other adults**.

- **Birth order.** Like **Adler** (Ch 4), Bandura considered **birth order** important. He argued that **first-born and only children** have **different bases for judging their own abilities** than **later-born** children have. A first-born compares himself with adults; a third-born compares herself with older siblings who are always ahead.
- **Sibling sex.** **Same-sex siblings** are likely to be **more competitive** than opposite-sex siblings, which also bears on self-efficacy.
- **Playmates.** Among peers, the children who are **most experienced and successful** at tasks and games serve as **high-efficacy models** for the others. Peers provide **comparative reference points** for appraising one's own level of achievement.
- **Teachers.** Teachers shape self-efficacy through their effect on **cognitive abilities and problem-solving skills**, which are vital for effective adult functioning. Children often rate their own competence according to their **teachers' evaluations** of them.

Bandura was critical of two school practices. **Ability grouping** (streaming children into sections by ability) undermines the self-efficacy and self-confidence of students placed in the **lower groups**. And competitive practices such as **grading on a curve** condemn poor achievers to average or low grades however much they improve.

### Adolescence

Adolescence is **transitional**: it brings new demands and pressures, from a growing awareness of **sexuality** to the choice of **college and career**. Adolescents have to establish **new competencies** and new appraisals of their abilities. Bandura noted that how well they manage this usually **depends on the self-efficacy built up in childhood**: a teenager who arrives with a store of mastery experiences meets these challenges very differently from one who arrives with a history of failure.

### Adulthood

Bandura divided adulthood into **two periods**.

**Young adulthood** brings new adjustments: **marriage, parenthood and establishing a career**. High self-efficacy is needed for these to go well; people low in self-efficacy are unlikely to cope adequately and are likely to fail to adjust.

Research on parents illustrates this:

- **Mothers' parenting self-efficacy.** A mother who feels capable as a parent tends to **foster self-efficacy in her children**, and she herself suffers **less despondency and emotional strain** in the parenting role than a mother who doubts her parenting (Olioff & Aboud, 1991; Teti & Gelfand, 1991).
- **Working mothers (Bandura, 1995).** Among mothers with jobs outside the home, those high in self-efficacy reported **significantly less physical and emotional strain** arising from the conflict between **work and family** demands than those low in self-efficacy.

**The middle years** are also stressful, as people **re-evaluate** their careers and their family and social lives. Confronting our limitations and redefining our goals, we must **reassess our skills** and look for new opportunities to strengthen our self-efficacy. Compare Erikson's generativity-versus-stagnation crisis (Ch 6): both theorists see middle adulthood as a time of stock-taking, but Bandura frames it as a re-appraisal of competence rather than of care for the next generation.

### Old age

Reassessing self-efficacy in **old age** is difficult. Older people face **failing mental and physical powers**, **retirement** from work and a **pulling back from social life**, and each of these can push them into fresh self-appraisal. If self-efficacy falls as a result, the fall can itself worsen physical and mental functioning, a **self-fulfilling prophecy**:

- Losing confidence in one's **sexual performance** can make a person **less sexually active**.
- A weaker sense of **physical efficacy** can bring **fatigue** and lead the person to **cut back on physical activity**.

If we stop believing we can do something we used to enjoy and do well, we may stop even trying, and the unused ability then genuinely declines. A retired teacher who decides she is "too old to learn the computer" never tries, never gains a success experience, and confirms her belief.

Bandura's conclusion across the whole sequence is one of the most quoted lines in the chapter: **self-efficacy is the single most important factor in determining success or failure throughout the entire life span**.

| Stage | Main sources and influences | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Childhood** (from infancy) | Parents first (responsive, stimulating, allowing exploration); then siblings, peers, teachers; birth order | Schneewind (1995) sex differences; ability grouping and grading on a curve harm low achievers |
| **Adolescence** | New demands: sexuality, college, career | Success depends on self-efficacy built in childhood |
| **Young adulthood** | Marriage, parenthood, career | High self-efficacy needed to adjust; parenting self-efficacy protects mothers |
| **Middle years** | Re-evaluation of career, family, social life | Reassess skills, find new routes to self-efficacy |
| **Old age** | Declining abilities, retirement, social withdrawal | Low self-efficacy acts as a self-fulfilling prophecy |

> **Trap.** Bandura's developmental scheme is **not** a stage theory in Erikson's or Freud's sense: there are no fixed crises or fixations and no fixed ages. The labels (childhood, adolescence, adulthood, old age) mark the **changing sources and demands** that self-efficacy must meet. Also note that he splits **adulthood** into **two** periods, young adulthood and the middle years.

## 13.6 Behaviour Modification

Bandura developed his social-cognitive theory with a practical aim: to **modify learned behaviours** that society considers **undesirable or abnormal**. Like Skinner's approach to therapy, his focuses on the **external** side, the inappropriate or destructive behaviours themselves, on the assumption that they were **learned**, as all behaviour is. He made no attempt to treat supposed **underlying unconscious conflicts**. The target is the **behaviour or symptom**, not a presumed inner neurosis.

He developed **three forms of behaviour therapy**:

1. **Modelling** (watching a model, live or filmed, cope with the feared situation),
2. **Guided participation** (watching a live model and then joining in with the model),
3. **Covert modelling** (imagining a model cope, without seeing one).

### Fears and phobias

The logic is direct: if modelling is how behaviour is learned in the first place, it should also be an effective way to **unlearn or change** it. Bandura used modelling to remove fears and other intense emotional reactions.

**Children afraid of dogs (Bandura, Grusec, & Menlove, 1967).**

- **Participants:** children who were afraid of dogs.
- **Model:** a child **of the same age** playing with a dog (note the similarity principle from 13.2).
- **Procedure:** the fearful children watched from a **safe distance** while the model made **progressively bolder** approaches: first **petting the dog through the bars of a playpen**, then **going into the pen** and playing with it.
- **Result:** the observers' fear of dogs was **considerably reduced**.

**Adults with snake phobia (Bandura, Blanchard, & Ritter, 1969).** In this classic study Bandura and his colleagues eliminated an **intense fear of snakes in adults**.

- **Procedure:** the participants watched a **film** in which **children, adolescents and adults** made **progressively closer contact** with a snake. The filmed models first handled **plastic snakes**, then **touched live snakes**, and finally let a **large snake crawl over their bodies**.
- **Control by the client:** phobic participants could **stop the film** whenever the scenes became too threatening, so exposure was graded by their own tolerance.
- **Result:** gradually, their fear of snakes was **overcome**.

Notice the design features that recur in all modelling therapy: a **graded hierarchy** of increasingly threatening acts, **models who cope successfully**, and **control in the client's hands**. Classic background: the 1969 study compared several treatments (filmed or symbolic modelling, live modelling combined with guided participation, and systematic desensitisation, against an untreated control), and **live modelling with guided participation** proved the most powerful, which is why guided participation is the technique Schultz describes next.

**Guided participation.** Here the client watches a **live** model and then **participates with the model**. For a snake phobia:

1. The client watches through an **observation window** while a live model handles a snake.
2. The client **enters the room** with the model and watches the handling **at close range**.
3. **Wearing gloves**, the client is coaxed into **touching the middle of the snake** while the model holds its **head and tail**.
4. Eventually the client touches the snake **without gloves**.

**Covert modelling.** Modelling works **even when there is no model to see**. In **covert modelling**, clients are told to **imagine a model coping** with the feared or threatening situation; they do not actually watch anyone. Covert modelling has been used successfully to treat **snake phobias** and **social inhibitions**. A shy student might be asked to picture, in detail, a classmate walking up to a group at a party, introducing himself and joining the conversation.

> **Trap.** Keep the three techniques apart by asking **where the model is**. Modelling: the model is **seen**, live or on film. Guided participation: the model is **live** and the client **joins in** physically. Covert modelling: the model is **imagined**; nothing is seen. In covert modelling the client imagines **a model** coping, not himself.

**Why curing a snake phobia matters.** You might think fear of snakes is a minor problem, but removing it has changed many people's lives, even people who rarely meet a snake. Besides raising **self-esteem and self-efficacy**, it can change personal and working habits:

- A woman was able to **wear a necklace** for the first time after modelling therapy; before, necklaces had reminded her of snakes.
- A **realtor** increased his income because he no longer avoided visiting properties in **rural areas**.
- Many others treated by modelling therapy were freed from **nightmares** about snakes.

Phobias restrict daily life. Many people who fear **spiders** react to merely seeing a **picture** of one with a racing heart, breathlessness and vomiting. Phobic people **doubt their self-efficacy** in the feared situation and have little confidence that they can deal with it. Freeing them from the fear greatly enlarges their world and raises their self-efficacy.

### Advantages of modelling therapy

Modelling therapy, especially when delivered through **online video**, has practical advantages:

- **Complex behaviours can be seen as a whole.**
- **Irrelevant behaviour can be edited out**, so the client's time is spent only on what matters.
- The **same videos can be reused** with many patients and by **several therapists at once**.
- It can be used with **groups**, saving time and money when several people share the same problem.

The approach has proved effective with **phobias, obsessive-compulsive disorders and sexual dysfunction**, and the benefits have been reported to **last for years**.

A large body of research has examined **self-efficacy during and after** behaviour-modification therapy. As clients' self-efficacy **rose during treatment**, they became increasingly able to face the source of their fear, and it was the **therapeutic procedure itself** that raised their self-efficacy. In Bandura's view this is the common mechanism of successful therapy: treatments work by giving people mastery experiences that change what they believe they can do.

### Anxiety

Modelling can also reduce anxiety. Schultz gives two examples: fear of medical treatment and test anxiety.

#### Fear of medical treatment

Some people fear medical situations so intensely that they avoid treatment altogether.

**Children facing surgery (Melamed & Siegel, 1975).**

- **Participants:** children **scheduled for surgery** who had **never been in hospital** before.
- **Groups:** an **experimental group** watched a film of a **boy's experience in hospital**; a **control group** watched a film of a **boy going on a trip**.
- **The model:** the boy in the hospital film was an exemplary **coping model**: he was somewhat anxious at first but coped well with the doctors and the procedures.
- **Results:** the hospital film **reduced anxiety**, and children who had seen it showed **fewer behaviour problems after hospitalisation** than the controls.

Classic background: the hospital film in this study is usually named in textbooks as *Ethan Has an Operation*. Note also that the model was a **coping** model (anxious at first, then managing) rather than a fearless **mastery** model; a model who shows some fear and then copes is closer to the frightened child's own state, which is generally thought to make such models more effective.

Similar procedures have reduced **fear of hospitalisation in adults** and **fear of dental treatment**.

**A procedure most patients refused (Allen, Danforth, & Drabman, 1989).** One medical procedure was so stressful that **more than 80 percent** of patients initially **refused it or quit before finishing**. Patients who watched a **video of a model undergoing the procedure and describing how he coped with his distress** were more likely to **complete the treatment**, did so with **less anxiety**, and had a **shorter hospital stay**.

#### Test anxiety

For some college students **test anxiety** is so severe that their exam marks do not reflect what they actually know.

**Coping models and test anxiety (Sarason, 1975).**

- **Participants:** college students divided by **personality test scores** into **high** and **low test anxiety** groups.
- **Three conditions:**
  1. a filmed (female) model **talked about her own test anxiety and how she coped with it**;
  2. the same model talked about test anxiety but said **nothing about coping**;
  3. the model talked about **other college activities**.
- **Task:** participants memorised a list of **nonsense syllables** and were tested on recall.
- **Results:** **high-anxiety** students were **most affected by the coping model**: they recalled significantly better than high-anxiety students in the other two conditions.
- **What it shows:** it is not exposure to a model as such, nor talk about anxiety, but a model who **demonstrates coping** that helps, and it helps most those who need it most.

### Ethical issues in behaviour modification

The results are impressive, but some **educators, politicians and even psychologists** have attacked behaviour modification as **exploitation**: manipulating and controlling people against their will. Bandura's reply had three parts.

1. **Awareness.** Behaviour modification does **not happen without the client's awareness**. On the contrary, **self-awareness and self-regulation** are essential to any programme of change: the techniques fail unless the person understands **which behaviours are being reinforced**. People are not being treated against their will.
2. **Client choice.** The **clients decide what they want to change**; nobody else controls them. People come to a therapist to remove specific fears and anxieties that stop them functioning or coping with daily life. The relationship is a **contract between two consenting individuals**, not, in Bandura's image, a sinister master-controller pulling the strings of a spineless puppet.
3. **Freedom.** Far from enslaving people, modelling techniques **increase personal freedom**. Someone too frightened to leave the house, or compelled to wash her hands over and over, is not free; she lives within the narrow limits her phobia or compulsion sets. Removing those limits increases freedom of choice and opens the way to personal growth.

Many techniques derived from Bandura's work have become popular **alternatives to psychoanalysis** and other therapies.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT one of Bandura's answers to the charge that behaviour modification is manipulative?" The three real answers are client **awareness** (it cannot work without it), client **choice** of goals (a contract between consenting people), and **increased freedom**. An option such as "the therapist is the best judge of which behaviours should change" contradicts him.

## 13.7 Questions about Human Nature

In the 11th edition this module is a single paragraph, on free will versus determinism; Schultz's chapter summary adds his positions on nature versus nurture, the past versus the present, and the ultimate goal of life. Where the book is silent, the positions below are marked as inferences from the theory, so you can tell what is the book's rating and what is reasoning.

**Free will versus determinism: neither, both (reciprocal determinism).** This is the one issue on which the book says Bandura's position is clear. Behaviour is controlled **by the person**, through cognitive processes, **and by the environment**, through external social situations. Bandura called this **reciprocal determinism**. In his own phrasing, people are neither "powerless objects controlled by environmental forces" nor "free agents who can become whatever they choose"; people and their environments **determine each other** (Bandura, 1977, p. vii).

Work through an example. A shy student avoids the college debating society (person influences behaviour). Because she never attends, nobody invites her to the informal gatherings afterwards (behaviour shapes her environment). The lack of invitations confirms her belief that she does not fit in (environment feeds back on the person). The student is not a puppet of her surroundings, because her beliefs and choices helped create them; nor is she free to become anything she likes, because the surroundings she created now constrain her.

> **Trap.** Schultz's glossary defines reciprocal determinism as control of behaviour **by the individual (through cognitive processes) and by the environment (through external social stimulus events)**: two influences, person and environment. Classic background: many other textbooks present Bandura's later formulation, **triadic reciprocal determinism** (or triadic reciprocal causation), in which **behaviour (B), personal/cognitive factors (P) and the environment (E)** all influence one another, usually drawn as a triangle. Both are Bandura's; if an option lists all three as mutually influencing, it is correct, and if an option says behaviour is determined by the environment alone, it is Skinner, not Bandura.

**Nature versus nurture: overwhelmingly nurture.** The chapter summary states that **most behaviour is learned** and that **genetic factors play only a minor role**. The research module adds one route by which inherited characteristics do matter: Bandura suggested that **physical appearance** can influence the **reinforcers** people receive from others, and hence how they feel about themselves (see the Andreoletti study in 13.9). Even here, the gene acts **through** the social environment.

**The past versus the present: childhood matters, but does not imprison.** According to the summary, learning in **childhood may be more influential** than learning in adulthood, but **adults are not victims of their childhood experiences**. People keep learning from new models and new experiences throughout life, and self-efficacy is re-appraised in every period (13.5). Compare Freud, for whom the adult personality is essentially formed by about age 5.

**Uniqueness versus universality: not rated in the 11th edition.** Reasoning from the theory: the **processes** of learning (attention, retention, production, motivation; the four sources of self-efficacy) are **universal**, but each person's history of models, reinforcements and standards is different, so the **content** of each personality is individual. The fairest answer is "universal processes producing individual differences".

**Equilibrium versus growth (the ultimate goal): competence through realistic standards.** The chapter summary puts it plainly: our **ultimate goal** is to set **realistic performance standards** so as to maintain an **optimal level of self-efficacy**. This is not Freud's tension reduction. Nor is it Maslow's or Rogers's self-actualisation; it is a goal of effective, realistic coping and mastery, which places Bandura on the growth side but in a more modest, measurable form.

**Optimism versus pessimism: not rated in the 11th edition, but the theory is plainly optimistic.** Reasoning from the theory: whatever was learned from models can be **relearned**; phobias that blighted lives for decades can be removed in weeks; self-efficacy can be deliberately **raised** by arranging success experiences, models and persuasion; and Bandura argued that behaviour modification **increases freedom**. A theory with these features takes a hopeful view of human beings' capacity to change.

| Issue | Bandura's position | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Reciprocal determinism**: person (cognition) and environment control behaviour together | Stated in the 11th-ed text |
| Nature vs nurture | **Nurture**: most behaviour learned; genetics minor (e.g., appearance affects reinforcers received) | Chapter summary; research module |
| Past vs present | Childhood learning may weigh more, but **adults are not victims of childhood** | Chapter summary |
| Uniqueness vs universality | Universal learning processes, individual content | Inference; not rated in the 11th ed |
| Equilibrium vs growth | Growth in the sense of **competence**: realistic standards for optimal self-efficacy | Chapter summary (ultimate goal) |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimistic**: learned behaviour can be changed; self-efficacy can be raised | Inference; not rated in the 11th ed |

## 13.8 Assessment in Bandura's Theory

Like Skinner, Bandura concentrated on **overt behaviour** rather than internal motivating forces, so he used none of the classic personality-assessment tools of the psychoanalytic tradition: **no free association, no dream analysis, no projective techniques**. Unlike Skinner, he accepted that **cognitive variables** operate, and therefore **both behaviour and cognitive variables can be assessed**.

The chapter's own studies show what this looks like in practice:

| Study or topic | What was assessed | How |
|---|---|---|
| Children about to have surgery (Melamed & Siegel, 1975) | Anxiety and behaviour | **Direct observation** of behaviour, **self-report inventories** and **physiological measurements** |
| Self-efficacy research in general | Behavioural and cognitive variables | Measured **quantitatively** |
| Self-efficacy in phobias | Self-efficacy for approaching the feared object | Participants' **self-ratings of how many tasks on a behavioural-avoidance test they expected to complete** |
| Test anxiety in college students (Sarason, 1975) | Level of test anxiety | **Personality inventories** |

The **behavioural-avoidance test** deserves a word of explanation because it is the signature measure of the phobia work. The client is presented with a graded series of steps towards the feared object: for a snake phobia, entering the room, approaching the glass case, looking in, touching the snake with a gloved hand, holding it, and so on. Before treatment, the client rates **how many of the steps he expects to manage**; that rating is his self-efficacy. His **actual** performance on the steps is the behavioural measure. Comparing the two before and after treatment shows whether a rise in self-efficacy goes with a rise in approach behaviour, which is exactly the relationship Bandura's theory predicts.

> **Trap.** "Which assessment technique did Bandura NOT use?" Free association, dream analysis and projective tests (Rorschach, TAT) are the answers. He **did** use direct observation, self-report inventories, physiological measures, personality inventories and self-efficacy ratings on behavioural-avoidance tests.

## 13.9 Research on Bandura's Theory

Bandura preferred **well-controlled laboratory investigation** in the rigorous tradition of experimental psychology. You have already seen the hallmarks: **experimental and control groups**, **precise measurement of independent and dependent variables**, **large groups of participants**, and **statistical comparison of group averages**. This is the opposite of the single-case clinical method of Freud or the idiographic method Allport favoured. Schultz illustrates the research the theory has generated with three lines of work: **self-efficacy**, **collective efficacy**, and the effect of **televised and online models on aggression**.

### Self-efficacy

#### Age and gender differences

- **Gender.** On average, **men score higher** than women in self-efficacy. The gender difference **peaks in the 20s** and then **declines** in later years.
- **Age.** For both sexes, self-efficacy **rises through childhood and early adulthood, peaks in middle age, and declines after age 60** (Gecas, 1989; Lachman, 1985).

Despite the average decline, people differ widely in their beliefs about their own capabilities, and those beliefs appear to matter.

- **Memory beliefs (Valentijn et al., 2006).** In a study of adults in the **Netherlands** with an average age of **66**, people who believed their memory was getting worse performed **significantly worse on memory tests six years later** than people whose sense of self-efficacy included confidence in their memory.
- **Meta-analysis (Beaudoin & Desrichard, 2011).** An analysis of **more than 100 studies** confirmed that low **memory self-efficacy**, our belief about how good our memory is, can affect performance on memory tests.

The conclusion Schultz draws is that **belief in our abilities can affect the abilities themselves**: the more we believe we can do something, the more likely we are to do it.

> **Trap.** Two age facts get mixed up. The **gender gap** peaks in the **20s**. **Self-efficacy itself** peaks in **middle age** and declines **after 60**. Also, men score higher **on average**, not in every domain (see career self-efficacy below).

#### The role of parental self-efficacy

Parents, siblings and peers shape self-efficacy (13.5), and parents' **own** self-efficacy turns out to matter.

- **Italy (Steca, Bassi, Caprara, & Fave, 2011).** Adolescents whose parents scored high in **parental self-efficacy** (believing they were effective parents) scored higher in their own self-efficacy. They also had **fewer behaviour problems, less anxiety, better school performance**, and were **more open and honest** in communicating with their parents.
- **African-American boys in public housing (Nebbitt, 2009).** Teenage boys whose parents gave more **support, control and had higher self-efficacy** had higher self-efficacy themselves. **Lack** of parental support and guidance and low parental self-efficacy were linked to more **substance abuse and delinquency**.
- **Chicago teenagers (McCoy & Bowen, 2014).** The more **supportive** the parents, the **higher** their children's self-efficacy.
- **Mexican-American families (Dumka, Gonzales, Wheeler, & Millsap, 2010).** High parental self-efficacy went with **higher self-efficacy and fewer behaviour problems** in both male and female adolescents.
- **Over-control (Givertz & Segrin, 2014).** American young adults whose parents had kept **tight control** over their upbringing had **lower** self-efficacy than those whose parents were less controlling.
- **Fathers in Malaysia (Yap & Baharudin, 2015).** **Fathers'** involvement in upbringing was **just as important** as mothers' for teenagers' self-efficacy and overall happiness.

Read the Nebbitt and Givertz findings together: parental **control** helped in one study and harmed in the other. The difference is between structure and guidance offered within a supportive relationship, and control that leaves no room for the young person to act and succeed on their own, which removes the performance attainments self-efficacy is built from.

#### Physical appearance

Recall Bandura's suggestion that appearance affects the reinforcers people get from others, and so how they feel about themselves.

**Appearance and control beliefs (Andreoletti, Zebrowitz, & Lachman, 2001).**

- **Participants:** adult men and women aged **25 to 76**.
- **Main finding:** physical appearance had a **greater effect on feelings of being in control** of one's life than either **self-esteem or health** did.
- **Baby-faceness:** a round face, large eyes, a small nose bridge and a small chin was **strongly related to low control beliefs in young and middle adulthood**. **Older** baby-faced adults, however, reported **stronger** feelings of control; a plausible reason is that they looked younger than their thin-faced age-mates and so were treated differently. The effects were **larger for women**, and looking youthful in later life carried clear advantages socially and at work.
- **Attractiveness:** people rated **less attractive** reported **lower** feelings of control in both job and social situations.
- **Height:** **shorter** people reported **lower** feelings of control in young adulthood than taller or average-height people.

> **Trap.** Baby-faceness is linked to **low** control beliefs in **young and middle** adulthood but to **stronger** control beliefs in **older** adults. A question that says "baby-faced people have low control beliefs throughout life" is wrong.

#### Academic performance

There is a **significant positive relationship between self-efficacy and academic performance**.

- **Teachers** high in self-efficacy, confident in their teaching ability, create **more opportunities** for students to achieve at a high level.
- **Students'** self-efficacy is positively related to **motivation, effort, level of aspiration and persistence** in the classroom (e.g., Bassi, Steca, Fave, & Caprara, 2007; Gibson & Dembo, 1984; Multon, Brown, & Lent, 1991; Zimmerman, 1995).
- **Germany (Holzberger, Philipp, & Kunter, 2013).** Teachers high in self-efficacy were rated by their students as **more effective**. And teachers whose students rated their instruction highly **developed higher self-efficacy** in turn: positive feedback from students made them surer of themselves. The relationship runs **both ways**.
- **School climate (Bandura, 1997).** In **high-achieving schools**, principals cared more about **education** than about enforcing policies and rules, and teachers set **high expectations and standards**. In **low-achieving schools**, principals acted more as **administrators and disciplinarians** than as educators, and teachers expected little academically.

**Culture.**

- **East and West Germany (Oettingen & Maier, 1999).** Elementary school students in **grades 2 to 6** were studied in East and West Germany **before reunification in 1990**. Children in the **East German communist-collectivist** culture scored **lower** in self-efficacy than those in the **West German capitalist-individualist** culture; they were less confident about doing well at school and saw themselves as less intelligent.
- **Iran (Hejazi & Hasany, 2014).** High school students with a stronger sense of **ethnic identity** had **higher** self-efficacy than those with a weak sense of ethnic identity.

#### Career choice and job performance

**Gender and career self-efficacy (Hackett, 1995).** Men rated their self-efficacy **high for both traditionally "male" and traditionally "female" occupations**. Women rated themselves **high for traditionally female occupations but low for traditionally male ones**. Yet the men and women performed **at comparable levels on standardised tests of verbal and quantitative skills**. They had similar measurable abilities but **perceived** those abilities differently, so their sense of competence for different careers differed by gender. This is a clear case where self-efficacy, not ability, channels choices.

Self-efficacy affects **how long people spend looking for jobs** and their **later success** in them.

- **Goals (Locke & Latham, 1990).** Employees high in self-efficacy set **higher personal goals** and are **more committed** to them. On the job they concentrate on **analysing and solving problems**, whereas low self-efficacy employees dwell on **personal deficiencies and fear of failure**, which can undermine productivity.
- **Failure lowers self-efficacy (Hardy, 2014).** Failing at a **computer-based task** reduced a person's self-efficacy.
- **Meta-analysis (Stajkovic & Luthans, 1998).** **114 studies** with **more than 21,600 participants** showed a significant positive relationship: the higher the self-efficacy, the **better the job performance**.
- **Job complexity (Judge, Jackson, Shaw, Scott, & Rich, 2007).** An update found self-efficacy predicted performance **better for low-complexity jobs** than for jobs of medium or high complexity.
- **Engagement (Ventura, Salanova, & Llorens, 2015).** High self-efficacy employees are much more **engaged** in their work and suffer **less burnout**.
- **Feedback (Schmidt & DeShon, 2010).** High self-efficacy people perform better when they get **more feedback** on their performance, and often **do not** perform well when there is **little or no feedback**. Not knowing how they are doing is a handicap for them.
- **Training and satisfaction (Gupta, Ganster, & Kepes, 2013; Salas & Cannon-Bowers, 2001).** High self-efficacy people do better in **job training programmes** and report more **job satisfaction, organisational commitment and job performance**.

> **Trap.** Judge et al. (2007): self-efficacy predicts performance **better for low-complexity** jobs, not high-complexity ones. Stajkovic and Luthans (1998): **114** studies, **21,600+** participants. Schmidt and DeShon (2010): high self-efficacy people do **worse** without feedback.

#### Physical health

Self-efficacy affects several aspects of physical well-being: **tolerating pain, maintaining a healthy lifestyle and recovering from illness**.

**Pain tolerance.**

- **Childbirth (Manning & Wright, 1983).** Pregnant women taught **relaxation and breathing exercises** for labour pain believed they had **more control** over the pain than women not taught these techniques. The higher a woman's self-efficacy and sense of control, the **longer she tolerated** the discomfort of delivery before asking for pain medication, and the **less medication** she needed.
- **China (Jackson, Wang, Wang, & Fan, 2014).** In a study of **more than 15,000 patients**, those higher in self-efficacy had **less impairment, less emotional distress and less severe pain**.
- **Endorphins.** Coping techniques that improve self-efficacy produce **substantial increases in endorphins**, the body's natural painkillers, which gives a physiological route for the effect.
- **Low back pain (Altmaier, Russell, Kao, Lehmann, & Weinstein, 1993).** Patients in a **three-week rehabilitation programme** completed a pain-rating scale and a self-efficacy scale. **Six months later**, those higher in self-efficacy reported **better physical functioning and less back pain**.

**Maintaining a healthy lifestyle.** Bandura (1995) argued that because lifestyle habits can either improve or damage health, people have some behavioural control over their own vitality, and that efficacy beliefs enter **every phase of personal change**: whether people even consider changing a health habit, whether they can summon the motivation and persistence to succeed, and how well they keep up the change once made.

- **Older adults (French, Olander, Chrisholm, & McSharry, 2014).** Techniques that raised self-efficacy in adults aged **60 and over** led to **more physical activity**; those whose self-efficacy stayed low remained more **sedentary**.
- **Alcohol (Taylor, 2000).** In **Native American and native Alaskan** adults, the **lower** the self-efficacy, the **greater** the alcohol consumption.
- **Smoking (Schwarzer & Fuchs, 1995; Stacy et al., 1992).** Adolescents with **higher** self-efficacy were **more resistant to peer pressure** to start smoking.

**Recovering from illness.**

- **Pulmonary disease.** Patients high in self-efficacy responded better to **cognitive and behavioural treatment** for pulmonary disease.
- **Heart attack (Kaplan, Atkins, & Reinsch, 1984; McLeod, 1986).** After a heart attack, men were more likely to get back to their normal activities, and felt **less fear and depression**, if they **and their spouses** were both confident about their cardiac fitness. Patients with higher self-efficacy also **kept to their prescribed exercise programmes** more faithfully, and improved more.
- **Italy (Greco, Steca, Monzani, Malfatto, & Parati, 2015).** A large study of heart patients confirmed that higher self-efficacy **eased the impact of illness** and led to greater **health satisfaction**.
- **Diabetes in Israel (Mishali, Omer, & Heymann, 2011).** High self-efficacy patients were far more likely to **persist with self-care**.
- **Orthopaedic surgery (Waldrop, Lightsey, Ethington, Woemmel, & Coke, 2001).** After **hip or knee replacement**, high self-efficacy patients did significantly better in **rehabilitation**.
- **Breast cancer (Carver et al., 2000).** The stronger the expectation of **remaining cancer-free**, the better the **emotional adjustment** to the disease.

#### Mental health

Self-efficacy has been linked to **depression and anxiety** and to **social interest and self-esteem**.

**Depression and anxiety.**

- **Italy and the United States (Bandura, Pastorelli, Barbaranelli, & Caprara, 1999).** Italian children (average age **11.5**) who rated themselves **low in social and academic efficacy** were significantly more likely to be **depressed**. Low **social** efficacy was also significantly related to depression in a sample of **American adolescents**.
- **Netherlands (Muris, 2002).** In adolescents, low **social** efficacy was related to high **anxiety, neuroticism and depressive symptoms**.
- **China and Nigeria (Li, 2010; Onyeizugbo, 2010).** In both cultures, people high in self-efficacy reported **less job stress and less test anxiety**.
- **Adults (Holahan & Holahan, 1987).** Low social efficacy contributed to **depression** partly because poor coping skills prevented people from building a **social support network**.

These findings may describe a **circle** rather than a one-way cause. Low self-efficacy can lead to depression, and depression further lowers self-efficacy: depressed people believe they are much **less capable** than others in many areas of life and have **little control** over their situations (Bandura, 1997). A depressed client who says "there's no point applying, I won't get it" is showing exactly this loop.

**Social interest and self-esteem.**

- **Adler's criteria (Dinter, 2000).** American college students high in self-efficacy scored higher on several of **Alfred Adler's** marks of mental health: **social interest**, the **striving for perfection**, and a **sense of belonging**.
- **Canada, Iran and the United States (Hoseinzadah, Azizi, & Tavakoli, 2014; Lightsey, Burke, Ervin, Henderson, & Yee, 2006; Steel, 2007).** High school students and adults high in self-efficacy also scored high in **self-esteem**, and were **less likely to procrastinate** or to give up when they met an obstacle.

#### Coping with stress

Higher self-efficacy and a sense of control over life events go with a **better ability to cope with stress** and to limit its harmful effects on the body. Bandura (2001) wrote that a strong sense of coping efficacy lowers vulnerability to stress and depression and builds resilience in the face of adversity. High self-efficacy has been associated with a **stronger immune system**, **lower release of stress-related hormones**, and **reduced susceptibility to respiratory infections**.

- **Abortion (Cozzarelli, 1993).** Women higher in self-efficacy adjusted better after an abortion, with significantly **less depression** and **better mood**.
- **First child (Ozer, 1995).** Women higher in self-efficacy coped better with the demands following the **birth of their first child**.
- **German migrants (Jerusalem & Mittag, 1995).** People who moved from **East to West Germany** after the Berlin Wall came down (the book dates this to 1990) faced a change from an economically disadvantaged communist life to an affluent capitalist one. Those **higher in self-efficacy adapted significantly better**. The researchers described self-efficacy as a powerful personal resource: high-efficacy migrants saw the demands of their new life as **challenges rather than threats**, and had **lower anxiety, better health and fewer health complaints**.
- **Facial disfigurement (Hagedoorn & Molleman, 2006).** Dutch adults disfigured by treatment for **head or neck cancer** who were **lower** in self-efficacy felt **more stress** when others reacted to them unpleasantly or rejectingly. Those higher in self-efficacy felt less stress because they believed they could exercise **some control over how others reacted** to them.

### Collective efficacy

Self-efficacy has a group-level counterpart. When people work together on a shared enterprise towards specific goals, the group can develop **collective efficacy**: a shared belief that **together they can overcome obstacles and achieve their goals** (Dithurbide & Feltz, 2012). Examples are a **sports team**, a **department** in a large organisation, a **military combat unit**, or **neighbours uniting against drug dealers**. Think of a resident welfare association that has already got the municipality to fix the drains once: it now believes it can get the streetlights fixed too.

**College basketball (Watson, Chemers, & Preiser, 2001).** High collective efficacy developed in teams that had **highly competent leaders early in the season** and had **won most of their games the season before**. Teams with the **highest collective efficacy at the start** of the season **placed better** in the end-of-season standings than teams low in collective efficacy. Note how this maps onto the individual sources: past wins are the group's performance attainment, and competent leaders supply models and persuasion.

#### Collective efficacy in families and schools

- **Families (Bandura, Caprara, Barbaranelli, Regalia, & Scabini, 2011).** High family collective efficacy produced **more open family communication**, **more truthful disclosure by teenagers to their parents**, and **greater satisfaction with family life** for both parents and children.
- **Teachers in the Netherlands (Moolenaar, Sleegers, & Daly, 2012).** High collective efficacy among teachers led to **higher student achievement and better grades**.
- **US students (Williams & Guerra, 2011).** Higher collective efficacy among a large group of **5th-, 8th- and 11th-grade** students led to **less bullying**.
- **Greece (Sapouna, 2010).** **11- to 14-year-olds** in classes high in collective efficacy showed, and received, **far less bullying** than those in low collective efficacy classes.

#### Collective efficacy in neighbourhoods and organisations

- **Neighbourhoods.** High collective efficacy in a neighbourhood, even a tough inner-city one, has been linked to **less alcoholism, drug use, violence and crime, including murder** (Ahern et al., 2013; Fagan, Wright, & Pinchevsky, 2014; Maxwell, Garner, & Skogan, 2011).
- **Organisations.** The collective efficacy of employing organisations has been shown to **raise job performance and helping behaviour** among employees and to **reduce workplace stress**, in Western cultures and in **China** (Du, Shin, & Choi, 2015; Esnard & Roques, 2014).
- **Italian air force (Borgogni, Petitta, & Mastrorilla, 2010).** Personnel in units higher in collective efficacy were **more committed to the organisation** and **more satisfied with their jobs**.

> **Highlights: Research on self-efficacy.** Schultz's box summarises the findings. Studies show that **men score higher than women when younger**; self-efficacy **rises into adulthood, peaks in middle age and declines after 60**; people whose **parents were high in parental self-efficacy** are high in self-efficacy themselves and did better at school with less anxiety and fewer behaviour problems; and self-efficacy is **higher in individualistic cultures**. People high in self-efficacy **get better grades**; set **higher career goals**, are more committed to them and **perform better at work**; are in **better health**, **tolerate pain better** and **recover faster** from illness; are **less likely to drink and smoke**; are less likely to suffer **depression, test anxiety, job stress or neuroticism**; and have **higher self-esteem**.

### Self-efficacy and the Internet

**Internet self-efficacy** is our confidence in our ability to use the Internet effectively.

- **Taiwan (Tsai & Tsai, 2010).** Among **8th-grade** students there was **no overall gender difference** in Internet self-efficacy. But **girls** were more confident about **communicating** online, and **boys** more confident about **exploring** online.
- **United States (Buse, 2010).** Among American college students, **men** scored higher than women in **computer self-efficacy**.
- **Turkey (Iskender & Akin, 2010).** College students high in **social self-efficacy** (confidence in starting social contacts and making friends easily) had **higher self-esteem and emotional well-being**, but were **far more likely to become addicted to the Internet** than students low in social self-efficacy.
- **Germany (Kramer & Winter, 2008).** Adults high in self-efficacy about **making a good impression** posted more **informal personal photos** (at parties, for example) and presented themselves as **relaxed, funny and cool** on their Facebook pages.

> **Trap.** The Taiwan result is reported differently in two places. The text says there was **no overall gender difference** among 8th graders, with girls ahead on **communicating** and boys ahead on **exploring**. The chapter's Highlights box condenses this to "girls in the 8th grade score higher than boys". If a question is built on the Highlights wording, choose "girls higher"; if it asks for the detailed finding, give the communicating/exploring split. Also, high **social** self-efficacy went with **more** Internet addiction, not less, which is counter-intuitive and therefore testable.

### The relationship between aggressive behaviour and televised and online violence

Bandura and researchers in many countries have shown, in the laboratory and in real life, that **seeing violence breeds violence**, whether the violence is on a television or computer screen, in films or video games, or in homes, streets and schools (Elson & Ferguson, 2014). The evidence is strong enough that the **US Surgeon General, the National Institute of Mental Health, the American Psychological Association, the American Medical Association and the American Psychiatric Association** all agree that exposure to violence is a **significant risk factor** for violence in those who witness it (Pozios, Kamban, & Bender, 2013).

#### Television

- **Literature review (Rogoff, Paradise, Arauz, Correa-Chavez, & Angelillo, 2003).** A large-scale review confirmed the link between watching violent television in **childhood** and **later aggression**.
- **Longitudinal (Huesmann, Moise-Titus, Podolski, & Eron, 2003).** In people in their **early and mid-20s**, there was a **strong positive correlation** between how much television violence they had watched **between ages 6 and 10** and how aggressive they were as **adults**.
- **Germany (Krahe, Busching, & Moller, 2012).** In teenagers, exposure to media violence was **highly related** to aggressive behaviour.

A different approach looks at **real-world aggression shortly after people see televised violence**. These are Phillips's natural-experiment studies, and their numbers are favourite MCQ material:

| Study | Televised event | What followed |
|---|---|---|
| **Phillips (1985)** | Highly publicised **riots** | A brief, sharp rise in violent acts, **peaking 3 to 4 days** later |
| **Phillips (1983)** | Televised **championship boxing matches** | US **murder rates rose more than 12 percent** above the expected level over the **three days** following the fight; the pattern held across a **15-year period** |
| **Phillips (1974)** | Suicide of a **film star or other celebrity** widely reported in the news | The incidence of **suicide tends to rise** (self-directed violence) |

Classic background: the rise in suicides after a publicised celebrity suicide is widely known as the **Werther effect**, Phillips's own term, after the hero of Goethe's novel whose suicide was said to have inspired imitations.

> **Trap.** Match the Phillips numbers to the right event: **riots, peak 3 to 4 days (1985)**; **boxing, murders up more than 12 percent for 3 days, over 15 years (1983)**; **celebrity suicide, suicides rise (1974)**. These are correlational, real-world studies, not laboratory experiments like Bobo.

#### Video games

Playing **violent video games** has been studied in **large samples** of **children, teenagers and college students**, in the **United States, Japan and several other countries**. Compared with non-players, players showed:

- **More aggressive and hostile behaviour**, and **more drinking and drug use**;
- **More fights**, **arguments with teachers** and **poorer school performance**;
- **Less helping** of others;
- **Higher cardiovascular arousal**.

In general, **the more violent the game, the more violent the resulting behaviour** (the book cites eight studies here, among them Anderson et al., 2010; Gentile, Lynch, Linder, & Walsh, 2004; Huesmann, 2010; and Bartholow, Sestir, & Davis, 2005; the others are Holtz & Appel, 2011; Krahe & Moller, 2004; Uhlmann & Swanson, 2004; and Padilla-Walker et al., 2010).

Notice why games might be an even stronger case than television in Bandura's terms: the player does not merely watch a model but **performs** the aggressive acts and is **directly reinforced** with points and progress for them. All four processes of observational learning, and direct reinforcement too, are engaged at once.

#### Cyberbullying

- **Unsupervised computers (Calvete, Orue, Estevez, Villardon, & Padilla, 2010; Law, Shapka, & Olson, 2010; Werner & Bumpus, 2010).** Research in the **United States, Canada and Spain** found that when boys and girls aged **12 to 17** have computers that parents **neither control nor monitor**, levels of **online aggression, including cyberbullying**, can be high.
- **Targets (Raskauskas, Rubiano, Offen, & Wayland, 2015).** People **high in self-efficacy and self-esteem** are much **less bothered** by being cyberbullied.
- **Perpetrators (Bussey, Fitzpatrick, & Raman, 2015; Udris, 2014).** People **high in disinhibition**, whose moral restraints are weakened by watching a model do something harmful, are **much more given to cyberbullying**.

> **Highlights: Research on Bandura's ideas.** Schultz's second box summarises the rest of the module. Groups high in **collective efficacy** win more **basketball** games and get **better grades**, are more **committed to their organisation**, score high in **job satisfaction and performance**, **bully less** in the classroom, and enjoy more **openness and satisfaction in the family**. On **Internet self-efficacy**: American **male** college students score higher than females; in **Taiwan**, 8th-grade **girls** score higher than boys (see the trap above); people high in **social** self-efficacy are **more likely to become addicted** to the Internet; and those confident of making a good impression post more **informal photos such as selfies** on Facebook. **Aggression** is related to watching **violence on television and online in childhood** and to playing **violent video games** in childhood, adolescence and young adulthood. **Cyberbullying** is practised more by people **high in disinhibition** and affects people **high in self-efficacy** less.

## 13.10 Reflections on Bandura's Theory

### Criticisms

**Neglect of the inner person.** Social-learning theory concentrates on **overt behaviour**, and critics say this leaves out distinctly human inner aspects of personality such as **motivation and emotion**. They make the point with a medical analogy. Imagine a doctor whose patients come in with stomach pain, and who, attending only to overt behaviour, tells them to stop groaning, stop complaining and stop clutching their stomachs. What the patients may really need is medication or surgery for a diseased **internal organ**, the **underlying cause** of the pain. Treat only the symptom and leave the cause, the critics argue, and **substitute symptoms** may appear.

The chapter summary lists the criticisms in one place:

1. A focus on **overt behaviour to the exclusion of emotions and conflicts**.
2. Treating **symptoms rather than possible internal causes** (hence the symptom-substitution worry).
3. **Failing to state precisely how cognitive variables affect behaviour.** Bandura put cognition back into behaviourism, but critics say the theory does not specify exactly how attention, expectation and self-evaluation translate into action.

How far do these hold? The first two have more force against Skinner than against Bandura, because Bandura does assess cognitive variables (self-efficacy ratings, expectations) and his theory has a self-system. But it is true that unconscious conflict and deep emotional life have no place in it, which is exactly what a psychoanalytic critic would object to. The symptom-substitution charge is the classic psychoanalytic objection to all behaviour therapy; Schultz notes that modelling-therapy effects have been reported to **last for years** (13.6), which is not what a pure symptom-substitution account predicts.

### Contributions

**1. Objectivity and empirical support.** The approach is **objective** and suited to **laboratory investigation**, which makes it compatible with the emphasis of **experimental psychology**. Most experimental psychologists reject personality theories built on unconscious or other inner driving forces that cannot be **manipulated or measured** in the laboratory. Bandura's approach, by contrast, has a **great amount of empirical support**, and this is especially true of **self-efficacy**, whose usefulness research continues to confirm both in the laboratory and in real-world settings.

**2. Practical, functional value.** Observational learning and behaviour modification fit the **functional, pragmatic spirit of American psychology**. More readily than other approaches, their techniques can be taken out of the laboratory and **applied to practical problems**. They also give the **practitioner** more immediate reinforcement than other approaches: in clinical work, dramatic changes in a client's behaviour can be seen within **weeks or even days**, compared with the years a psychoanalysis may take.

### The widespread use of role models

Behaviour change on a much larger scale has been shown in **some 60 nations**. Bandura's central idea, that people learn behaviour from **role models they want to emulate**, has been used in **radio and television programmes in less developed nations** to promote social goals such as **population control, improving the status of women, and reducing the spread of AIDS** (Kaufman, Cornish, Zimmerman, & Johnson, 2014).

These serial dramas revolve around **characters who model behaviours** intended to achieve public-health goals, for themselves and for society. Studies show significant changes in **safe-sex practices and family planning among millions of people** after exposure to these models, supporting the idea that Bandura's principles can be applied to **national** as well as individual problems (Smith, 2002).

Classic background with an Indian connection: this "entertainment-education" method was developed from Bandura's social-learning theory by the Mexican television producer **Miguel Sabido** in the 1970s, and India's first long-running television serial, **Hum Log** (Doordarshan, 1984 to 1985), was built on the same model, with characters designed to model positive or negative behaviour on family planning and the status of women.

It is not surprising, then, that researchers and clinicians continue to study and promote social-learning theory. The steady stream of books, articles and studies still derived from it shows its **continuing popularity**, both as a way of studying behaviour in the laboratory and as a way of changing behaviour in the real world.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a criticism of Bandura's theory?" Real criticisms: ignores **inner** aspects (emotion, motivation, conflicts); treats **symptoms** not causes (possible symptom substitution); does not say **precisely how cognitive variables** affect behaviour. **Not** criticisms: "lacks empirical support" (the opposite is true, especially for self-efficacy), "relies on case studies of neurotic patients" (it relies on controlled experiments with normal people), "ignores cognition" (that is a criticism of Skinner).

## How the chapter fits together

**One idea, three levels.** The whole chapter is a single claim worked out at three levels. At the level of **learning**, people acquire behaviour by watching models and noting the consequences (observational learning, vicarious reinforcement, the four processes). At the level of the **self**, what they learn includes standards, and judging themselves against those standards produces self-reinforcement and, above all, **self-efficacy**. At the level of **causation**, the person and the environment shape each other (**reciprocal determinism**). Behaviour modification, the research on health, work and schools, collective efficacy and the media-violence work are all applications of those three levels.

**The thread from modelling to self-efficacy.** Notice how the second half of the chapter grows out of the first. Vicarious experience, the core of modelling, reappears as the **second source of self-efficacy**. Modelling therapy for phobias works, on Bandura's account, because it **raises self-efficacy**. Children first learn standards from models and later reinforce themselves against those standards. If you can explain why a snake-phobic client who watches a coping model improves, using both "observational learning" and "self-efficacy", you have the chapter.

**Bandura and Skinner (Ch 12).** Shared: overt behaviour, learning, laboratory method, rejection of the unconscious, behaviour modification. Different: humans versus animals, social versus isolated settings, cognition as mediator versus the empty organism, vicarious and self-reinforcement versus external reinforcement only, models versus reinforcers as controllers of behaviour, reciprocal determinism versus environmental determinism.

**Bandura and the cognitive approach (Kelly, Ch 11).** Kelly also put thinking at the centre, but through personal constructs studied clinically. Bandura brought cognition into the **learning** tradition and studied it experimentally. That is why Schultz calls his work both cognitive and behavioural.

**Bandura and the limited-domain theorists (Ch 14).** The next part of the book (Rotter's **locus of control**, Zuckerman's sensation seeking, Seligman's **learned helplessness** and positive psychology) studies narrower, measurable facets of personality. Self-efficacy belongs to the same family of control beliefs: Rotter asks whether **outcomes** are controlled by me or by outside forces; Bandura asks whether **I can perform** the behaviour; Seligman's learned helplessness describes the person who has stopped trying because nothing seemed to work, which is close to Bandura's description of extremely low self-efficacy. Bandura himself, though, is **not** the limited-domain approach in Schultz; he has his own part.

**Bandura and the psychoanalytic theorists.** Self-reinforcement resembles the **superego** but Bandura denied the identity. He rejected free association, dream analysis and projective tests. The chief criticism of his therapy (symptom substitution) is the psychoanalytic one. Two neo-analytic echoes: he shared **Adler's** interest in **birth order**, and self-efficacy correlates with Adler's **social interest**, striving for perfection and belonging (Dinter, 2000). His life-span view of self-efficacy parallels **Erikson's** interest in development across the whole life, without Erikson's fixed crises.

**Bandura and the humanists.** Like Maslow and Rogers he is optimistic and believes people can change, but his "self" is a set of cognitive processes, not an organising inner core, and his goal is realistic competence, not self-actualisation.

## Worked example

**Referral.** Meera, 26, is a software engineer in Bengaluru. She asks for help because of an intense fear of dogs. She takes an auto-rickshaw for a 600-metre trip to the metro station to avoid the stray dogs on the road, has stopped visiting a friend whose family keeps a Labrador, and recently turned down a promotion that would have involved site visits to campuses with dogs. At the sight of a dog her heart races, she sweats and feels she will faint. She remembers her mother screaming and pulling her indoors whenever a dog came near the house, and being chased by a dog once at about age 8. Her mother is still afraid of dogs.

**How would Bandura explain the origin?** Primarily through **modelling** of a **non-rational fear**. Her mother was a **same-sex, high-status, constantly available model**, and the fear was shown with strong **consequences** (screaming, rescue), which commands attention. Meera **attended** to it, **retained** it as vivid images and a verbal rule ("dogs are dangerous, get inside"), could easily **produce** the avoidance, and was **motivated** by the relief it brought. The single chase at 8 was a direct experience, but Bandura would note that the fear was in place before it, learned vicariously. He would **not** look for an unconscious conflict that the dog symbolises; the fear itself is the target.

**Why has it lasted?** Through **low self-efficacy** and **reciprocal determinism**. Because she avoids dogs, she never gains a **performance attainment** (the strongest source of self-efficacy). Her racing heart is **physiological arousal**, which she reads as proof that she cannot cope, lowering self-efficacy further. Her avoidance also reshapes her **environment** (autos, no visits, a job without site visits), and that narrowed environment removes every opportunity to learn otherwise. Person, behaviour and environment are keeping each other in place.

**Assessment.** A **behavioural-avoidance test**: a graded series from looking at a photograph of a dog, to watching a calm dog through a window, to standing in the same room, to touching a dog held on a leash by its handler. Before treatment Meera rates **how many steps she expects to complete** (her self-efficacy), then attempts them; the clinician records her **actual** performance and can add **physiological** measures (heart rate) and a **self-report** fear inventory.

**Treatment plan, using all three of Bandura's techniques and all four sources of self-efficacy.**

1. **Filmed modelling.** Short videos of young women of her age (similar models) approaching dogs step by step, starting with toy dogs and ending with a dog resting its head in the model's lap. She can **stop the video** whenever it becomes too threatening, as in Bandura, Blanchard and Ritter (1969). Ideally the models are **coping** models who show some nervousness and then manage it (vicarious experience).
2. **Guided participation.** A therapist (live model) handles a calm, trained dog while Meera watches through a door, then from close range, then strokes the dog's back while the therapist holds the head and lead, and finally does it on her own (performance attainment, the strongest source).
3. **Covert modelling** between sessions: she imagines a confident friend walking past the dogs near the metro station and greeting them calmly.
4. **Verbal persuasion** that is **realistic**: the therapist points out each completed step rather than promising she will love dogs.
5. **Arousal**: brief relaxation and breathing training, so that her body sends calmer signals that she can read as competence.

**Ethics.** Meera chose the goal and knows exactly which behaviours are being practised and reinforced, so on Bandura's argument this is a **contract between consenting individuals**, and success will **increase her freedom** (walking to the metro, taking the promotion).

**Interview follow-up.** *"Would Bandura say her mother caused her phobia?"* Not in a one-way sense. The mother modelled the fear, but Meera's own **cognitions** (her interpretation of arousal, her low efficacy expectations) and her **choices** (avoidance) have maintained it, and by changing her behaviour she can change both her beliefs and her environment. That is reciprocal determinism, and it is why Bandura's view is optimistic: she is neither a victim of her childhood nor free to wish the fear away; she can learn her way out of it.

## MCQ traps

1. **Bandura vs Skinner**: both study **overt behaviour** and **learning** in the laboratory; Bandura adds **cognitive mediation**, studies **humans in social settings**, and says **models**, not reinforcers, control behaviour.
2. Schultz's part name is **The Social-Learning Approach**; Bandura is **not** in the Behavioural Approach (Skinner) or the **Limited-Domain Approach** (Rotter, Zuckerman, Seligman).
3. The approach is also called **cognitive-behavioural**; Bandura later used **social cognitive theory** (classic background: 1986).
4. **Observational learning** = learning new responses by watching others; **vicarious reinforcement** = learning from the **consequences to others**.
5. Reinforcement **facilitates** but is **not necessary** for learning; it can be **external, vicarious or self-administered**.
6. Bobo doll was about **4 feet** tall; participants were **preschool** children; **Bandura, Ross & Ross (1963)**.
7. Experimental children were **twice as aggressive** as controls; aggression appeared with **live, televised and cartoon** models.
8. Yet imitation **decreased as similarity decreased** (live child, filmed child, cartoon); even the cartoon beat **no model**.
9. **Bandura (1965)**: model **rewarded** (praise, soda, candy) vs **punished** (verbal and physical); punished-model group showed **significantly less** aggression.
10. Classic background: in the 1965 study incentives later **abolished** the group differences, showing consequences affect **performance, not acquisition**.
11. Three 1963 studies: **Bandura, Ross & Ross** (Bobo media), **Bandura & Walters** (parents of aggressive vs inhibited children), **Walters, Bowen & Parke** (eye-movement disinhibition).
12. **Walters, Bowen & Parke (1963)**: participants were **male** undergraduates; the model was a **spot of light** said to be a previous viewer's eye movements; modelling changed **perception**.
13. **Disinhibition** = weakening of restraints by a model (riots, trolling); it is **not** acquisition of a new response.
14. Trolls among frequent gamers were **younger and male**; motives were **revenge, boredom, amusement** (Thacker & Griffiths, 2012).
15. Disinhibition is **strong at 18 to 19**, **greatly reduced at 22 to 25** (Vaidya et al., 2010).
16. Three factors in modelling: **model characteristics, observer characteristics, reward consequences**; consequences can **override** the other two.
17. **Same sex**, **same age**, **similar**, **high status** models are imitated more; **complex** behaviour less readily; **aggressive** behaviour strongly.
18. Jaywalking follows a **well-dressed** model more than a poorly dressed one.
19. Infants of **16 months** imitate rake use **only after** seeing its purpose (Esseilly et al., 2013).
20. Canadian **9th and 10th graders** gained weight in schools with **overweight older students** (Leatherdale & Papadakis, 2011).
21. Observers **low** in self-confidence and self-esteem imitate **more**.
22. Infants: **immediate** imitation only; delayed imitation from about **age 2**.
23. Reinforcers by age: **physical** (food, affection, punishment), then **approval/disapproval**, then **self-administered**.
24. Four processes in order: **attentional, retention, production, incentive and motivational**; older names: **motor reproduction**, **motivational**.
25. Retention uses **imaginal** and **verbal** representational systems; production = **practice plus feedback**.
26. Bandura's **self** is a set of cognitive processes and structures, **not a psychic agent**.
27. **Self-reinforcement** resembles the **superego/conscience**, but Bandura **denied** they are the same.
28. Self-efficacy = feelings of **adequacy, efficiency and competence** in coping with life; "the power of believing you can" is **Maddux (2002)**.
29. Four sources: **performance attainment (most influential)**, vicarious experiences, **verbal persuasion (may be most common)**, physiological and emotional arousal.
30. Arousal is **inverse**: more fear and tension, **lower** self-efficacy.
31. **Schneewind (1995)**: high-efficacy **men** had **warm fathers** and demanding mothers; high-efficacy **women** had **fathers pressing for achievement**.
32. Bandura, like **Adler**, stressed **birth order**; **same-sex siblings** compete more; **ability grouping** and **grading on a curve** harm low achievers.
33. Adulthood has **two** periods: **young adulthood** and the **middle years**; self-efficacy is the **single most important factor** across the life span.
34. Three therapies: **modelling, guided participation, covert modelling** (covert = **imagined model**).
35. Dogs: **Bandura, Grusec & Menlove (1967)**, **children**, same-age **child model**, playpen. Snakes: **Bandura, Blanchard & Ritter (1969)**, **adults**, film of children, adolescents and adults; clients could **stop the film**.
36. Guided participation: client wears **gloves**, touches the **middle** of the snake while the model holds **head and tail**.
37. **Melamed & Siegel (1975)**: hospital film vs **trip** film; less anxiety and **fewer post-hospital behaviour problems**.
38. **Allen, Danforth & Drabman (1989)**: **more than 80 percent** initially refused or quit the procedure.
39. **Sarason (1975)**: three film conditions; **nonsense syllables**; **high-anxiety** students helped most by the **coping** model.
40. Modelling therapy effective for **phobias, OCD and sexual dysfunction**; effects **last for years**.
41. Ethics: awareness, client choice ("contract between two consenting individuals", not master and puppet), **increased freedom**.
42. Reciprocal determinism quote: **Bandura (1977, p. vii)**; people are neither powerless objects nor free agents.
43. Assessment: **no** free association, dream analysis or projective tests; **yes** to observation, self-report, physiological measures, behavioural-avoidance self-ratings.
44. Self-efficacy: **men higher**; gender gap peaks in the **20s**; self-efficacy peaks in **middle age**, declines after **60** (Gecas, 1989; Lachman, 1985).
45. Dutch adults average age **66**: memory beliefs predicted memory **6 years** later (Valentijn et al., 2006); **100+ studies** (Beaudoin & Desrichard, 2011).
46. **Andreoletti et al. (2001)**: ages **25 to 76**; appearance outweighed **self-esteem and health** for control beliefs; baby-faceness low control in young/middle adulthood but **higher** in older adults; stronger in **women**.
47. East vs West Germany, grades **2 to 6**, before **1990** reunification: **East (collectivist) lower** (Oettingen & Maier, 1999).
48. **Hackett (1995)**: men high self-efficacy for **both** male and female jobs; women **low** for male jobs; **equal** test abilities.
49. **Stajkovic & Luthans (1998)**: **114** studies, **21,600+** participants; **Judge et al. (2007)**: better predictor for **low-complexity** jobs.
50. **Manning & Wright (1983)**: childbirth pain; China **15,000+** patients (Jackson et al., 2014); low back pain: **three-week** programme, **six-month** follow-up (Altmaier et al., 1993).
51. **Watson, Chemers & Preiser (2001)**: **college basketball** collective efficacy from competent leaders and last season's wins.
52. **Phillips**: riots peak **3 to 4 days** (1985); boxing, murders up **more than 12 percent** for **3 days**, over **15 years** (1983); celebrity suicides (1974).
53. **Huesmann et al. (2003)**: TV violence at ages **6 to 10** predicted aggression in the **20s**.
54. Internet: Taiwan 8th graders **no overall gender difference** (girls communicating, boys exploring); US men higher **computer** self-efficacy; high **social** self-efficacy, **more** Internet addiction (Turkey).
55. Criticism "lacks empirical support" is **false**; real criticisms: neglects **emotion and conflict**, treats **symptoms**, vague on **how cognition** affects behaviour.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Social-learning approach** | Schultz's part heading for Bandura: behaviourist in method, but with cognition mediating learning in social settings |
| **Cognitive-behavioural** | Alternative label for Bandura's approach, recognising its use of cognitive variables |
| **Social cognitive theory** | Bandura's later name for his theory (classic background: 1986) |
| **Observational learning** | Acquiring new responses by watching how other people behave |
| **Vicarious reinforcement** | Learning or strengthening a behaviour by seeing its consequences for someone else, without experiencing them oneself |
| **Modelling** (glossary sense) | A behaviour-modification technique in which a person watches models and joins them in performing the desired behaviour |
| **Model** | The person (live, filmed, cartoon or described) whose behaviour is observed and copied |
| **Verbal modelling** | Inducing behaviour by describing it fully in words, as in instructions |
| **Bobo doll** | Inflatable figure about 4 feet tall used to show children's imitation of aggression |
| **Disinhibition** | The weakening of inhibitions or restraints through watching a model |
| **Trolling** | Posting inflammatory, derogatory or hateful messages online; a form of disinhibition aided by anonymity |
| **Attentional processes** | Noticing the model and perceiving the behaviour accurately enough to imitate it |
| **Retention processes** | Encoding and remembering the observed behaviour in images and words for later use |
| **Imaginal representational system** | Storing a model's behaviour as vivid, retrievable mental pictures |
| **Verbal representational system** | Storing a model's behaviour as silent verbal descriptions that can be rehearsed |
| **Production processes** | Turning stored representations into one's own overt behaviour through practice and feedback |
| **Incentive and motivational processes** | Expecting that performing the behaviour will bring the consequences the model received, which moves learning into action |
| **Self** (Bandura) | A set of cognitive processes and structures for thought and perception, not an agent that causes behaviour |
| **Self-reinforcement** | Rewarding or punishing oneself for meeting, exceeding or falling short of one's own standards |
| **Internal standards of performance** | Personal reference points against which one judges one's own behaviour; raised after success, lowered after failure |
| **Self-efficacy** | One's sense of adequacy, efficiency and competence in coping with life |
| **Performance attainment** | Information from one's own past successes and failures; the most influential source of self-efficacy |
| **Vicarious experiences** | Seeing similar others succeed (or fail), which raises (or lowers) self-efficacy |
| **Verbal persuasion** | Being told one has the ability to succeed; possibly the most common source; must be realistic |
| **Physiological and emotional arousal** | Bodily and emotional state used to judge coping ability; calm raises, tension lowers self-efficacy |
| **Collective efficacy** | A group's shared belief that, working together, it can overcome obstacles and reach its goals |
| **Parental self-efficacy** | Parents' belief that they are effective parents; predicts their children's self-efficacy |
| **Memory self-efficacy** | Belief about how good one's own memory is; affects later memory performance |
| **Baby-faceness** | Round face, large eyes, small nose bridge and small chin; linked to low control beliefs in young and middle adulthood |
| **Internet (computer) self-efficacy** | Confidence in one's ability to use the Internet or computers effectively |
| **Social self-efficacy** | Confidence in starting social contacts and making friends; linked to Internet addiction in one study |
| **Cyberbullying** | Online aggression directed at others, more common among the highly disinhibited and unmonitored |
| **Behaviour modification** | Changing undesirable learned behaviour directly, without treating presumed underlying causes |
| **Guided participation** | Watching a live model and then performing the feared behaviour together with the model |
| **Covert modelling** | Imagining a model coping with a feared situation, with no model actually seen |
| **Behavioural-avoidance test** | Graded series of approach tasks; clients rate how many they expect to complete (self-efficacy) |
| **Coping model** | A model who shows some initial anxiety and then copes, as in the hospital film of Melamed and Siegel |
| **Reciprocal determinism** | Behaviour is controlled both by the person, through cognitive processes, and by the environment, through external social events |
| **Triadic reciprocal determinism** | Classic background: behaviour, personal factors and environment all influence one another |
| **Symptom substitution** | Critics' claim that treating only a symptom lets another symptom replace it |
