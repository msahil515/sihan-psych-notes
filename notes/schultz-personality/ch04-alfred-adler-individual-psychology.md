# Chapter 4: Alfred Adler: Individual Psychology

**What this chapter is really about.** Freud gave psychology a person who is pushed from behind: by sexual and aggressive instincts, by an unconscious he cannot see, and by a childhood that fixed his character before he could argue with it. Alfred Adler, who sat in Freud's own discussion circle for nine years, ended up drawing almost the opposite picture. In Adler's account a person is pulled from in front, by goals and ideals he has set for himself; he lives first of all among other people rather than inside a body full of drives; he is mostly aware of what he is doing; and he is the author, not the victim, of his own character. The engine of the whole system is a single, homely observation. Every human being starts life small, weak and dependent on bigger people, and so every human being starts life feeling inferior. Personality, for Adler, is simply the particular route each of us invents to climb out of that position. The way the climb goes well (compensation, striving for perfection, social interest) and the ways it goes wrong (the inferiority complex, the superiority complex, the self-serving styles of life) are the content of the chapter.

Schultz and Schultz place Adler in the part of the book they call **the neo-psychoanalytic approach**, between Carl Jung (Chapter 3) and Karen Horney (Chapter 5). The label tells you how to hold all three. Each began inside Freud's movement, each kept some psychoanalytic habits (case study of patients, interest in childhood, attention to dreams), and each broke away over Freud's claim that sexuality and biological instinct sit at the root of personality. Jung kept the unconscious and made it bigger. Adler and Horney moved the centre of gravity outward, into the family and society. If you remember Adler as "the social one" and "the future-goals one" of the early dissenters, most comparison questions answer themselves.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 4.1 (Adler's life) is not decoration: nearly every idea in 4.2 to 4.6 can be traced to an event in it, and Indian papers ask exactly that. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 give you the motivational core (inferiority, compensation, striving for superiority, fictional finalism); learn the three-way distinction between inferiority feelings, the inferiority complex and the superiority complex before going further. Section 4.4 shows how the striving is packaged into a style of life and why Adler insisted the person creates it. Sections 4.5 and 4.6 give the two social forces Adler cared most about, social interest and birth order. Section 4.7 places him on Schultz's six human-nature issues. Sections 4.8 to 4.10 cover how he assessed people, what research has done with his ideas (a long and very testable section, especially on birth order), and how the theory has been judged.

## 4.1 The Life of Adler (1870 to 1937)

Schultz opens with a comparison worth keeping in your head. Freud and Adler were only **14 years apart in age**, grew up in the **same city** (Vienna) in the same era, and trained as physicians at the **same university**. Yet they produced two theories of human nature that disagree on almost every basic question. Schultz's explanation, as with Freud, is biographical: each man's childhood seems to have shaped what he later believed about everybody's childhood.

### Childhood and Adolescence

Adler's early years were dominated by three things: **illness**, **closeness to death**, and **jealousy of his older brother**.

The illness was **rickets**, a deficiency of vitamin D in which the bones soften. It kept the small boy on the sidelines while other children played. Adler later described sitting bandaged on a bench, watching his healthy elder brother run and jump with ease while every movement of his own cost him effort. If you want one image to carry into the exam hall, it is that bench: a child who is physically less capable than the people around him, watching, comparing, and feeling the gap.

The closeness to death came twice. When Adler was **3**, a **younger brother died in the bed next to his**. At **4**, Adler himself nearly died of **pneumonia**, and he overheard the doctor telling his father that the boy was lost (the episode is reported by Orgler, 1963). Adler survived and, by his own account, decided then that he would become a doctor. Hold on to this detail; it reappears in 4.3 as Adler's own "fictional" life goal, the goal of defeating death.

His relations with his parents were the reverse of Freud's. Adler was **pampered by his mother at first**, but at about **age 2** he was **dethroned** by the arrival of another baby, and some biographers think his mother turned away from him afterwards. He was, clearly, **his father's favourite**. Freud, by contrast, had been closer to his mother than to his father. As an adult Adler saw no use for the **Oedipus complex**: it simply did not match the family he had grown up in.

Towards his **older brother** Adler felt lifelong jealousy. The brother was strong and healthy and could do everything Alfred could not. Adler felt inferior to him and to the other children in the neighbourhood, who all seemed fitter. His response is the template for his whole theory. Rather than withdraw, he **forced himself into games and sports**, despite being small, clumsy and plain (all legacies of the illness). Slowly he won **self-esteem and acceptance** from his peers, and with it a liking for company that stayed with him for life. In the theory this shows up as an insistence that **peers and siblings** (children inside and outside the family) matter far more for personality than Freud ever allowed.

School followed the same arc. Adler attended the same school Freud had attended, was unhappy there, and was at first only a **mediocre** student. One teacher thought so little of him that he advised Adler's father to take the boy out and **apprentice him to a shoemaker**, which terrified Alfred. Mathematics was his worst subject. He kept at it and, by sheer effort, went from **failing to the top of his class**.

Schultz's comment is that the childhood reads like a tragedy and also like a worked example of the theory: a weak, sickly, overshadowed child who turns inferiority into achievement. Adler himself said that anyone who knew his life would see how closely his ideas matched the facts of his childhood (quoted in Bottome, 1939).

> **Trap.** Two biographical details are built for MCQs. First, Adler's **older brother was named Sigmund**. A question that says Adler "competed all his life with Sigmund" may be about the brother, not Freud. Second, Adler was **pampered by his mother, then dethroned at 2, then his father's favourite**. That is why he rejected the Oedipus complex; Freud was the one who was closer to his mother.

### Adulthood

Adler carried out his childhood resolution and studied **medicine at the University of Vienna**, again finishing with an unremarkable record. His career moved through three stages that are often asked in sequence:

1. **Ophthalmology** (eye medicine) in private practice;
2. **General medicine**, where he took an interest in incurable illnesses but was so upset by his helplessness against death, especially in young patients, that he gave it up;
3. **Neurology and psychiatry**, his final specialty.

Notice how the middle step echoes the pneumonia episode: the boy who decided to defeat death could not bear to watch it win.

### Relations with Freud

Adler's association with Freud lasted about **nine years** and began in **1902**, when Freud invited Adler and **three others** to meet weekly at Freud's home to discuss psychoanalysis. (Classic background: this group is usually called the **Wednesday Psychological Society**, the forerunner of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society.) The two men were never close, but Freud at first admired Adler as a physician who won his patients' trust.

The single most examined fact about this period: **Adler was never Freud's student or disciple, and Freud never psychoanalysed him.** He joined as a colleague. One of Freud's associates claimed that Adler lacked the ability to probe the unconscious and analyse people. Schultz raises the possibility, as speculation only, that this weakness is why Adler built a theory around **consciousness**, which is easier to reach, and played down the unconscious.

By **1910** Adler held two senior posts, **president of the Vienna Psychoanalytical Society** and **co-editor of its journal**, while openly criticising Freudian theory. Soon afterwards he cut every tie with psychoanalysis and set out on his own. (Counting nine years from 1902 puts the break at about 1911.)

The parting was ugly on both sides. Freud mocked Adler's height (Adler was about **5 inches shorter** than Freud) and described him in a string of insults: abnormal, paranoid, jealous, sadistic, consumed by ambition; he also dismissed the theory as worthless (Fiebert, 1997; Gay, 1988; Wittels, 1924). Adler gave as good as he got, calling Freud a swindler and psychoanalysis filth (Roazen, 1975), and he became furious whenever anyone introduced him as Freud's pupil. Schultz adds an irony: in later life Adler treated people who defected from *his* school with the same bitterness Freud had shown him, and he could flare into anger when his authority was questioned (Hoffman, 1994).

> **Trap.** "Adler was a disciple of Freud who was trained and analysed by him" is false on both counts. Adler was an early **colleague** and member of Freud's circle, rose to **president** of the society by 1910, and broke away. Jung, by contrast, was the one Freud openly called his heir (Chapter 3). Do not merge the two stories.

### Becoming a Celebrity in America

The rest of Adler's career is a sequence of dates:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1912** | Founds the **Society for Individual Psychology** |
| **1914 to 1918** | Serves in the **Austrian army** in World War I |
| After the war | Organises **government-sponsored child-counselling clinics** in Vienna, where he introduces **group training and guidance**, forerunners of modern **group therapy** |
| **1926** | First of several visits to the **United States**; teaching and very popular lecture tours |
| **1929** | Moves to **New York City** |
| **1937** | Dies of a **heart attack in Scotland** during an exhausting **56-lecture** European tour |

In America Adler's warmth, optimism and sociability, combined with a fierce ambition, made him a recognised expert very quickly (Hoffman, 1994). His books and lectures reached a national audience, and Schultz calls him **America's first popular psychologist**, a celebrity of his day. (Classic background: the city in Scotland was **Aberdeen**.)

The child-guidance clinics deserve a second look, because they connect the life to the later reputation. Adler was working with children, teachers and families in groups, in public, decades before group therapy became a mainstream technique. When Section 4.10 calls him a forerunner of group therapy and social psychology, this is the evidence.

### How the life fed the theory

Indian interview panels like to ask "what in Adler's life shaped his theory?" The book makes the links in several places; here they are together.

| Life event | Idea it fed |
|---|---|
| Rickets, physical weakness, watching a healthy brother | **Inferiority feelings**, **organic inferiority**, **compensation** |
| Forcing himself into games until accepted | Compensation succeeding; **peer group** importance |
| Pampered, then dethroned at 2 by a new baby | **Dethronement**; the dangers of **pampering** |
| Father's favourite, not close to mother | Rejection of the **Oedipus complex** |
| Second-born competing with older brother Sigmund | **Second-born** profile: competitive, ambitious |
| Near death at 4; vow to become a physician | **Fictional finalism** (his own goal: to conquer death) |
| From failing maths to top of the class | Striving and effort overcome inferiority |
| Early power struggles in Freud's circle, later mellowing | Shift from **power/masculine protest** to **social interest** |
| Fear of the cemetery path (a memory later found to be false) | **Early recollections** reveal the style of life |
| Ship-sinking dream before the first US trip | Dreams as rehearsal for **current problems** |

## 4.2 Inferiority Feelings: The Source of All Human Striving

### Inferiority feelings and compensation

Adler's starting point is that feeling inferior is not a symptom. It is the **normal condition of every human being**, and it is what drives us forward. In one of his best-known lines, he wrote that being human means feeling oneself inferior (Adler, 1933/1939). Because everyone shares it, an inferiority feeling is **not** a sign of weakness or abnormality.

What we do about the feeling is called **compensation**: the effort to overcome inferiorities, whether they are real or only imagined, and in doing so to reach higher levels of development. Adler's claim is strong. Inferiority feelings are the source of **all** human striving; every bit of growth, from a toddler learning to walk to an adult earning a degree, is compensation for some felt shortfall. The striving never stops, because each level reached reveals the next one above it.

Where does the feeling come from? From **infancy**. A baby is tiny, helpless and wholly dependent on adults. Adler believed infants sense how much bigger and stronger their parents are and how powerless they are to resist that power, so they come to feel inferior to the large people around them.

Two consequences of this explanation are exam favourites:

1. **Inferiority feelings are environmental, not inherited.** Everyone has them, but not because of genes. They arise because the situation of infancy (helplessness and dependence on adults) is the same for every child. Universal does not mean innate.
2. **They are necessary, not merely inevitable.** Without the discomfort of feeling small, there would be no motive to grow.

A simple illustration: a seven-year-old in a cricket-mad family who is the worst batter among her cousins does not, on Adler's view, have a problem. She has a motive. If she spends the summer practising against a wall until she can hold her own, that is compensation working exactly as it should.

> **Trap.** "Adler held that inferiority feelings are a genetically determined weakness found in neurotics" is wrong three ways. They are **environmental** (a product of the universal helplessness of infancy), **normal** (found in everyone), and the **source of healthy striving**. What Adler did treat as partly innate are the **potential for social interest** and the **striving for perfection**, which come later in the chapter.

### The Inferiority Complex

Now take the case where compensation fails. Suppose a child does not outgrow the helpless position, cannot overcome the feeling of being smaller and less able, and the feeling, instead of fading as she grows, gets **stronger**. Adler called the result an **inferiority complex**: a condition that develops when a person **cannot compensate for normal inferiority feelings**. People with an inferiority complex think poorly of themselves and feel helpless and unequal to the demands of life. Adler found such a complex in the childhood histories of many adults who came to him as patients.

The distinction from ordinary inferiority feelings is the core of the module. The feeling is universal and useful. The complex is what you get when the feeling is **not compensated** and so **intensifies**. In our cricket example, the girl who gives up batting, stops playing altogether, and begins to describe herself as "useless at everything" is moving from the feeling to the complex.

### Causes of Inferiority Complexes

Adler named **three childhood sources** of an inferiority complex: **organic inferiority**, **spoiling** and **neglect**. Notice that two of them are opposites (too much attention and too little); the third is about the body.

#### Organic Inferiority

Adler argued that **defective organs or parts of the body shape personality**, because the person's efforts to make up for the defect organise much of what he or she does. This is Adler's own story: a child weakened by rickets who pushed himself into sport. A physically weak child might fix on the weakness and work until it becomes a strength.

History supplies the book's two examples:

- **Demosthenes**, the ancient Greek statesman, overcame a **stutter** to become a great orator.
- **Theodore Roosevelt**, the sickly child who became the **26th president of the United States**, grew into a model of physical fitness.

Adler also saw such cases in his own practice. His Vienna office was near an **amusement park**, and among his patients were **circus performers and gymnasts**, many of whom had built their remarkable physical skills by working hard to overcome a childhood disability.

The same mechanism has two possible endings. Successful efforts to compensate for an organic weakness can lead to outstanding artistic, athletic or social achievement; failed efforts can produce an **inferiority complex**.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition says **organic inferiority**. Classic background: older textbooks and older exam keys usually say **organ inferiority**, after Adler's early monograph on the inferiority of organs and their psychological compensation (usually dated 1907). They are the same idea; do not reject an option because it uses the older term.

#### Spoiling

**Spoiling** (pampering) produces an inferiority complex by a less obvious route. The spoiled child is the centre of the household; every wish is granted and very little is refused. Such a child reasonably concludes that he is the most important person in any room and that others should give way to him.

The crash comes at **school**, where he is suddenly one child among many and nobody is organising the day around him. He arrives unprepared. He has little feeling for other people and little patience with them; he has never had to wait for anything, never had to work through a difficulty, never had to fit in with anyone else's needs. When obstacles finally block him, he concludes that something must be wrong with **him**, some personal deficiency, and that conclusion is the inferiority complex.

The point to understand is that pampering removes the very experiences (frustration, waiting, effort, compromise) through which children learn to compensate. The pampered child has not been protected from inferiority; he has been deprived of the tools for dealing with it.

#### Neglect

The third source is the easiest to grasp. Children who are **neglected, unwanted or rejected** grow up without love or security because their parents are indifferent or hostile. They come to feel **worthless**, sometimes **angry**, and they view other people with **distrust**. Schultz notes that neglect is now treated as a **major form of child abuse** (Hickman, 2009).

Keep the three sources together as a set; papers ask for "the three causes of an inferiority complex according to Adler":

| Source | What happens to the child | Why a complex results |
|---|---|---|
| **Organic inferiority** | A real bodily defect or weakness | Compensation is attempted; if it fails, the complex forms |
| **Spoiling / pampering** | Every wish met; centre of attention | Never learns to cope; at school, blames self for obstacles |
| **Neglect** | Unloved, rejected, insecure | Feels worthless and distrustful of others |

### The Superiority Complex

Whatever caused the inferiority complex, a person may try to escape it by going too far in the other direction. Adler called this **overcompensation**, and its product the **superiority complex**: an **exaggerated opinion of one's own abilities and accomplishments**.

Schultz describes two forms:

1. The person feels secretly self-satisfied and superior and feels **no need to prove it** with real achievement.
2. The person **does** feel the need to prove it and works extremely hard to become highly successful.

Either way, the outward signs are the same: **boasting, vanity, self-centredness, and a habit of running other people down**. Clinically, think of the colleague who cannot let anyone else's success pass without belittling it, or the student who announces that the examination is beneath him. Underneath, on Adler's reading, is an uncompensated inferiority that has been covered rather than overcome.

> **Trap.** Three terms, three different things. **Inferiority feelings**: normal, universal, the source of growth. **Inferiority complex**: what happens when a person **cannot compensate** (helplessness, poor self-opinion). **Superiority complex**: what happens when a person **overcompensates** (boasting, vanity, belittling others). The superiority complex is a disguised inferiority problem, not the opposite of one.

## 4.3 Striving for Superiority, or Perfection

If inferiority feelings supply the push, what are we pushing towards? Is the aim merely to stop feeling inferior? Adler said no: there is a positive goal beyond relief. But his account of that goal **changed over his career**, and the change is itself examinable.

### From the masculine protest to the striving for perfection

In his **early** formulation, Adler linked inferiority with a general sense of **weakness, or femininity**, reflecting the low status of women in the society of his day. He called the effort to compensate for that feeling the **masculine protest**. The aim of the compensation was a **will or drive toward power**, in which **aggression** (regarded then as a masculine quality) played a large role.

**Later** Adler dropped the equation of inferiority with femininity and replaced the drive for power with something broader: a **striving for superiority, or perfection**.

> **Trap.** The **masculine protest** is Adler's **early** idea, not his mature theory, and he **abandoned** it. A question that offers "masculine protest" as Adler's final account of human motivation is wrong; the final account is **striving for superiority (perfection)**, later coloured by **social interest**. Classic background: the early "will to power" language is often linked to Nietzsche; Schultz does not make that link.

### What "superiority" means

Adler called the striving for superiority **the fundamental fact of life** (Adler, 1930) and treated superiority as the **ultimate goal** of human effort. He was emphatic that the word was not to be taken in its ordinary sense. Striving for superiority is **not**:

- trying to be better than everybody else;
- an arrogant or domineering attitude;
- an inflated opinion of one's own abilities (that is the superiority **complex**).

What he meant was a **drive for perfection**. Schultz points to the root of the word: "perfection" comes from a Latin word meaning to **complete** or **finish**. To strive for superiority, then, is to strive to make oneself **complete or whole**, to finish becoming what one could be. A medical student who works towards competence, a widow who learns to manage the household accounts her husband used to keep, a patient recovering from a stroke who relearns to write: all are striving for superiority in Adler's sense, and none of them need be trying to beat anyone.

This goal is **innate** and it points to the **future**. Here is the sharpest contrast with Freud. Freud explained behaviour by the **past** (instincts and childhood experiences pushing from behind). Adler explained it by **expectations and aspirations** (goals pulling from in front), and he argued that instincts and primitive impulses could not by themselves explain personality. Only the final goal of superiority or perfection could do that. (Classic background: textbooks often call this a **teleological** explanation, explanation by purpose or end, as opposed to Freud's **causal** one.)

### Fictional Finalism

Adler used the word **finalism** for the idea that we have an ultimate goal, a final state of being, and a need to move towards it. The goals we move towards, however, are **potentialities, not actualities**. They exist in us **subjectively**, as ideals, and they cannot be checked against reality. In that sense they are **fictional**: imagined, not false. Belief that all people are created equal, or that people are basically good, are examples of such guiding ideals; nobody can prove them, yet people organise their lives around them.

The book's illustration is belief in an **afterlife**. If someone believes that behaving well will be rewarded in heaven, that belief shapes how they behave and how they treat others. It is not based on objective reality, but for the believer it is entirely real, and it works.

Adler's own life supplies the personal example. His life goal was to **conquer death**, the death he had nearly met with pneumonia at 4. His way of pursuing that goal was to **become a physician** (Hoffman, 1994). The goal is fictional in a precise sense: death cannot finally be beaten, yet the goal organised his whole career.

Adler formalised the concept as **fictional finalism**: the idea that **imagined or potential goals guide our behaviour** as we strive towards a complete or whole state of being. We are steered by many such fictions, but the most **pervasive** is the ideal of **perfection**, and Adler suggested that the best formulation of that ideal humanity has produced so far is the concept of **God**.

One terminological point is classic MCQ material. Adler himself preferred the terms **subjective final goal** or **guiding self-ideal**, but the concept is still generally known as **fictional finalism** (Watts & Holden, 1994).

> **Trap.** "Fictional" does **not** mean "false" or "pathological". It means **subjective and untestable**: an ideal that cannot be checked against reality but still guides conduct. Adler's preferred names were **subjective final goal** and **guiding self-ideal**. Classic background: Adler borrowed the "fiction" idea from the philosopher **Hans Vaihinger**, whose *Philosophy of "As If"* (1911) argued that people live by useful fictions; older texts and Hall and Lindzey mention this source, Schultz does not.

### Two more features of the striving

Adler made two further claims about striving for superiority, and both mark him off from Freud.

**First, striving increases tension rather than reducing it.** Freud's model of motivation was tension-reduction: instincts build up tension and behaviour discharges it, aiming at a quiet state. Adler rejected the idea that reducing tension is our only motive. Striving for perfection costs enormous energy and effort; it is the opposite of resting in equilibrium. Keep this in mind for Section 4.7, where it makes Adler a **growth** theorist.

**Second, striving operates at the level of society as well as the individual.** Because we are social beings, we strive for perfection not only as individuals but as members of groups; we try to perfect our **culture**. Individuals and society are interdependent, and people must work constructively with others for the common good. This is the seed of **social interest** (Section 4.5).

So human beings are always moving towards an imagined ideal of perfection. The obvious next question is how, in daily life, any particular person pursues it. Adler's answer is the **style of life**.

## 4.4 The Style of Life

### What a style of life is

Everyone, Adler said, has the same ultimate goal, superiority or perfection, but no two people pursue it the same way. Each of us develops a **unique pattern of characteristics, behaviours and habits** for the purpose, which Adler called a **distinctive character**, or **style of life** (lifestyle). The glossary version: a unique character structure or pattern of personal behaviours and characteristics through which each of us strives for perfection.

To see how a style of life forms, go back to inferiority and compensation. The infant feels inferior and tries to compensate for being helpless and dependent. In trying, the child picks up a set of behaviours. A sickly child, for example, might set out to build physical strength by running or lifting weights. Those behaviours become part of the style of life, which is therefore, at root, **a pattern of behaviour designed to compensate for inferiority**.

Once formed, the style of life runs everything:

- It shapes **everything we do**.
- It determines which parts of the environment we **notice** and which we **ignore**.
- It determines the **attitudes** we hold.

It is **learned through social interactions**, and it is **firmly crystallised by the age of 4 or 5**, after which it is hard to change. From then on it is the framework for all later behaviour. Its content depends on social factors, above all the person's **order of birth** in the family and the nature of the **parent-child relationship**.

Schultz links this back to neglect. A neglected child may feel unable to meet the demands of life and so become distrustful and hostile towards others. That child's style of life may then revolve around **seeking revenge, resenting other people's success, and taking whatever he feels is owed to him**. You can see the whole Adlerian chain in one person: a social situation (neglect) produces a felt inferiority, the child invents a way of compensating (getting even, grabbing), and that invention hardens into a lifelong style.

> **Trap.** The style of life is fixed by **age 4 or 5**, and that is exactly why Adler's **early recollections** are drawn from the **first 4 or 5 years**. What is fixed is a self-created strategy for striving, not a structure built from instinctual conflict.

### The Creative Power of the Self

A careful reader will spot an apparent contradiction. Adler said we control our fate and are not its victims, and his theory was advertised as less deterministic than Freud's. Yet now the style of life is laid down by early social relationships and barely changes after age 5. That sounds almost as deterministic as Freud's insistence that the adult is made in early childhood.

Adler's way out is a concept he called **the creative power of the self**: the **ability to create an appropriate style of life**.

His argument runs as follows. We **create our selves**: our personality, our character (Adler used these words interchangeably with style of life). We are **not passively shaped** by childhood experience. What matters is not the experiences themselves but our **conscious attitude towards them**. Neither **heredity** nor **environment** fully explains personality; what explains it is the way each of us **perceives and interprets** those influences and uses them as building material for an attitude towards life.

So Adler assumed **individual free will**. Each of us takes the abilities and experiences we have been handed, by our genes and by our social surroundings, and from them builds a style of life. The materials are given; the building is ours. Schultz notes that Adler was **unclear on the specifics** of how this happens, but he was insistent that the style of life is **chosen, not imposed**. The catch is in the last step: once created, the style of life **stays constant** throughout life.

A concrete contrast makes the idea clearer. Two brothers grow up with the same alcoholic and violent father. One becomes a frightened, compliant adult who avoids conflict at any cost; the other becomes a social worker who runs a de-addiction programme. Freud would look for differences in their instinctual development. Adler would say the brothers took the **same material** and **interpreted it differently**: one concluded "the world is dangerous and I must keep my head down", the other "nobody should have to live like that, and I can do something about it". The difference is the creative self at work.

> **Trap.** The creative power of the self is Adler's answer to the charge of **determinism**: early experiences provide materials, but the person **actively constructs** the style of life from them. It is **not** a claim that childhood does not matter, and it does **not** make the style of life easy to change later (once built, it is stable). It is also one of the targets of later criticism, because Adler never explained **how** a child under 5 makes such momentous choices (Section 4.10).

### Four Basic Styles of Life

Adler grouped the universal problems of life into **three categories**:

1. problems of our **behaviour towards others**;
2. problems of **occupation**;
3. problems of **love**.

(Classic background: Adlerians usually call these the three **life tasks**, often labelled society or friendship, work, and love.)

He then proposed **four basic styles of life** for meeting these problems:

| Style of life | Core attitude | What it looks like | Healthy? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dominant (ruling) type** | Rules others with little social awareness; disregards other people | Extreme forms attack others: **sadists, delinquents, sociopaths**. Milder forms attack themselves: **alcoholics, drug addicts, suicides**, believing they hurt others by hurting themselves | No |
| **Getting type** | Expects to receive satisfaction from others | Becomes **dependent** on others; Adler thought this the **most common** human type | No |
| **Avoiding type** | Makes no attempt to face life's problems | Avoids difficulties so as to avoid any chance of **failure** | No |
| **Socially useful type** | Cooperates with others and acts in line with their needs | Copes with problems within a well-developed framework of **social interest** | **Yes** |

The first three have something in common. None is equipped to cope with ordinary life, because none can **cooperate** with other people. The clash between their style of life and the real world produces abnormal behaviour, which shows up as **neuroses and psychoses**. What they all lack, Adler came to say, is **social interest**. Only the socially useful type has it.

Notice the interesting placement of addiction and suicide. Most students file alcoholism under "avoiding". Adler filed the alcoholic, the addict and the suicidal person under the **dominant** type, in its less virulent form, because he read their self-destruction as an indirect attack on others.

> **Trap.** The **getting** type, not the avoiding type, is the **most common** in Adler's view. Alcoholics, addicts and suicides belong to the milder form of the **dominant** type. The chapter summary also calls the dominant type the **ruling** type; both names are correct. Only the **socially useful** type is healthy, and the defining difference is **social interest**. Classic background: some textbooks arrange the four types on two dimensions, degree of **activity** and degree of **social interest** (ruling: high activity, low social interest; getting and avoiding: low activity, low social interest; socially useful: high on both); Schultz does not present this grid.

## 4.5 Social Interest

### What social interest is

For Adler, **getting along with others is the first task life sets us**. How well we manage it becomes part of our style of life, and so it affects how well we handle every other problem. He called the relevant capacity **social interest**: the individual's **innate potential to cooperate with other people in order to achieve personal and societal goals**.

Adler's German word was **Gemeinschaftsgefühl** (often printed without the umlaut), which is best translated as **"community feeling"** (Stepansky, 1983). "Social interest" is simply the term that stuck in English.

Read the definition carefully, because it combines a biological and a social element:

- The **potential** for social interest is **innate**. In that limited sense Adler's theory has a biological component, even though he held that social forces influence us more than biological ones.
- How far the potential **develops** depends on **early social experience**.

Adler's reasoning for why social interest matters is practical and historical. Nobody can avoid other people or escape obligations to them. From the earliest times human beings have lived in families, tribes and nations, because communities are essential for protection and survival. Cooperation has therefore always been necessary, and each person must cooperate with and contribute to society to reach both personal and shared goals. Adler believed people have a **fundamental need to belong** in order to be healthy and function well (Ferguson, 2010).

> **Trap.** "Adler held that social interest is learned entirely from the environment" is wrong; so is "social interest is fully formed at birth". The **potential** is **innate**; its **development** depends on **early social experiences**, above all with the **mother**. Contrast this with **inferiority feelings**, which are universal but **not** innate (they come from the infant's situation).

### The Role of the Mother in Developing Social Interest

The mother is the first person the baby has contact with, and so she is the first person who can either **foster** or **thwart** the child's social interest. Adler gave her an enormous role, not only for social interest but for every aspect of personality. In a passage quoted by Grey (1998), he argued that the bond between mother and child is so close and so far-reaching that by adulthood no trait can be put down to heredity alone, because every inherited tendency has already been trained and reshaped by her, skilfully or otherwise.

What must she teach? Three things: **cooperation, companionship and courage**. The logic links them. Only children who feel a **kinship** with others will find the **courage** to tackle life's demands. Children who regard other people with suspicion and hostility will approach life in the same spirit as adults. People with no social interest may become **neurotics** or even **criminals**. Adler went further still and traced a whole catalogue of social evils, from **war** to **racial hatred** to **public drunkenness**, to a lack of community feeling.

A clinical picture helps. A mother who responds to her toddler's attempts to help with the cooking ("let me stir!") by letting him try, praising the effort and showing him how to share the task, is teaching cooperation and courage at once. A mother who is too anxious to let him near the stove, or too irritated to bother, teaches him that other people are either obstacles or servants. Multiply such moments by a few thousand and you have a style of life with or without social interest.

### The Role of Adler's Life Experiences in Developing Social Interest

The concept of social interest itself has a biography. **Early** in his career, Adler held that people were driven by a **lust for power** and a need to **dominate**. He proposed this while he was **fighting to establish his own position within Freud's circle**. After he broke with Freud and won recognition for his own work, he changed his view and argued that people are motivated **more by social interest** than by power and dominance.

His personality appears to have changed in step. In Freud's group he had a reputation for being quarrelsome and ambitious, forever arguing over who had thought of an idea first. In later years he **mellowed**, and his theory moved from **power and domination** to the gentler force of **social or community interest**. Schultz presents this as one more case of the theory mirroring the theorist's life.

> **High-yield.** Adler's motivational concept evolved in three steps: **masculine protest / will to power** (early, inside Freud's circle) → **striving for superiority or perfection** → increasingly framed by **social interest** (later, after independence and success). Each step tracks a change in his own circumstances.

## 4.6 Birth Order

Schultz counts **order of birth** among Adler's **most enduring contributions**. The idea is that birth order is a major **social** influence in childhood, and one of the materials from which the child builds a style of life.

The argument begins with a fact that sounds obvious once stated. Siblings share parents and a house, but they do **not** share a social environment. One child is older and one younger; parents are anxious novices with the first and relaxed veterans with the third; an only child spends evenings among adults, a fourth child among a crowd. These different positions, and the different parental attitudes each attracts, create different childhood conditions, and different conditions encourage different personalities.

Adler enjoyed showing off this idea at lectures and dinner parties by **guessing people's birth order from their behaviour**. He wrote about **four positions**: the first-born, the second-born, the youngest and the only child.

### The First-Born Child

The first-born starts in an enviable position. Parents are usually delighted with their first baby and pour time and attention into it, so first-borns typically receive **instant and undivided attention**. Their early life is happy and secure, until a second child arrives.

#### Dethronement

With the birth of the second child, the first-born is suddenly no longer the centre of attention and no longer receives constant care. In Adler's word, the first-born is **dethroned**. Attention and affection that were once all his must now be shared. He is made to wait while the baby's needs are met, and told to be quiet so as not to wake it.

Nobody, Adler thought, accepts such a demotion without a struggle. The first-born tries to **win back** the lost position of power and privilege, but the battle is **lost from the start**; the family will never return to the old arrangement. For a while the child may become **stubborn, badly behaved and destructive**, and may **refuse to eat or go to bed**. He is lashing out in anger. The parents, however, have far stronger weapons, and when they punish him he reads the punishment as further proof of his fall. He may come to **hate the second child**, who after all caused the whole problem.

Two factors set how hard the dethronement hits:

1. **Prior pampering.** Every first-born feels the shock, but those who were **excessively pampered** lose more.
2. **Age when the rival arrives.** In general, the **older** the first-born is when the second child is born, the **less** dethronement he experiences. Schultz's example: an **8-year-old** will be less upset by a new sibling than a **2-year-old**.

Clinically, the picture is familiar to anyone who has worked in child guidance: a three-year-old who was dry at night starts wetting the bed again, demands a bottle, and hits the new baby when nobody is looking, within weeks of the sibling's birth. Adler's reading is not "regression to an earlier psychosexual stage" but "a dethroned child fighting, with the only weapons he has, to get his throne back".

#### Characteristics of First-Borns

Adler's portrait of the adult first-born mixes disadvantages and advantages.

**The costs.** First-borns are often **oriented towards the past**, caught up in **nostalgia**, and **pessimistic about the future**. Having once tasted power, they remain **concerned with power** all their lives. They can exercise power over younger siblings, but they are also more subject to their **parents'** power, because more is expected of them.

**The benefits.** As the children grow, the first-born is often cast as **teacher, tutor, leader and disciplinarian**, expected to help look after the younger ones. These roles often let the first-born **mature intellectually** further than the younger children. Schultz backs this with **Zajonc (2001)**, who argued that younger siblings keep asking older ones what words mean, how things work and where things are, and that having to explain gives the older child practice that builds **verbal fluency** sooner. In short, the tutor learns by teaching. (Classic background: this tutoring account is part of Zajonc's broader **confluence model** of family intellectual climate.)

**Order and authority.** Adler also thought first-borns take an unusual interest in **maintaining order and authority**. They become good **organisers**, **conscientious** and **scrupulous about detail**, and **authoritarian and conservative** in their attitudes. Adler described **Sigmund Freud**, a first-born, as a **typical eldest son**.

**The risk.** First-borns may also grow up feeling **insecure** and **hostile** towards others. Adler believed that **neurotics, perverts and criminals** were often first-borns.

> **Trap.** Adler's first-born is oriented to the **past** and **pessimistic** about the future; the second-born is **optimistic**. Students often reverse this because "first-borns achieve more". Both are true in Adler: first-borns can be high achievers and intellectually advantaged **and** nostalgic, power-conscious and pessimistic.

### The Second-Born Child

The child who caused all that upheaval is in a unique position too. Second-borns **never experience** the powerful, sole-child position the first-born once held, so even if a third child arrives, they do not suffer the first-born's sense of dethronement.

By the time a second child arrives, the parents have also usually **changed**. A second baby is less of a novelty; they are less anxious about their own parenting and may take a more **relaxed** approach.

Most importantly, the second-born has a **pacesetter** from day one. The older sibling is always there as a **model**, a **threat**, or a **source of competition**.

Adler was himself a **second-born**, locked in lifelong competition with his older brother, **Sigmund**. Even after Adler became famous he felt overshadowed by this brother. Hoffman (1994) describes him as always feeling eclipsed by a "model brother" whose favoured position in the family he resented, and reports that in middle age Adler would remark, a little wearily, that his brother, a prosperous businessman, had always been ahead of him and still was.

#### Characteristics of Second-Borns

**The benefit.** Competition with the first-born can **motivate** the second-born to catch up with and overtake the older sibling, and that goal speeds the second child's **language and motor development**. Never having held power, second-borns are less **concerned** with it. They are **more optimistic about the future** and likely to be **competitive and ambitious**, as Adler was.

**The risk.** If the older sibling is outstanding, say at sport or studies, the second-born may conclude that he can **never catch up** and **stop trying**. Then competitiveness never becomes part of the style of life, and the second-born may become an **underachiever**, performing below his ability in many areas.

### The Youngest Child

The youngest, or last-born, child **never faces dethronement** and often becomes the **pet of the family**, especially if the older siblings are several years older. Driven by the need to **surpass** the older ones, youngest children often **develop remarkably fast**, and last-borns are often **high achievers** in whatever work they take up as adults.

The opposite outcome is also possible. If the youngest is **excessively pampered** and comes to believe he never needs to learn to do anything for himself, he may carry childhood **helplessness and dependency** into adult life. Used to being looked after and unused to struggle, such people find it **hard to adjust to adulthood**. (Section 4.9 shows how this prediction was tested through alcoholism and binge drinking.)

### The Only Child

The only child **never loses** the position of primacy and power, and remains the focus of family attention. Spending more time with **adults** than a child with siblings would, the only child often **matures early** and shows adult behaviour and attitudes.

The problem arrives outside the home. At **school** and elsewhere, the only child discovers that he is no longer the centre of attention. He has learned **neither to share nor to compete**. If his abilities do not win him enough recognition, he is likely to feel **keenly disappointed**.

### Birth order sets probabilities, not rules

Adler did **not** claim that a child automatically acquires a certain character because of his place in the family. His claim was that certain styles of life are **likely** to develop from **birth order combined with early social interactions**, and that the **creative self** uses both influences in building the style of life. Two first-borns can end up very different, because each interprets the situation in his own way.

**Table: Adler's four birth positions at a glance** (summary of 4.6)

| Position | Situation in the family | Likely strengths | Likely risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First-born** | Undivided attention, then **dethroned** by the second child | Intellectual maturity (tutor role), organising, conscientious, interest in order and authority | Past-oriented, nostalgic, **pessimistic**, power-concerned, authoritarian/conservative; insecure, hostile; neurotics, perverts and criminals often first-borns |
| **Second-born** | Never held sole power; relaxed parents; older sibling as **pacesetter** | Competitive, **ambitious**, **optimistic**; faster language and motor development | Gives up if the elder excels: **underachiever** |
| **Youngest** | Never dethroned; often the **family pet** | Develops fast; often a **high achiever** | If pampered: helpless, dependent, poor adjustment to adulthood |
| **Only child** | Never loses primacy; much time with adults | **Matures early**, adult attitudes | Has learned **neither to share nor to compete**; disappointed when not the centre of attention (school) |

> **Trap.** Dethronement is a **first-born** experience; the second-born and the youngest **never** suffer it, and the only child never loses primacy at all. The **only child**'s shock comes at **school**, not at the birth of a sibling. Classic background: later Adlerians distinguish **ordinal** position (the number) from **psychological** position (how the child experiences his place, e.g. a first-born overtaken by a gifted younger sibling may function like a second-born); this fits Schultz's point that birth order sets probabilities, not rules.

## 4.7 Questions about Human Nature

Schultz's verdict is that Adler offers a **hopeful, flattering** image of human nature, the **antithesis of Freud's dreary, pessimistic view**. It is more satisfying to think of ourselves as able to shape our own development and destiny consciously than to see ourselves ruled by instincts and by childhood events we could not control. Take the six issues in turn.

**Free will versus determinism: free will.** People are not driven by unconscious forces; they have the **free will** to shape the social forces acting on them and to use those forces creatively to build a unique style of life. This is the job of the **creative power of the self**. One qualification matters for "which is NOT" questions: early in his career Adler **accepted** determinism, which was standard in the science of the time and central to Freud's theory; his **final** formulation **rejected** it (Section 4.10).

**Nature versus nurture: mainly nurture, with an innate core.** Adler granted that some aspects of human nature are **innate**, namely the **potential for social interest** and the **striving for perfection**, but held that **experience** determines how these inherited tendencies are realised. Social forces matter more than biological ones. Even inferiority feelings, universal as they are, arise from the infant's **situation**, not from heredity.

**Past versus present (and future): the past matters, but the future matters more.** Childhood influences are important, particularly **birth order** and **interactions with parents**, and the style of life is set by age 4 or 5. But we are **not victims** of our childhood; we **use** it to create a style of life. And behaviour is explained above all by **future goals** (fictional finalism), not by past events.

**Uniqueness versus universality: uniqueness.** Each person creates a **unique** style of life; the very name "individual psychology" signals the focus on the uniqueness of each person. Schultz contrasts this with the depressing **universality and sameness** of human nature in Freud's system. (Some elements are universal, inferiority feelings and the striving for perfection, but each person's way of handling them is unique.)

**Equilibrium versus growth: growth.** The striving for superiority **increases** tension instead of reducing it; people perpetually strive towards perfection. Adler's model is one of growth and self-completion, not of returning to a tension-free state.

**Optimism versus pessimism: optimism.** Adler believed in the creative power of the individual and was **optimistic about social progress**; he saw humanity as a whole, like each person, striving towards perfection.

| Issue | Adler's position | Reason in the theory |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Free will** (in his final formulation) | Creative power of the self builds the style of life |
| Nature vs nurture | **Mostly nurture** | Social interest and striving are innate potentials, but experience decides how they develop |
| Past vs present/future | **Future goals outweigh the past**; childhood important but not decisive | Fictional finalism; we use childhood, we are not its victims |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Uniqueness** | Each person's style of life is unique; "individual" psychology |
| Equilibrium vs growth | **Growth** | Striving for perfection increases tension |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimism** | Faith in the creative self and in social progress |

> **Trap.** Adler is **optimistic** and **growth**-oriented, unlike Freud. Do not let the importance of early childhood (style of life fixed by 4 or 5) push you into marking him a determinist: the style of life is fixed early **but chosen**, and future goals carry more explanatory weight than past events.

## 4.8 Assessment in Adler's Theory

### How Adler worked with patients

Like Freud, Adler built his theory from his patients: what they said and how they behaved in therapy. His manner, however, was very different.

- **Setting.** Freud's patients lay on a **couch** with Freud seated out of sight behind them. Adler and his patients sat in **comfortable chairs facing each other**, and the sessions resembled **conversations between friends** more than Freud's formal arrangement.
- **Humour.** Adler liked to use humour, sometimes teasing patients in a friendly way. He kept a stock of jokes suited to different neuroses, believing that a joke could help a patient see how absurd his symptom was. Hoffman (1994) records an example: when a teenage patient confessed that he masturbated and then felt guilty about it, Adler told him, in effect, that doing both was excessive and he should settle for one or the other. The joke deflates the guilt by treating it as an unnecessary second burden.
- **Observation of everything.** Adler assessed patients by watching **how they walked and sat, how they shook hands, even which chair they chose**. He held that the way we use our bodies reveals something of our style of life.

He even read meaning into **sleeping positions**:

| Sleeping posture | Adler's reading |
|---|---|
| Restless sleep, or lying flat on the back | Wants to seem more important than he is |
| Lying on the stomach | Stubborn, negative personality |
| Curled in the fetal position | Fearful of dealing with other people |
| Arms stretched out | Needs to be nurtured and supported |

### The entrance gates to mental life

Adler's **primary methods of assessment**, which he called the **entrance gates to mental life**, were three:

1. **order of birth** (Section 4.6),
2. **early recollections**,
3. **dream analysis**.

Later psychologists added **tests of social interest** built on his concepts. The **purpose** of all this assessment, for Adler, was to uncover the patient's **style of life** and to judge whether it was the **most appropriate** one for that person.

> **Trap.** Adler's three "entrance gates" are **birth order, early recollections and dream analysis**. **Free association** is Freud's method and is **not** one of them; **personality tests** are not either (Adler disliked them). The social-interest scales are **later** instruments developed by others.

### Early Recollections

Because personality is created in the **first 4 or 5 years**, Adler reasoned that **early recollections**, a person's memories from that period, should reveal the style of life that still characterises the adult. The glossary definition: a personality assessment technique in which our earliest memories, **whether of real events or fantasies**, are assumed to reveal the **primary interest of our life**.

That last clause is the surprising one. Adler found that it made **little difference whether the recollection was true or imagined**. Either way, the remembered scene centred on what mattered most in the person's life, so either way it revealed the style of life. Early recollections, in this tradition, are regarded as the **most satisfactory single indicators of lifestyle** (Manaster & Mays, 2004). Adler also noticed that many early memories featured **actual physical objects** as part of the scene (Clark, 2009).

Each memory, Adler said, should be read in the context of the particular person's style of life, but he did notice **common patterns**:

| Content of the early memory | What it suggested to Adler |
|---|---|
| Danger or punishment | A tendency towards **hostility** |
| The birth of a sibling | A continuing sense of **dethronement** |
| A focus on one parent | A **preference** for that parent |
| Improper behaviour | A **warning** against repeating that behaviour |

**Adler versus Freud on early memories.** The most testable point in this subsection comes from Kopp and Eckstein (2004). On the Adlerian view, people recall from early childhood only those images that **confirm their present view of themselves and the world** and that fit their current **direction of striving** for significance and security. Adler's approach therefore studies **what is remembered** (selective memory in the service of the lifestyle). Freud's approach to early memories studies **what is forgotten**, through **repression**.

> **Trap.** Adler: **what is remembered**, selected to fit the present style of life, and real or fantasy makes **little difference**. Freud: **what is forgotten**, because it was **repressed**. An option that says Adler used early memories to recover repressed infantile wishes is describing Freud.

### Adler's Strange Early Memory

Adler's own case shows the principle at work. As an adult he recalled a memory from about **age 5**, when he had just started school. The road to school, as he remembered it, ran through a **cemetery**, and every day he was frightened. What made it worse was that the other children did not seem to mind, so his fear set him apart and deepened his sense of being inferior. One day he decided to end it: he ran through the cemetery about **a dozen times** until he felt he had mastered the fear, and after that he walked to school without being afraid (Adler, 1924/1963).

**Thirty years later** he met an old schoolmate and asked whether the cemetery was still there. The man was puzzled: there had **never been a cemetery** near their school. Adler, who had remembered it so vividly, was shocked, questioned other classmates, and got the same answer every time. He accepted that the memory was **false**.

Yet it was still **true to his style of life**. The memory captured exactly what characterised him: **fear and inferiority, and the deliberate effort to overcome them** by facing the feared thing head-on. The recollection was inaccurate as history and accurate as a portrait of the man, which is precisely Adler's claim about early recollections.

### Dream Analysis

Adler agreed with Freud that dreams are valuable for understanding personality but **disagreed about how to interpret them**. He did **not** believe dreams fulfil wishes or reveal hidden conflicts. Instead, dreams express our **feelings about a current problem** and **what we intend to do about it**.

His own dream before his first trip to America illustrates the point. He was anxious about how he and his theory would be received. The night before he was due to sail, he dreamed that the ship sank with him aboard and all his possessions were lost; thrown into the sea, he had to swim alone through rough water, and by sheer determination he reached land safely (Hoffman, 1994). The dream expressed both his **fear** of what awaited him in the United States and his **intention to land safely**, that is, to succeed for himself and for individual psychology.

Two general claims follow. First, in the fantasies of both **night dreams and daydreams** we imagine we can overcome the hardest obstacle or simplify the most complicated problem, so dreams are oriented to the **present and future**, not to past conflicts. Second, a dream is an **expression of the style of life** and so is unique to the dreamer; Adler insisted that a dream should **never be interpreted without knowing the person and his or her situation**.

He nevertheless offered some common interpretations. Freud had read dreams of **falling or flying** in **sexual** terms; Adler read them in terms of **status and striving**:

- **Falling**: the dreamer's emotional outlook involves a **demotion or loss**, for example fear of losing **self-esteem or prestige**.
- **Flying**: a sense of **striving upwards**, an **ambitious** style of life in which the person wants to be above or better than others.
- **Flying and falling together**: fear of being **too ambitious and so failing**.
- **Being chased**: a feeling of **weakness** in relation to other people.
- **Being naked**: fear of **giving oneself away**.

**Table 4.1** Adlerian meanings of common dream events (adapted by Schultz from Grey, 1998)

| Dream event | Adlerian interpretation |
|---|---|
| Being paralysed, unable to move | Facing problems that seem to have no solution |
| Taking a school examination | Feeling unprepared for situations in life |
| Wearing the wrong clothes | Being troubled by one's own faults |
| Sexual content | Retreating from sex, or lacking adequate information about it |
| Rage | A hostile or angry style of life |
| Someone's death | Unresolved issues about the person who died |

An everyday example: a postgraduate student, two weeks before her viva, dreams she has turned up to an exam hall in her nightclothes, unable to read the questions. Freud would hunt for a disguised wish. Adler would read the dream directly as her present problem (feeling **unprepared**, troubled by her **faults**) and look at how the dream ends for her intended response: does she walk out, or sit down and start writing?

> **Trap.** Adler's dreams are about the **present and future** (current problems and intended solutions), **not** wish fulfilment and **not** past conflicts. Flying and falling are about **ambition and status** in Adler, **sexual** in Freud. An exam dream means feeling **unprepared**; a paralysis dream means **insoluble problems**; a chasing dream means **weakness** relative to others.

### Measures of Social Interest

Adler himself **did not want to use psychological tests** to assess personality. He argued that tests create **artificial situations** and give **ambiguous results**, and that therapists should develop their **intuition** instead. He was not against all testing, though: he **supported tests of memory and intelligence**. His objection was to **personality** tests.

Later psychologists built tests to measure his concepts anyway. Three are named in the chapter:

**Social Interest Scale (SIS)** (Crandall, 1981). The SIS consists of **pairs of adjectives**. For each pair, the respondent picks the word describing an attribute he or she would **like to possess**. Choices such as **helpful, sympathetic and considerate** are taken to indicate social interest. Notice that it measures what traits a person **values**, not a direct self-description.

**Social Interest Index (SII)** (Greever, Tseng, & Friedland, 1973). The SII is a **self-report inventory**: respondents rate how far each statement describes them. Items were chosen to reflect Adler's ideas and to tap a person's ability to **accept and cooperate with others** (a typical item expresses willingness to help friends out). People scoring **high** on the SII tend to be high in **friendliness, empathy, cooperation, tolerance and independence**, and lower in **anxiety, hostility, depression and neuroticism** (Leak, 2006a, 2006b).

**Basic Adlerian Scales for Interpersonal Success, Adult form (BASIS-A)** (Peluso, Peluso, Buckner, Curlette, & Kern, 2004). A **65-item** self-report inventory that assesses **lifestyle** as well as social interest. It measures **five** dimensions:

1. social interest,
2. going along,
3. taking charge,
4. wanting recognition,
5. being cautious.

| Measure | Authors (year) | Format | What it captures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SIS** | Crandall (1981) | Adjective pairs; choose the attribute you would like to have | Social interest (values) |
| **SII** | Greever, Tseng, & Friedland (1973) | Self-report statements rated for self-description | Social interest (ability to accept and cooperate) |
| **BASIS-A** | Peluso et al. (2004) | 65-item self-report | Lifestyle + social interest; 5 dimensions |

> **Trap.** **SIS = adjective pairs** (Crandall, 1981); **SII = self-report statements** (Greever, Tseng, & Friedland, 1973); **BASIS-A = 65 items, five dimensions** (social interest, going along, taking charge, wanting recognition, being cautious). Adler **opposed personality tests** but **supported memory and intelligence tests**; none of these scales is his.

## 4.9 Research on Adler's Theory

### Adler's own method and its limits

Adler's main research method was the **case study**, and very little of his data has survived. He did not publish case histories apart from **two fragments**: one written **by a patient** and one written **by a patient's physician**. Adler **did not know** either patient; he analysed their personalities from what they had written.

His data and method therefore attract the **same criticisms** Schultz levelled at Freud and Jung:

- the observations **cannot be repeated or duplicated**;
- they were **not collected in a controlled, systematic way**;
- Adler did **not verify** the accuracy of what patients told him;
- he did **not explain** how he analysed the data;
- he had **no interest in the experimental method**.

One of his own followers admitted that although Adler wanted individual psychology to be a science, it has not been easy to verify scientifically (Manaster, 2006).

Most of Adler's propositions have resisted scientific testing, but several have been studied: **dreams, inferiority feelings, early recollections, pampering and neglect in childhood, social interest, and birth order**. Take them in the book's order.

### Dreams

**Grieser, Greenberg, and Harrison (1972)** tested Adler's claim that dreams help us deal with **current problems**. Participants were put in a situation in which **failing to solve a puzzle** was framed as a **threat to the personality** (an ego threat). They then slept, under two conditions:

- One group was allowed to **dream**: they were woken only during **non-rapid-eye-movement (NREM)** sleep, so their REM periods, when most vivid dreaming happens, were left alone.
- The other group was woken during **rapid-eye-movement (REM)** sleep, so that they **could not dream**.

Afterwards, those who had dreamed **recalled significantly more of the uncompleted puzzle** than those who had not. The researchers concluded that dreaming had helped participants **deal effectively with the current threatening situation**, the failure to solve the puzzle. The logic: if dreams work on present problems, then preventing dreams should leave the problem less processed, and that is what happened.

> **Trap.** In Grieser et al. (1972) the **dream** group was woken in **NREM** sleep (so its REM dreaming was undisturbed); the **no-dream** group was woken in **REM** sleep. Students who remember "REM = dreaming" sometimes reverse the groups. The dreamers recalled **more** of the failed puzzle.

**Breger, Hunter, and Lane (1971)** collected dreams from two groups facing something stressful: **college students anticipating a stressful psychotherapy session** and **patients about to undergo major surgery**. In both groups the dreams centred on the dreamers' **conscious worries, fears and hopes**; people dreamed about the **current problems** in front of them. This is the Adlerian position (dreams reflect present concerns) rather than the Freudian one (dreams disguise old, unconscious wishes).

### Inferiority Feelings

Adults who score **low** on inferiority feelings tend to be **more successful, more self-confident and more persistent** in pursuing their goals than adults who score high.

The more interesting finding is curvilinear. In **American college students**, those with **moderate** inferiority feelings had **higher grade point averages** than those with either **low or high** inferiority feelings (**Strano & Petrocelli, 2005**). That fits Adler neatly: some inferiority feeling supplies motivation (compensation), too little supplies none, and too much shades into the paralysis of an inferiority complex.

A study of **teenagers and young adults in China** found that what people feel inferior **about** shifts with age (**Kosaka, 2008**):

| Age group | Main source of inferiority feelings |
|---|---|
| Younger teens | **Poor grades** |
| Older teens | **Physical attractiveness** |
| College students | **Lack of social skills** |

### Early Recollections

A long line of studies links the **themes of early memories** to current clinical status, which is just what Adler's claim (early recollections reveal the present style of life) predicts.

| Study | Group | Theme of early recollections |
|---|---|---|
| **Jackson & Sechrest (1962)** | Patients diagnosed with **anxiety neurosis** | **Fear** |
| | **Depressed** patients | **Abandonment** |
| | Patients with **psychosomatic** complaints | **Illness** |
| **Hafner, Fakouri, & Labrentz (1982)** | **Alcoholics** | **Threatening events**, and situations in which they were **controlled by external circumstances** rather than their own decisions; neither theme in non-alcoholic controls |
| **Patterson (2014)** | **US police officers** | Those with **traumatic** early recollections had **more pronounced PTSD symptoms** |
| **Hankoff (1987)** | **Adult criminals** | **Disturbing or aggressive interactions** with others; **more unpleasant events** than controls |
| **Davidow & Bruhn (1990)** | **Adolescent delinquents** | **Breaking rules**, difficulty forming relationships, inability to cope alone; parents seen as **untrustworthy** and more likely to hurt than help; absent in controls |
| **Tobey & Bruhn (1992)** | Psychiatric patients judged **dangerous** to self and others | **More aggressive** memories than non-dangerous patients; felt **vulnerable and powerless**, saw others as **hostile and abusive** |

Read across the rows and a pattern appears: the emotional tone of the remembered childhood scene matches the adult's present difficulty. The anxious remember being afraid, the depressed remember being left, the dangerous remember a world of attackers. Note also that these are **correlational** findings. They show that early recollections and current problems go together, which is all Adler claimed; they do not show that the remembered events caused the problems (and, as the next subsection shows, the events may never have happened).

### The Nature of Early Recollections

Studies using **objective scoring systems** for early recollections have found that these memories tend to be **subjective re-creations** rather than records of events that actually occurred, much like Adler's cemetery (**Statton & Wilborn, 1991**).

A neat follow-up asked people to **invent** early recollections that **might have happened to someone else**. The themes of these made-up memories were **similar to the themes of the participants' own** early recollections (**Buchanan, Kern, & Bell-Dumas, 1991**). This supports Adler's contention that early recollections reveal one's **current** style of life, and therefore that they can be used therapeutically: whatever you remember, or even invent, carries your present outlook.

**Table 4.2** Kinds of early recollection and the style-of-life theme each may reveal (adapted by Schultz from Bruhn, 1992, the Early Memories Procedure)

| Kind of memory asked for | Possible style-of-life theme |
|---|---|
| Earliest memory of school | Attitudes to achievement, mastery and independence |
| Earliest memory of being punished | Attitude to people in authority |
| Earliest memory involving a sibling | Signs of sibling rivalry |
| Earliest memory of the family | How the person functions in social situations |
| Clearest memory of the mother | Attitudes towards women |
| Clearest memory of the father | Attitudes towards men |
| Memory of someone admired | What the person's role models are based on |
| Happiest memory | How the person's strongest needs are best satisfied |

**Career preferences.** Early recollections studied in adults in the **United States** and **Israel** predict **career preferences**. For example, the early memories of **physicists, mathematicians and psychologists** contained themes of **curiosity, independent thought, and scepticism** about what authority figures say (**Clark, 2005; Kasler & Nevo, 2005**).

### Neglect in Childhood

Adler predicted that neglected or rejected children develop **feelings of worthlessness**. The research is consistent.

- **Crook, Raskin, and Eliot (1981)**: **adults hospitalised for depression** rated their parents as **hostile, detached and rejecting**. Crucially, the researchers did not rely on the patients' word alone: **interviews with siblings, relatives and friends** confirmed that the parents really had behaved in hostile, neglectful ways. That check matters, because a depressed person might be expected to remember his parents more harshly than they deserved.
- **Lefkowitz and Tesiny (1984)**: parents of **8-year-olds** filled in a questionnaire on their child-rearing and their satisfaction with their children. **Ten years later**, at **18**, the children took the **depression scale of the MMPI**. Those with higher depression scores had been **neglected** by their parents; those whose parents had not been indifferent or unloving scored **lower**. The prospective design (measure neglect first, depression a decade later) makes this stronger evidence than a retrospective report.
- More recent studies find that neglected children experience more **shame, depression, PTSD symptoms** and have **poorer socialisation skills** than non-neglected children (Bennett, Sullivan, & Lewis, 2010; Lowell, Viezel, Davis, & Castillo, 2011; Milot, Plamondon, Ethier, Lemelin, St-Laurent, & Rousseau, 2013).
- In **China**, neglected children and adolescents aged **5 to 17** were far more likely to develop **high anxiety** (Guan, Deng, & Luo, 2010).
- Neglected children may also be more prone to **violence** and **excessive alcohol use** later in life (Widom, Czaja, Wilson, Allwood, & Chauhan, 2013).

Schultz's conclusion: children neglected early pay a heavy price as they grow up.

### Pampering in Childhood

Adler argued that pampering could produce a **pampered style of life** with little or no social feeling for others. Research supports this and adds that pampering can feed excessive **narcissism**: a lack of responsibility or empathy for others, an **inflated sense of self-importance**, and a tendency to **exploit** people. Studies have distinguished **four types of pampering**:

| Type of pampering | What the parent does | Outcome in the child |
|---|---|---|
| **Overindulgence** | Gratifies the child's needs and wishes persistently | Sense of **entitlement**; tyrannical and **manipulative** behaviour |
| **Overpermissiveness** | Lets the child do as he pleases with no thought for the effect on others | **Disregard for social rules** and the rights of others |
| **Overdomination** | Makes all the decisions for the child | **Low self-confidence**; **dependence** on others in adulthood |
| **Overprotection** | Constantly warns the child about dangers in the environment | **Generalised anxiety**; avoiding or hiding from social situations |

**Capron (2004)**, studying **college students**, found:

- children of **over-domineering mothers** were **more likely to seek psychotherapy** in college;
- students who rated their parents as both **overindulgent and overprotective** tended to be **low in self-esteem**;
- students whose parents were **overindulgent and over-domineering** scored **high in narcissism**.

> **Trap.** Match the pampering type to its outcome. **Overdomination** → dependence, low self-confidence (and therapy-seeking, Capron). **Overprotection** → generalised anxiety, social avoidance. **Overindulgence** → entitlement, manipulation. **Overpermissiveness** → disregard of rules and others' rights. Overindulgent + overprotective → **low self-esteem**; overindulgent + over-domineering → **narcissism**.

### Social Interest

The social-interest findings are the most uniformly favourable in the chapter.

**Using the SIS:**

- High scorers report **less stress, depression, anxiety and hostility** than low scorers, and score higher on **cooperation, empathy, responsibility and popularity** (Crandall, 1984; Watkins, 1994; Watkins & St. John, 1994).
- In college students, high social interest goes with high **spirituality and religiosity**, but of a **positive, tolerant, helping** kind, not necessarily **religious ethnocentrism or fundamentalism** (Leak, 2006a).

**Well-being.** Studies in the **United States and China** link high social interest to **subjective well-being, hope and optimism, agreeableness, self-identity, self-determination**, and a strong sense of **purpose in life** (Barlow, Tobin, & Schmidt, 2009; Foley, Matheny, & Curlette, 2008; Leak & Leak, 2006).

**Adolescents.** High school students in **grades 9 to 12** who were high in social interest scored significantly higher in **overall life satisfaction** and in satisfaction with **friends and family** (Gilman, 2001).

**Crime.** Among **male criminal offenders aged 18 to 40**, high social-interest scorers were **far less likely to reoffend** after release from jail (Daugherty, Murphy, & Paugh, 2001). **Juvenile delinquents** scored **lower** on social interest than non-delinquents (Newbauer & Stone, 2010).

**Using the SII:**

- **Women** high in social interest scored significantly higher in **self-actualisation**, Maslow's mark of the healthy personality (see Chapter 9).
- Social interest was **higher in women than in men** and **increased with age** in both sexes (Greever, Tseng, & Friedland, 1973).

**Culture.** Among **Latino men and women living in the United States**, those well adjusted to **both** cultures scored **higher** on social interest than those acculturated mainly to only the Latino or only the US way of life (Miranda, Frevert, & Kern, 1998).

**Physical health.** Social interest, with its feelings of belonging, cooperation, and giving to or receiving support from a social network, has been linked positively to physical and mental well-being. High scorers tended to have **stronger immune systems, fewer colds, lower blood pressure**, and greater subjective well-being (Nikelly, 2005).

### Highlights: Research on Adler's Ideas

Schultz gathers the main findings on neglect, pampering and social interest in a Highlights box. In summary:

| Group | Tends to show |
|---|---|
| **Neglected children** | Feelings of **worthlessness and shame**; **depression**; **anxiety** |
| **Pampered children** | **Low self-esteem**; **narcissism**; **lack of empathy** for others |
| **People high in social interest** | **Low** depression, anxiety and hostility; **empathy** for others; being **happy and agreeable** |

### Birth Order

Birth order is one of the most heavily researched of Adler's ideas (Eckstein & Kaufman, 2012).

**Occupations.** A survey of **200 birth-order studies** (Eckstein et al., 2010) concluded that first-borns showed **higher success and achievement**, and were more likely to become **accountants, lawyers, architects, surgeons, college professors or astronauts**. Later-borns were more likely to become **firefighters, high school teachers, musicians, photographers, social workers or stuntmen**.

**Older brothers and sexual orientation.** Simply having older or younger siblings, whatever one's own position, can matter. Studies of nearly **20,000 people in England, Scotland and Wales** and about **3,500 in the United States** found that the **number of older brothers** a man had predicted his sexual orientation: boys with older brothers were more sexually attracted to men than boys without, and the **more older brothers**, the **greater** the same-sex attraction. Having **older sisters did not** predict sexual orientation in **women** (**Bogaert, 2003**). (Classic background: this pattern is widely known as the **fraternal birth order effect**.)

**Friendship and romance.** People tend to associate with others of the **same birth position**: first-borns with first-borns, second-borns with second-borns, only children with only children, in both **friendships and romantic relationships** (Hartshorne, 2010; Hartshorne, Salem-Hartshorne, & Hartshorne, 2009).

#### First-Born Children

**Children shape parents.** A **Finnish** study found that a first-born's behaviour and characteristics can influence whether the parents have more children **within 5 years** of the first birth. Parents whose first child showed **high intelligence, few behaviour problems and adaptability** to new situations were **more likely** to have more children (**Jokela, 2010**). The direction of influence here is the reverse of the usual birth-order story: the child is affecting the family, not only the family affecting the child.

**Achievement and eminence.** Adler said first-borns are concerned with **power and authority**, and one adult route to both is achievement at work. In many areas, from **college attendance to high-level management**, first-borns are **over-represented** relative to their share of the population. More first-borns become **eminent**, and they tend to reach greater **intellectual achievement** in academic settings and greater **power and prestige** in careers (Breland, 1974; Schachter, 1963).

**Intelligence, education, occupation.** Studies in the **United States and Poland** found that first-borns scored higher on **intelligence**, completed **more years of education**, and worked in **more prestigious occupations** than later-borns (Herrera, Zajonc, Wieczorkowska, & Cichomski, 2003). In **Sweden**, first-borns scored higher on tests of **managerial or executive functioning** (Holmgren, Molander, & Nilsson, 2006). A study of **more than 240,000 male army recruits in Norway** found that **older siblings scored higher on IQ tests** than younger ones (**Kristensen & Bjerkedal, 2007**).

**PTSD.** **British** research found first-borns **far less likely to suffer PTSD** than those who were the **fifth child or later** in large families; the later-borns were more vulnerable to **stress and adjustment disorders** (Green & Griffiths, 2014). Schultz judges these results, in general, **supportive** of Adler.

**The IQ gradient and its explanation.** Not everyone agrees that first-borns are more intelligent (see Rodgers, 2001), but the classic evidence is striking. **Belmont and Marolla (1973)** analysed the IQ scores of **400,000 European men** by birth order: first-borns scored higher than second-borns, second-borns higher than third-borns, and so on down the line. (Classic background: the sample was **Dutch** military conscripts.) **Zajonc, Markus, and Markus (1979)** confirmed the pattern for **men and women in several nations**. The explanation offered is **not genetic**. The first-born has a period of **exclusive exposure to adults**, and so may enjoy a more **intellectually stimulating** environment than children who arrive later into a household already full of children.

**IQ is not grades.** First-borns may be more intelligent, but they do not always get **better marks**. Studies of **US high school students** confirm first-borns' **higher IQ**, but find that **later-borns tend to work harder** and earn **higher grade point averages** (Frank, Turenshine, & Sullivan, 2010; Rettner, 2010).

**Suggestibility, dependence and anxiety.** First-borns tend to be more **suggestible** and **dependent on other people**, **anxious in stressful situations**, and higher in the **need for social relationships** (**Schachter, 1963, 1964**). Adler's theory predicts this: dethroned first-borns feel anxiety and try to regain their position by **conforming to parental expectations**. As a result, first-borns lean more on **other people's standards** to guide behaviour and build their self-concept (Newman, Higgins, & Vookles, 1992).

**Mixed findings on mood and personality.**

- First-borns scored **lower** than later-borns on **depression and anxiety** tests and **higher** on **self-esteem** (Gates, Lineberger, Crockett, & Hubbard, 1988).
- First-borns may be more **extraverted and conscientious** (Sulloway, 1995). But later research in the **Netherlands** found the **opposite** on extraversion: **later-borns** were more extraverted, in the sense of being more **dominant and assertive** (Pollet, Dijkstra, Barelds, & Buunk, 2010).
- **First-born girls** were more **obedient and socially responsible** than later-borns and felt **closer to their parents** (Sutton-Smith & Rosenberg, 1970).
- Studies in **France, Croatia, Canada and England** found that first-borns were **more closely supervised** in childhood, rated by their mothers as **less fearful**, reported **more frightening childhood dreams**, and scored higher on **dominance** in college (Beck, Burnet, & Vosper, 2006; Begue & Roche, 2005; Kerestes, 2006; McCann, Stewin, & Short, 1990).

> **Trap.** Two first-born findings look contradictory and are both in the book. Schachter (1963, 1964): first-borns are **anxious in stressful situations** (a situational reaction, plus suggestibility and dependence). Gates et al. (1988): first-borns score **lower** on **depression and anxiety** tests and **higher** on self-esteem. Also: Sulloway (1995) found first-borns **more extraverted**; Pollet et al. (2010, Netherlands) found **later-borns** more extraverted (dominant, assertive). And first-borns have **higher IQ** but later-borns earn **higher GPAs**.

#### Second-Born Children

Second-borns have been studied less.

- A **3-year** study of first- and second-born siblings found that the **attitudes, personalities and leisure activities** of second-borns were influenced **more by their older siblings than by their parents** (McHale, Updegraff, Helms-Erikson, & Crouter, 2001). This is the "pacesetter" in data.
- Among **major league baseball players**, **younger brothers** were **10 times more likely** than older brothers to try the risky move of **stealing bases**, and had **higher batting averages** (**Sulloway & Zweigenhaft, 2010**).

#### Last-Born Children

Adler predicted that **pampered** last-borns would have **adjustment problems** as adults. One popular account of **alcoholism** is that some people cannot cope with the demands of daily life; on Adler's theory, therefore, **more last-borns than early-borns** should become alcoholics. **Considerable research** on alcoholism and birth order supports this prediction. **Binge drinking in college** is significantly **higher among last-borns** than first-borns (**Laird & Shelton, 2006**). Last-borns may also feel **more academic rivalry** with their siblings than first-borns do (Badger & Reddy, 2009).

#### Only Children

Adler thought only-born adults remain overly concerned with being the **centre of attention** and are more **selfish**. Research has **not consistently** supported him.

- **Falbo (1978)**: only children were **more cooperative** than first-borns or last-borns.
- **Jiao, Ji, and Jing (1986)**: only children were **more self-centred and less popular** than children raised with siblings.
- **Falbo and Polit (1986)**, analysing **115 studies**: only-borns showed **higher achievement and intelligence** and **comparable social and emotional adjustment** to people with siblings.
- **Mellor (1990)** confirmed this and found only children had greater **initiative, aspiration, industriousness and self-esteem**.
- Only children are strongly **achievement-motivated** and score high in **confidence and organisational skills** (Siribaddana, 2013).
- Across several studies, **number of siblings** consistently predicts **educational success**: people with **fewer siblings do better** in school (**Downey, 2001**). Only-borns may also enjoy **more educational opportunities and parental resources**, which would help them outperform children with siblings.

> **Trap.** The only-child evidence mostly **contradicts** Adler's selfish, attention-seeking picture: Falbo (1978) more **cooperative**; Falbo and Polit (1986, **115 studies**) higher achievement and intelligence, **comparable** adjustment; Mellor (1990) more initiative and self-esteem. Only **Jiao, Ji, and Jing (1986)** found them more self-centred and less popular.

### Birth-Order Effects: A Matter of Belief?

After all those findings, Schultz adds a twist. People who **believe** that birth order shapes personality differ from people who do not. In one study, believers scored **significantly lower in openness to new experience** and **significantly higher in neuroticism** than non-believers (**Gundersen, Brown, Bhathal, & Kennedy, 2011**). The finding does not overturn the birth-order research, but it is a reminder that belief in birth-order effects is itself a characteristic of the believer, and that some of what people "see" in first-borns and last-borns may be expectation.

### Highlights: Research on Birth Order

Schultz's second Highlights box summarises the birth-order research. Note that two of its last-born entries go beyond the main text.

| Birth position | Research tendencies |
|---|---|
| **First-borns** | **Intelligent and hardworking**; **successful** in college and career; **low** on depression and anxiety; **high self-esteem** |
| **Second-borns** | **Influenced by older siblings**; more likely to **take risks** (stealing bases in baseball) |
| **Last-borns** | **High in social interest and agreeableness**; can also be **rebellious** |
| **Only-borns** | High in **achievement and intelligence**; **industrious** and do well at school; **high self-esteem** |

(Classic background: the "rebellious later-born" idea is most associated with **Sulloway's** *Born to Rebel* (1996), which argued that later-borns are more open to radical new ideas.)

## 4.10 Reflections on Adler's Theory

### Contributions

Schultz judges Adler's influence on psychology to be **substantial**, enough to make his one of the most **enduring** personality theories. In one sense he was **ahead of his time**: his **cognitive and social** emphases fit modern psychology better than they fitted the instinct-centred psychology of his own day (LaFountain, 2009). More than 30 years after Adler's death, **Abraham Maslow** wrote that Adler was proving more correct year after year as evidence accumulated in support of his picture of human nature (Maslow, 1970a).

**Influence on later theorists.** Several later chapters of the book carry Adler's fingerprints:

| Later theorist | What came from Adler |
|---|---|
| **Karen Horney** (Chapter 5) | Emphasis on **social forces** in personality |
| **Gordon Allport** | Focus on the **whole person** and the **unity of personality** |
| **Abraham Maslow** | The **creative power** of people to shape their own lives; **future goals** outweighing past events |
| **Julian Rotter** (social-learning theorist) | Said he remained **impressed by Adler's insights** into human nature (Rotter, 1982) |

**Influence inside psychoanalysis.** Adler's ideas reached even Freud's own system. It was Adler who proposed an **aggressive drive** more than **12 years before** Freud added aggression to sex as a primary motive. (Classic background: Adler's aggression paper is usually dated 1908; Freud's revised instinct theory, with a death or aggressive instinct, appeared in 1920.) The **neo-Freudian ego psychologists**, with their focus on **conscious, rational** processes rather than the unconscious, follow **Adler's** lead more than Freud's.

**Women.** Adler rejected Freud's view of women. He denied any **biological basis**, such as **penis envy**, for women's supposed sense of inferiority; he called that notion a **myth invented by men** to prop up their own claimed superiority. He accepted that women might **feel** inferior but attributed this to **social conditioning and sex-role stereotyping**. He believed in **equality of the sexes** and supported the **women's emancipation movements** of his time.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Adler's early **masculine protest** (which equated inferiority with femininity) with his **mature view of women**. In his later, better-known position he denied any biological basis for female inferiority, rejected **penis envy** as a male myth, blamed **social conditioning and sex-role stereotypes**, and supported women's emancipation. Horney (Chapter 5) makes a similar social argument.

**Concepts of lasting importance.** Schultz lists:

- the early work on **organic inferiority**, which influenced the study of **psychosomatic disorders**;
- the **inferiority complex**;
- **compensation**;
- **order of birth**.

Adler is also regarded as a **forerunner of social psychology and of group therapy**, the latter rooted in his group methods in the Vienna child-guidance clinics. And, as Figure 4.1 shows, he was a pioneer of popular psychology itself.

### Criticisms

Influential as it is, the theory has critics, and Indian papers like "which of the following is NOT a criticism of Adler" questions. These are the criticisms Schultz actually makes.

**1. Oversimplification.** **Freud** charged that Adler's psychology was **oversimplified**, and that it would be popular precisely because it dropped the complicated unconscious, contained no difficult concepts, and ignored the problems of sex. Freud quipped that psychoanalysis took two years or more to learn, whereas Adler's ideas could be learned in two weeks because there was so little to know (quoted in Sterba, 1982).

Schultz's evaluation: Adler's theory **does** look simpler than Freud's or Jung's, but the simplicity was **deliberate**. Adler said it had taken him **40 years** to make his psychology simple. The charge is reinforced by how readable his books are, partly because he wrote for the **general public** and partly because several were compiled from his **popular lectures**.

**2. Common sense.** A related charge is that Adler's concepts rest heavily on **commonsense observations** of everyday life. A *New York Times* reviewer, meaning it as a compliment, noted that although Adler was among the world's most eminent psychologists, nobody could match the simplicity and non-technical language of his writing (quoted in Hoffman, 1994). To a critic, the same quality suggests a theory that says little beyond what ordinary people already know.

**3. Inconsistency, gaps and unanswered questions.** Critics say Adler was **inconsistent and unsystematic**, leaving gaps. Are inferiority feelings the **only** problem we face? Do **all** people strive mainly for perfection? Can a person **accept** some degree of inferiority and stop trying to compensate? Adler's system cannot answer all of these well, though Schultz notes that most theories leave questions open.

**4. Determinism versus free will.** Some psychologists question Adler's position here. **Early** in his career he did **not** oppose determinism, which was widely accepted in science and was central to Freud's theory. **Later** he felt the need to give the self more **autonomy**, and his **final** formulation **rejected** determinism. The shift itself invites criticism of consistency.

**5. The creative self is unexplained.** The **creative self** is supposed to fashion a style of life **before age 5** from the materials of heredity and environment. But it is **not clear how a young child** could make such momentous decisions. Adler's stance is clear (he favoured free will and rejected the idea that we are victims of innate forces and childhood events), but the **mechanism** by which the style of life is formed is not.

**6. Method** (from Section 4.9). The case-study data cannot be **replicated**, were **not gathered systematically**, were **not verified**, and Adler did not describe his **procedures**; most of his propositions have **resisted scientific validation**.

| Is a criticism of Adler | Is NOT a criticism of Adler |
|---|---|
| Oversimplified; too easy (Freud's charge) | Too complex or obscure (that is aimed at **Jung** and **Freud**) |
| Relies on common sense | Overemphasises **sex** and biology (that is aimed at **Freud**) |
| Inconsistent, unsystematic, gaps | Ignores the social environment (the reverse is true) |
| Shifted from determinism to free will | Pessimistic view of human nature (Adler is optimistic) |
| Creative self vague: how does a child under 5 choose? | Ignores conscious processes (Adler centred them) |
| Unverifiable case-study data | |

### Later Recognition and Influence

Although his ideas were widely absorbed, Adler's **public recognition declined after his death in 1937**, and he has received relatively **little credit** since. Many of his concepts were **borrowed without acknowledgement**. Schultz gives a telling example:

- The ***Times*** of London, in its **obituary of Sigmund Freud**, named **Freud** as originator of the term **inferiority complex**.
- When **Carl Jung** died, the ***New York Times*** said **Jung** had coined it.
- **Neither** paper mentioned **Adler**, who actually originated it.

Adler did receive one unusual honour: a **British composer named a string quartet** after him.

Adler's followers argue that individual psychology remains popular with **psychologists, psychiatrists, social workers and educators**. The **North American Society of Adlerian Psychology** publishes a quarterly journal, ***Individual Psychology: The Journal of Adlerian Theory, Research and Practice***. Other **Adlerian journals** appear in **Germany, Italy and France**, and **Adlerian training institutes** exist in **New York, Chicago** and other cities.

> **Trap.** The term **inferiority complex** is **Adler's**. Freud's obituary in the London *Times* wrongly credited **Freud**; Jung's obituary in the *New York Times* wrongly credited **Jung**. An MCQ asking "who coined inferiority complex" has only one right answer. Classic background: many writers also see Adler as a precursor of the **cognitive therapies** (Albert Ellis, Aaron Beck) because of his stress on the beliefs and goals through which people interpret experience; Schultz's own list of heirs is Horney, Allport, Maslow, Rotter and the ego psychologists.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the whole chapter. If you can follow each from beginning to end, you understand Adler rather than merely remembering him.

1. **From a felt minus to a striven-for plus.** Every module is one stage of the same movement. Infant helplessness produces **inferiority feelings** (4.2); the push to overcome them is **compensation**; the direction of the push is **striving for superiority, or perfection**, steered by **fictional** goals (4.3); the personal route a given person takes is the **style of life** (4.4). The pathologies are this movement going wrong: stalled (**inferiority complex**), faked (**superiority complex**), or bent towards private gain instead of the common good (**dominant, getting, avoiding** types).
2. **The future pulls; the past does not push.** Fictional finalism, the tension-increasing striving, dreams as rehearsal for current problems, early recollections as a reflection of the **present** lifestyle: in each case Adler explains behaviour by where the person is heading. This is the deepest break with Freud, and it is why Maslow saw Adler as an ally.
3. **The person is social before he is biological.** Inferiority comes from the infant's social situation; social interest is the measure of health; the mother is the first teacher of cooperation; birth order is a social position, not a biological one. The one biological concession is that the **potential** for social interest and the striving for perfection are innate.
4. **Materials versus the builder.** Heredity, organic weakness, pampering, neglect, birth order and parents supply **materials**; the **creative power of the self** does the building. This is how Adler reconciles "style of life fixed by 4 or 5" with "we are not victims of our past", and it is also the theory's weakest joint (how does a four-year-old choose?).
5. **The theory is the theorist's life.** Rickets, the healthy older brother Sigmund, dethronement at 2, the near-death at 4, the false cemetery memory, the shipwreck dream, the power struggles in Freud's circle and later mellowing: each maps onto a concept. Schultz uses this, as with Freud, both to explain the theory and to hint at its limits.
6. **Research is kindest to the simplest ideas.** Social interest, neglect, pampering, the themes of early recollections and the present-focused function of dreams have good support. Birth order is heavily researched but mixed (first-borns' IQ and achievement fairly robust; only-child predictions largely disconfirmed), and the creative self remains untestable.

**Where Adler sits among the theorists in the book.**

| Compared with | Similarity | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Freud** (Ch 2) | Childhood matters; case-study method; dreams as data | Adler: social not sexual; conscious not unconscious; future goals not past causes; unique not universal; optimistic; rejects Oedipus complex and penis envy |
| **Jung** (Ch 3) | Fellow defector from Freud; future-oriented strivings; both opposed Freud's sexual emphasis | Jung enlarged the unconscious (collective unconscious, archetypes); Adler shrank it and put consciousness at the centre |
| **Horney** (Ch 5) | Social forces shape personality; rejects penis envy; cultural causes of women's inferiority | Horney builds on basic anxiety and neurotic needs; Adler on inferiority and striving |
| **Erikson** (Ch 6) | Social influences across development | Erikson extends development across the whole life span through stages; Adler fixes the style of life by 4 or 5 |
| **Allport** (Ch 7) | Unity and uniqueness of the whole person | Allport works through traits and functional autonomy |
| **Maslow** (Ch 9) | Creative, growth-oriented, future-directed person; striving for completion foreshadows self-actualisation | Maslow builds a needs hierarchy; SII findings link social interest to self-actualisation in women |
| **Rotter** (social learning) | Expectancies and goals guide behaviour; Rotter praised Adler's insight | Rotter's framework is experimental and cognitive-behavioural |

## Worked example

*Rahul, 26, is referred to a college counselling centre by his father after failing for the third time to clear a professional examination he says he "should have passed easily". He is the youngest of three; his elder brother is a surgeon and his sister a chartered accountant. He describes himself as "the baby of the family" whose mother "did everything" for him; she still fills in his forms and phones his teachers. At home he talks about the exam as beneath him and says the papers were "badly set". He has stopped meeting friends who cleared it. Asked for his earliest memory, he recalls standing at the top of a staircase, aged about four, crying until his mother came and carried him down while his brother and sister ran ahead. He reports a recurring dream of arriving at an exam hall in the wrong clothes and finding his legs will not move.*

Work through it with Adler's concepts rather than labels.

**Which birth position, and what does Adler predict?** Rahul is the **youngest**. Adler's youngest child has two possible paths: the drive to catch older siblings can make him a fast developer and high achiever, or, if he is **excessively pampered**, he may keep the **helplessness and dependency** of childhood and find adulthood hard. Everything in the history (mother filling in forms, carrying him down the stairs) points to the second path. Note that birth order sets a **probability**, not a fate; it is the combination of position and **pampering** that matters. Research gives the prediction some teeth: last-borns show higher **binge drinking** in college (Laird & Shelton, 2006), so the counsellor should ask about alcohol.

**Is his problem inferiority feelings or an inferiority complex?** Feeling outshone by a surgeon brother and an accountant sister is a perfectly normal **inferiority feeling**; on its own it could have motivated him. But he has **not compensated**. Faced with an obstacle, he withdraws from peers and stops trying, which is the picture of an **inferiority complex**. Of Adler's three sources, **spoiling** fits best: the pampered child meets an obstacle he was never trained to handle and concludes, privately, that something is wrong with him.

**Then why does he talk as if the exam is beneath him?** That is the **superiority complex** sitting on top of the inferiority complex: an exaggerated opinion of his abilities, voiced as boasting and disparagement ("badly set papers"), without the achievement to back it. Adler would read it as **overcompensation**, a cover for, not a cure of, the felt inferiority.

**What is his style of life?** Most likely the **getting** type (he expects satisfaction and help to come from others, especially his mother) shading into the **avoiding** type (he now avoids the exam and the friends who passed, so that he cannot fail again in front of them). Both lack **social interest**, which is why his difficulties are spreading from work into friendship, two of Adler's three life problems (behaviour towards others, occupation, love).

**What do the early recollection and the dream add?** The staircase memory is a scene of **helplessness rescued by mother** while the siblings **run ahead**: exactly the lifestyle in miniature. Whether it really happened makes **little difference** to Adler, because what Rahul remembers is selected to fit his present view of himself (the Adlerian emphasis on **what is remembered**, not what is repressed). The dream combines two Table 4.1 themes, **wrong clothes** (troubled by his own faults) and **paralysis** (facing a problem that feels insoluble), and an **exam setting** (feeling unprepared). Adler would not hunt for a disguised wish; he would treat the dream as Rahul's **current problem** and his present intention towards it, which at the moment is to stand still.

**What is his fictional final goal?** Something like "to be recognised as just as capable as my brother and sister without having to risk being seen to fail". Like all fictional goals it is subjective and untestable, but it explains the behaviour better than any past cause: avoiding the exam protects the fiction.

**What would an Adlerian do?** Sit face to face, not behind a couch; use some gentle humour about the "papers were badly set" line; collect more early recollections; and work on **encouragement** and **social interest** (cooperation with peers, contribution rather than rescue) rather than on insight into the past. The **creative power of the self** is the hopeful part of the formulation: the style of life is stable, but Rahul built it, and he is not simply its victim. The counsellor could also measure social interest (the **SII** or **BASIS-A**) at the start and again later.

**What would you not say?** That his difficulties are caused by repressed Oedipal wishes towards his mother (Adler rejected the Oedipus complex), or that his inferiority feelings are a sign of abnormality (they are universal; the problem is that he has not compensated).

## MCQ traps

1. **Born 1870, died 1937**; Freud was born 1856, so they were **14 years apart**, from the **same city** and the **same university** (Vienna).
2. Adler had **rickets** (vitamin D deficiency, softened bones); a **younger brother died in the bed next to him** when he was **3**; he nearly died of **pneumonia at 4** and decided to become a doctor (Orgler, 1963).
3. He was **pampered by his mother**, **dethroned at 2**, then **his father's favourite**, which is why he rejected the **Oedipus complex**.
4. His rival **older brother was named Sigmund**; Adler was a **second-born**. Freud was a **first-born**, called by Adler a **typical eldest son**.
5. A teacher advised apprenticing him to a **shoemaker**; he was worst at **mathematics** and rose to the top of the class.
6. Medical career sequence: **ophthalmology → general medicine → neurology and psychiatry**.
7. Joined Freud's circle in **1902** (one of **four** invited to weekly meetings at Freud's home); a **9-year** association; **never Freud's student, disciple or analysand**.
8. By **1910**, **president** of the Vienna Psychoanalytical Society and **co-editor** of its journal; then broke away.
9. **1912**: Society for Individual Psychology; **1914 to 1918**: Austrian army; post-war **child-counselling clinics** with **group** methods (forerunner of group therapy); **1926** first US visit; **1929** moved to New York; died **1937** in **Scotland** on a **56-lecture** tour.
10. **Inferiority feelings** are **universal and environmental** (infant helplessness), **not genetic**, and are the **source of all striving**.
11. **Inferiority complex** = **inability to compensate**; **superiority complex** = **overcompensation** (boasting, vanity, denigrating others).
12. Three causes of an inferiority complex: **organic inferiority, spoiling, neglect**. 11th ed says "organic"; older texts say "**organ** inferiority".
13. **Demosthenes** (stutter → orator) and **Theodore Roosevelt** (sickly → fitness, 26th US president) are the compensation examples; Adler's circus and gymnast patients came from a nearby **amusement park**.
14. **Masculine protest** (inferiority equated with femininity; will to power, aggression) is Adler's **early, abandoned** idea.
15. **Striving for superiority** = drive for **perfection** (Latin root: to complete or finish); **not** being better than others, **not** the superiority complex; Adler called it the **fundamental fact of life** (1930).
16. Striving **increases** tension (contrast Freud's tension reduction) and operates for **society** as well as the individual.
17. **Fictional finalism**: imagined, untestable goals guide behaviour; most pervasive fiction = **perfection**, best formulated as **God**; Adler preferred "**subjective final goal**" or "**guiding self-ideal**" (Watts & Holden, 1994).
18. Adler's own fictional goal: to **conquer death**, pursued by becoming a **physician**.
19. **Style of life** is crystallised by **age 4 or 5** and stays constant; shaped by **birth order** and the **parent-child relationship**.
20. **Creative power of the self** = ability to create an appropriate style of life; Adler's answer to **determinism**.
21. Three life problems: **behaviour towards others, occupation, love**. Four styles: **dominant (ruling), getting (most common), avoiding, socially useful (the only healthy one)**.
22. **Alcoholics, drug addicts, suicides** = the **less virulent dominant** type (they hurt others by hurting themselves); sadists, delinquents and sociopaths = the extreme dominant type.
23. **Social interest** = **Gemeinschaftsgefühl** = "**community feeling**" (Stepansky, 1983); **potential innate**, development depends on early experience, especially the **mother** (cooperation, companionship, courage).
24. Adler's motivational concept moved from **power/dominance** (while struggling inside Freud's circle) to **social interest** (after independence and recognition).
25. **Dethronement** hits harder if the first-born was **pampered** and if **younger** when the rival arrives (8-year-old less upset than 2-year-old).
26. First-borns: **past-oriented, nostalgic, pessimistic**, power-concerned, authoritarian, conservative; second-borns: **optimistic, competitive, ambitious**; youngest: fast development, or dependency if pampered; only child: **matures early**, never learned to **share or compete**.
27. **Entrance gates to mental life** = **birth order, early recollections, dream analysis** (not free association, not tests).
28. Early recollections: **real or fantasy makes little difference**; Adler studies **what is remembered**, Freud **what is forgotten** (repression) (Kopp & Eckstein, 2004).
29. Adler's cemetery memory: age **5**; ran through it about a **dozen** times; **30 years later** classmates said **there was no cemetery**.
30. Adlerian dreams: **current problems and intentions**, not wish fulfilment; falling = **loss of prestige**; flying = **ambition**; both = fear of being too ambitious and failing; chased = **weakness**; naked = fear of **giving oneself away**; exam = **unprepared**; paralysed = **insoluble problems**.
31. Adler **opposed personality tests** but **supported memory and intelligence tests**.
32. **SIS** (Crandall, **1981**) = **adjective pairs**; **SII** (Greever, Tseng, & Friedland, **1973**) = self-report statements; **BASIS-A** = **65 items, 5 dimensions** (Peluso et al., 2004).
33. Adler published only **two case fragments**, one written by a **patient**, one by a **patient's physician**; he knew **neither** patient.
34. **Grieser, Greenberg, & Harrison (1972)**: dreamers (woken in **NREM**) recalled **more** of the failed puzzle than the REM-woken no-dream group.
35. **Breger, Hunter, & Lane (1971)**: students before stressful **psychotherapy**, patients before **surgery**; dreams were about **current** worries.
36. **Strano & Petrocelli (2005)**: **moderate** inferiority feelings → **highest GPA**. **Kosaka (2008, China)**: grades → attractiveness → social skills with age.
37. **Jackson & Sechrest (1962)**: anxiety → **fear**; depression → **abandonment**; psychosomatic → **illness**.
38. **Lefkowitz & Tesiny (1984)**: neglect at **8**, MMPI depression at **18**. **Crook, Raskin, & Eliot (1981)**: depressed inpatients' reports **confirmed by relatives**.
39. **Capron (2004)**: overindulgent + overprotective → **low self-esteem**; overindulgent + over-domineering → **narcissism**; over-domineering mothers → more **therapy-seeking**.
40. SII: social interest **higher in women** and **increases with age** (Greever et al., 1973); high-SII women higher in **self-actualisation**.
41. **Belmont & Marolla (1973)**: **400,000** European men, IQ declines with birth order. **Kristensen & Bjerkedal (2007)**: **240,000+** Norwegian recruits. Explanation: first-born's **exclusive exposure to adults**, not genes.
42. **Bogaert (2003)**: number of **older brothers** predicts male same-sex attraction (nearly 20,000 in Britain, about 3,500 in US); **older sisters do not** predict women's orientation.
43. **Sulloway & Zweigenhaft (2010)**: younger brothers **10 times** more likely to **steal bases**. **Laird & Shelton (2006)**: **binge drinking** higher in **last-borns**.
44. **Falbo & Polit (1986)**: **115 studies**, only-borns higher achievement and intelligence, **comparable** adjustment; **Falbo (1978)** more cooperative; **Jiao, Ji, & Jing (1986)** more self-centred.
45. **Gundersen et al. (2011)**: **believers** in birth-order effects are **lower in openness, higher in neuroticism**.
46. Adler proposed an **aggressive drive more than 12 years before Freud**; he rejected **penis envy** as a male myth and supported women's emancipation.
47. Freud: Adler's ideas could be learned in **two weeks**; Adler: it took him **40 years** to make his psychology simple.
48. London **Times** credited **Freud**, **New York Times** credited **Jung** with "inferiority complex"; it was **Adler's**. A British composer named a **string quartet** after him.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Individual psychology** | Adler's theory of personality; stresses the uniqueness of each person and rejects the universal biological motives Freud proposed |
| **Inferiority feelings** | The normal, universal sense of being smaller or less able, rooted in infant helplessness; the source of all human striving |
| **Compensation** | The motivation to overcome real or imagined inferiority and so reach higher levels of development |
| **Inferiority complex** | The condition that develops when a person cannot compensate for normal inferiority feelings; poor self-opinion and helplessness |
| **Organic inferiority** | A defective or weak organ or body part that shapes personality through efforts to compensate for it (older term: organ inferiority) |
| **Spoiling (pampering)** | Satisfying a child's every wish so that he never learns to wait, cope or cooperate; a cause of the inferiority complex |
| **Neglect** | Parental indifference or hostility producing a child who feels worthless, angry and distrustful; a cause of the inferiority complex |
| **Superiority complex** | The condition that develops when a person overcompensates for normal inferiority feelings; boasting, vanity, belittling others |
| **Overcompensation** | Going beyond compensation into an exaggerated display of ability, producing the superiority complex |
| **Masculine protest** | Adler's early, later abandoned idea that inferiority equals femininity and is compensated by a drive to power and aggression |
| **Striving for superiority** | The innate urge towards perfection or completion that motivates every person; not the wish to dominate |
| **Finalism** | The idea that we have an ultimate goal, a final state of being, and a need to move towards it |
| **Fictional finalism** | The idea that imagined or potential goals, untestable against reality, guide our behaviour (Adler's preferred terms: subjective final goal, guiding self-ideal) |
| **Style of life** | The unique character structure or pattern of behaviours by which each person strives for perfection; set by age 4 or 5 |
| **Creative power of the self** | The ability to create an appropriate style of life from the materials of heredity and environment |
| **Dominant (ruling) type** | Style of life with a ruling attitude and little social awareness; ranges from sadists and sociopaths to addicts and suicides |
| **Getting type** | Style of life that expects satisfaction from others and so becomes dependent; Adler's most common type |
| **Avoiding type** | Style of life that dodges life's problems to avoid any possibility of failure |
| **Socially useful type** | The healthy style of life; cooperates with others and meets problems through social interest |
| **Social interest** | Our innate potential to cooperate with other people to achieve personal and societal goals |
| **Gemeinschaftsgefühl** | Adler's German term for social interest, best rendered as "community feeling" |
| **Birth order** | A person's position among siblings, which Adler treated as a major social influence on the style of life |
| **Dethronement** | The first-born's loss of sole attention and privilege when a second child is born |
| **Entrance gates to mental life** | Adler's three primary assessment methods: order of birth, early recollections, dream analysis |
| **Early recollections** | An assessment technique in which a person's earliest memories, real or imagined, are assumed to reveal the primary interest of his or her life |
| **Social Interest Scale (SIS)** | Crandall's (1981) measure in which people choose, from pairs of adjectives, the attribute they would like to have |
| **Social Interest Index (SII)** | Greever, Tseng and Friedland's (1973) self-report inventory of statements reflecting the ability to accept and cooperate with others |
| **BASIS-A** | Basic Adlerian Scales for Interpersonal Success, a 65-item self-report measure of lifestyle and social interest on five dimensions |
| **Overindulgence** | Pampering by persistent gratification; leads to entitlement and manipulative behaviour |
| **Overpermissiveness** | Pampering by letting the child do anything; leads to disregard for rules and others' rights |
| **Overdomination** | Pampering by making all the child's decisions; leads to low self-confidence and adult dependence |
| **Overprotection** | Pampering by constant warnings of danger; leads to generalised anxiety and social avoidance |
| **Narcissism** | Inflated self-importance with little empathy or responsibility towards others and a readiness to exploit them; linked to pampering |

## Likely exam points

1. **Individual psychology** emphasises the uniqueness of each person, social rather than biological forces, consciousness rather than the unconscious, and a minimal role for sex.
2. **Life-to-theory links**: rickets and a healthy older brother (inferiority, compensation); dethronement at 2 (dethronement, pampering); father's favourite (rejection of the Oedipus complex); pneumonia at 4 (fictional goal of conquering death); false cemetery memory (early recollections).
3. **Relations with Freud**: 1902 to about 1911; one of four invited; never a disciple or analysand; president of the Vienna Psychoanalytical Society by 1910; mutual hostility after the break.
4. **Career dates**: 1912 Society for Individual Psychology; child-guidance clinics with group methods; 1926 US; 1929 New York; 1937 death in Scotland on a 56-lecture tour; "America's first popular psychologist".
5. **Inferiority feelings** are universal, environmental (not genetic), necessary, and the source of all striving through **compensation**.
6. **Inferiority complex vs superiority complex**: failure to compensate vs overcompensation; three causes of the complex are organic inferiority, spoiling and neglect.
7. **Organic inferiority** examples: Demosthenes, Theodore Roosevelt, Adler's circus and gymnast patients; influenced the study of psychosomatic disorders.
8. **Masculine protest** (early) → **striving for superiority/perfection** (mature) → increasingly framed by **social interest**.
9. **Striving for superiority** means completion or perfection, is innate and future-oriented, increases tension, and operates for society as well as the individual.
10. **Fictional finalism**: untestable imagined goals guide behaviour; perfection is the most pervasive; God its best formulation; preferred names "subjective final goal" and "guiding self-ideal".
11. **Style of life** is fixed by 4 or 5, learned socially, shaped by birth order and parents, and stable thereafter.
12. **Creative power of the self** resolves the determinism problem; experiences are materials, the person is the builder; criticised as vague.
13. **Four styles of life**: dominant/ruling, getting (most common), avoiding, socially useful (healthy); the first three lack social interest and yield neuroses and psychoses.
14. **Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl, community feeling)**: innate potential, developed through early experience, especially the mother's teaching of cooperation, companionship and courage.
15. **Birth order**: dethronement of the first-born (worse if pampered or very young); first-born past-oriented and authoritarian; second-born competitive and optimistic; youngest high achiever or dependent; only child mature early but unable to share or compete.
16. **Entrance gates to mental life**: birth order, early recollections, dream analysis; Adler's face-to-face, informal, humorous therapy style; reading posture, handshake and sleep position.
17. **Early recollections**: real or fantasy equally useful; what is remembered (Adler) vs what is repressed (Freud); Table 4.2 kinds of memories and themes.
18. **Dreams**: current problems and intended solutions, future-oriented, unique to the dreamer; Adler's shipwreck dream; falling, flying, chasing, nakedness and Table 4.1 meanings.
19. **Social-interest measures**: SIS (Crandall, 1981, adjective pairs), SII (Greever, Tseng, & Friedland, 1973), BASIS-A (65 items, five dimensions); Adler himself opposed personality tests.
20. **Research method criticisms**: two case fragments only; unverifiable, unsystematic, unreplicated data; no experimental method.
21. **Dream research**: Grieser, Greenberg, & Harrison (1972) REM-deprivation and puzzle recall; Breger, Hunter, & Lane (1971) pre-therapy and pre-surgery dreams.
22. **Inferiority research**: moderate inferiority → best GPA (Strano & Petrocelli, 2005); age-shifting sources in China (Kosaka, 2008).
23. **Early-recollection research**: Jackson & Sechrest (1962) fear/abandonment/illness; alcoholics, police PTSD, criminals, delinquents, dangerous patients; recollections are subjective re-creations (Statton & Wilborn, 1991); invented memories share own themes (Buchanan et al., 1991); career prediction.
24. **Neglect and pampering research**: Lefkowitz & Tesiny (1984) longitudinal MMPI depression; Crook et al. (1981); four types of pampering and Capron (2004).
25. **Social-interest research**: lower stress, depression, anxiety, hostility; better well-being, life satisfaction, health; less reoffending; higher in women and with age.
26. **First-born research**: achievement and eminence, Belmont & Marolla (1973) IQ gradient explained by exposure to adults, higher IQ but lower GPA than later-borns, Schachter's suggestibility and anxiety under stress.
27. **Other birth-order research**: Bogaert (2003) older brothers; Sulloway & Zweigenhaft (2010) base-stealing; Laird & Shelton (2006) binge drinking; Falbo & Polit (1986) only children; Gundersen et al. (2011) believers' personalities.
28. **Human nature**: free will, mostly nurture, future over past, uniqueness, growth, optimism; the antithesis of Freud.
29. **Contributions**: influence on Horney, Allport, Maslow, Rotter and the ego psychologists; aggressive drive before Freud; rejection of penis envy; inferiority complex, compensation, birth order; forerunner of social psychology and group therapy; popular self-help books (Fig 4.1).
30. **Criticisms**: oversimplified (Freud's "two weeks"), common sense, inconsistent with gaps, shift from determinism to free will, unexplained creative self; Adler's reply that simplicity took 40 years.
31. **Later recognition**: decline after 1937; inferiority complex misattributed to Freud (London Times) and Jung (New York Times); string quartet; NASAP's quarterly journal; institutes in New York and Chicago.
