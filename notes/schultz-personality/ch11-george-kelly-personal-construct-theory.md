# Chapter 11: George Kelly: Personal Construct Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** Every theory you have met so far in Schultz explains personality with some kind of force: instincts and the unconscious for Freud, inherited archetypes for Jung, inferiority and striving for Adler, basic anxiety for Horney, traits and motives for Allport, needs and self-actualisation for Maslow and Rogers. George Kelly throws all of that out. His claim is that you can describe a whole personality by asking one question: **how does this person interpret the world, and what does that interpretation lead them to expect?** People, for Kelly, behave like working scientists. Each of us builds private hypotheses about what people and events are like, called **personal constructs**, uses them to predict what will happen next, acts on the predictions, and keeps or revises the constructs depending on how well they work. Personality is the organised system of these constructs. Because constructs are interpretations rather than facts, they can always be replaced by better ones, and Kelly built his theory of freedom on that point (**constructive alternativism**). The formal theory is set out like a scientific system: one **fundamental postulate** and **eleven corollaries**. From it came a distinctive clinical toolkit: the interview taken at face value, the **self-characterisation sketch**, the **Role Construct Repertory (Rep) Test** with its grid, and **fixed role therapy**. A large research programme grew out of the Rep Test, most importantly the study of **cognitive complexity versus cognitive simplicity**.

**Where Kelly sits in the book: the Cognitive Approach.** Schultz gives Kelly a part of the book to himself, called **The Cognitive Approach**. *Cognition* means knowing, the act or process by which we come to know something, and a cognitive approach to personality asks how people come to know their surroundings and themselves: how they perceive, judge, learn, think, decide and solve problems. Schultz calls this a "truly psychological" approach because it deals only with **conscious mental activity**. In it you will not find needs, drives or emotions working as separate engines of personality; they are treated as aspects of personality that the cognitive processes govern. Cognition is not new to the book. Present-day psychoanalysts grant it importance; Erikson gave the ego and its thinking much more independence than Freud had; Maslow and Rogers wrote about how we perceive and evaluate our experience; Allport wrote about human reasoning; Adler's **creative self** grows out of how we perceive and interpret experience; and the social-learning theorists of Chapter 13 also rely on cognitive processes. The difference is one of totality. Those theorists added cognition to an account built on something else. **Kelly tried to explain every part of personality, emotion included, purely in terms of cognitive processes.**

**How to read this chapter.** Section 11.1 explains why Kelly's theory looks so different from everything else in the book, what "the person as scientist" means, and why the modern cognitive movement did not adopt him. Section 11.2 is his life, and it is unusually useful here: a physics graduate who drifted through odd jobs and then learnt therapy on the road among Kansas schoolchildren during the Depression, which is almost exactly the theory's picture of a rational, problem-solving client. Sections 11.3 and 11.4 are the core and the most examined part: the construct, constructive alternativism, the fundamental postulate, and all eleven corollaries in the book's order. Learn the corollaries as pairs that examiners like to confuse (individuality and commonality, commonality and sociality, organisation and fragmentation, experience and modulation). Section 11.5 gives Kelly's answers to Schultz's six human-nature questions. Section 11.6 teaches the assessment and therapy techniques, including how a Rep grid is filled in, with the book's sample grid. Section 11.7 walks through the research, and 11.8 weighs the theory up. Where this chapter needs standard textbook material that the 11th edition does not print (Kelly's formal wording of the postulate, his redefinitions of anxiety, threat, fear, guilt and hostility, Bieri's role in cognitive complexity, his habit of giving clients invented interpretations), it is marked **Classic background**.

## 11.1 The Cognitive Movement in Psychology

### A theory without the usual machinery

Open any other chapter in Schultz and you will find some of the following: the unconscious, the ego, needs, drives, stimuli and responses, reinforcement, motivation, emotion. Kelly told his readers, in advance, that **none of these would appear in his system**. Not even motivation and emotion. That is the first thing to understand about personal construct theory and the first thing examiners test, because it is so unlike every other theory in the syllabus.

The obvious objection is that you cannot understand people without their motives and feelings. Kelly's reply rests on a single idea. Each of us develops a set of **cognitive constructs** about our surroundings. That is, we take the events and relationships of our lives and arrange them into a system, a pattern of interpretation. The pattern lets us make **predictions** about ourselves, about other people and about events, and the predictions decide how we respond and what we do. So the route into anyone's personality is to discover the pattern: the particular way that person organises, or *construes*, their world.

The slogan that follows is worth memorising because Schultz states it directly: for Kelly, **our interpretation of an event matters more than the event itself**. Picture two students who get the same poor mark on the same internal assessment. One construes it as "proof that I am not cut out for psychology" and stops attending lectures. The other construes it as "the paper tested the two units I skipped" and changes how she revises. The event was identical; the behaviour that followed was opposite, because the constructs were different. Kelly would say that to predict what each student does next you need to know nothing about her drives or her childhood. You need her constructs.

The formal definition in Schultz's margin: **personal construct theory** is Kelly's account of personality in terms of cognitive processes, in which people are able to interpret behaviour and events and to use that understanding both to guide their own behaviour and to predict the behaviour of others.

### Kelly against behaviourism and psychoanalysis

Kelly shared one conviction with Maslow: he rejected both of the dominant schools of his day. In his reading, **behaviourism** made people passive responders to stimuli in their environment, and **psychoanalysis** made them passive responders to unconscious forces within. Either way the person was being pushed. Both schools, he argued, denied what he took to be the central human capacity: the ability to run our own lives, make our own decisions and follow the course of action we have chosen. Fransella and Neimeyer (2003, p. 25) summarise his position by saying that, for Kelly, people are "forms of motion" who propel themselves; nobody and nothing does it to us.

> **Trap.** Kelly's opposition to both behaviourism and psychoanalysis is the same opposition Maslow voiced, and his view of people is optimistic and free-willed, so students sometimes file Kelly under the humanistic approach. Schultz files him under **the Cognitive Approach**, as its only theorist. If an MCQ asks which approach Kelly represents, the answer is cognitive, not humanistic, not social-learning.

### An Experiential-Based Theory

Kelly's theory came out of his **clinical work**, as Freud's did, but he read his clinical experience in a completely different way, for reasons his biography (11.2) makes clear. The model of human nature he arrived at is unusual: **people function the way scientists do**.

Think about what a scientist actually does. She constructs a theory, derives a hypothesis from it, and tests the hypothesis against reality, usually by experiment. If the results support the theory, she keeps it. If they do not, she must throw the theory out or modify it and test it again. Schultz points out that this is exactly how personality psychologists themselves work.

Here is Kelly's sharp observation. Psychologists credit **themselves** with this rational, hypothesis-testing intelligence, but they rarely credit **their subjects** with it. It is as if psychology runs two theories of human nature side by side: one for the scientist, who reasons, predicts and revises, and another for everyone else, who is moved by conflicting drives, swamped by unconscious forces, and lives mainly at the level of emotion, seldom using thought to learn, evaluate or solve problems. Nobody, Kelly noted, describes psychologists that way.

### Are Psychologists Superior Beings?

Kelly's answer is the obvious one: **no**. Psychologists are no different from the people they study. Whatever explains the behaviour of one must explain the behaviour of the other. Both are trying to **predict and control** the events of their lives, and both are capable of doing so **rationally**. The chapter's epigraph captures how Kelly reached this view: he reasoned that whatever seemed true of himself was probably just as true of others, so if he was the initiator of his own actions, so were they.

So, like scientists, all of us build theories. Kelly called these theories **personal constructs**: the working hypotheses through which an individual tries to anticipate and manage events. And he drew the methodological conclusion directly: **to understand someone's personality, examine his or her personal constructs.**

*Classic background:* this model is usually summarised by Kelly's own phrase **"man the scientist"** (Kelly, 1955), or in modern texts "the person as scientist". It is a metaphor about **method** (forming, testing and revising predictions), not a claim that ordinary people are cool, objective or well-informed. A paranoid patient testing the hypothesis that colleagues are plotting against him is, in Kelly's sense, doing science badly, not failing to do it at all.

> **Trap.** "Person as scientist" does **not** mean that people are accurate or unbiased. It means that everyone predicts, tests and revises. The scientist metaphor concerns the *process* of construing, and Kelly's whole therapy assumes that people often hold constructs that predict poorly.

### Kelly and the Cognitive Movement

A natural assumption is that Kelly, as the "cognitive" personality theorist, must be a founder of **cognitive psychology**, the movement that began around **1960** and now dominates mainstream experimental psychology. He is not. Despite the shared vocabulary, the cognitive movement **did not take up Kelly's work**, because his theory fits neither its subject matter nor its methods.

The contrast is worth setting out:

| | Kelly | The cognitive movement |
|---|---|---|
| Who | A clinician | Mainly experimental psychologists |
| What is studied | The conscious constructs by which a person arranges his or her life | Cognitive variables **and** overt behaviour |
| Where | The clinic and therapy room | The laboratory, an experimental setting |
| Scope | Personality | Not limited to personality; overt behaviour and learning in social situations |
| Key assumption | Behaviour follows from how a person construes and anticipates events | Cognitive processes such as learning shape a person's response to a given stimulus situation |

Although cognitive psychology took hold after Kelly had published his theory, **his theory had little influence on it**. At most, Schultz says, Kelly can be considered a **precursor** of contemporary cognitive psychology. The two share the word *cognitive*, and with it an interest in conscious activity, and very little else. Schultz's balanced verdict: Kelly's recognition of cognition was notable, but his work is not part of mainstream American psychology as experimentalists define it, and that does not reduce its usefulness for understanding personality.

> **Trap.** "Kelly founded the cognitive revolution" or "Kelly's theory shaped modern cognitive psychology" are false. The cognitive movement began around 1960 and **did not embrace** Kelly; Schultz allows him only the status of a **precursor**. The social-learning theorist whose work belongs to that broader cognitive turn, Bandura, comes in Chapter 13, not here.

## 11.2 The Life of Kelly (1905 to 1967)

Kelly's life matters for the exam more than its brevity suggests, because almost every distinctive feature of his theory can be traced to it: the scientist model to his physics degree, the exclusion of stimulus-response psychology and Freud to his reactions as a student, the role-playing therapy to his years around drama, and above all the theory's **rational, articulate client** to the kind of people he happened to treat.

### A Kansas childhood

George Kelly was born in **1905 on a farm in Kansas**, the **only child** of his parents. He was given plenty of attention and affection. His parents were **religious fundamentalists** with a strong commitment to helping people less fortunate than themselves, and they disapproved of every kind of frivolous amusement; dancing and card games were out. When he was **4**, the family made a trip by **covered wagon to Colorado** to try farming there, but they soon came back to Kansas.

It is reasonable to see two threads beginning here. One is a practical, useful-to-others seriousness that runs through Kelly's later career (travelling clinics for schoolchildren referred by their teachers, lectures on easing international tension). The other is self-reliance: a boy raised largely outside formal institutions, moving where the family's prospects took them, is well placed to arrive at a theory in which each person builds and tests his own map of the world.

### An Erratic Education

Kelly's early schooling was patchy, delivered as much by his parents as by teachers. At **13** he went off to high school in **Wichita**, and from then on he rarely lived at home.

In **1926** he graduated from **Park College** in Parkville, Missouri, with a bachelor's degree in **physics and mathematics**. Hold on to that fact: the most distinctive feature of Kelly's personality theory is that it is written like a theory in physics, one fundamental postulate followed by formal corollaries, and its model of the person is a scientist who forms and tests hypotheses. By the time he graduated, though, his interests had moved from science to **social problems**, and he had no clear idea what to do next.

What followed was a string of unrelated jobs, and the sheer variety is itself an exam point:

1. A short spell as an **engineer**.
2. Teaching at a **labour college in Minneapolis**.
3. Teaching **speech** for the **American Banking Association**, and teaching **citizenship classes to immigrants**.
4. Graduate study, ending in a **master's degree in educational sociology** from the **University of Kansas** at Lawrence.
5. A post at a **junior college in Iowa**, where he taught a range of courses and **coached the drama programme**.

The last item deserves a note. Kelly's most original therapy, fixed role therapy (11.6), asks a client to act out a written character, first in the therapist's office and then in daily life, and his self-characterisation technique asks the client to describe himself as the principal character in a play. Schultz traces fixed role therapy to a friend Kelly watched being changed by a part in a college play; the years spent coaching drama are part of the same background.

### No Interest in Psychology

Nothing in this career pointed towards psychology, and Kelly had not liked the subject in college. He later described his first psychology course with some humour (Kelly, 1969). He sat at the back of a large class, chair tipped against the wall, half listening. After a few weeks of preliminaries the lecturer wrote on the board an **S, an arrow and an R**, and Kelly sat up, expecting that the real content was about to begin. He gave it a few more classes and then gave up, because he could not work out **what the arrow between stimulus and response stood for**, and he says he never did.

He tried **psychoanalysis** too, with no better result. He could not later recall which book of Freud's he had attempted, only his growing disbelief that anyone "could write such nonsense, much less publish it" (Kelly, 1969, p. 47).

Both reactions reappear in the theory. Kelly's system has **no stimuli and responses and no reinforcement**, because he could never see what the S-R arrow explained; and it has **no unconscious**, because psychoanalysis struck him as unbelievable. His personal constructs are what he put in place of both: the person stands between the event and the action, and what connects them is the person's **interpretation**.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Kelly's dismissal of Freud with Allport's (Chapter 7). Allport's turn away from psychoanalytic digging came from a single meeting with Freud in Vienna; Kelly's came from **reading** Freud as a student and finding it incredible. Kelly also rejected **behaviourism** from his very first course, which Allport did not do in the same way.

### Finding Himself and Psychology

The turn came in **1929**, when Kelly won a **fellowship to the University of Edinburgh** in Scotland. During that year he earned a **Bachelor of Education** degree, and it was there that he **finally became interested in psychology**. He came back to the United States for doctoral work at the **State University of Iowa** and received his **Ph.D. in 1931**. His specialty, notably, was **physiological psychology**.

> **Trap.** Sequence and places: B.A. physics and mathematics, **Park College, 1926**; M.A. educational sociology, **University of Kansas**; B.Ed., **Edinburgh, 1929 fellowship**; Ph.D., **State University of Iowa, 1931**. Kelly's doctoral training was in **physiological** psychology, not clinical psychology; he became a clinician only because of the Depression.

### Finding an Intellectual Approach to Counselling

Kelly's first academic job was at **Fort Hays Kansas State College**, which he began in the middle of the economic **depression of the 1930s**. There was no money for research in physiological psychology, the field he had trained in, so he **switched to clinical psychology**, where there was a real need. He built a **clinical psychology service for the local public schools** and for the students of his own college, and he set up **travelling clinics** that went from school to school. The circuit exposed him to a wide range of problems and let him experiment with different ways of treating them.

Kelly was not tied to any one therapeutic method or any one theory of personality. He felt free to use standard assessment and treatment techniques alongside methods he invented himself. In effect, Schultz says, he **taught himself therapy**, and he did it by assuming that **each new client was every bit as much a scientist as he was** (Routh, 2011). That assumption is the theory in embryo.

*Classic background:* Kelly later told a story from these Kansas years (Kelly, "The autobiography of a theory", reprinted in Kelly, 1969). Sceptical of the psychoanalytic explanations he had been taught, he began giving some clients **interpretations he had made up**, deliberately unconventional accounts of why they had their problems. He found that many clients improved anyway, provided the new account gave them a **different way of looking at their situation** that they could act on and test. He concluded that what helped was not the *truth* of the therapist's explanation but the client gaining a **workable alternative construction** of events. This is the clinical seed of **constructive alternativism** (11.3): there is never only one way to construe a situation, and a person stuck with one construction can be freed by trying another. It is also the seed of fixed role therapy, which hands the client a new construction deliberately and invites him to live it experimentally.

Now the point Schultz makes most strongly, and the one most often examined about Kelly's life. **The people Kelly treated were not severely disturbed.** They were not psychotic patients in mental hospitals, and they were not neurotics with crippling emotional problems. They were **students referred by their teachers** for counselling. Compared with the patients on a psychiatric ward or on an analyst's couch, these clients were far better able to talk about their problems **rationally**, to describe them in **intellectual terms**, which is exactly the level of functioning a school or college expects. The classroom trains people to analyse and to process information logically, and that attitude came with them into the counselling room.

Schultz's counterfactual makes the dependency explicit: if Kelly's formative clinical years had been spent with **schizophrenic patients in an institution**, his theory might not have leaned so heavily on cognitive, information-processing abilities.

> **Trap.** Freud's theory is criticised for resting on neurotic, middle-class Viennese patients; Kelly's is criticised for the mirror-image reason. His sample was **Midwestern students and young adults** who were articulate and rational, so his theory overstates rationality. In both cases the criticism is that the **clinical sample was unrepresentative** (see 11.8).

### Becoming a Major Force

**World War II** interrupted Kelly's career. He joined the **U.S. Navy** and worked as a psychologist in the **Bureau of Medicine and Surgery** in Washington, DC. After the war ended in **1945** he taught for a year at the **University of Maryland**, and then joined **Ohio State University**, where he **replaced Carl Rogers**.

He stayed at Ohio State for **19 years**, teaching, refining the theory and doing research. *Classic background:* it was from Ohio State that he published his major work, the two-volume ***The Psychology of Personal Constructs*** **(1955)**; Schultz cites Kelly (1955) throughout the chapter and refers to the scholarly style of "his two major books". Kelly also lectured at universities around the world on how personal construct theory might be used to **reduce international tensions**, an application that follows directly from the **sociality corollary** (11.4): two parties can work together only to the extent that each understands how the other construes events.

In **1965** he accepted an invitation from **Abraham Maslow** to take an **endowed chair at Brandeis University**, but he died soon afterwards (in 1967).

Kelly was a major figure in the growth of the **clinical psychology profession** after World War II. He held several honoured posts, including the **presidencies of the Clinical and the Consulting divisions of the American Psychological Association** and of the **American Board of Examiners in Professional Psychology**.

> **Trap.** Two different humanistic psychologists appear in Kelly's career and are easily swapped: Kelly **replaced Carl Rogers at Ohio State**; Kelly was **invited by Abraham Maslow to Brandeis** (1965). Neither makes Kelly a humanistic theorist.

**Life-to-theory summary.** Physics and mathematics degree: the scientist model and the postulate-and-corollaries format. Baffled by the S-R arrow and by Freud: no stimuli, responses, reinforcement or unconscious in the theory. Drama coaching and a friend changed by a stage role: fixed role therapy and the self-characterisation "as if in a play". Depression-era school clinics with articulate students: a rational, intellectual model of the person, and the main criticism of the theory. Teaching himself therapy by treating each client as a fellow scientist, and finding that invented interpretations helped when they offered a new way of seeing: constructive alternativism.

## 11.3 Personal Construct Theory

### Seeing the world through a pattern

Kelly's starting proposal is that ordinary people make sense of experience the way scientists do: they form **hypotheses** about their surroundings and test them against what daily life actually delivers. We take in the events of our lives, the raw facts or data of experience, and then **interpret** them in our own way. That personal act of interpreting, explaining or **construing** is what gives each of us a unique view of events (Winter & Proctor, 2014). It is the frame we fit events into. Kelly's own image is that we look at the world through "transparent patterns" laid over the realities the world is made of (Kelly, 1955, pp. 8 to 9).

Schultz turns this into an analogy that is easy to carry into an exam: **tinted sunglasses**. If your lenses are faintly blue and mine are faintly green, we can stand side by side, look at the same street, and come away with different pictures of it. Nothing in the street differs; the lenses do. Our constructs are the lenses. The particular, individual pattern of them that each person develops is what Kelly called the **construct system**.

### Life Is a Construct

A **construct** is a person's own way of looking at some part of life: an **intellectual hypothesis** he or she has devised to explain or interpret events. The margin definition adds the feature that the rest of the theory depends on: constructs are **bipolar, or dichotomous**, such as tall versus short or honest versus dishonest.

Constructs are not idle opinions. We **act on them**, in the expectation that they will predict and explain what happens, and like scientists we keep checking them: we behave on the basis of a construct and then evaluate the results.

Schultz's example is a student who is about to fail an introductory psychology course and wants the professor to pass him anyway. Over the semester he has watched the professor and has concluded that she is lordly and authoritarian in class and thinks rather too much of herself. That observation becomes a construct, which in turn yields a testable hypothesis: *flattering her inflated self-image will get me a better response.* He runs the experiment. He reads a paper the professor has published and tells her how impressive it is. Now the outcome decides the fate of the construct:

- If the professor is flattered and the grade comes back good, the construct has been **confirmed**. It has proved useful, and he will use it again with her, or with any other professor who seems to behave the same way.
- If the grade comes back as a fail, the construct has been **disconfirmed**. It was the wrong way to read her, and he will need a different one for dealing with this professor.

Notice what the example teaches, apart from cynicism about students. The student's behaviour (praising an article) cannot be explained by a drive, a reinforcement history or an unconscious wish. It is explained by **a prediction derived from a construct**, and what happens next to his behaviour depends on whether the prediction comes true.

A clinical version makes the same point. A young man construes his father as "critical versus accepting" and places him firmly at the critical pole. He predicts that any news he brings home will be criticised, so he stops telling his father anything. Because he never tests the construct, it is never disconfirmed, and the distance between them grows. A personal construct therapist would not ask what drive lies under this; she would ask what the construct is, how well it predicts, and what experiment might test it.

### Constructive Alternativism: Adapting to the World

Across a lifetime we build up a great many constructs, one for nearly every kind of person and situation we meet. The stock grows every time we encounter a new person or a new kind of situation. Just as important, we **change or drop constructs** from time to time as circumstances change. Kelly treated revision as a **necessary and continuous** process: for any situation we must always have **some alternative construct** available to apply.

Consider the alternative. If constructs could never be revised, which is what would follow if personality were fixed once and for all by childhood experience, we would have no way of coping with anything new. Kelly's name for our capacity to keep revising is **constructive alternativism**: the view that **we are not controlled by our constructs**, because we are always free to revise them or to replace them with alternatives. The margin definition is short: the idea that we are free to revise or replace our constructs with alternatives as needed.

*Classic background:* Kelly's most quoted statement of this principle is that no one needs to be completely hemmed in by circumstances or to be "the victim of his biography" (Kelly, 1955). Constructive alternativism is therefore Kelly's answer to determinism. Events do not dictate how we see them; there are always alternative constructions available, some of which will predict better than others, and choosing among them is up to us.

Constructive alternativism is the philosophical foundation of everything in 11.4 to 11.6. The corollaries describe how a construct system can change; the therapy techniques are ways of helping it change.

> **Trap.** Three words look alike and mean different things. A **construct** is a single bipolar interpretation (friendly versus hostile). **Constructive alternativism** is the philosophical principle that any construction of events can be replaced by an alternative, so we are free to revise. The **construction corollary** (11.4) is the specific corollary about anticipating events from their recurring themes. Older Indian keys sometimes write "alternative constructivism"; the correct term is **constructive alternativism**.

> **Trap.** Constructive alternativism does **not** say that every construction is equally good. It says alternatives always **exist**. Constructions are judged by how well they **predict** (their predictive efficiency), which is why some must be discarded.

## 11.4 Ways of Anticipating Life Events

### The fundamental postulate

Kelly laid out his theory the way a scientist or mathematician sets out a formal system: **one fundamental postulate** and **eleven corollaries** derived from it (Table 11.1). The **fundamental postulate** says that **our psychological processes are directed by the ways in which we anticipate events.**

Schultz singles out two words in it.

- **Processes.** Kelly chose a word that describes something moving, not a fixed structure. Personality, for him, is a **continually flowing process**, not a set of stable entities like the id, ego and superego. Those processes are directed by our constructs, by the way each of us construes the world.
- **Anticipate.** Constructs face **forwards**. We use them to **predict the future**, so that we have some idea of what will follow from our actions and of what is likely to happen if we behave in a particular way. This is the heart of the theory: people are oriented to what is coming, not driven by what has been.

*Classic background:* Kelly's own formal wording is that "a person's processes are psychologically channelized by the ways in which he anticipates events" (Kelly, 1955). Older textbooks and Indian question papers often quote this version, with the word **channelized**; Schultz paraphrases it with **directed**. They mean the same thing.

> **Trap.** The postulate is about **anticipation**, not memory, reinforcement or need reduction. An option such as "a person's processes are channelised by the ways in which he has been reinforced" or "by past events" is wrong. And count carefully: **one** postulate, **eleven** corollaries.

**Table 11.1** The eleven corollaries of personal construct theory, in the book's order (wording ours)

| # | Corollary | What it says | A one-line illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Construction** | Events never repeat exactly, but repeated events share recurring features, and these let us anticipate how we will experience a similar event in future | You expect to enjoy a festival this year because past ones had the same food, music and crowds |
| 2 | **Individuality** | People construe the same events differently | Two siblings remember the same family move, one as an adventure, one as a loss |
| 3 | **Organisation** | We arrange constructs into a pattern, usually a hierarchy, according to how we see their similarities and differences | "Good person" may sit above "intelligent" and "moral" |
| 4 | **Dichotomy** | Every construct is bipolar; to have a view about honesty is also to have a view about dishonesty | Honest versus dishonest |
| 5 | **Choice** | We choose the pole of a construct that works best for us, the one that lets us anticipate outcomes, trading security against adventure | Picking the familiar easy course or the new, uncertain one |
| 6 | **Range** | A construct applies to some people or situations and not others; ranges can be broad or narrow | Tall versus short fits buildings, not weather |
| 7 | **Experience** | We keep testing constructs against experience to check that they still work | A construct useful at 16 may fail at 40 |
| 8 | **Modulation** | Constructs can be modified by new experience, to the extent that they are permeable | A bigot's impermeable construct survives any contrary evidence |
| 9 | **Fragmentation** | Contradictory or inconsistent subordinate constructs can coexist within the overall system | Someone is a friend in class and an enemy at a rally |
| 10 | **Commonality** | Constructs are individual, but people in compatible groups or cultures can hold similar ones | Shared cultural expectations produce similar construing |
| 11 | **Sociality** | We try to understand how others construe events, predict what they will do, and adjust our behaviour, which is the basis of social roles | Anticipating that other drivers will stop at a red light |

A memory aid for the order, since examiners do ask "which corollary comes after X": **C-I-O-D-C-R-E-M-F-C-S** (Construction, Individuality, Organisation, Dichotomy, Choice, Range, Experience, Modulation, Fragmentation, Commonality, Sociality). Note that the list **starts and ends** in a predictable way: it begins with how *one* person anticipates (construction), moves through how constructs are built, chosen, applied and changed, and ends with how *people relate to one another* (commonality, sociality).

### The Construction Corollary

*Similarities among repeated events.* Kelly held that **no event can ever be experienced twice in exactly the same way**. An event can recur, but the person meeting it has changed. Schultz's example is watching a film again a month after you first saw it. Your mood is different; in the meantime things have happened that have shifted your attitudes and feelings; perhaps you have read something unflattering about one of the actors, or perhaps you are in a better frame of mind because your marks have been improving. The film is the same; the experience is not.

And yet repeated events are not wholly new either. **Recurrent features or themes** show up, and parts of the situation resemble what we met before. Those **similarities** are what allow us to form anticipations about how we will handle that kind of event next time. Our predictions rest on the assumption that future events, while never exact copies of past ones, will **resemble** them. The car chases in the film probably affect you the same way each time; if you liked them before, you expect to like them again, and that expectation is exactly why you chose to watch the film a second time. Themes from the past turn up again in the future, and we build our constructs out of those recurring themes.

A clinical example: a student who has frozen in three viva voce examinations does not experience the fourth as identical to the others (different examiners, a different topic), but she picks out the recurring theme, "a panel staring at me while I search for an answer", and anticipates freezing again. Her anticipation shapes her behaviour before the event even begins.

*Classic background:* Kelly's formal statement is that "a person anticipates events by construing their replications" (Kelly, 1955). "Replications" means the recurring, repeated aspects of events, the themes that make prediction possible.

> **Trap.** The construction corollary is about **similarity across repeated events** (recurring themes make prediction possible). It is not the corollary about people differing from one another; that is the **individuality** corollary. And it is not the same thing as **constructive alternativism**.

### The Individuality Corollary

*Individual differences in interpreting events.* This corollary brings **individual differences** into the theory. People differ in how they perceive and interpret the same event, and because they construe events differently they **form different constructs**. So a construct is not a mirror of the objective event. It is the particular **interpretation** each of us places on that event.

Return to the sunglasses. A teacher's sharp remark in class is one event; one student construes it as "she is trying to push me to do better", another as "she has it in for me", a third as "she is having a bad day and it has nothing to do with me". Each student now holds a different construct of the teacher and will behave differently towards her. Individuality is the source of personality differences in Kelly's system: two people differ to the extent that their constructions of events differ.

*Classic background:* formally, "persons differ from each other in their construction of events" (Kelly, 1955).

### The Organisation Corollary

*Relationships among constructs.* We do not keep our constructs as a loose heap. We **organise** them into a pattern according to how we see their relationships, their similarities and differences. This has a consequence worth stating carefully: **two people can hold very similar constructs and still differ as personalities**, if they organise those constructs in different patterns. Individuality lies not only in which constructs you have but in how you have arranged them.

The usual arrangement is a **hierarchy**, with some constructs **subordinate** to others. A higher-level construct can contain one or more lower-level ones. For example, a person's construct **good** may have **intelligent** and **moral** among its subordinates. The practical result is that when this person meets someone who fits the construct *good*, she expects that person to be intelligent and to have high moral standards too. The hierarchy produces expectations that go beyond the evidence she actually has.

The **relationships among constructs are usually more stable than the individual constructs themselves**, but they too can change. Suppose this person is made to feel small by someone who seems cleverer than she is. She may move the construct *intelligent* out from under *good* and place it under *bad*: from now on, cleverness signals danger rather than virtue. The building blocks are the same; the organisation has shifted, and with it her predictions about intelligent people.

What decides whether an organisation survives? Kelly's answer is single and strict: **the only valid test of a construct system is its predictive efficiency.** If the way our constructs are organised stops giving us a useful way of predicting events, we change the organisation.

*Classic background:* Kelly's formal wording is that each person develops, "for his convenience in anticipating events, a construction system embracing ordinal relationships between constructs" (Kelly, 1955). "Ordinal relationships" means exactly this ranking of **superordinate** over **subordinate** constructs.

> **Trap.** The **individuality** corollary says people differ in **which constructions** they make of events; the **organisation** corollary says they also differ in **how those constructs are arranged** (the hierarchy). One of the book's review questions asks you to distinguish them. Two people with the same list of constructs can still be different personalities because of organisation.

### The Dichotomy Corollary

*Two mutually exclusive alternatives.* **All constructs are bipolar, or dichotomous.** Kelly argued that this is not an arbitrary feature but a necessity if we are to anticipate events correctly. Noticing how people or events are **alike** is only half the job; we also have to register how they **differ**. It is not enough to have a construct for a friend that picks out her honesty. We need the contrasting pole, **dishonesty**, to say how the honest person differs from someone who is not.

Run the argument the other way. If we assumed that everyone was honest, a construct about honesty would tell us nothing; it could not help us predict anything about the next person we met. Expecting honesty from someone only means something **in contrast to** expecting dishonesty from someone else. So the real construct in this example is **honest versus dishonest**, and in general every construct is framed as a pair of **mutually exclusive alternatives**.

*Classic background:* formally, "a person's construction system is composed of a finite number of dichotomous constructs" (Kelly, 1955). Two further points from the classic literature help with application questions. First, the opposite pole of a construct is the **person's own contrast**, not necessarily the dictionary antonym: for one client the opposite of *ambitious* may be *lazy*, for another *contented*, and the difference tells the therapist a great deal. The sample grid in 11.6 shows this vividly. Second, because each construct has two poles, Kelly's later assessment method records both: the **emergent pole** (the way two people are seen as alike) and the **implicit pole** (the contrast that sets the third apart).

> **Trap.** "Constructs are bipolar" belongs to the **dichotomy** corollary. It is also the **assumption underlying the Rep Test**, which is why the test is built on comparing two people with a third. An item that links the Rep Test to the dichotomy corollary is correct.

### The Choice Corollary

*Freedom of choice.* Kelly's writings return again and again to the idea that **people are free to choose**. The choice corollary builds on the dichotomy corollary: since every construct has two opposing poles, in every situation we must decide which pole to apply, and **we choose the alternative that works best for us**, the one that lets us **anticipate or predict the outcome** of future events.

Kelly allowed that we have some room for manoeuvre in this choice, and he described it as a choice between **security** and **adventure**. Schultz's example is a student choosing between two courses for next semester.

- The first is **easy**. It overlaps heavily with a course she has already taken, and it is taught by a professor known for handing out high grades for little work. It carries almost no risk, but also little reward: the professor is dull and she already knows much of the material. It is the **secure** choice because she can predict its consequences very accurately.
- The second is a **gamble**. The professor is new and said to be demanding, and she knows little about the subject, but it would open up a field she has been curious about. She cannot predict the outcome well. More risk, but a greater potential reward and satisfaction.

#### Low risk versus high risk

So the choice is between a **low-risk, low-reward, secure** option and a **high-risk, high-reward, adventurous** one. The secure option has **high predictive efficiency**; the adventurous one has **lower** predictive efficiency. Kelly believed we meet choices of this kind throughout life, and he described them as choices between **defining** and **extending** our construct system:

| | Secure choice | Adventurous choice |
|---|---|---|
| Risk and reward | Low risk, minimal reward | High risk, high potential reward |
| Predictive efficiency | High | Lower |
| What it does to the construct system | **Defines** it: further sharpens and limits the system by repeating familiar experiences | **Extends** it: takes in new experiences and events |
| Example | The easy, familiar course | The new course in an unknown field |

This framework explains something that drive and reinforcement theories find awkward: why people keep doing things that **do not reward them**. Why does someone go on being aggressive towards others even though he is repeatedly rebuffed? Kelly's answer is that aggression is, for him, the **low-risk choice**. He knows exactly how people respond when he is hostile, so aggression keeps his world predictable. He has very little idea how people would respond to friendliness, because he has hardly ever tried it. Friendliness might pay off better, but it is also much less certain.

The key sentence of the corollary, then: **our choices are made according to how well they let us anticipate events, not necessarily according to what is best for us.** Kelly held that each of us, like a good scientist, wants to predict the future with the **highest possible certainty**. Clinically this is a powerful idea. A woman who returns again and again to a partner who mistreats her may be choosing the pole of her construct that keeps life predictable, painful as it is, over the unknown pole of being alone or with someone kind. Therapy, in Kelly's terms, has to make the adventurous choice safe enough to try.

*Classic background:* Kelly's formal wording is that a person chooses the alternative in a dichotomised construct "through which he anticipates the greater possibility for extension and definition of his system" (Kelly, 1955). Textbooks often call this the **elaborative choice**: we choose whichever pole promises to elaborate our system, either by defining it more securely or by extending it.

> **Trap.** **Secure = defines (limits) the system; adventurous = extends it.** Students often reverse these. Also note the motive: people choose for **predictability**, not for pleasure or reward. Kelly's account of persistent aggression is that the aggressive person is taking the **low-risk** option, not that he is being reinforced for it.

### The Range Corollary

*The range of convenience.* Very few constructs fit every situation. Take **tall versus short**. It works well for buildings, trees or basketball players, but it is useless for describing a pizza or the weather. The set of things a construct can sensibly be applied to is its **range of convenience** (also called its range of applicability). The margin definition: the spectrum of events to which a construct can be applied; some constructs are relevant to only a few people or situations, others are broader.

Some constructs have very wide ranges and can be applied to many people and situations; others are narrow, relevant perhaps to one person or one kind of situation. And here is the personal twist: **how wide a construct's range is depends on the person**. One person applies **loyal versus disloyal** to everyone she meets; another applies it only to family members; a third uses it only about his dog. Same construct, very different ranges, and very different personalities as a result.

Kelly drew a methodological lesson: to understand a personality fully, it is **as important to know what a construct excludes as what it includes**. If a man applies *trustworthy versus untrustworthy* to his business partners but never to his wife, that exclusion is itself informative; perhaps the question of her trustworthiness is one he cannot afford to ask.

*Classic background:* Kelly distinguished the **range of convenience** from the **focus of convenience**. The range is the whole set of events a construct can be applied to; the focus is the narrower set of events for which the construct is **most useful**, the cases it was first built to handle and fits best. *Tall versus short* can stretch to describe a person's ambitions ("he has tall plans"), but its focus is physical height. Kelly's formal statement of the corollary is that "a construct is convenient for the anticipation of a finite range of events only" (Kelly, 1955). The 11th edition of Schultz uses only the term range of convenience; the focus of convenience appears in older and fuller treatments.

> **Trap.** **Range of convenience** (all the events a construct can cover) versus **focus of convenience** (where it applies best). Do not confuse range of convenience with **permeability** either: range is *how far* a construct currently reaches; permeability (modulation corollary) is *whether new events can be admitted* into that range.

### The Experience Corollary

*Exposure to new experiences.* Each construct, remember, is a **hypothesis** generated from past experience to predict future events. Every construct is then **tested against reality** by how well it predicts what actually happens. Since most of us meet new experiences every day, this testing never stops. When a construct fails to predict the outcome of a situation, it has to be **reformulated or replaced**.

As a result, we evaluate and reinterpret our constructs as our circumstances change. Schultz's example is age: constructs that served us at **16** may be useless, or even harmful, at **40**. In between, experience will have led us to rework the construct system. A teenager's construct "adults who set rules versus adults who leave you alone" may serve well enough at school and fail completely once she is a parent herself. If you never had new experiences your construct system would never need to change, but for nearly everyone life keeps bringing new people and new challenges, so we must keep **re-construing** our experiences and constructs.

*Classic background:* formally, "a person's construction system varies as he successively construes the replications of events" (Kelly, 1955). Kelly stressed that experience in his sense is not the mere passage of events but the **reconstruing** they force; a person who meets many events and never revises a construct has, in Kelly's terms, had very little experience.

### The Modulation Corollary

*Adapting to new experiences.* Constructs differ in their **permeability**. To *permeate* is to pass through or penetrate. A **permeable construct** is one that lets **new elements** into its range of convenience: it is open to new events and experiences and can be **revised or extended** by them. Schultz's margin definition of permeability: the idea that constructs can be revised and extended in light of new experiences.

The modulation corollary states the limit on change: **how far a construct system can be modulated, or adjusted, by new experience and learning depends on the permeability of the individual constructs.** An **impermeable**, rigid construct cannot be changed, whatever our experience tells us.

Schultz's example is prejudice. A bigoted person applies the construct *high intelligence versus low intelligence* to members of a particular ethnic minority in a fixed, impermeable way: all members of that group, he believes, are of low intelligence. New experiences cannot get through. He can meet any number of highly intelligent members of the group and the construct will not budge; he will call each one an exception. The construct becomes a **barrier to learning and to new ideas**, precisely because it cannot be revised.

The clinical significance is large. Much of what therapists call rigidity, in obsessional thinking, in depressive beliefs ("I always fail"), in paranoid suspicion, is in Kelly's language a matter of **impermeable constructs** in important areas of life. Therapy aims to make them permeable enough to learn from experience.

*Classic background:* Kelly's formal wording is that "the variation in a person's construction system is limited by the permeability of the constructs within whose ranges of convenience the variants lie" (Kelly, 1955). Read it as: change happens only where the relevant superordinate constructs are permeable enough to admit it. Note that Schultz's Table 11.1 gives only the simple version ("we may modify our constructs as a function of new experiences"), while the text explains the permeability limit.

> **Trap.** **Experience** corollary = constructs are **continually tested and revised** against experience. **Modulation** corollary = **how much** they can be revised is **limited by permeability**. If an option mentions *permeability*, it is the modulation corollary. The **bigot** example belongs to modulation (an **impermeable** construct), not to range, fragmentation or dichotomy.

### The Fragmentation Corollary

*Competition among constructs.* Kelly believed that some constructs within a single system may be **incompatible with one another** even while they live side by side in the overall pattern. The construct system changes as we evaluate new experience, but new constructs do not have to be **derived from** old ones. A new construct may sit comfortably with an old one in one situation, and then, when the situation changes, the two can turn out to be inconsistent.

Schultz's example: a man meets a woman in a psychology class and is drawn to her. She is a psychology major too, and her interests seem to match his. She fits the **friend** pole of his construct *friend versus enemy*: someone to like and respect. The next day he sees her at a political rally, loudly arguing conservative positions directly opposed to his own liberal ones. In that setting she fits the other pole: she is the **enemy**.

So his construing of this one woman is **inconsistent**: friend in one situation, enemy in another. But the inconsistency is at a **subordinate** level of his system. His broader, superordinate construct, that **liberals are friends and conservatives are enemies**, is untouched. According to Kelly, this is how we manage to **tolerate inconsistencies at subordinate levels without damaging the overall construct system**. Schultz's photograph caption makes the same point in everyday terms: people can be friends over a board game and opponents in a political debate.

A clinical illustration: a nurse may construe a particular consultant as "a brilliant doctor" in the operating theatre and as "an arrogant bully" on the ward round. Both constructions are held at once, each in its own context, and the nurse's superordinate construct ("skill matters more than manners in an emergency") lets her work with him without her system breaking down.

*Classic background:* formally, a person "may successively employ a variety of construction subsystems which are inferentially incompatible with each other" (Kelly, 1955).

> **Trap.** **Organisation** corollary = constructs are arranged in a **consistent hierarchy**. **Fragmentation** corollary = **inconsistent** subordinate constructs can **coexist** within that hierarchy. One of the review questions asks "how is it possible to hold incompatible constructs?"; the answer is fragmentation, and the mechanism is that the inconsistency is **subordinate** while the superordinate construct stays intact.

### The Commonality Corollary

*Similarities among people in interpreting events.* People differ in how they construe events, so each develops individual constructs. But people also **resemble one another** in their construing. Kelly proposed that **if several people construe an experience in a similar way, we can conclude that their cognitive processes are similar.** Note where the similarity is located: not in the events people have lived through but in how they construe them.

Consider a group who share the same cultural norms and ideals. Their expectations of one another will overlap heavily, and they will construe many experiences in the same way. So people from the same culture can come to resemble one another in behaviour and characteristics **even though they have lived through different individual events**. Two Indian students from different cities and families may both construe a younger cousin's decision to choose her own spouse along the same lines, because they share a cultural construction of family and marriage, not because anything identical happened to them.

*Classic background:* formally, to the extent that one person's construction of experience is similar to another's, "his psychological processes are similar to those of the other person" (Kelly, 1955).

> **Trap.** **Individuality** (people construe events differently) and **commonality** (people in the same culture construe events similarly) are the two halves of Kelly's position on **uniqueness versus universality** (11.5), which is why Schultz rates him as **moderate** on that issue.

### The Sociality Corollary

*Interpersonal relationships.* People in the same culture tend to construe events similarly, and that explains some commonalities among them. But similarity of construing does **not by itself produce good social relationships**. It is not enough for me to construe experiences the way you do. I must also **construe your constructs**: I have to understand **how you think** if I am to anticipate how you will predict events and therefore what you will do.

We do this all the time without noticing. Schultz's example is **driving**. Every time we get into a car we stake our lives on predicting what other drivers will do: that they will stop at a red light and move off at a green one. Only when we can predict with some confidence what drivers, friends, bosses or teachers will do can we fit our own behaviour to theirs; and while we are adjusting to them, they are adjusting to us. (On an Indian road the construing is more elaborate: you must anticipate how a particular auto-rickshaw driver construes a gap in traffic. The principle is the same.)

From this comes Kelly's concept of **role**. Each of us takes up a role in relation to other people: one with a partner, another with a child, another with a supervisor at work. A **role**, in Kelly's sense, is a **pattern of behaviour that grows out of understanding how the other person construes events**. In a sense, we fit ourselves into the other person's constructs. A good therapist, a good teacher and a good negotiator are all, in Kelly's terms, skilled at construing other people's construction processes.

This is why Kelly's assessment test is called the **Role Construct** Repertory Test: it asks about the constructs we apply to the people who play significant **roles** in our lives. And it is why Kelly lectured on international tension: nations, like individuals, can work together only to the extent that each construes how the other construes.

*Classic background:* formally, "to the extent that one person construes the construction processes of another, he may play a role in a social process involving the other person" (Kelly, 1955).

> **Trap.** **Commonality** = having **similar constructs** to another person. **Sociality** = **construing the other person's constructs** (understanding how they think). You can share someone's constructs and still be unable to relate to them; you can relate well to someone whose constructs differ completely from yours, provided you understand them. Kelly's **role** concept comes from the **sociality** corollary.

### Classic background: emotions as transitions in the construct system

Schultz says (in 11.8) that Kelly did not deal explicitly with emotions, though he recognised them as personal constructs formed like any other. The 11th edition does not go further, but older textbooks (Hall and Lindzey, Pervin) and many Indian question papers do, because Kelly (1955) gave a famous set of cognitive redefinitions of familiar emotional terms. Everything in this subsection is **Classic background**, offered because the question is asked, not because Schultz prints it.

Kelly's move was to treat each emotion not as a drive or a feeling-state but as an **awareness that one's construct system is in transition, or is failing**. Two terms are needed first. **Core constructs** are the ones that govern a person's basic identity and maintenance as a person; **peripheral constructs** can be changed without much effect on the core. A person's **core role** is his or her construing of who he or she is in relation to significant others.

| Term | Kelly's cognitive redefinition (paraphrased) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anxiety** | Recognising that the events one faces lie **outside the range of convenience** of one's construct system: one has no constructs that apply | A villager arriving in a megacity for the first time, with no way of making sense of what is happening around her |
| **Threat** | Awareness of an **imminent, comprehensive change in one's core constructs** | Being told one has a life-threatening illness; one's whole construction of the future is about to change |
| **Fear** | Like threat, but the impending change is **incidental rather than comprehensive**: a new, more limited construct is about to take over | Sudden fear of a dog in the lane, which does not shake who you are |
| **Guilt** | Perceiving that one has been **dislodged from one's core role structure**: one is acting in ways that contradict one's own construing of who one is in relation to others | A devoted mother who finds herself neglecting her child |
| **Hostility** | The continued effort to **extort validating evidence** for a social prediction that has **already failed**: forcing events to fit a construct rather than revising it | A father who keeps pressuring his son to become a doctor, the son he "knows" him to be, long after the son has shown otherwise |

Two links to the corollaries make these easy to remember. **Anxiety** is a failure of **range** (the events fall outside the range of convenience). **Hostility** is a refusal to let **experience** revise a construct, the opposite of constructive alternativism. *Classic background:* Kelly also redefined **aggression**, unusually, as a positive thing: the **active elaboration** of one's perceptual field, actively extending one's construct system into new areas.

> **Trap.** In Kelly's system **anxiety** is not Freud's ego signal of danger from the id or superego (Chapter 2) and not Horney's basic anxiety (Chapter 5). It is a **cognitive** state: being confronted with events your constructs cannot handle. Likewise **guilt** is not the superego punishing the ego but displacement from one's **core role**, and **hostility** is not an aggressive drive but **extorting evidence for a failed prediction**. If an option gives Kelly a drive-based or unconscious account of any emotion, it is wrong.

*Classic background:* two further process ideas from Kelly (1955) occasionally appear in MCQs. The **C-P-C cycle** describes how a person moves to a decision: **Circumspection** (considering a situation through several constructs, tentatively), **Pre-emption** (choosing one construct as the one that matters here) and **Control** (choosing the pole of that construct and acting on it). Impulsive people shorten the circumspection phase; obsessional people may never leave it. The **creativity cycle** moves from **loosened** construing (constructs held loosely, so new combinations are possible, as in brainstorming or dreams) to **tightened** construing (constructs made precise enough to test). Neither cycle is in the 11th edition of Schultz.

## 11.5 Questions about Human Nature

Kelly's image of human nature is **optimistic**, Schultz says even **flattering** (Kelly, 1969). He saw people as **rational** beings, able to build a framework of constructs through which to view the world. In his view we are the **authors of our destiny, not its victims**.

**Free will.** Kelly gives us free will: the ability to choose the direction our lives take. We can change course when we need to by revising old constructs and building new ones. We are **not locked into a path laid down in childhood or adolescence**. This follows directly from constructive alternativism (11.3) and the choice corollary (11.4).

**The future, not the past.** Our direction is plainly towards the **future**, since the whole purpose of constructs is to anticipate events. Kelly therefore **rejected determinism**: he did not treat past events as the causes of present behaviour. Schultz lists what we are *not* prisoners of: toilet training, early sexual experiences, parental rejection. Nor are we bound by **biological instincts** or **unconscious forces**.

**Motivation.** We need no push from internal drives or needs, because we are **motivated simply by the fact of being alive**. Kelly saw no reason to add any other explanation. A living person is already in motion; the question for psychology is not what pushes him but which way he moves, and that is answered by his constructs.

*Classic background:* Kelly (1958) mocked the two standard kinds of motivational theory as **"pitchfork" theories** (we are pushed by drives) and **"carrot" theories** (we are pulled by incentives), and joked that since his own theory looked instead at the nature of the animal, it might be called a **"jackass" theory**. The joke makes the exam point: Kelly needs no separate concept of motivation at all.

**Heredity and environment.** Kelly did not discuss the part played by **heredity**. He did point out that we are **not wholly determined by environment** either. We live by constructs built from our **interpretation** of events, so it is the working of our rational mental processes, not the particular events themselves, that shapes personality. Put simply: what happens to you matters less than how you construe it.

**Goal of life.** Kelly proposed **no ultimate, necessary life goal**, but Schultz infers one: our aim is to build a **construct system that lets us predict events**.

**Uniqueness versus universality.** Kelly takes a **moderate** position. The **individuality** corollary stresses how many of our constructs, and so our selves, are unique; the **commonality** corollary says that people in the same culture develop similar constructs.

| Issue | Kelly's position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Free will** | Constructive alternativism: we can always revise or replace constructs and choose our direction; authors, not victims, of destiny |
| Nature vs nurture | Neither determines personality; heredity **not discussed**, environment **not wholly determining** | Personality is shaped by how rational mental processes **interpret** events, not by the events themselves |
| Past vs present | **Present and future** | Constructs are anticipatory; past events (toilet training, early sex, parental rejection) are not determinants |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Moderate**, both | Individuality corollary (unique constructs) plus commonality corollary (shared cultural constructs) |
| Equilibrium vs growth (life goal) | No ultimate goal stated; **inferred goal is a construct system that predicts events** | Motivation is simply being alive; the aim is predictive efficiency, reached by defining and extending the system |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimistic**, even flattering | People are rational, self-directing and able to change at any time |

> **Trap.** Schultz does **not** say that Kelly's life goal is tension reduction (Freud) or self-actualisation (Maslow, Rogers). He says Kelly named **no ultimate goal**, and the inferred goal is a **construct system that enables prediction**. On nature versus nurture, the answer is not "environment": Kelly expressly said we are **not totally determined by environmental influences**, and he did **not discuss heredity** at all.

> **Trap.** "Kelly's people are motivated by the need to predict" is a tempting paraphrase, but Kelly himself refused any motivational construct: we are motivated by **being alive**. The wish to predict with certainty is how Kelly describes the *direction* of choices (choice corollary), not a separate drive.

## 11.6 Assessment in Kelly's Theory

Kelly's assessment methods all aim at one target: the client's **constructs**, the bipolar interpretations through which he or she anticipates the world. Every technique below is a different way of getting the client to show you those constructs. And because Kelly was a working clinician, assessment and therapy run into each other: the self-characterisation sketch becomes the raw material of fixed role therapy.

### The Interview

Kelly's **primary** assessment technique was simply the **interview**. His own summary has become famous: "If you don't know what is going on in a person's mind, ask him; he may tell you!" (Kelly, 1958, p. 330).

He interviewed with what he called a **credulous attitude**: he took the client's words **at face value**, because he believed that was the best route to the person's constructs. This was not naivety. He knew a client might deliberately lie or distort an account of events. His point was that what the client says must be **respected even when it is not fully believed**. A lie is itself a construction, and the way a person chooses to present events reveals how he construes them.

Compare this with the psychoanalytic interview, where the client's account is treated as a surface to be seen through (resistance, defence, disguised wishes), and the difference in attitude is complete. For Kelly the client is a fellow scientist reporting on his own hypotheses; the therapist's job is to listen to the hypotheses, not to replace them with an interpretation of what they "really" mean.

> **Trap.** The **credulous attitude** means accepting the client's words **at face value** and respecting them, not believing everything uncritically and not interpreting hidden meanings. Kelly fully recognised that clients may lie. Contrast Freud's use of free association to get *past* what the patient consciously says.

### Self-Characterisation Sketches

A second technique is to have the person write a **self-characterisation sketch**. Kelly's instruction (Kelly, 1955, p. 323) was to write a character sketch of oneself, using one's own name and the third person, **as if one were the principal character in a play**, written by a friend who knew one "very intimately and very sympathetically", perhaps better than anyone really could.

Each feature of that instruction has a purpose. Writing in the **third person** gives the client a little distance from himself. Imagining a **sympathetic and intimate** friend as the author discourages both a harsh self-condemnation and a public-relations exercise. The **play** framing invites the client to describe himself as a character with a role among other characters. Kelly found the method especially useful for discovering **how clients perceive themselves in relation to other people**. The margin definition: a technique designed to assess a person's construct system, that is, how a person perceives himself or herself in relation to other people.

What does a therapist read in a sketch? The constructs the client uses spontaneously (a client who describes himself entirely in terms of *success versus failure* is telling you which construct dominates his system), what he leaves out, and where he places himself relative to others. In the case of Roy Taylor below, the sketch was one of three sources used to work out his constructs.

### The Role Construct Repertory Test

Kelly designed the **Role Construct Repertory Test (Rep Test)** to bring out the constructs we apply to the **important people in our lives**. The procedure has two stages.

**Stage 1: naming the role figures.** The client is given a list of **role titles** and asked to write the name of the person in his or her life who fits each one: mother, father, spouse, closest friend, the most intelligent or interesting person he or she knows, and so on (Table 11.2).

**Table 11.2** The role title list of the Rep Test, grouped (24 titles; wording ours)

| Group of role titles | Number of titles | What the client supplies |
|---|---|---|
| Teachers | 2 | One liked and one disliked |
| Partner | 1 | Spouse or present boyfriend or girlfriend |
| Employers, supervisors or officers | 2 | One found hard to get on with and one liked |
| Parents | 2 | Mother and father, or the people who filled those roles |
| Siblings | 2 | The brother and the sister nearest one's age, or the people most like them |
| Co-workers | 2 | One easy to get on with and one hard to understand |
| Neighbours | 2 | One easy to get on with and one hard to understand |
| School-age peers | 4 | A boy and a girl liked, and a boy and a girl disliked, in high school |
| Travel companions | 2 | A same-sex person one would enjoy, and one would dislike, as a companion on a trip |
| Someone who seems to dislike you | 1 | A recent close associate who appears to dislike you |
| Someone you pity or would help | 1 | The person you would most like to help, or feel most sorry for |
| Outstanding acquaintances | 3 | The most intelligent, the most successful and the most interesting person you know personally |
| **Total** | **24** | |

Look at how the list is built. Almost every category comes in a **liked versus disliked**, or **easy versus hard to understand**, pair. The design guarantees that the client's sample of significant people contains both poles of the evaluations that matter to him, so that the constructs which emerge will be ones that actually discriminate among the people in his life.

**Stage 2: the triads.** The names are then presented **three at a time**. For each group of three the client must pick the **two who are most alike** in some important way and say **how they differ from the third**. For example, the client might be given the people he named as the most threatening person, a successful person and an attractive person, and asked how two of them resemble each other in behaviour or character and how the third differs. Whatever he says is a construct: the way two are alike is one pole, the way the third differs is the other.

#### The Repertory Grid

The information is recorded on a diagram called a **repertory grid**. Each **sort** (one triad) has its own line in the grid. The three people being compared in that sort are indicated by **circles**. The client thinks about them and formulates a construct, for instance **happy versus sad**:

1. He writes the word or phrase that describes **the two who are alike** (say, *happy*) in the column headed **Emergent Pole**.
2. He writes the **contrasting** word that describes **the third person** (*sad*) in the column headed **Implicit Pole**.
3. He then goes along the line and puts a **check mark** against **everyone else** in the grid who also fits the emergent pole: every other significant person in his life who could be described as happy.

Repeat for every sort, and the grid becomes a map of which constructs the client uses and which people he groups together under each.

Figure 11.1 shows a grid that a client has actually filled in. Read it in four parts.

- **The role figures.** Down the side are **19** people, numbered from **1 (Self)** through mother, father, brother, sister, spouse, ex-boyfriend or girlfriend, pal, ex-pal, a rejecting person, a pitied person, a threatening person, an attractive person, an accepted teacher, a rejected teacher, the boss, a successful person and a happy person, to **19 (an ethical person)**. Notice that this sample grid uses a shortened form of the role list (19 figures, including the self) rather than all 24 titles of Table 11.2.
- **The sorts.** Across the grid run **22 numbered sorts**. In each sort exactly three cells are marked: **two circles containing a cross**, for the pair the client judged alike, and **one solid dot**, for the person who differs.
- **The constructs.** At the top, in the client's own handwriting, each sort's construct is written as an **emergent pole** and an **implicit pole**.
- **The check marks.** Scattered across the rest of each line are **ticks** for the other people who share the emergent pole.

Two sorts show how it works. In **sort 1** the triad is the ethical person, the happy person and the successful person; the client paired the happy and successful people as "don't believe in God" (emergent pole) and set the ethical person apart as "very religious" (implicit pole). In **sort 22** the triad is the self, the pal and the spouse; self and pal "both appreciate music", while the spouse is the one who doesn't understand music.

The grid also illustrates the **dichotomy corollary** in its true, personal form. Several implicit poles are not dictionary opposites at all. In one sort the client grouped his mother and father together simply as "parents", and his contrast for the third person was "ideas different". In several sorts the pair is described as "both girls" and the contrast as "a boy". A therapist would notice both things: that this client sometimes construes people by role or by sex rather than by character, and that for him the opposite of "parents" is "having different ideas", which hints at how he construes his parents' views.

*Classic background:* the analyst then looks for patterns across the grid: constructs whose ticks fall on nearly the same people (suggesting they function as one construct for this client, whatever their labels), the constructs on which the **self** is grouped with or against particular figures, and how many genuinely different constructs the client uses at all. That last measure is the origin of the research on **cognitive complexity** in 11.7.

### The Role of Dichotomies

The **assumption underlying the Rep Test** is the **dichotomy corollary**: people construe events in **dichotomies**, in terms of like versus unlike, similar versus dissimilar. By making the client judge his social relationships over and over again in this two-against-one format, Kelly believed he could uncover the client's **anticipations and expectations**. The dichotomies by which we guide our lives reveal the **pattern of our personal constructs**.

#### Interpreting the REP Test

How a Rep Test is interpreted depends on the **skill and training of the psychologist** who gives it. Kelly **did not intend it as a standardised, objective self-report inventory**. He designed it as a way of assessing constructs as a necessary **stage in psychotherapy**, a way of getting clients to reveal the constructs by which they organise their world. Since then, however, **computer programs** have been developed to analyse individual repertory grids in more objective terms (**Grice, 2002**).

> **Trap.** The Rep Test is **not** a standardised objective personality inventory like the MMPI, and it is **not** a projective test like the Rorschach. It is an **idiographic** clinical tool for eliciting a person's own constructs, and its interpretation depends heavily on the clinician's skill. **Emergent pole** = what the **two alike** share; **implicit pole** = the contrast describing the **third**; **check marks** = others who share the **emergent** pole. The **role title list has 24 titles** (Table 11.2), while the sample grid in Fig 11.1 uses **19** figures and **22** sorts.

### Fixed Role Therapy

Assessment was never the end point for Kelly. Once he had worked out a client's system of constructs, he tried to **change the constructs that were undesirable or ineffective**. The form of psychotherapy he developed for this is **fixed role therapy**. It starts with the assessment technique you have just met: to help clients build new constructs and drop old ones, he asked them to write a **self-characterisation sketch** describing themselves as the lead character in a play.

The margin definition, in our words: **fixed role therapy** is a therapy in which the client plays out the constructs of an invented person, and in doing so learns that these new constructs can serve him better than the ones he has been living by.

#### Playing a Role

The steps are these.

1. The client writes the **self-characterisation sketch**, which reveals his negative self-perceptions and the constructs behind them.
2. The **therapist** then writes a **fixed role sketch**: a description of a character whose constructs **differ** from the client's negative self-perceptions.
3. The client is told that the sketch describes a **fictitious character**, and is asked to **act out** that character, first in the therapist's office and later in everyday life.
4. Through the role-play, the client is expected to **project his own needs and values** onto the fictitious character.
5. The therapist expects the client to **discover that the new constructs predict events better** than the old ones he has been living by. Once the client sees this, he can **build the new constructs into his overall system** and function in a more satisfying and effective way.

Kelly's inspiration came from watching a **friend in a college play**. The friend was so affected by his part that his behaviour off stage gradually came to resemble the character's. Hence the goal of fixed role therapy: **first play a role, then come to live it.**

Why should acting work, in Kelly's theory? Because the client is not being told that his constructs are wrong, which would invite hostility in Kelly's sense (defending a failing prediction). He is being invited to run an **experiment**: to behave *as if* he held different constructs and to see what happens. The fictional frame makes the experiment safe; if it goes badly, "it was only the character". This is constructive alternativism put into practice.

*Classic background:* Kelly advised that the fixed role sketch should **not** simply be the mirror-image opposite of the client (shifting from one pole of an old construct to the other, which he regarded as too easy and too shallow a change). It should instead introduce **new dimensions** of construing. The Roy Taylor sketch below follows that rule: it says nothing at all about his central complaint.

#### A Sample Fixed Role Therapy

The case is reported by **Winter (1992, pp. 270 to 271)**. The client, a man called **Roy Taylor**, was assessed through **interviews**, a written **self-characterisation sketch** and the **Rep Test**. From these the therapist formed the following picture.

- Roy was **preoccupied with finding a female partner**, and his efforts were damaging his other social relationships.
- He found it hard to be **open and assertive**, because in his construct system **assertiveness and extraversion were negative** characteristics.
- At the same time, in his dealings with people he was certain that **his opinions were right** and everyone else's were wrong.
- He felt cut off from his workmates (**isolated**), whom he regarded as socially beneath him (he saw himself as of a **higher social class**).

The fixed role sketch the therapist wrote **did not mention Roy's wish for an intimate relationship at all**. Instead it used something Roy was already good at, **tennis**, as its framework, and through the fictitious character it encouraged him to become **more curious about, and more tolerant of, different kinds of people and their views**.

The sketch (printed in full in the book; summarised here) describes a man whose approach to life mirrors his approach to tennis: what matters is playing as well as he can, not whether he wins. He does things wholeheartedly and earns respect for it, yet he also knows how to enjoy himself. When things go wrong he learns from the disappointment and looks forward rather than brooding. His great strength on court is **anticipating the moves of other players**, opponents and partners alike, and off court he likewise tries to see the world **through other people's eyes**. His curiosity about what makes people tick draws people to him. When he disagrees with someone, he still makes the effort to understand that person's point of view, so he is known both for his convictions and for his **tolerance** of other people's right to differ.

Read the sketch against the corollaries and you can see how carefully it was built. "Anticipating other players' moves" and "seeing through other people's eyes" are the **sociality** corollary turned into a character trait, aimed straight at Roy's certainty that only his opinions were right. "Learning from disappointment and looking ahead" is the **experience** corollary and the anticipatory stance of the fundamental postulate. Tolerance of differing opinions makes his constructs about other people more **permeable**. And framing all this through tennis, a field where Roy was already competent, makes the adventurous choice feel secure enough to try.

#### The Role of the Therapist

The therapist went through the fixed role sketch with Roy and asked whether the character seemed like someone he **might like to know**. Roy agreed to try behaving like the character during the session, and was then asked to **act, think and talk like the character for the next two weeks**. Behavioural changes produced by fixed role therapy are reported to **last well beyond the two-week role-playing period**.

Schultz adds the necessary caution. Encouraging case reports like this one must be weighed against the fact that there has been **little controlled research** on whether the technique works. One of the few studies found that fixed role therapy **reduced social anxiety** in a **small sample of clients in Japan** (**Abe, Imai, & Nedate, 2011**).

> **Trap.** Two different sketches, written by two different people. The **self-characterisation sketch** is written by the **client** (about himself, as a sympathetic friend would). The **fixed role sketch** is written by the **therapist** (about a fictitious character with new constructs). Fixed role therapy uses **both**: the first for assessment, the second for change.

> **Trap.** Fixed role therapy is Kelly's, not Moreno's. *Classic background:* J. L. Moreno's **psychodrama** also uses role-play, but it dramatises the client's own conflicts and relationships on a stage with other actors. Kelly's client plays a **fictitious character with new constructs** in **everyday life**, for about **two weeks**, as an experiment in construing.

## 11.7 Research on Kelly's Theory

Nearly all the research in this section uses the **Rep Test** or a version of it, which is itself a point worth remembering: unlike Freud's or Jung's concepts, Kelly's constructs came with a ready-made measuring instrument, and that made them easy to study. The research falls into five groups: how stable constructs are, how constructs relate to emotional state and psychopathology, applied uses, recent studies of personal constructs in everyday settings, and the large literature on cognitive complexity.

### Stability of Constructs over Time

**Constructs are stable.** In one study, a group of participants took the Rep Test **twice**, naming **different people** as the role figures on each occasion. Even though the people being judged had changed, the **constructs that were important to the participants stayed the same**. That is good evidence that the Rep Test is measuring something about the person doing the construing, not just about the particular people being construed. The same body of research also found, however, that the **validity** of the Rep Test depends heavily on the **skill of the psychologist** who interprets the results, which is the price of a non-standardised clinical instrument.

**Construct systems grow more complex across the life span.** A Rep Test study of the complexity of construct systems found that the system becomes **increasingly differentiated and integrated** over the life span: it can handle more information and work in more abstract terms (**Crockett, 1982**). Differentiated means the person uses more, and more distinct, constructs; integrated means those constructs are related to one another in an organised hierarchy (the organisation corollary in action).

**Friendship depends on similar constructs that are already there.** Another line of work asked whether friends come to construe alike, or whether people who already construe alike become friends. **Duck and Spencer (1972)** gave the Rep Test to students in their **first week at college** and again **six months later**. The similarity in constructs and attitudes found among friends **had not developed during the six months**; it was **present before the friendships formed**. The researchers concluded that **we choose as friends people whose constructs already resemble ours**. In Kelly's terms, commonality makes sociality easier: it is much simpler to construe the constructs of someone whose constructs are like your own.

**Marriage.** Among married participants, couples whose constructs were **more alike** reported **greater happiness with their marriages** than couples whose constructs were less alike (**Neimeyer, 1984**).

> **Trap.** Duck and Spencer (1972) is a **selection** finding, not a **convergence** finding. Construct similarity between friends was there **before** the friendship, not produced by it. An option saying "friends gradually come to share constructs over six months" reverses the result.

### Effects of Emotional State

Other studies found a **correspondence between a person's own characteristics and the constructs he or she uses to judge others**. Among **student nurses**, those identified as **highly anxious** tended to evaluate other people on an **anxious versus non-anxious** construct, and those whom their peers judged **friendly** tended to view others in terms of **friendly versus unfriendly** (**Sechrest, 1968**). The dimensions most salient in our own make-up become the dimensions through which we read other people.

**Psychopathology.** The Rep Test has been used with people diagnosed with **schizophrenia**, **neuroses**, **depression** and **organic brain damage**. The most cited findings concern schizophrenia. Compared with normal participants, people with schizophrenia were **unstable and inconsistent in how they construed other people**, but their **construing of objects was stable and consistent**. This suggests that their thought disorder applied specifically to **social situations**. Their thinking was also marked by **paranoid delusions** and **irrational links between constructs** (see, for example, **Bannister, Fransella, & Agnew, 1971; Bannister & Salmon, 1966; Winter, 1992**).

*Classic background:* this line of work, led by **Don Bannister** in Britain, gave rise to the "serial invalidation" hypothesis: repeated disconfirmation of one's social predictions may lead a person to loosen the links between constructs until they no longer predict anything, which is one way of describing schizophrenic thought disorder in Kelly's language.

**Repeat versus first-time psychiatric patients.** A study using a modified Rep Test compared the construct systems of patients who had been in psychiatric hospital **repeatedly** with those hospitalised for the **first time**. The repeat patients construed their social network as **small**, limited to a few people they believed they could depend on; first-time patients construed theirs as **significantly larger** (**Smith, Stefan, Kovaleski, & Johnson, 1991**). A narrow social construing may be part of what keeps people returning to hospital.

**Offenders.** Needs (1988) reviewed Rep Test work with **juvenile and adult offenders**, and each group construed itself in a characteristic way. **Delinquent** youngsters chose **action heroes from television**, not real adults, as the figures they identified with. Prisoners just **released** from custody held **low self-esteem** and expected little of their futures (**lowered aspirations**). **Rapists** saw themselves as **inadequate and immature**, and were **preoccupied with their own failure**.

> **Trap.** People with schizophrenia were **unstable in construing people** and **stable in construing objects**. The finding locates the thought disorder in **social** construing. Do not reverse it.

### Real-World Applications

The Rep Test has travelled well beyond the clinic. In **market research** it has been used to find out which criteria **consumers** use to evaluate products: present three brands, ask how two differ from the third, and you have the consumer's constructs. **Industrial-organisational psychologists** have used it for **vocational counselling**, **employee selection**, **evaluating job performance** and **evaluating training programmes** (**Benjafield, 2008**).

### Research on Personal Constructs

Studies outside therapy have looked at a range of behaviours that personal constructs might influence.

- **Reckless driving (Australia).** The degree of **reckless behaviour by drivers** on the road could be related to the **constructs they held** (**McNally & Bradley, 2014**).
- **Teaching statistics (Paris).** An **American professor teaching in Paris** coached his **beginning statistics** students not only to understand the subject but to become **enthusiastic** about it, by connecting the statistical methods to their **personal constructs** (**Clayson, 2013**).
- **Terrorism (India).** A study by **British psychologists** of the personal constructs of **convicted Islamic terrorists in India**, described by Schultz as groundbreaking, found that those who **could not re-construe their future lives** in ways unconnected to terrorism were the **least likely to disengage** from future terrorist acts (**Canter, Sarangi, & Youngs, 2014**). This is constructive alternativism with very high stakes: a person who has no alternative construction of his future has nowhere else to go.

> **Trap.** Canter, Sarangi and Youngs (2014) were **British** psychologists studying terrorists **in India**. The finding is about **inability to re-construe** the future, which predicted **failure to disengage**. McNally and Bradley (2014) is the **Australian** driving study; Clayson (2013) is the **statistics teacher in Paris**.

### Cognitive Complexity and Cognitive Simplicity

One outgrowth of Kelly's work concerns **cognitive styles**: differences in how we perceive or construe the people, objects and situations around us. This research was **derived from the Rep Test** and centres on the concept of **cognitive complexity**.

- **Cognitive complexity** is the ability to **discriminate** when applying personal constructs to other people. People high in cognitive complexity **see variety among people** and can readily place a person in **many categories**. The margin definition: a cognitive style, or way of construing the environment, characterised by the ability to perceive differences among people.
- **Cognitive simplicity**, the other extreme, means being **less able to perceive differences** when judging other people. People high in cognitive simplicity tend to put others into only **one or two categories** and see little variety. The margin definition: a cognitive style characterised by a relative inability to perceive differences among people.

Think of how two interviewers might describe the same candidate. One says "nervous, but clearly well prepared; a bit rigid in his answers, yet he handled the ethics question thoughtfully". The other says "good" or "bad". The first is using several independent constructs and can hold apparently inconsistent information together; the second has, for this purpose, one construct.

*Classic background:* the concept was introduced by **James Bieri (1955)**, who measured it from a person's Rep grid: if a person's different constructs sort the role figures in very similar ways, he is effectively using only a few dimensions (cognitive simplicity); if his constructs sort people in many different ways, he is using many independent dimensions (cognitive complexity). Schultz does not name Bieri, but Indian papers often ask "who introduced cognitive complexity-simplicity?", and the answer is Bieri, building on Kelly's Rep Test.

#### Effects on Personality and Behaviour

Research has found personality differences linked to cognitive style. People high in **cognitive complexity**, compared with those high in cognitive simplicity:

- are **better at predicting** other people's behaviour;
- more readily **recognise differences between themselves and others**;
- show **more empathy** towards others; and
- **deal better with inconsistent information** when construing others (**Crockett, 1982**).

**College students.** Studies of college students in the **United States and Canada** found that cognitively complex students show **less anxiety and instability**, **cope better with the pressures of college**, and tend to display **more than the traditional five factors** of personality. Those **lower** in cognitive complexity show **fewer than five factors**, suggesting they are **less emotionally complex** (**Bowler, Bowler, & Phillips, 2009; Lester, 2009; Pancer, Hunsberger, Pratt, & Alisat, 2000**). The "five factors" are the Big Five of the trait theorists (Chapter 8); the idea is that a complex person's self-descriptions cannot be compressed into five dimensions, while a simple person's collapse into fewer.

**Politicians.** Studies of politicians in the **United States and England** found that **conservatives were high in cognitive simplicity**, while **moderates and liberals** showed **higher cognitive complexity** (**Tetlock, 1983, 1984**).

**Therapists.** **Mental health counsellors and therapists** with **more training and experience** showed **greater cognitive complexity** than those with less training and fewer years in practice (**Granello, 2010; Owen & Lindley, 2010**).

**Why complexity is the better style, in Kelly's terms.** In Kelly's theory **cognitive complexity is the more desirable and useful style**. The point of building a construct system is to **reduce uncertainty** by being able to predict what people will do, which then guides our own behaviour. People with a more complex cognitive style will do this better than people with a simpler one. That is why Schultz calls cognitive style an important dimension of personality.

#### Changes over Time

Cognitive complexity **increases with age**: adults generally show more of it than children. But age is not the whole story, since **many adults remain cognitively simple**. Much depends on how complex one's **childhood experiences** were. Adults high in cognitive complexity typically had **more diverse experiences in childhood**, and their **parents were less authoritarian and more willing to grant autonomy** than the parents of adults high in cognitive simplicity (**Sechrest & Jackson, 1961**). This fits Kelly well: a child who meets a wide range of people and situations, and is allowed to test her own constructs against them, has more occasions to extend her system.

#### Becoming Alike or Being Alike?

A study of **40 couples** found that **women scored significantly higher in cognitive complexity than men**, but that there was a **high correlation** in cognitive complexity **between partners**. The researcher offered two possibilities: the partners may have **chosen each other** because their levels of complexity were already similar, or they may have **grown similar through living together**. Either way, partners tended to **construe their worlds in similar ways** (**Adams-Webber, 2001**). Note that, unlike Duck and Spencer's friendship study, this study could **not** separate selection from convergence; it leaves both explanations open.

#### Cultural Factors

Researchers compared **monocultural Anglo-American** college students with **bicultural Chinese-American** college students. The Chinese-American students had been **born in China** and had lived **at least five years in the United States**. The **bicultural** students scored **higher in cognitive complexity** than the monocultural students (**Benet-Martinez, Lee, & Leu, 2006**). Living between two cultures means routinely construing the same events through two different sets of constructs, which is a form of training in complexity. Many Indian students who have grown up between a home language and an English-medium school would recognise the experience.

#### Attributional Complexity

A variant of cognitive complexity is **attributional complexity**: the extent to which people **prefer complex rather than simple explanations for social behaviour**. People high in attributional complexity explain what others do in terms of **complicated, multifaceted and more sophisticated causes**. Faced with a classmate who has stopped attending, the attributionally simple person says "lazy"; the attributionally complex person wonders about family pressures, health, a falling-out with a friend, and a timetable clash, all at once.

People high in attributional complexity have been found to be **more sensitive to, and more perceptive of, subtle signs of racism**, and to show **greater empathy and understanding** of other people (**Foels & Reid, 2010; Reid & Foels, 2010**). In business, **leaders** who score high on attributional complexity **manage more effectively** than low scorers (**Sun & Anderson, 2012**).

> **Trap.** **Cognitive complexity** = how many different ways you can **discriminate among people** (how many independent constructs you use). **Attributional complexity** = whether you prefer **complex explanations of why** people behave as they do. Attributional complexity is described as a **variant** of cognitive complexity, not as the same thing, and not as Kelly's own concept.

> **Trap.** In Tetlock's studies **conservatives** were **high in cognitive simplicity**; **moderates and liberals** were higher in complexity. In the couples study **women** scored higher than men. **Bicultural** students scored higher than **monocultural** ones. **More experienced** therapists scored higher than less experienced ones. Do not invert any of these.

### Highlights: Research on Kelly's Ideas

Schultz's summary box, in our words:

**Rep Test research shows that:**
- personal constructs **stay stable** over time;
- we choose **friends whose constructs resemble ours**;
- **spouses** with similar constructs are **happier**;
- people with **schizophrenia** construe **objects** stably but **people** unstably;
- **delinquents** identify with **action heroes** rather than real people.

**People high in cognitive complexity tend to:**
- score **low in anxiety**;
- show **more than the traditional five** personality factors;
- be **good at predicting** how others will behave;
- hold **moderate to liberal** political views;
- have had **more diverse childhood experiences**;
- **adjust better** to the stresses of college.

**People high in attributional complexity:**
- explain others' behaviour by **complex causes**;
- show **greater empathy and understanding**;
- are **sensitive to subtle signs of racism**.

## 11.8 Reflections on Kelly's Theory

### An original theory, built from one man's constructs

Kelly produced a **unique and radical** theory that was **not derived from, and did not build on, any earlier theory**. It came out of his own interpretation, which is to say his own construct system, of the data his clinical practice supplied. Schultz notes the neat fit between the theory's origin and its message: a theory that says each of us can build the framework for our own life was itself built by one man constructing his own framework.

### Criticisms

Schultz sets out several points of criticism. Each is worth understanding, not just listing, because "which is NOT a criticism of Kelly" is a common question format.

**1. Too rational; emotion left out.** The theory concentrates on the **intellectual and rational** side of human functioning to the **exclusion of the emotional** side. Kelly's picture of a person who rationally constructs the present and future, forms and tests hypotheses, and predicts before acting does not match the daily experience of clinical psychologists who see **more extreme** human behaviour: the psychotic patient, the person in a rage, the person overwhelmed by grief. To them, Kelly's rational being looks like an **ideal that exists in the abstract but not in reality**. The defence available to Kelly is limited: he did not deal with emotions explicitly, though he **recognised them as personal constructs**, formed in the same way as other constructs. *Classic background:* his redefinitions of anxiety, threat, fear, guilt and hostility (end of 11.4) are the fullest form of that answer, but they still translate emotion into cognition rather than treating it as a force in its own right, which is exactly what critics object to.

**2. Familiar concepts omitted.** The chapter summary puts the related criticism bluntly: the theory **omits motivation and emotion**, concepts that most personality psychologists regard as central.

**3. An unrepresentative sample.** Freud's theory was criticised for resting on neurotic, middle-class Viennese patients, a distorted sample of humanity, and other theorists have faced similar charges. Kelly's own sample was **also unrepresentative**: largely **Midwestern young adults** in the process of building construct systems to cope with **college life**. As 11.2 showed, this was precisely the group most likely to present problems rationally and intellectually.

**4. Unanswered questions.** Like many theories, Kelly's leaves important questions open. Each of us can construe events in a unique way, but **why** does one person construe an event one way and someone else construe it differently? **What process or mechanism** produces the difference? The individuality corollary describes the fact but does not explain it. Likewise, a person chooses between defining and extending the construct system, but **what determines whether he opts for security or for adventure**, the safe alternative or the risky one? The choice corollary names the choice without saying what decides it.

> **Trap.** Criticisms of Kelly are: overemphasis on **rationality**, neglect of **emotion** and **motivation**, an **unrepresentative sample** (Midwestern college-age clients), and **unanswered questions** (the mechanism behind individual differences in construing; what decides the security-versus-adventure choice). The following are **NOT** criticisms of Kelly: overemphasis on sex or instincts, biological or childhood determinism, pessimism about human nature, reliance on the unconscious, or lack of an assessment method (he had several).

### Support abroad, neglect at home

Personal construct theory **continues to have a large and growing base of support**, but that support is **much broader in Europe, Canada and Australia than in the United States**. Three markers of its organised following are examinable:

- **England, mid-1980s:** the **Centre for Personal Construct Psychology** was founded, with a double brief: training clinicians in Kelly's therapy and spreading the theory's applications.
- **Late 1980s:** two journals were launched, the ***International Journal of Personal Construct Psychology*** and the ***Journal of Constructivist Psychology***.
- In **1990** the first volume of the series ***Advances in Personal Construct Psychology*** appeared.

Why has Kelly been less popular in the United States? Schultz gives two reasons.

1. **Too different.** Many psychologists see the theory as too far from prevailing ideas. Personality psychologists tend to think in terms of motivation and emotion, unconscious forces, drives and needs, and none of these is part of Kelly's system.
2. **Too little published, and too scholarly.** Kelly wrote **few books, articles or case studies**; he spent most of his time on **clinical work and training graduate students**. His two major books are written in a **scholarly** style, not aimed at the general public or at therapists looking for explanations of human passions, loves and hatreds, fears and dreams. Schultz remarks that this simply was not the style of the man or of his theory.

### Kelly's own view, and his legacy

Kelly was candid about the limitations of his programme and **never claimed to have produced a finished theory**. In keeping with the theory itself, he expected it to **change with further research and application**, just as a person's constructs change in the light of new experience. A theory of revisable hypotheses was offered as a revisable hypothesis. His contributions have been recognised with **honours from the profession and from his former students**.

Schultz's final verdict is that Kelly's is **one of the most unusual theories** to appear in a century of theorising about personality. Its followers continue to apply it in **clinical psychology, industrial psychology, anthropology, criminology and urban planning**, as a way of modifying and predicting behaviour in many areas of life (**Butt, 2008; Walker & Winter, 2007**).

> **Trap.** Kelly's following is **stronger outside the United States** (Europe, Canada, Australia), and the training centre was founded in **England** in the **mid-1980s**. An option placing the main centre of personal construct psychology in the US, or giving it a 1950s date, is wrong.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the whole chapter. If you can follow each one from the postulate to the research, you understand Kelly rather than merely remembering him.

1. **Anticipation, not causation from the past.** The fundamental postulate says psychological processes are directed by how we **anticipate** events. Every corollary elaborates that: we anticipate from recurring themes (construction), with constructs that are bipolar (dichotomy), choose the pole that best predicts (choice), and revise when prediction fails (experience). Human nature follows: future-oriented, free, not determined by childhood or instinct.

2. **Interpretation over event.** Constructs are interpretations, not copies of reality (individuality), so they can always be replaced by better ones (**constructive alternativism**). That single principle grounds Kelly's optimism, his free-will position and his therapy.

3. **Predictive efficiency is the only test.** Constructs, their organisation, and the whole system are judged by how well they predict. This explains odd behaviour (the aggressive man choosing the predictable pole), why cognitive complexity is the better style (it predicts others better), and why hostility in Kelly's sense is pathological (forcing evidence to fit a prediction that has already failed).

4. **Change and its limits.** Experience pushes constructs to change; permeability (modulation) sets how far they can; fragmentation explains how inconsistencies survive at subordinate levels. Pathology, in Kelly's language, is largely impermeability: constructs that can no longer learn.

5. **The social person.** Commonality (similar constructs) and sociality (construing another's constructs) explain friendship, marriage and roles. The research follows directly: friends already share constructs (Duck & Spencer, 1972), similar spouses are happier (Neimeyer, 1984), repeat psychiatric patients construe a smaller social world (Smith et al., 1991), schizophrenic thought disorder is specifically social (Bannister and colleagues).

6. **Assessment and therapy as experiments.** The interview with a credulous attitude, the self-characterisation sketch and the Rep Test all elicit constructs; fixed role therapy invites the client to test new ones. The therapist is a research supervisor and the client a fellow scientist.

**How Kelly relates to the other theorists in Schultz.**

- **Freud (Ch 2):** the opposite pole on almost every issue. No unconscious, no instincts, no psychosexual determinism; the client's words are taken at face value instead of being decoded. Both theories are criticised for an unrepresentative clinical sample, from opposite directions.
- **Adler (Ch 4):** the closest ancestor in spirit. Adler's **creative self** and **fictional finalism** (behaviour guided by subjective ideas about the future that may not be literally true) anticipate Kelly's view that we live by our interpretations and expectations. Schultz's part opener names Adler's creative self as one of the cognitive elements in earlier theories.
- **Erikson (Ch 6):** gave the ego and cognition more autonomy, but within a psychoanalytic stage theory; Kelly has no stages at all.
- **Allport (Ch 7):** shares the focus on the conscious, rational adult and the individual case; the Rep Test is as idiographic as Allport could have wished. But Allport's units are traits; Kelly's are constructs.
- **Maslow (Ch 9) and Rogers (Ch 10):** share Kelly's rejection of behaviourism and psychoanalysis, his optimism and his emphasis on free will and the person's own view of experience. The difference is that the humanists centre on feelings, needs and self-actualisation, while Kelly reduces everything to construing. His career crossed both men: he replaced Rogers at Ohio State and was invited to Brandeis by Maslow.
- **Skinner (Ch 12):** the stimulus-response arrow Kelly could not understand in his first psychology course. Skinner's empty organism is exactly the passive responder Kelly rejected.
- **Bandura (Ch 13):** the social-learning approach shares the interest in cognition (expectations, self-efficacy) but, unlike Kelly, studies overt behaviour experimentally and keeps reinforcement; it belongs to the broader cognitive movement that, Schultz says, did not adopt Kelly.

## Worked example

*Arjun is a 24-year-old M.A. student referred by his department to the college counselling service. He has failed to submit two assignments and has stopped attending a seminar group. In the intake interview he says that the other students in the seminar are "show-offs" who talk in order to impress the professor, and that he will not "lower himself" to compete with them. He describes himself as "a serious person, not a performer". He has one close friend from his home town and says he has never needed more. His father, he says, is "a man who never wasted words". He admits he is lonely and that he dreads the seminar, where he expects to be laughed at if he speaks. Asked what would happen if he did speak, he says, "I don't know. I've never tried."*

Reason it through in Kelly's terms, step by step.

**1. Assessment: what are his constructs?** A personal construct therapist begins with the **interview** and a **credulous attitude**. She does not treat "show-offs" as a defence hiding envy; she takes it as a genuine construct and asks what its contrast pole is. Arjun's answers suggest a dominant construct something like **serious versus performer (show-off)**, with *serious* the pole he applies to himself and his father, and *performer* the pole he applies to anyone who speaks easily in public.

She then asks him to write a **self-characterisation sketch** in the third person, as an intimate, sympathetic friend would. Suppose it describes "a quiet man who keeps his thoughts to himself and does not trust people who talk too much". This confirms the construct and shows how he places himself in relation to others.

She gives him the **Rep Test**. In sort after sort he pairs figures as "talk too much" against "keep to themselves"; the ticks for "talk too much" fall on almost everyone except himself, his father and his friend. Two things stand out. First, many of his constructs sort people in nearly the same way, suggesting **cognitive simplicity**: he discriminates among people on very few independent dimensions. Second, the implicit pole of "serious" is "performer", not, say, "frivolous" or "dishonest". In his system, **speaking up is itself construed as shallow**. That is the personal, non-dictionary contrast the **dichotomy corollary** leads us to look for.

**2. Formulation: why does he keep doing what hurts him?** The **choice corollary** explains the puzzle. Staying silent is the **secure, low-risk** choice: Arjun knows exactly what happens when he says nothing (loneliness, but no surprises). Speaking is the **adventurous** choice: he has, by his own account, never tried it, so he cannot predict the result. People choose for **predictability**, not for what is best for them, exactly like Schultz's aggressive man who keeps being aggressive because he knows what it brings.

His construct "my classmates are show-offs" also looks **impermeable** (**modulation corollary**): every fluent speaker is admitted to the "show-off" pole, and no counter-example (a classmate who speaks well *and* is clearly serious) gets through; it is simply dismissed. The construct is acting as a barrier to learning.

There is a hint of **hostility** in Kelly's technical sense (Classic background): by refusing to test his prediction that he would be laughed at, and by reading every fluent classmate as a performer, Arjun keeps his social predictions from being disconfirmed rather than revising them.

His isolation fits the **sociality corollary**: he is not construing his classmates' constructs. He does not know how they see the seminar, or him, because he has never found out, so he cannot build a workable **role** in the group. And the seminar setting is new: as a student from a small town in a large metropolitan department, he faces events that partly lie outside the **range of convenience** of his existing constructs, which is Kelly's definition of **anxiety** (Classic background).

**3. Intervention: fixed role therapy.** Following **constructive alternativism**, the therapist does not argue that his construct is wrong. She writes a **fixed role sketch** about a fictitious character, perhaps called "Vikram", and keeps it deliberately away from a mirror-image reversal (Vikram is not a "performer"). Like the Roy Taylor sketch, it builds on something Arjun already does well, his careful reading, and introduces a **new dimension**: Vikram is a serious student who is **curious about how other people think**, who asks one genuine question in each seminar because he wants to understand the other person's argument, and who judges people by whether they have read the material, not by whether they talk.

She asks whether Vikram is someone Arjun might like to know, rehearses being Vikram in the session, and asks him to **act, think and talk like Vikram for two weeks**. Each seminar becomes an **experiment**: what actually happens when Vikram asks a question? If classmates answer seriously, the prediction "I will be laughed at" is disconfirmed, and the construct "talking in public = showing off" has met evidence that a serious person can talk. Because it is Vikram doing it, the experiment feels safe (the adventurous choice made secure).

**4. Expected change, in Kelly's terms.** If the role works, Arjun should **extend** his construct system rather than merely define it; his construct about speaking should become more **permeable**; he should begin to **construe his classmates' constructs** (sociality) and so find a role in the group; and on a repeat Rep Test his constructs should sort people in more varied ways, a move towards **cognitive complexity**. He has not been "cured" of a drive or a complex. He has acquired a construction of events that predicts better, which, for Kelly, is what psychological change is.

**5. What a critic would say.** Arjun is articulate, rational and a student: just the kind of client Kelly's theory was built on. A critic would point out that the formulation says little about how much his dread *feels* like, or why he, rather than another student from the same town and the same kind of family, came to construe speaking as shallow (the **unanswered question** of what produces individual differences in construing). These are exactly the criticisms in 11.8.

## MCQ traps

1. **What Kelly leaves out:** the unconscious, the ego, needs, drives, stimuli and responses, reinforcement, and even **motivation and emotion**. Any option crediting Kelly with an unconscious or a drive is wrong.
2. **Kelly's approach is the Cognitive Approach**, and he is its **only** theorist in Schultz. He is not a humanist, despite sharing Maslow's opposition to behaviourism and psychoanalysis.
3. **The cognitive movement (from about 1960) did not embrace Kelly**; Schultz calls him at best a **precursor**. They share the word "cognitive" and little else.
4. **Birth and family:** **1905**, a **farm in Kansas**, **only child**, **fundamentalist** parents opposed to frivolous entertainment (dancing, cards); covered-wagon move to **Colorado at age 4**, then back to Kansas.
5. **Education:** high school in **Wichita** from **13**; **B.A. physics and mathematics, Park College (Parkville, Missouri), 1926**; **M.A. educational sociology, University of Kansas**; **B.Ed., Edinburgh** (fellowship **1929**); **Ph.D., State University of Iowa, 1931**.
6. **Odd jobs before psychology:** engineer; teacher at a **labour college in Minneapolis**; **speech** instructor for the **American Banking Association**; **citizenship classes for immigrants**; junior college in **Iowa**, coaching **drama**.
7. **Kelly trained in physiological psychology** and switched to **clinical** psychology at **Fort Hays Kansas State College** because the **1930s Depression** left no research money. He ran **travelling school clinics**.
8. **His clients were students referred by teachers**, not psychotic or severely neurotic patients; hence the theory's rational, intellectual person. Schultz: with schizophrenic patients the theory might have been less cognitive.
9. **He taught himself therapy by assuming each client was as much a scientist as himself (Routh, 2011).** *Classic background:* in Kansas he also gave clients **invented interpretations**, which helped when they offered a usable new way of construing; the seed of constructive alternativism.
10. **Later career:** U.S. **Navy**, **Bureau of Medicine and Surgery**, Washington DC; **University of Maryland** for one year after 1945; **Ohio State University for 19 years**, **replacing Carl Rogers**; **1965** invitation from **Maslow** to an endowed chair at **Brandeis**; died shortly afterwards (1967).
11. **Honours:** president of the **Clinical** and **Consulting** divisions of the **APA** and of the **American Board of Examiners in Professional Psychology**.
12. **"Transparent patterns"** laid over reality (Kelly, 1955, pp. 8 to 9); Schultz's analogy is **tinted sunglasses**; the individual pattern is the **construct system**.
13. **Constructive alternativism** (not "alternative constructivism") = we are free to **revise or replace** constructs; we are not controlled by them. It does not say all constructions are equally good.
14. **Fundamental postulate:** psychological **processes** are **directed** by how we **anticipate** events. *Classic background:* Kelly's wording is "**psychologically channelized**". One postulate, **eleven** corollaries.
15. **Corollary order:** Construction, Individuality, Organisation, Dichotomy, Choice, Range, Experience, Modulation, Fragmentation, Commonality, Sociality.
16. **Construction corollary** = anticipation from **similarities among repeated events** (recurring themes; Kelly's "replications"); Schultz's example is **re-watching a film**.
17. **Individuality vs organisation:** individuality = people **construe events differently**; organisation = constructs arranged in a **hierarchy**, so people with similar constructs can still differ by **arrangement**.
18. **Organisation corollary:** *good* may contain *intelligent* and *moral*; relationships among constructs are **more enduring** than the constructs themselves; the **only valid test** of a construct system is **predictive efficiency**.
19. **Dichotomy corollary:** **all** constructs are **bipolar** (honest versus dishonest); a construct with no contrast predicts nothing. It is the **assumption underlying the Rep Test**.
20. **Choice corollary:** **secure** (low risk, high predictive efficiency) **defines/limits** the system; **adventurous** (high risk) **extends** it. People choose for **predictability**, not benefit (the aggressive man keeps choosing aggression).
21. **Range corollary:** **range of convenience** (tall versus short fits buildings, not pizza or weather); knowing what a construct **excludes** matters as much as what it includes. *Classic background:* **focus of convenience** = where a construct applies best.
22. **Experience corollary:** constructs are continually **tested** and revised; constructs useful at **16** may be useless or harmful at **40**.
23. **Modulation corollary = permeability.** **Permeable** constructs admit new elements to their range and can be revised; **impermeable** ones cannot. Schultz's example is the **bigot**.
24. **Fragmentation corollary:** **inconsistent subordinate** constructs coexist (the woman who is a **friend** in psychology class and an **enemy** at a political **rally**) while the superordinate construct (liberals friends, conservatives enemies) stays intact.
25. **Commonality vs sociality:** commonality = **similar constructs** (same culture); sociality = **construing another person's constructs** (understanding how they think). Sociality, not commonality, produces **roles** and good relationships; Schultz's example is **driving**.
26. **Human nature:** **optimistic** (even flattering), **free will**, **future**-oriented, **not** determined by the past, instincts or the unconscious; **motivated by the fact of being alive**; **heredity not discussed**, environment **not totally determining**; uniqueness-universality **moderate**; no ultimate goal, inferred goal = a **construct system that predicts events**.
27. **Interview:** Kelly's **primary** technique; "If you don't know what is going on in a person's mind, ask him; he may tell you!" (**1958**, p. 330). **Credulous attitude** = accept words at **face value**, respect them even if not fully believed.
28. **Self-characterisation sketch** (Kelly, **1955**, p. 323): written by the **client**, in the third person, as the **principal character in a play**, as an **intimate, sympathetic friend** would write it; shows how the client sees himself **in relation to others**.
29. **Rep Test mechanics:** **24 role titles** (Table 11.2); names presented in **threes**; pick the **two alike** and say how the **third** differs; **emergent pole** = the two alike; **implicit pole** = the contrast for the third; **check marks** = everyone else sharing the emergent pole. Fig 11.1 shows **19 figures and 22 sorts**.
30. **The Rep Test is not a standardised objective self-report inventory**; it is a stage in therapy, and its interpretation (and validity) depends on the **skill** of the psychologist. Computer analysis of grids came later (**Grice, 2002**).
31. **Fixed role therapy:** therapist writes a **fixed role sketch** about a **fictitious** character; client acts it in the office and in daily life for **two weeks**; inspired by a **friend changed by a role in a college play**; "first **play** a role, then **live** it". Not Moreno's psychodrama.
32. **Roy Taylor (Winter, 1992, pp. 270 to 271):** preoccupied with finding a female partner, saw **assertiveness and extraversion as negative**, sure he was right, felt socially superior at work; the sketch **never mentioned women** and used his **tennis** skill to build **curiosity and tolerance**. Controlled evidence is thin: **Abe, Imai, & Nedate (2011)**, **social anxiety**, small sample, **Japan**.
33. **Duck & Spencer (1972):** Rep Test in the **first week of college** and **six months later**; construct similarity among friends **pre-dated** the friendships (selection, not convergence). **Neimeyer (1984):** spouses with **similar constructs** were **happier**.
34. **Sechrest (1968):** anxious **student nurses** used *anxious versus non-anxious*; peer-rated friendly ones used *friendly versus unfriendly*.
35. **Schizophrenia:** **unstable** construing of **people**, **stable** construing of **objects**; paranoid delusions and irrational links between constructs (**Bannister, Fransella, & Agnew, 1971; Bannister & Salmon, 1966; Winter, 1992**).
36. **Smith, Stefan, Kovaleski, & Johnson (1991):** **repeat** psychiatric inpatients construed a **small** social network; first-timers a **significantly larger** one. **Needs (1988):** delinquents identified with **action-oriented TV heroes**; released prisoners had low self-esteem and aspirations; rapists felt **inadequate, immature**, preoccupied with failure.
37. **Applied and recent studies:** market research and I/O uses (**Benjafield, 2008**); reckless drivers in **Australia** (**McNally & Bradley, 2014**); statistics teaching in **Paris** (**Clayson, 2013**); convicted terrorists in **India**, studied by **British** psychologists (**Canter, Sarangi, & Youngs, 2014**).
38. **Cognitive complexity** = ability to perceive **differences among people**; **cognitive simplicity** = relative inability. Derived from the **Rep Test**; *Classic background:* concept introduced by **Bieri (1955)**.
39. **Cognitive complexity correlates:** better prediction of others, more empathy, handles inconsistent information (**Crockett, 1982**); lower anxiety, better college adjustment, **more than five** personality factors (Bowler et al., 2009; Lester, 2009; Pancer et al., 2000); **conservatives high in simplicity**, moderates and liberals more complex (**Tetlock, 1983, 1984**); experienced therapists more complex (**Granello, 2010; Owen & Lindley, 2010**).
40. **Development and groups:** complexity rises with **age** but many adults stay simple; complex adults had **diverse childhoods** and **less authoritarian, autonomy-granting parents** (**Sechrest & Jackson, 1961**); **40 couples**, **women higher**, partners highly correlated (**Adams-Webber, 2001**); **bicultural Chinese-Americans** (born in China, **at least five years** in the US) higher than monocultural Anglo-Americans (**Benet-Martinez, Lee, & Leu, 2006**).
41. **Attributional complexity** is a **variant** of cognitive complexity: preferring **complex explanations of social behaviour**; linked to sensitivity to **subtle racism**, empathy (**Foels & Reid, 2010; Reid & Foels, 2010**), and **more effective business leadership** (**Sun & Anderson, 2012**).
42. **Criticisms:** too rational, neglects **emotion** and **motivation**, **unrepresentative sample** (Midwestern young adults coping with college), **unanswered questions** (why people construe differently; what decides security versus adventure). **Not** criticisms: pessimism, sexual or biological determinism, overreliance on the unconscious.
43. **Following:** stronger in **Europe, Canada and Australia** than the US; **Centre for Personal Construct Psychology, England, mid-1980s**; *International Journal of Personal Construct Psychology* and *Journal of Constructivist Psychology*, **late 1980s**; *Advances in Personal Construct Psychology*, **1990**. Reasons for US neglect: too different; Kelly published little, in a scholarly style.
44. *Classic background:* **Kelly's emotions:** **anxiety** = events outside the **range of convenience** of one's constructs; **threat** = imminent **comprehensive** change in **core** constructs; **fear** = imminent **incidental** change; **guilt** = dislodgement from one's **core role**; **hostility** = **extorting evidence** for a prediction that has already failed; **aggression** = active elaboration of one's perceptual field. Not in the 11th edition.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cognition** | The act or process of knowing; the cognitive approach studies how people perceive, evaluate, learn, think, decide and solve problems |
| **Personal construct theory** | Kelly's account of personality in terms of cognitive processes: we interpret behaviour and events and use that understanding to guide our own behaviour and predict other people's |
| **Person as scientist** | Kelly's model of human nature: everyone forms hypotheses about the world, tests them against experience, and keeps or revises them (*Classic background:* Kelly's phrase "man the scientist") |
| **Construct** | An intellectual hypothesis a person devises to interpret or explain life events; always bipolar (tall versus short, honest versus dishonest) |
| **Personal construct** | One of the individual's own theories by which he or she tries to predict and control events |
| **Construct system** | The unique, organised pattern of constructs through which a person views the world (the "tinted lenses") |
| **Constructive alternativism** | The principle that we are free to revise or replace our constructs with alternatives as needed, so we are not controlled by them |
| **Fundamental postulate** | Our psychological processes are directed by the ways in which we anticipate events (*Classic background:* "psychologically channelized") |
| **Corollary** | One of the eleven propositions derived from the fundamental postulate |
| **Construction corollary** | Repeated events are never identical but share recurring themes, which let us anticipate similar events |
| **Individuality corollary** | People differ in how they construe events, so they form different constructs |
| **Organisation corollary** | We arrange constructs into a pattern, usually a hierarchy, according to their perceived similarities and differences |
| **Superordinate / subordinate construct** | A higher-level construct that contains others / a lower-level construct contained by another (good above intelligent and moral) |
| **Predictive efficiency** | How well a construct or construct system predicts events; Kelly's only valid test of a construct system |
| **Dichotomy corollary** | Every construct is bipolar, a pair of mutually exclusive alternatives |
| **Choice corollary** | We choose the pole of a construct that best lets us anticipate outcomes, trading security against adventure |
| **Secure (low-risk) choice** | The predictable option with high predictive efficiency and little reward; defines and limits the construct system |
| **Adventurous (high-risk) choice** | The uncertain option with lower predictive efficiency and greater potential reward; extends the construct system |
| **Range corollary** | Each construct applies only to a limited set of people or situations, broad or narrow by personal choice |
| **Range of convenience** | The spectrum of events to which a construct can be applied |
| **Focus of convenience** | *Classic background:* the events for which a construct is most useful, within its range |
| **Experience corollary** | Constructs are continually tested against experience and revised when they fail to predict |
| **Modulation corollary** | How far a construct system can change with new experience depends on the permeability of its constructs |
| **Permeability** | The capacity of a construct to admit new elements and be revised or extended by new experience |
| **Impermeable construct** | A rigid construct that no experience can change (the bigot's construct) |
| **Fragmentation corollary** | Inconsistent or contradictory subordinate constructs can coexist within the overall system |
| **Commonality corollary** | People in compatible groups or cultures construe events similarly, so their psychological processes are similar |
| **Sociality corollary** | We try to construe how others think, predict what they will do, and adjust our behaviour accordingly |
| **Role** | A pattern of behaviour arising from understanding how another person construes events |
| **Credulous attitude** | Kelly's stance of accepting a client's words at face value and respecting them even when not fully believed |
| **Self-characterisation sketch** | A technique for assessing a person's construct system: the client writes a third-person character sketch of himself as a sympathetic, intimate friend would, showing how he perceives himself in relation to others |
| **Role Construct Repertory Test (Rep Test)** | Kelly's method for eliciting the constructs a person applies to the significant people in his or her life |
| **Role title list** | The 24 roles (liked teacher, mother, most successful person known, and so on) for which the client names people |
| **Sort (triad)** | One comparison in the Rep Test: three figures, of whom the client says how two are alike and differ from the third |
| **Repertory grid** | The matrix recording role figures, sorts, constructs and check marks for a Rep Test |
| **Emergent pole** | The characteristic the client says the two similar figures in a sort share |
| **Implicit pole** | The contrasting characteristic the client assigns to the third figure |
| **Fixed role therapy** | A therapy in which the client acts out constructs appropriate for a fictitious person, showing that new constructs can work better than old ones |
| **Fixed role sketch** | The therapist-written description of the fictitious character the client enacts |
| **Cognitive style** | A characteristic way of perceiving or construing people, objects and situations |
| **Cognitive complexity** | A cognitive style marked by the ability to perceive differences among people |
| **Cognitive simplicity** | A cognitive style marked by a relative inability to perceive differences among people |
| **Attributional complexity** | A variant of cognitive complexity: the preference for complex, multifaceted explanations of social behaviour |
| **Core constructs / core role** | *Classic background:* constructs governing a person's basic identity / one's construing of oneself in relation to significant others |
| **Anxiety (Kelly)** | *Classic background:* recognising that events lie outside the range of convenience of one's construct system |
| **Threat / fear (Kelly)** | *Classic background:* awareness of an imminent comprehensive / incidental change in one's core constructs |
| **Guilt (Kelly)** | *Classic background:* perceiving one's dislodgement from one's core role |
| **Hostility (Kelly)** | *Classic background:* the continued effort to extort evidence for a social prediction that has already failed |
| **C-P-C cycle** | *Classic background:* circumspection, pre-emption, control: the sequence by which a person arrives at a choice and acts |

## Likely exam points

1. **Kelly's distinctiveness:** a theory with no unconscious, drives, needs, reinforcement, motivation or emotion; personality explained entirely by cognitive constructs. Interpretation of events matters more than the events.
2. **The Cognitive Approach part opener:** cognition = the process of knowing; other theorists (psychoanalysts, Erikson, Maslow, Rogers, Allport, Adler's creative self, social-learning theorists) used cognition; Kelly alone tried to explain all of personality, emotion included, cognitively.
3. **Person as scientist:** we form, test and revise hypotheses like scientists; psychologists wrongly credit only themselves with rationality ("Are psychologists superior beings?").
4. **Kelly versus the cognitive movement:** clinical, conscious constructs and personality versus experimental study of cognition and overt behaviour; the movement (about 1960) did not adopt him; he is a precursor.
5. **Life and theory:** Kansas farm, only child, fundamentalist parents; physics and mathematics (Park College, 1926); varied jobs including drama coaching; disliked S-R psychology and Freud; Edinburgh B.Ed. (1929); Iowa Ph.D. (1931) in physiological psychology.
6. **Depression-era Fort Hays:** switch to clinical work, travelling school clinics, clients were students referred by teachers, hence a rational, intellectual theory; taught himself therapy by treating clients as scientists (Routh, 2011).
7. **Later career:** Navy psychologist; Maryland; Ohio State for 19 years replacing Rogers; Brandeis invitation from Maslow (1965); APA Clinical and Consulting division presidencies.
8. **Construct and construct system:** bipolar intellectual hypotheses; transparent patterns; sunglasses analogy; the student-and-professor flattery example of testing a construct.
9. **Constructive alternativism:** freedom to revise or replace constructs; foundation of Kelly's free-will position and of his therapy.
10. **Fundamental postulate:** processes directed by anticipation; key words *processes* and *anticipate*; Kelly's wording "psychologically channelized" (classic).
11. **All eleven corollaries in order with meanings** (Table 11.1), and the commonly confused pairs: individuality/organisation, organisation/fragmentation, experience/modulation, commonality/sociality.
12. **Choice corollary:** security versus adventure, defining versus extending, predictive efficiency, and why aggressive people persist.
13. **Range corollary:** range of convenience (tall versus short), personal width of range, importance of exclusions; focus of convenience (classic).
14. **Modulation corollary and permeability:** permeable versus impermeable constructs; the bigot as the example of an impermeable construct.
15. **Fragmentation corollary:** the friend-in-class, enemy-at-the-rally example; subordinate inconsistency tolerated, superordinate construct intact.
16. **Sociality corollary and role:** construing others' constructs; the driving example; roles as behaviour patterns built on understanding another's construing.
17. **Kelly's reinterpretation of emotions (classic):** anxiety, threat, fear, guilt, hostility, aggression defined as states of the construct system.
18. **Human nature:** optimistic, free will, future, non-deterministic, motivated by being alive, heredity not discussed, moderate on uniqueness; inferred goal of a predictive construct system; the six-row table.
19. **Interview and credulous attitude:** "ask him; he may tell you" (1958).
20. **Self-characterisation sketch:** third person, as a sympathetic intimate friend would write it; reveals self in relation to others.
21. **Rep Test procedure:** 24 role titles; triads; emergent and implicit poles; check marks; based on the dichotomy corollary; not standardised; interpretation needs skill; computer grid analysis (Grice, 2002). Be able to describe Fig 11.1.
22. **Fixed role therapy:** fixed role sketch by therapist, fictitious character, two weeks, origin in a friend's college play, "play a role, then live it"; Roy Taylor case (Winter, 1992); Abe, Imai and Nedate (2011) in Japan.
23. **Stability and relationships:** constructs stable across different role figures; Crockett (1982) life-span differentiation and integration; Duck and Spencer (1972) friendship selection; Neimeyer (1984) marital happiness.
24. **Clinical research:** Sechrest (1968) nurses; schizophrenia (people unstable, objects stable; Bannister and colleagues); Smith et al. (1991) repeat patients; Needs (1988) offenders.
25. **Applications:** market research, I/O psychology (Benjafield, 2008); reckless driving (McNally & Bradley, 2014); statistics teaching (Clayson, 2013); terrorists in India unable to re-construe their futures (Canter, Sarangi, & Youngs, 2014).
26. **Cognitive complexity versus simplicity:** definitions; derived from the Rep Test; Bieri (1955, classic); correlates (prediction, empathy, low anxiety, more than five factors, liberal or moderate politics, experienced therapists).
27. **Origins of complexity:** age plus diverse childhood experience and less authoritarian parents (Sechrest & Jackson, 1961); couples (Adams-Webber, 2001); biculturalism (Benet-Martinez, Lee, & Leu, 2006).
28. **Attributional complexity:** definition and correlates (racism sensitivity, empathy, leadership).
29. **Criticisms and NOT-criticisms:** too rational, emotion and motivation omitted, unrepresentative sample, unanswered questions; not pessimistic, not deterministic, not unconscious-based.
30. **Status and legacy:** stronger in Europe, Canada and Australia; Centre for Personal Construct Psychology (England, mid-1980s); journals (late 1980s); Advances series (1990); reasons for US neglect; applied in clinical, industrial, anthropology, criminology and urban planning (Butt, 2008; Walker & Winter, 2007).
