# Chapter 9: Abraham Maslow: Needs-Hierarchy Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** Every theory you have met so far in Schultz was built, at least partly, out of people who were struggling: Freud's hysterical patients, Horney's neurotics, Adler's discouraged children, even Erikson's adolescents in crisis. Maslow turns the question round. If you want to know what human beings are capable of, he argued, look at the best of them, not the worst. His answer is a theory of **motivation**: every person is born with the same set of needs, arranged in a **hierarchy** from the most urgent (food, water, safety) to the most fragile (the need to become everything one is capable of becoming, which he called **self-actualisation**). The lower needs are **deficit needs**: when they go unmet, the person is in trouble. The higher needs are **growth needs**: going without them is not an emergency, but meeting them makes a person healthier, happier and more fully human. On top of that hierarchy Maslow built a portrait of the rare people (about one in a hundred, by his estimate) who have reached the top, and a vocabulary to describe them: **metamotivation**, **metaneeds**, **metapathology**, **peak experiences**, and the **Jonah complex** that stops the rest of us from following them. The chapter also covers the modern research that grew out of the theory, especially on **self-esteem**, and the theory's most successful descendant, **self-determination theory**.

**How to read this chapter.** The opening sets out what the **humanistic approach** was rebelling against; learn it, because "the Third Force" and its two opponents are a standard interview question. Section 9.1 is Maslow's life, and almost every event in it is tied to a later idea (a mother who withheld love, a boy who compensated for feeling ugly, a behaviourist undone by the birth of his daughter, two teachers he admired so much that he built a theory around them). Section 9.2 is the core: the five needs in order, their six characteristics, the conditions for self-actualisation, and the separate **cognitive needs**. Section 9.3 describes self-actualisers, metamotivation and the reasons most people never get there. Sections 9.4 and 9.5 cover Maslow's image of human nature and how he and later workers assessed self-actualisation (above all the **Personal Orientation Inventory**). Sections 9.6 and 9.7 are the research, which is long but mostly a list of named studies with one finding each. Section 9.8 weighs the theory. If you can draw the hierarchy with the right labels, say which needs are deficit and which growth, give the five hypothetical satisfaction percentages, explain metamotivation against D-motivation, and list the criticisms of his sample, you hold the high-yield centre.

## Opening: The Humanistic Approach, Psychology's Third Force

Schultz places Maslow at the head of a part of the book called **The Humanistic Approach**, which also contains the next chapter on Carl Rogers. The part opener explains what the movement was for, and you cannot understand Maslow without it.

### Two forces, and a revolt against both

By the middle of the twentieth century American psychology was dominated by two schools. **Psychoanalysis**, coming out of Freud's consulting room, explained personality through unconscious conflict, childhood trauma and the struggle of the instincts against civilisation. **Behaviourism**, coming out of the animal laboratory, explained behaviour through conditioning and refused to talk about the mind at all. The humanistic psychologists, whose movement peaked in the **1960s and 1970s** and still influences the field, set out to change both what psychology studied and how it studied it. Their complaint was that both dominant schools gave a **narrow and demeaning picture of what a person is**.

Against psychoanalysis the charge was a sampling problem. Freud and his followers built their theories out of neurotic and psychotic patients. If you only ever study people at their worst, the humanists asked, how will you ever learn anything about courage, kindness, creativity or joy? They proposed to study human **strengths and virtues**, the best of what people do rather than the worst.

Against behaviourism the charge was sterility. By throwing out conscious and unconscious mental life and keeping only what could be observed from the outside, behaviourism reduced the person to a machine that emits conditioned responses to stimuli. People, the humanists insisted, are not oversized laboratory rats or slow computers, and human behaviour is too complex to be captured by those methods alone.

What the humanists offered instead was a theory centred on **human strengths and aspirations, conscious free will, and the fulfilment of human potential**. The person in this picture is **active and creative**, oriented towards **growth and self-actualisation**. Schultz describes the resulting image as flattering and optimistic, and the two theorists who represent it in the book are **Abraham Maslow** and **Carl Rogers**.

> **Classic background.** Maslow himself called the humanistic movement the **"Third Force"** in psychology: psychoanalysis was the first force and behaviourism the second. The 11th edition does not use the phrase in the text (it appears only in the description of a suggested reading on "third-force psychology"), but older Indian keys and Hall & Lindzey-based papers ask it directly. Late in life Maslow also spoke of a **"Fourth Force"**, **transpersonal psychology**, concerned with experiences that go beyond the individual self, such as mystical and peak states. If a question asks which force humanistic psychology is, the answer is the third; transpersonal psychology is the fourth.

### Maslow's starting point: study the fastest runner

Maslow is usually called the **founder and spiritual leader** of the humanistic movement. He rejected both behaviourism and psychoanalysis, Freud's version above all. His objection was that a psychology built only on disturbed people leaves out every positive human quality (happiness, contentment, peace of mind), and so **underestimates human nature**.

His alternative was a deliberate change of sample. If you want to know how fast a human being can run, he reasoned, you do not time the average jogger; you time the fastest runner you can find. In the same way, if you want to know how far a personality can develop, you study the **most creative, healthy and mature** people available, society's outstanding representatives. This is the single most important methodological fact about Maslow, and it explains both the strengths of his theory and the main criticism of it. His theory does not come from **clinical case histories**, as Freud's, Horney's and Adler's largely did. It comes from his study of creative, independent, self-sufficient and fulfilled adults. From that study he concluded that **every person is born with the same set of instinctive needs**, and that these needs make it possible for us to grow, develop and fulfil our potential.

> **Trap.** Maslow's data came from **healthy, outstanding people**, not from patients. A question asking "the theory was derived mainly from clinical case histories" is describing Freud, Adler or Horney, not Maslow. Equally, his method was **not** experimental or correlational; it was his own observation, interviews and the reading of biographies (9.5).

## 9.1 The Life of Maslow (1908 to 1970)

Indian papers like to ask what shaped a theory, and Maslow is one of the clearest cases in the book: the boy who received almost no love grew into the man who put love and esteem at the centre of human motivation, and the boy who felt ugly and inferior became, in his own words, a textbook case of Adler.

### A miserable childhood

Maslow was born in **1908** in **Brooklyn, New York**, the **oldest of seven children**. His parents were immigrants with little schooling and little hope of escaping serious poverty. He remembered his childhood as deeply unhappy; he once told an interviewer that, given the childhood he had, it was surprising he had not become psychotic (Hall, 1968), and in papers published after his death he called his family miserable and his mother a horrible creature (Hoffman, 1996).

He grew up lonely, without close friends and without affectionate parents. His **father** was cold and distant and repeatedly walked out on the family for periods; Maslow characterised him as a man devoted to drink, women and brawling (Wilson, 1972). As a boy and teenager he felt anger and hostility towards his father, though the two were **reconciled** later in life.

The relationship with his **mother** was worse, and it was never repaired. A biographer, Edward Hoffman (1988), reports that Maslow reached adulthood hating her and never achieved even the slightest reconciliation. She openly preferred his younger brothers and sisters, showed him no affection at all, punished him harshly for small offences, and warned him that God would pay him back for misbehaving. One episode stayed with him for life: when the boy brought two stray kittens into the house, his mother killed them in front of him. He never forgave her and, when she died many years later, he would not attend her funeral.

What makes this more than biography is that Maslow himself drew the line from his mother to his work. He wrote that the whole direction of his philosophy of life, and all his research and theorising, grew out of his hatred of and revulsion against everything his mother represented (Hoffman, 1988). Read the theory with that in mind. A mother who gave no love and no security, who was cruel rather than kind, is precisely the kind of parent Maslow later identifies as blocking the love and esteem needs (9.3). His insistence that human nature is basically good, and that cruelty comes from a bad environment rather than from inborn wickedness (9.4), reads as a lifelong argument against her.

> **Trap.** It is Maslow's **mother**, not his father, with whom he was never reconciled. He made his peace with his father as an adult. Exams sometimes reverse the two, or attribute the kitten incident to the father.

### Alone in the world

His difficulties did not stop at home. He was convinced he was **ugly**, largely because of his **large nose**, and he felt inferior about his **thin, scrawny build**. His parents made it worse by mocking his looks and telling him how unattractive and clumsy he was. At one large family gathering his father pointed him out and asked, in front of everyone, whether this was not the ugliest child they had ever seen (Hoffman, 1996).

Looking back on his adolescence, Maslow described a massive **inferiority complex**: a deep, almost physical sense that something about him was wrong, with no compensating feeling of being superior at anything (Milton, 2002). He also described what he did about it: he tried to make up for his perceived physical shortcomings by pushing himself towards **athletic achievement** (Hoffman, 1988).

That is Adler's theory acted out. In Chapter 4 you met **compensation**: a person who feels inferior in some respect strives to overcome that feeling, often by working hardest at precisely the area of weakness. The young Maslow, who would later be fascinated by Adler, was what Schultz calls a living example of **compensation for inferiority feelings**.

### Finding a new style of life

The athletic compensation did not work; he did not win the recognition and acceptance he wanted as a sportsman. So he turned to **books**. The public library became the playground of his childhood and adolescence, and reading and education became his route out of poverty and loneliness.

Schultz reads Maslow's early memories in Adlerian terms too. In Chapter 4, early recollections reveal a person's **style of life**, and Maslow's earliest memories already show the scholarly life he would build. He remembered arriving at the neighbourhood library early in the morning and sitting on the steps until it opened, and getting to school an hour before lessons began so that a sympathetic teacher could let him read, alone in an empty classroom, books she had lent him.

His school grades were only mediocre, but they were enough to get him into the **City College of New York**. The start was shaky: he **failed a course in his first semester** and was on **academic probation** by the end of his first year. With persistence his grades improved. To please his father he began to study **law**, gave it up after **two weeks**, and decided that what he really wanted to study was, in his word, "everything".

### Becoming a behaviourist

Alongside his hunger for learning ran a passion for his **cousin Bertha**. Maslow left home, first for **Cornell** and then for the **University of Wisconsin**, where Bertha joined him. They married when he was **20** and she was **19**. Marriage supplied what his childhood had not, a feeling of **belonging**, and with it a purpose; looking back, Maslow dated the point at which his life acquired meaning to the combination of Bertha and Wisconsin. (Notice how neatly this anticipates the theory: the third level of his hierarchy is the belongingness and love need, and here is a young man whose life only began to make sense once that need was met.)

Psychology did not appeal to him at first. A course he took at Cornell struck him as dreary and lifeless, with nothing to do with real people, and he turned away from it (Hoffman, 1988). At Wisconsin, however, he discovered the **behaviourism of John B. Watson**, who was leading the drive to make psychology a science of observable behaviour. Like many people in the early **1930s**, Maslow was captivated, believing that behaviourism could solve all the world's problems. His training in experimental psychology included research on **dominance and sexual behaviour in primates**. Schultz stresses how far he would travel from there: from monkeys to self-actualisation.

> **Classic background.** At Wisconsin Maslow worked under **Harry Harlow**, later famous for the infant monkeys who clung to a soft cloth "mother" rather than a wire one that gave milk. Maslow was Harlow's **first doctoral student**, and his doctoral research was on **dominance and sexual behaviour in monkeys**. The 11th edition does not name Harlow, but an examiner who asks "under whom did Maslow do his primate research" wants Harlow. Do not confuse this with the postdoctoral work under **Thorndike** at Columbia, which came after the PhD.

### From monkeys to self-actualisation

Several influences moved Maslow away from behaviourism. Two that Schultz names are the outbreak of **World War II** and the **birth of his first child**. Of the baby he said that he was overwhelmed by the mystery of it and felt small and powerless before it; he concluded that anyone who had a baby could not be a behaviourist (Hall, 1968). The point is that a behaviourist expects to predict and control behaviour from the outside; watching a new human being develop from within, on its own schedule, persuaded Maslow that something important was being left out.

After his **Wisconsin PhD (1934)** he went back to New York, first as a **postdoctoral fellow with E. L. Thorndike at Columbia University** and then as a teacher at **Brooklyn College**, his base until **1951**. Along the way he took several intelligence and scholastic aptitude tests and scored an **IQ of 195**, which Thorndike placed in the genius range. Maslow was surprised at first, then came to treat the result as a personal triumph, and he often found a way of mentioning it in social conversation. (For the boy who had been told he was the ugliest child in the room, this is compensation succeeding at last, in the intellectual rather than the athletic field.)

#### Two teachers who became a theory

Teaching in New York in the **late 1930s and early 1940s** put Maslow in contact with the wave of European intellectuals who had come to the city, many of them escaping Nazism, among them **Karen Horney** and **Alfred Adler**. Bertha Maslow later recalled that he talked about Adler constantly and was tremendously excited by his ideas (Hoffman, 1988). He also came to know the **Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer** and the **American anthropologist Ruth Benedict**. His admiration for these two was so great that it later set off his whole line of thinking about **self-actualisation**: as 9.5 explains, his research began as an attempt to understand what made Benedict and Wertheimer so different from ordinary people.

> **Trap.** The two models for self-actualisation were **Ruth Benedict (anthropologist)** and **Max Wertheimer (Gestalt psychologist)**. Horney and Adler were influences on his thinking, but they were not the people he set out to study. Do not swap Wertheimer for Köhler or Koffka, and do not swap Benedict for Margaret Mead.

#### A parade changes his life

In **1941**, shortly after Japan's surprise attack on the American naval base at **Pearl Harbor**, which brought the United States into World War II, Maslow watched a patriotic parade. The experience changed his direction. He resolved to devote himself to a psychology that would deal with the **highest human ideals**: one that would help improve human personality and show that people are capable of something better than **prejudice, hatred and aggression**. Everything in the hierarchy above the safety needs, and the whole study of self-actualisers, can be read as the fulfilment of that resolution.

#### Becoming famous

From **1951 to 1969** Maslow taught at **Brandeis University** in Massachusetts. A foundation grant then took him to **California**, where he worked on a philosophy of politics, economics and ethics grounded in humanistic psychology. By then he was enormously popular, both within psychology and with the general public; he received many honours and was elected **president of the American Psychological Association in 1967**.

Success did not bring ease. At the height of his fame he developed a series of illnesses: **stomach disorders, insomnia, depression and heart disease**. Rather than slow down, he drove himself harder to finish his project of humanising psychology. In a 1968 interview he admitted that his work had swallowed the rest of his life: he had dropped plays, poetry and new friendships, and everything except the work had come to seem smaller and smaller (Frick, 2000).

Maslow died in **1970** of a **massive heart attack** while jogging around his swimming pool, exercise his cardiologist had prescribed.

> **Classic background.** Maslow first set out the hierarchy in a 1943 paper in *Psychological Review*, "A Theory of Human Motivation", and developed it in the book *Motivation and Personality* (first edition 1954). Schultz cites the second edition (1970b) and the third (1987), together with *Toward a Psychology of Being* (2nd ed., 1968) and *The Farther Reaches of Human Nature* (1971), the source of the metaneeds table. "Maslow 1943" is a frequent answer in older keys to "when was the needs hierarchy first proposed".

**Table: Maslow's life events and the ideas they fed**

| Life event | Idea it connects to |
|---|---|
| Unloving, cruel mother; no reconciliation | Love and esteem needs as central; rejecting parents block self-actualisation; evil comes from environment, not nature |

## 9.2 Personality Development: The Hierarchy of Needs

### Five instinctoid needs, in a fixed order

Maslow's central proposal (1968, 1970b) is that human behaviour is set in motion and steered by **five innate needs**, arranged in a **hierarchy**. From the bottom up they are:

1. **Physiological needs** (food, water, sex; the chapter summary adds air and sleep)
2. **Safety needs** (security, order, stability)
3. **Belongingness and love needs**
4. **Esteem needs** (from oneself and from others)
5. **The need for self-actualisation**

Put simply, the **hierarchy of five innate needs** is a ranking of inborn needs by their power, **strongest at the base and weakest at the top**, and that ranking is what starts behaviour off and gives it direction. Learn the order as a sentence you can say without thinking (body, safety, belonging, esteem, actualisation), because the single most common Maslow MCQ simply scrambles it.

Figure 9.1 draws the hierarchy as five stacked bands, widest at the bottom and narrowest at the top. The base band is **physiological needs: food, water and sex**; above it, **safety needs: security, order and stability**; then **belongingness and love needs**; then **esteem needs (from self and others)**; and at the top the **need for self-actualization**. Read the shape as a statement about strength and breadth. The bottom needs are the most powerful and the most widely shared: everybody must eat. The top band is the narrowest because the self-actualisation need is the weakest and, as 9.3 shows, the one fewest people ever satisfy. Note two details on the figure that examiners use: **sex is placed among the physiological needs**, not among the love needs; and the esteem band explicitly includes esteem **from self as well as from others**.

Maslow called these needs **instinctoid**, meaning that they have a **hereditary component**: we do not learn to need food or love or esteem. What we do learn is the **behaviour** we use to satisfy each need, and that behaviour varies from one person to the next. Two people may have the same esteem need; one satisfies it by topping the class, another by being the best cricketer in the colony, a third by caring for a younger sibling in a way the family admires. The need is universal and inborn; the route is personal and acquired.

> **Trap.** **Instinctoid** does not mean the behaviour is instinctive. The **needs** are innate (instinct-like, with a hereditary component); the **ways of satisfying them are learned**. An option saying "Maslow held that the behaviours by which we satisfy our needs are innate" is wrong. This is also how Maslow gets both nature and nurture into the theory (9.4).

### Prepotency: the lower need comes first

The needs are ranked by strength, strongest at the bottom. The rule that makes it a hierarchy is that a **lower need must be at least partly satisfied before a higher need can influence behaviour**. A starving person feels no pull towards esteem; he is occupied entirely with finding food, not with earning other people's approval. Only when people have enough to eat, somewhere safe to live, and the rest of the lower needs reasonably met, do the higher needs begin to move them.

It follows that we are **not driven by all five needs at once**. Usually **one need dominates** the personality at a given time, and which one depends on how far the others have been satisfied. A person with a secure, successful career is no longer pushed by physiological and safety needs, which have been taken care of many times over; such a person is more likely to be driven by esteem or by self-actualisation.

The order is not fixed for life, however. Circumstances can push a person back down. If a recession costs people their jobs, the physiological and safety needs may take priority again; being able to pay the home loan instalment suddenly matters more than the respect of colleagues or an award from a civic body. The hierarchy describes which need is currently pressing, and a person can move down it as well as up.

### Characteristics of needs

Maslow attached six characteristics to the needs. These are the most heavily examined lines in the chapter, so take them one at a time.

**1. The lower the need, the stronger it is.** The further down the hierarchy a need sits, the greater its **strength, potency and priority**. The higher needs are the **weaker** needs. This is easy to remember and easy to get backwards: the top of the hierarchy is the "highest" in value but the **lowest in strength**.

**2. Higher needs appear later in life.** The needs unfold developmentally:

- **Physiological and safety needs** arise in **infancy**.
- **Belongingness and esteem needs** arise in **adolescence**.
- **Self-actualisation** does not arise until **midlife**.

This is why Maslow found almost no self-actualisers among college students (9.5): on his own theory the need had not yet properly emerged in them.

**3. Higher needs can be postponed; lower needs cannot. Hence "deficit needs".** Because the higher needs are less necessary for survival, their satisfaction can be put off. Failing to satisfy a higher need **does not produce a crisis**. Failing to satisfy a lower need **does**. For this reason Maslow called the lower needs **deficit** or **deficiency needs**: when they are not met, the person suffers an actual lack. The label is literal: leave one of these needs unmet and the organism is left short of something it requires.

**4. Higher needs promote growth. Hence "growth" or "being" needs.** Although the higher needs matter less for staying alive, satisfying them contributes to **personal growth**: Maslow linked it to improved **health, happiness, contentment, fulfilment and longevity**. So he called the higher needs **growth** or **being needs**. Survival does not depend on them as it does on the deficit needs; what depends on them is whether a person's potential is ever realised.

**5. Higher needs require better external conditions.** Satisfying a higher need demands more favourable **social, economic and political circumstances** than satisfying a lower one. Pursuing self-actualisation requires freedom of expression and opportunity in a way that pursuing safety does not. A person living under censorship, or in a society where caste or gender closes off most occupations, can still eat and still find safety, but self-actualisation becomes much harder.

**6. A need does not have to be fully satisfied before the next one matters.** Maslow did not mean that you must be 100 percent fed before you think about safety. He proposed a **declining percentage of satisfaction** as you go up. In a hypothetical example he described a person who had satisfied:

| Need | Hypothetical satisfaction |
|---|---|
| Physiological | **85 percent** |
| Safety | **70 percent** |
| Belongingness and love | **50 percent** |
| Esteem | **40 percent** |
| Self-actualisation | **10 percent** |

These numbers are illustrative, not measured, but they are examined exactly as given. A useful way to hold them: 85, 70, 50, 40, 10 (each lower than the last, with the big drop at the top).

> **Trap.** Which needs are deficit and which are growth? In this chapter's wording, **lower needs = deficit (deficiency) needs** and **higher needs = growth (being) needs**. Schultz does not draw the line by number in this section, but the D-motivation passage in 9.3 says deficiency motivation applies to the **physiological, safety, belongingness and love, and esteem** needs, which leaves **self-actualisation** as the growth need proper. That matches the standard teaching: the **first four are D-needs, self-actualisation is the B-need**. If an option lists esteem as a growth need, check the stem carefully; in the classic scheme esteem is still a deficiency need.

> **Trap.** "Higher" does not mean "stronger". The higher the need, the **weaker** it is, the **later** it appears, the **less** it is needed for survival, the more its satisfaction can be **postponed**, and the **better** the external conditions it requires. Every one of these runs in the same direction; if an option makes the self-actualisation need strong, early or urgent, it is wrong.

### Physiological needs

If you have ever struggled for breath under water, or gone a long time without eating, you know how quickly love, esteem and everything else become irrelevant when the body is short of something. A starving person wants nothing but food. Once the need is met, however, it stops driving the person; a **satisfied need no longer motivates behaviour**.

That is the ordinary condition of most people in affluent, industrialised societies. Middle-class people there rarely have to think about basic survival, so the physiological needs **play only a small role** in their motivation. The same needs have a much larger personal impact in places where survival is still an everyday worry. A daily-wage labourer during a monsoon without work is living near the bottom of the hierarchy in a way that a salaried professional is not.

> **Trap.** "A satisfied need is not a motivator" is a direct consequence of the theory, and it is why Maslow says physiological needs matter little for most of his readers. The physiological needs are the **strongest** needs, but in a well-fed person they are **not the operative** ones.

### Safety needs

The safety needs are needs for **security, stability, order, and freedom from fear and anxiety**. Maslow thought they are important drives mainly for **infants and neurotic adults**. Emotionally healthy adults have usually satisfied them.

In infants and children the safety needs are easy to see, because young children react visibly and immediately to any threat to their security, whereas adults have learned to hold back such reactions. Children also show the need in their **preference for routine and structure**, for a world that is orderly and predictable. Here Maslow says something with direct parenting implications. **Too much freedom and permissiveness** removes structure, and that absence of order makes a child **anxious and insecure**, because it threatens the child's security. Children must be given some freedom, but only as much as they can cope with, and it must come with **guidance**, because they cannot yet direct their own behaviour or foresee its consequences.

**Neurotic and insecure adults** also need structure and order, because in them the safety needs still dominate the personality. Maslow describes them as compulsively avoiding new experiences and organising their world so that nothing is unpredictable: every hour budgeted, every possession in its place, pencils lined up in exact rows, shirts all hanging the same way in the cupboard. A clinician will recognise the obsessional style; Maslow's point is that the person is still trying to satisfy a need that most adults have long since met.

Even in **normal adults** the safety needs have some influence. Many of us prefer the predictable to the unknown and order to chaos, which is why people save for the future, buy insurance, and stay in a secure government job instead of risking a new venture. But for a normal adult these needs are not the overwhelming force they are for a child or a neurotic.

### Belongingness and love needs

Once the physiological and safety needs are reasonably well met, the person turns to **belongingness and love**. These can be satisfied through a close relationship with a **friend, lover or spouse**, or through the social relationships of a **group**. Schultz adds that today they can also be developed and maintained through **social media**, which keeps people in contact at any moment from anywhere.

The need to **give and receive love** is met most fully in an intimate relationship. Maslow was careful **not to equate love with sex**: sex is a **physiological** need. He accepted that sex is one way of expressing the love need, but the two are not the same thing. And he made a strong clinical claim: **failure to satisfy the need for love is a fundamental cause of emotional maladjustment**.

> **Trap.** **Sex is a physiological need** in Maslow's hierarchy (it is printed in the bottom band of Figure 9.1). Love is a third-level need. An option placing sex in the belongingness and love needs is wrong, although Maslow agreed that sex can be one expression of love.

### Esteem needs

Once we feel loved and feel that we belong, we may be driven by **two forms of the esteem need**:

- **Esteem from ourselves**: self-respect, felt as **self-worth**.
- **Esteem from others**: respect shown as **status, recognition or social success**.

Satisfying the need for self-esteem lets us feel **confident** of our strength, worth and adequacy, and this confidence makes us more competent and productive in every area of life. When self-esteem is lacking, we feel **inferior, helpless and discouraged**, with little faith in our ability to cope. (Notice the Adlerian language: the person without esteem is the person Adler would describe as stuck in inferiority feelings.)

### The self-actualisation need

At the top is **self-actualisation**, the **maximum realisation and fulfilment of one's potentials, talents and abilities**. In a phrase, it is the self developed as far as it can go. The striking claim is what happens without it. A person might have satisfied every other need in the hierarchy and still, if he or she is not self-actualising, be **restless, frustrated and discontented**. Maslow's well-known line is that the musician must make music, the artist paint and the poet write if they are to be at peace with themselves (1970b).

Self-actualisation can take many forms, and Maslow insisted that it is **not reserved for creative or intellectual stars**. Everyone, whatever their occupation or interests, can make the most of their abilities and reach their fullest development. What matters is fulfilling **one's own** potential, whatever it happens to be, at the highest level possible. He made the point memorably by saying that a first-rate soup is more creative than a second-rate painting, and that cooking, parenting or making a home can be creative while poetry need not be (1987). A tailor who has made himself the finest in his town, or a mother who raises her children with real skill and imagination, may be more self-actualised than a mediocre novelist.

> **Trap.** Self-actualisation is about **fulfilling your own potential**, not about fame, genius or artistic achievement. The soup example is Maslow's own guard against that misreading.

### Conditions for achieving self-actualisation

Maslow named four conditions that must be met before the self-actualisation need can be satisfied:

1. We must be **free of constraints** imposed by **society and by ourselves**.
2. We must **not be distracted by the lower-order needs**.
3. We must be **secure in our self-image and in our relationships**, able to **love and be loved** in return.
4. We must have a **realistic knowledge of our strengths and weaknesses**, our virtues and our vices.

Each condition maps onto something else in the theory. The first is characteristic 5 (good external conditions) plus the Jonah complex (self-imposed constraint). The second is the prepotency rule. The third is the love and esteem needs being met. The fourth anticipates the self-actualiser's "efficient perception of reality" and "acceptance of self" in 9.3.

### Achieving self-actualisation in non-traditional ways

The hierarchy describes most people, but Maslow allowed **exceptions**. Some people dedicate their lives to an ideal and sacrifice everything for it. People have fasted to death for their beliefs, denying their physiological and safety needs. Religious figures have given away all worldly goods to keep a vow of poverty, satisfying the self-actualisation need while frustrating the lower needs. Artists throughout history have endangered their health and security for the sake of their work. (An Indian student can think of political hunger strikes or the renunciation of the sannyasi.)

A more ordinary reversal is putting **esteem before love**: some people believe that they can only satisfy the belongingness and love needs after they first feel **self-confident**, so they chase esteem first.

> **Trap.** The hierarchy is a **general tendency**, not an unbreakable law. Schultz explicitly lists reversals (martyrs, ascetics, artists, esteem before love). An option saying "Maslow held that no one can pursue a higher need while a lower need is unmet" is too strong.

### Cognitive needs

Later in his career Maslow proposed a **second set of innate needs**, the **cognitive needs**: the need **to know** and the need **to understand**. Three facts about them are examined repeatedly:

- They **exist outside** the five-need hierarchy. They are not a sixth step on the ladder in Schultz's account.
- They form a small hierarchy of their own: the **need to know is stronger** than the need to understand, so it must be at least partly satisfied before the need to understand can emerge.
- They **appear in late infancy and early childhood**, expressed as children's **natural curiosity**.

#### Evidence for the cognitive needs

Maslow (1970b) offered four lines of evidence:

1. **Animal studies.** Laboratory animals explore and manipulate their surroundings for no obvious reason other than curiosity, a desire to know and to understand.
2. **History.** People have often pursued knowledge at the risk of their lives, placing the needs to know and to understand **above the safety needs**.
3. **Healthy adults and mystery.** Psychologically healthy adults appear to be drawn to the puzzling and the unexplained, and to want to find out more about it.
4. **Maslow's own clients.** Emotionally healthy adults in his clinical practice complained of boredom and of lives without zest or excitement. He called them "intelligent people leading stupid lives in stupid jobs", and these clients got better once they found more demanding pursuits that fed their needs to know and to understand.

#### How the cognitive needs affect personality

Because the cognitive needs are innate, children do not have to be taught to be curious. But parents and teachers can **inhibit** a child's spontaneous curiosity, for instance by punishing questions or by rote teaching that leaves no room for wondering, and **failure to satisfy the cognitive needs is harmful**: it hampers the full development and functioning of the personality.

The two-need cognitive hierarchy **overlaps** the five-need hierarchy rather than sitting above or below it. Knowing and understanding, which amount to **finding meaning in our environment**, are basic to dealing with that environment in a healthy, mature way, and so they serve the satisfaction of every one of the five needs. Maslow's conclusion is strong: **it is impossible to become self-actualising if we fail to meet the needs to know and to understand**.

> **Trap.** Three cognitive-needs traps in one: (a) there are **two** cognitive needs, **to know** and **to understand**; (b) the need **to know is the stronger**; (c) in the 11th edition they lie **outside** the five-need hierarchy and **overlap** it, appearing in **late infancy and early childhood**. Review Question 10 in the book asks exactly "at what age, and which is strongest".

### Five levels, seven, or eight?

> **Classic background.** Many textbooks, management courses and older Indian keys show an **expanded hierarchy** rather than the five-level figure in Schultz. The common **seven-level version** inserts **cognitive needs** (to know and understand) and **aesthetic needs** (for order, symmetry and beauty) between esteem and self-actualisation. The common **eight-level version** adds **self-transcendence** (helping others reach their potential, or connecting with something beyond the self) above self-actualisation, drawing on Maslow's late writing about transcendence and his interest in transpersonal psychology. Maslow did write about aesthetic needs in *Motivation and Personality*, and late in life about transcendence, but the neat seven- and eight-step ladders are later textbook arrangements. The 11th edition of Schultz keeps the **five-level hierarchy**, treats the **cognitive needs as a separate set outside it**, and does not discuss aesthetic needs or self-transcendence as levels. In an exam, answer from the version the question uses: "five needs" means Schultz's list; "the need placed between esteem and self-actualisation in the expanded model" means cognitive (then aesthetic); "the need above self-actualisation" means transcendence. It is also worth knowing that the pyramid drawing itself became popular through later textbooks and management training; Maslow set out the hierarchy in prose.

| Version | Levels, bottom to top |
|---|---|
| Schultz 11th ed. (Figure 9.1) | Physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, self-actualisation (cognitive needs outside, overlapping) |
| Seven-level textbook version | Physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, cognitive, aesthetic, self-actualisation |
| Eight-level textbook version | As the seven-level version, plus self-transcendence at the top |

## 9.3 The Study of Self-Actualizers

For Maslow, what separates self-actualisers from everyone else is not mainly what they do but **why** they do it: their basic **motivation** is different in kind. He gave this different motivation its own name, **metamotivation**, also called **B-motivation** or **Being** motivation. The prefix *meta-* means "after" or "beyond", so the word signals a kind of motivation that goes **beyond psychology's traditional idea of motivation**.

### Metamotivation versus D-motivation

To see what is "beyond" about it, start with ordinary motivation. Maslow called the motivation of people who are **not** self-actualising **D-motivation**, for **Deficiency**. D-motivation is striving for **something specific** to make up for **something that is lacking** inside us. Take hunger. Not eating produces a deficiency in the body, which we feel as discomfort; the discomfort is a tension that pushes us to act; the action is aimed at a particular goal object (food); getting it removes the lack and reduces the tension. So a specific need (hunger) requires a specific goal object (food) and produces striving for what we lack (searching for food). Schultz notes that Maslow's (1971) account of the two kinds of motivation is **incomplete**, but D-motivation evidently applies not only to the physiological needs but also to the **safety, belongingness and love, and esteem needs**. That is Freud's and Hull's picture of motivation: a drive, a goal, tension reduction.

Metamotivation, by contrast, describes a state in which **motivation in that ordinary sense plays no part**. Self-actualisers are **not striving for a particular goal**. They are said to be **developing from within**. In short, metamotivation is what moves the self-actualiser: an urge to **make the most of personal potential, with no particular goal object in view**.

#### Fulfilling potential

What self-actualisers are concerned with is **fulfilling their potential** and with **knowing and understanding their environment**. In the state of metamotivation they are not trying to reduce tension, make up a deficiency or obtain a specific object. On the contrary, their aim is to **enrich their lives by increasing tension**, seeking out a variety of stimulating and challenging experiences. Because their lower, deficiency needs have been met, they function at a level beyond chasing goal objects to fill a gap. They are in a state of **"being"**, expressing their full humanity spontaneously, naturally and joyfully.

| | **D-motivation (Deficiency)** | **Metamotivation (B-motivation, Being)** |
|---|---|---|
| Whose | People who are not self-actualising | Self-actualisers |
| Needs involved | Physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem | Self-actualisation; metaneeds |
| Aim | Make up for something lacking | Fulfil potential; know and understand the world |
| Object | A specific goal object (food, safety, a partner, a promotion) | No specific goal object; states of being |
| Tension | **Reduced** | **Increased**, by seeking challenge and stimulation |
| Direction | Pushed by a lack | Developing from within |

> **Trap.** **Self-actualisers seek to increase tension; D-motivated people seek to reduce it.** This is the line examiners use to separate Maslow (and Allport, Chapter 7) from Freud's tension-reduction model. An option saying metamotivation aims at tension reduction is wrong.

> **Trap.** Metamotivation does **not** mean self-actualisers have no needs at all. It means they are not driven by **deficits** and do not strive for a **specific goal object**. They are, in Schultz's phrase, "in a sense, unmotivated", and they still move towards **metaneeds**.

### Metaneeds and metapathology

Having said that self-actualisers are in a sense unmotivated, Maslow then listed the **metaneeds** towards which they grow. They are not objects to be obtained but **states of being**: **goodness, uniqueness and perfection** are Schultz's examples. They are directions of growth, the conditions of being that self-actualisers gradually move towards. One does not "get" justice or beauty and then stop; one lives towards them.

Frustrating the metaneeds is harmful. It produces **metapathology**, that is, a blocking of the person's further development that follows when the metaneeds go unmet. Metapathology stops self-actualisers from expressing, using and fulfilling their potential. The person may feel **helpless and depressed** without being able to say why, or to name any goal that would relieve the distress; the chapter summary calls it a **formless illness** with no identifiable specific cause. Think of a gifted, financially comfortable teacher in a school run on rote and corruption, who gradually loses all zest and slides into a vague, cynical depression she cannot explain: on Maslow's account, her metaneeds for truth, justice and meaningfulness are being frustrated.

**Table 9.1** Maslow's metaneeds and the metapathologies that follow their frustration (adapted by Schultz from Maslow's *The Farther Reaches of Human Nature*, 1971)

| Metaneed | Metapathology when it is frustrated (in summary) |
|---|---|
| Truth | Mistrust, cynicism, scepticism |
| Goodness | Hatred, disgust, repulsion; relying only on oneself and living only for oneself |
| Beauty | Vulgarity, restlessness, loss of taste, bleakness |
| Unity, wholeness | Disintegration |
| Dichotomy-transcendence | Black-and-white, either/or thinking; a simplistic view of life |
| Aliveness, process | Deadness; feeling like a robot, wholly determined; loss of emotion and zest; inner emptiness |
| Uniqueness | Loss of a sense of self and individuality; feeling anonymous and interchangeable |
| Perfection | Hopelessness; nothing to work for |
| Necessity | Chaos, unpredictability |
| Completion, finality | A sense of things left unfinished; despair; abandoning effort |
| Justice | Anger and distrust; disregard for law; complete self-interest |
| Order | Feeling unsafe and on guard; the world seems unpredictable |
| Simplicity | Excessive complexity, confusion, bewilderment, loss of orientation |
| Richness, totality, comprehensiveness | Low mood, unease, the world ceasing to interest one |

The table lists **18 metaneeds**. You do not need all the pathologies verbatim, but the pairings that are easiest to test are the ones that are logical opposites: truth with mistrust, justice with anger and lawlessness, order with insecurity, meaningfulness with despair, uniqueness with feeling anonymous, dichotomy-transcendence with either/or thinking, self-sufficiency with giving responsibility to others.

> **Classic background.** Maslow also called the metaneeds the **B-values** (Being-values), and in older texts the same contrast between deficiency and being runs through several paired terms: **D-needs and B-needs**, **D-motivation and B-motivation**, **D-love** (needing another person to fill a lack) and **B-love** (unselfish love for the other's being), and **D-cognition** and **B-cognition** (perceiving the world in terms of one's needs versus perceiving it as it is). The 11th edition uses "metaneeds" and "B-motivation"; if an older key says "B-values", it means the same list.

> **Trap.** **Metapathology is not the same as the neurosis that follows a frustrated lower need.** Frustrating a **deficit** need produces a crisis or a deficiency (hunger, anxiety, maladjustment). Frustrating a **metaneed** produces metapathology, a vague, formless malaise (apathy, cynicism, despair) in a person whose lower needs may be fully met.

### Characteristics of self-actualisers

Maslow's research on emotionally healthy people is the basis of his personality theory (1970b, 1971). He found few examples. He estimated that self-actualisers make up **1 percent or less of the population**, but he concluded that they share a set of characteristics, summarised in Table 9.2. Here they are one at a time, with what each looks like in a person.

**An efficient perception of reality.** Self-actualisers see the world, including other people, **clearly and objectively**, without the distortions of prejudgment or preconception. They are hard to fool and quick to notice what is false, because they are not seeing through the filter of their own needs.

**Acceptance of themselves, others and nature.** They accept their own **strengths and weaknesses** as facts, see themselves without retouching the picture, and do **not torment themselves with guilt** over their shortcomings. They extend the same acceptance to the weaknesses of other people and of society.

**Spontaneity, simplicity and naturalness.** Their behaviour is **open, direct and natural**. They rarely hide their feelings or play a role to please society, although they may do so to avoid hurting someone. Note a fine distinction Schultz makes: they are **individualistic in their ideas and ideals but not necessarily unconventional in their behaviour**. They feel secure enough to be themselves without needing to be loud about it.

**A focus on problems outside themselves.** Every self-actualiser Maslow described was absorbed in some task bigger than personal comfort: a **sense of mission** that claimed most of his or her energy. Maslow treated this commitment to a cause or calling as a **requirement for self-actualisation**, not an optional extra. They find pleasure and excitement in hard work, and it is through that dedication that they satisfy the metaneeds. Their commitment stretches and develops their abilities and helps define their sense of who they are.

**A sense of detachment and a need for privacy.** They can tolerate **isolation** without harm and seem to need **solitude** more than other people. They depend on themselves rather than others for their satisfactions. That independence can make them seem aloof or unfriendly, but it is not intended that way; they are simply more **autonomous** than most and do not crave social support.

**A freshness of appreciation.** They can experience the world with **freshness, wonder and awe**. Something that has gone stale for most people (a sunset, a piece of music, a cricket match, a birthday present) they enjoy each time as if it were the first. Little is taken for granted; what they have, they value.

**Mystical or peak experiences.** Self-actualisers know moments of intense **ecstasy**, not unlike deep religious experiences, which can arise during almost any activity. Maslow named them **peak experiences**. During one, the ordinary sense of a separate **self is transcended**, and the person feels at the height of his or her power, certainty and decisiveness. Defined briefly, a peak experience is a burst of ecstasy, mystical or religious in quality, in which the person goes beyond the ordinary boundaries of the self. A scientist at the moment a problem finally yields, a mother holding her newborn, a climber on a summit at dawn, a musician lost in a raga: each is the kind of moment Maslow meant.

**Social interest.** Maslow borrowed **Alfred Adler's** term **social interest** (Chapter 4) for the sympathy and empathy self-actualisers feel for **all humanity**. They may be irritated by other people's behaviour, but they feel a kinship with others, understand them, and want to help them.

**Profound interpersonal relations.** Their circle of friends is **not large**, but their friendships are **deep and lasting**. Like anyone, they choose friends with qualities similar to their own. They often attract **admirers or disciples**, but these relationships tend to be **one-sided**: the admirer wants more than the self-actualiser can or will give.

**Creativeness.** They are highly **creative**, showing inventiveness and originality in work and elsewhere. They are flexible and spontaneous, unafraid of getting things wrong because errors are something to learn from. Their openness resembles that of a small child who has not yet been taught to fear looking silly.

**Resistance to enculturation.** They are **autonomous, independent and self-sufficient**, free to resist social and cultural pressure to think or behave in a particular way. They do **not openly rebel** against cultural norms or social codes; rather, they are governed by their own nature rather than by society's rules.

**Table 9.2** Characteristics of self-actualising people (as listed in the book's table)

| | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| 1 | Clear perception of reality |
| 2 | Acceptance of self, others and nature |
| 3 | Spontaneity, simplicity and naturalness |
| 4 | Dedication to a cause |
| 5 | Independence and need for privacy |
| 6 | Freshness of appreciation |
| 7 | Peak experiences |
| 8 | Social interest |
| 9 | Deep interpersonal relationships |
| 10 | Tolerance and acceptance of others |
| 11 | Creativeness and originality |
| 12 | Resistance to social pressures |

Schultz closes the list with a caution. On this description self-actualisers sound almost perfect, but they are not. They can occasionally be **rude, even ruthless**, and they have **doubts, conflicts and tension**. Such lapses are simply **rarer and less intense** than in people who are not self-actualising.

> **Trap.** The lists do not match exactly. The **text** gives **11** characteristics. **Table 9.2** gives **12**, adding "tolerance and acceptance of others". The **chapter summary** also gives 12 but includes **"democratic character structure"** (respecting people regardless of class, education, race or belief), a term that appears nowhere else in the chapter. If an option lists "democratic character structure" as a characteristic of self-actualisers, it is correct; it comes from Maslow's original list.

> **Classic background.** Maslow's original description in *Motivation and Personality* ran to about **15** characteristics. Besides those above it included a **democratic character structure**, the ability to **discriminate between means and ends** (and between good and evil), and a **philosophical, unhostile sense of humour** (humour that makes fun of the human condition in general, not of particular people). Older keys sometimes ask these; none is wrong as a characteristic of self-actualisers.

> **Trap.** Peak experiences are a **characteristic of self-actualisers**, but are they **necessary** for self-actualisation (the book's Review Question 13)? The 11th edition lists them among the shared characteristics and does not settle the question. **Classic background:** Maslow later argued that peak experiences are **not confined** to self-actualisers (most people have them occasionally), and that some self-actualisers rarely have them; he distinguished **"peakers"** from **"non-peakers"** and, late in life, described calmer, more lasting **"plateau experiences"**. The safe exam answer: peak experiences are typical of self-actualisers and more frequent in them, but Maslow did not make them a strict requirement. What he **did** call a requirement is **dedication to a cause outside oneself**.

> **Trap.** **Detachment and need for privacy** sits alongside **social interest** and **deep relationships** in the same list. It is not a contradiction: self-actualisers care about humanity and have a few deep friendships, but they do not **depend** on others for their satisfactions. An option claiming they "have a large circle of friends" or "crave social support" is wrong.

### Failure to become self-actualising

If the self-actualisation need is innate and does not have to be learned, why does **less than 1 percent** of the population get there? Maslow's answers form a chain of reasons, from the structure of the hierarchy itself to childhood to personal courage.

**The weakest need.** The higher a need sits, the weaker it is. Self-actualisation is the highest need and therefore the **weakest of all**, so it is easily **inhibited**. Hostile or rejecting parents make it hard for a child to satisfy the love and esteem needs; in that case the self-actualisation need may **never emerge at all**. Lower down, poor economic conditions make it hard to satisfy physiological and safety needs, and self-actualisation then matters less.

#### The importance of childhood in self-actualisation

Poor education and faulty child-rearing can stifle the drive for self-actualisation in adulthood. Maslow gave three examples.

- **Sex-role training.** Boys are typically taught to suppress qualities such as **tenderness and sentimentality**, so that side of their nature never develops fully.
- **Overprotection.** Children who are overprotected and not allowed to try new behaviours, explore new ideas or practise new skills are likely to grow into **inhibited adults**, unable to express themselves fully in the activities self-actualisation requires.
- **Excessive permissiveness.** The opposite error is also harmful. **Too much freedom** in childhood leads to **anxiety and insecurity**, undermining the **safety needs** (exactly the point made in 9.2).

The ideal childhood, for Maslow, is a **balance of permissiveness and regulation**. Beyond that, **sufficient love in childhood** is a prerequisite for self-actualisation, as is the satisfaction of the physiological and safety needs **within the first two years of life**. Children who feel secure and confident in the early years remain so as adults. Schultz links this to two earlier theorists: **Erikson's** emphasis on the development of **basic trust** in infancy (Chapter 6) and **Horney's** emphasis on the child's **need for safety and security** (Chapter 5). Without adequate parental love, security and esteem in childhood, striving for self-actualisation in adulthood is difficult.

> **Trap.** **Both overprotection and over-permissiveness** block self-actualisation. Overprotection produces an inhibited adult; too much freedom produces anxiety and insecurity. The ideal is a **balance**. An option saying Maslow favoured maximum freedom for children is wrong. And note the **two years**: love and the physiological and safety needs should be satisfied within the first two years of life.

#### The Jonah complex

A further reason is what Maslow called the **Jonah complex**, after the biblical prophet. In Maslow's retelling (Hoffman, 1996), Jonah was called by God to prophesy, was afraid of the task, and tried to run away from it; but there was nowhere to hide, and in the end he accepted his destiny.

The **Jonah complex** is our **doubt about our own abilities**: the fear that acting to make the most of our potential will lead us into situations we cannot cope with. Stated as a definition: it is the dread that fulfilling our potential will carry us into circumstances we cannot manage. We are frightened and thrilled by our possibilities at the same time, and **too often the fear wins**. Picture a talented young doctor offered a research fellowship abroad who keeps finding reasons not to apply; the reasons sound practical, but underneath them is the fear of the bigger stage. Classic background: in *The Farther Reaches of Human Nature* Maslow also described it as the **fear of one's own greatness** and the evasion of one's destiny, which is how older texts often summarise it.

> **Trap.** The Jonah complex is a fear of **success and growth**, of fulfilling one's potential. It is **not** a fear of failure in the ordinary sense, not a fear of punishment, and not Adler's inferiority complex (which is a belief that one is inadequate). Jonah ran from his **calling**, not from a threat.

#### It takes courage

Self-actualisation also requires **courage**. Even with the lower needs met, nobody is simply carried along a flowery path to fulfilment. The process takes **effort, discipline and self-control**. For many people it seems easier and safer to accept life as it is than to seek new challenges. Self-actualisers keep testing themselves, giving up secure routines and familiar behaviours and attitudes.

**Table: Why so few people self-actualise**

| Reason | How it blocks self-actualisation |
|---|---|
| Weakest need | Self-actualisation is the highest and therefore weakest need; easily inhibited |
| Unmet lower needs | Hostile or rejecting parents frustrate love and esteem; poverty keeps people at physiological and safety levels |
| Faulty child-rearing | Sex-role training (boys denied tenderness), overprotection, excessive permissiveness |
| Lack of early love and security | Love, physiological and safety needs not met in the first two years |
| Jonah complex | Fear that fulfilling one's potential will bring situations one cannot handle |
| Lack of courage | Self-actualisation needs effort, discipline and self-control; staying safe is easier |

## 9.4 Questions about Human Nature

Maslow's picture of personality is **humanistic and optimistic**. He concentrated on **health rather than illness**, **growth rather than stagnation**, and **virtues and potentials rather than weaknesses and limits**. He had great confidence in our capacity to shape our own lives and our society. Here is his position on each of Schultz's six issues.

**Free will vs determinism: free will.** The choice of how to meet our needs, and whether to actualise our potential, is ours: Maslow grants us **free will**. We can build an actualising self or decline to pursue that supreme achievement. It follows that we are **ultimately responsible** for the level of development we reach, or fail to reach. This is the sharpest contrast with Freud, for whom behaviour is determined by unconscious forces and early experience.

**Nature vs nurture: both, interacting.** The needs in both of Maslow's hierarchies (the five basic needs and the two cognitive needs) are **innate**, but the behaviours by which we satisfy them are **learned**. Personality is therefore the product of the **interaction of heredity and environment**, of personal and situational variables. This is the instinctoid idea from 9.2 applied to the big question.

**Uniqueness vs universality: uniqueness (implicitly).** Maslow did not say so explicitly, but he appears to have favoured the **uniqueness** of personality. Our needs and motives are **universal**; the ways we satisfy them vary from person to person because those ways are learned. Even self-actualisers, who share a list of qualities, do not behave identically.

**Importance of the past vs the present: childhood matters, but we are not its victims.** Maslow recognised that early childhood experience can foster or inhibit adult development (9.3 on love, security and child-rearing). But he did not believe we are **victims** of that experience. We have more potential than we realise to manage our lives and our society, and we would be happier and more productive if we learned to use it.

**Equilibrium vs growth: growth.** **Self-actualisation is the ultimate and necessary goal** of life, which expresses Maslow's belief that, given the right conditions, we can all reach the highest level of human functioning. Self-actualisers do not seek equilibrium or tension reduction; they increase tension through challenge (9.3).

**Optimism vs pessimism: optimistic.** Maslow held that human nature is basically **good, decent and kind**. He did not deny that evil exists. He believed some people were evil beyond any hope of reform, and he wrote bluntly in his journal (1979) that for such people only shooting would work in the end. But he regarded wickedness as **not inherited**: it is the result of an **inappropriate environment**. His compassion for humanity runs through his writing, and his optimism lies in the belief that each of us can fulfil a vast human potential.

| Issue | Maslow's position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | Free will | We choose how to satisfy needs and whether to actualise; we are responsible for the level we reach |
| Nature vs nurture | Both, interacting | Needs are innate (instinctoid); the behaviours that satisfy them are learned |
| Past vs present | Childhood important, but not decisive | Early love and security foster or inhibit growth, but we are not victims of our past |
| Uniqueness vs universality | Uniqueness (implicit) | Needs are universal; learned ways of satisfying them differ, even among self-actualisers |
| Equilibrium vs growth | Growth | Self-actualisation is the ultimate and necessary goal; self-actualisers increase tension |
| Optimism vs pessimism | Optimistic | Human nature is basically good; evil comes from a bad environment, not heredity |

> **Trap.** Maslow's view of evil is a two-step answer. He **did** accept that evil exists, and even that some people are beyond reclamation; but he held that evil is **environmental, not inherited**. An option saying "Maslow denied the existence of evil" is wrong, and so is "Maslow thought evil was inborn".

> **Trap.** On uniqueness, Schultz hedges: "although not explicit in his writings, Maslow **seemed** to favour uniqueness". If a question asks whether Maslow emphasised **universality**, remember that the **needs** are universal but the **personality** (the learned ways of satisfying them) is unique.

## 9.5 Assessment in Maslow's Theory

### How the study began: Benedict and Wertheimer

Maslow's work on self-actualisation did not start as a planned programme of assessment and research. It began as **personal curiosity** about two famous people who impressed him deeply: the anthropologist **Ruth Benedict** and the Gestalt psychologist **Max Wertheimer**. Wanting to understand what made these two so unlike everyone else, he watched them closely over a long period and decided that they had a set of qualities in common, qualities ordinary people lacked.

He then tried to find the same qualities in others. His first subjects were **college students**, but among about **3,000** of them he found **only one** whom he could call self-actualising. He concluded that the qualities he had found in Benedict and Wertheimer **had not yet developed** in young people (consistent with the claim in 9.2 that self-actualisation does not emerge until **midlife**). So he turned to **middle-aged and older people**. Even there, he found **less than 1 percent** of the population who met his criteria.

### Who the self-actualisers were

His final group numbered **several dozen** people, sorted into three grades of confidence: **certain or highly probable**, **partial**, and **potential** cases. A number of them were people alive in Maslow's own time; the rest were **historical figures**, among them:

- **Thomas Jefferson**
- **Albert Einstein**
- **George Washington Carver**, an African-American scientist of the early twentieth century
- **Harriet Tubman**, who escaped slavery and became an anti-slavery campaigner in the years before the American Civil War
- **Eleanor Roosevelt**, wife of the president and a prominent social activist

(A photo caption elsewhere in the chapter singles out Einstein and Tubman as people Maslow studied through biographies and other written records.)

> **Classic background.** Other names often given in older texts and Maslow's own lists include **Abraham Lincoln** (in his later years), **William James**, **Albert Schweitzer** and **Baruch Spinoza**. The 11th edition names only the five above; if a question lists one of the others, it is drawing on Maslow's original list, and the person is still a Maslow "case".

### Methods

Maslow used a mixture of techniques, chosen to suit the person:

- For **historical figures**, he worked from **biographical material**, analysing written records for similarities in personal characteristics.
- For **living subjects**, he relied on **interviews, free association and projective tests**.
- Many living subjects became **self-conscious** when questioned, so he often had to study them **indirectly**. He never explained exactly how he did this.

Note what is missing: no standardised measure, no comparison group, no stated criteria for deciding who counted as self-actualising. These gaps are the core of the criticisms in 9.8, and Maslow himself admitted them (9.6).

> **Trap.** Maslow's **own** assessment methods were **interviews, free association, projective tests and biographical analysis**. The **POI** was developed by **Shostrom**, not by Maslow, and Maslow did not use it to find his self-actualisers. An option saying "Maslow developed the Personal Orientation Inventory" is wrong.

> **Trap.** **1 in 3,000** is the college-student figure. **Less than 1 percent** is the figure for the middle-aged and older population (and Maslow's general estimate for everyone). Keep the two numbers apart.

### The Personal Orientation Inventory

The **Personal Orientation Inventory (POI)** was developed by the psychologist **Everett Shostrom (1964, 1974)** to **measure self-actualisation**. It is a **self-report questionnaire** of **150 pairs of statements**. For each pair, the person chooses the statement that applies to him or her more closely (a **forced-choice** format).

The POI is scored for **2 major scales** and **10 subscales**. The two major scales are the ones to remember:

- **Time competence**: the degree to which a person **lives in the present** (rather than being preoccupied with past regrets or future worries).
- **Inner directedness**: the degree to which a person **depends on himself or herself rather than on others** for judgements and values.

Both scales translate Maslow's portrait into something measurable. Living fully in the present is close to the self-actualiser's freshness of appreciation and spontaneity; inner direction is close to autonomy and resistance to enculturation.

**Table 9.3** Sample items from the Personal Orientation Inventory. The book prints eight sample pairs from Shostrom's 1964 paper. In every pair, one statement expresses dependence on external rules, approval or justification, and the other expresses freedom to act and feel according to one's own nature. Two examples in paraphrase: one pair contrasts doing what other people expect with feeling free not to; another contrasts believing that anger should always be kept under control with believing that honestly felt anger should be expressed. Choosing the second, inner-directed option in such pairs raises the self-actualisation score. The other pairs cover living by society's rules, needing reasons for one's feelings, expressing warm versus hostile feelings to friends, and growing through socially approved goals versus by being oneself.

> **Trap.** POI: **Shostrom** (**1964, 1974**), **150 paired statements**, **2 major scales** (**time competence**, **inner directedness**) and **10 subscales**. Do not confuse "inner directedness" with Rotter's internal locus of control (Chapter 14) or with Riesman's "inner-directed" social character from sociology; the POI scale is about relying on one's own values for judgement.

### The Smartphone Basic Needs Scale

A much newer instrument, the **Smartphone Basic Needs Scale**, is a **20-item self-report inventory** that assesses **how far smartphone use can satisfy the needs in Maslow's hierarchy**. It was developed with **college students in the United States and South Korea**, and its developers reported **high validity and reliability** (**Kang & Jung, 2014**).

The book gives the items used for the **belongingness need**. Respondents were asked to tick whichever statements applied to them about what their smartphone allows them to do; the statements concern things like becoming closer to the important people in one's life, meeting pleasant new people, and building and maintaining relationships. Equivalent sets of items tap the other needs. The scale shows how far the hierarchy has travelled: from a theory of the healthy personality to a framework for asking what a piece of technology does for its users.

## 9.6 Research on Maslow's Theory

### Maslow's own method: "not research at all"

Maslow's study of self-actualisers did not use **case studies**, **experiments** or **correlational methods**. Critics charged that his methods were neither rigorous nor controlled, and Maslow agreed. He knew his work did not meet the standards of scientific research, and he said so plainly, conceding that by ordinary laboratory standards it simply was not research at all (1971).

His defence was practical rather than methodological. Self-actualisation, he believed, could not be studied with the accepted scientific procedures of the day. The alternatives were to wait until suitable techniques were developed or to leave the topic alone, and he was unwilling to do either. He was too impatient, and too convinced that he could help humanity, to postpone the work; careful experiments, he wrote in his journal, would take longer than the years he had left and the amount he wanted to do (1979). So he described his programme as a set of **pilot studies**, was confident that his results were valid, and expected later researchers to confirm them. He even believed that, because he was sure his conclusions were right, he needed **less data** to support them than other theorists did.

That admission matters for the evaluation in 9.8. Everything below is **later** research by other people, testing parts of the theory with conventional methods, mostly questionnaires and correlations.

### The hierarchy of needs

Several studies have tested whether the needs exist, whether they are ordered as Maslow said, and whether satisfying them goes with better mental health.

**Need satisfaction and neuroticism.** In a study of male and female **college students**, **Williams and Page (1989)** found that the more the safety, belongingness and esteem needs were satisfied, the **lower** the students' scores on **neuroticism and depression**. Two further findings fit the theory. **Esteem needs were stronger than belongingness needs** in this group, and the students were **little concerned with safety needs**, which is what you would expect of young people who can afford to attend college.

**Lester (1990)** used the **Need Satisfaction Inventory**, a self-report questionnaire built to measure how well a person satisfies Maslow's needs, and correlated college students' scores with their scores on the **Eysenck Personality Inventory**. Again, **higher need satisfaction went with lower neuroticism**.

**Testing the order.** **Graham and Balloun (1973)** ran an elaborate test of the hierarchy on a **sample representative of the general population**. Their results **supported the order of the five needs**. They also found that **concern** about each need **rose from the lowest to the highest**: the physiological needs, presumably well satisfied in these people, worried them least, and the self-actualisation need was of **greatest interest**, presumably because it is the hardest to satisfy. Read that carefully: it is the satisfied-need principle at work. A need you have already met stops occupying your attention; the unmet one at the top is what you think about.

**Deprivation keeps people low.** Research on **economically disadvantaged young people in the Midwestern United States** found them mainly focused on satisfying **safety needs**, with little concern for the higher ones (**Noltemeyer, Bush, Patton, & Bergen, 2012**). That is what the prepotency rule predicts.

**A worldwide test.** **Tay and Diener (2011)** studied **more than 40,000 people in 123 countries**. The results backed Maslow: people reached **fulfilment and happiness only once their basic physiological and safety needs were met**. This is the largest-scale support for the theory in the chapter and a likely MCQ.

**Lower needs and higher needs; age.** Other studies found that the **more the basic needs are satisfied, the greater the chance that the higher needs will be satisfied too**, and that **people older than 36** are **far more likely** to achieve self-actualisation than younger people (**Taormina & Gao, 2013; Ivtzan, Gardner, Bernard, Sekhon, & Hart, 2013**). The age finding fits Maslow's claim that self-actualisation does not emerge until midlife and his failure to find self-actualisers among students.

> **Classic background.** Not all reviews were this favourable. A widely cited review by **Wahba and Bridwell (1976)** found little consistent evidence that the needs are ranked in exactly the order Maslow proposed, or that satisfying one need activates the next. The 11th edition presents mostly supportive findings; if a question asks for a criticism of the hierarchy, "the strict order of the needs has not been consistently confirmed" is a legitimate answer from the wider literature.

> **Trap.** **Graham and Balloun (1973)** found that people were **most** concerned with self-actualisation and **least** with physiological needs. This does **not** contradict the theory: the lowest needs are the **strongest**, but when they are already satisfied they no longer occupy the person, so the unsatisfied top need attracts the most concern.

### The belongingness need

Maslow's belongingness need can only be met through being with other people and, more importantly, being **accepted** by them. Some researchers rank this need alongside hunger and thirst in sheer motivating power. A set of studies tests what happens when it is frustrated.

> **Key study, Gardner, Pickett, & Brewer (2000).** American **college students** were led to believe they were taking part in an **Internet chat room** with other people. The others then **excluded** them: nobody answered their messages, comments or questions, while the rest appeared to be chatting happily among themselves. The participants had, in effect, been made social outcasts. Afterwards everyone read a **diary** supposedly written by a college student and wrote down as many of the activities in it as they could remember. Students whose belongingness had been **thwarted** recalled **significantly more of the social events** in the diary than students whose belongingness had been satisfied in the chat room.

What it shows: a frustrated need to belong changes **cognition**. It affects memory and which kinds of events a person notices and recalls. Rejected people become selectively tuned to social information, as if scanning for ways to reconnect.

> **Key study, Smith & Williams (2004).** Another group of college students were led to believe they had been **excluded or rejected** in an Internet chat room. Compared with students who had not been told they were ostracised, they reported **lower self-esteem, lower sense of control and lower belongingness**. These negative feelings appeared within **only 8 minutes** of learning about the exclusion.

What it shows: the belongingness need is easily and quickly frustrated, and its frustration spills into the esteem need, exactly as a hierarchy in which esteem builds on belonging would suggest.

Three further findings:

- In the **Netherlands and the United States**, people **high in the need to belong** assumed that **others must be high in it too**, and the need to belong **triggered nostalgia** for a past in which, at least as they remembered it, they had belonged more (**Collisson, 2013; Seehusen et al., 2013**).
- **American teenagers** who felt a sense of belonging were **physically healthier** than those who did not (**Begen & Turner-Cobb, 2012**).
- Taken together, satisfying (or failing to satisfy) the need to belong has real consequences for feeling, thinking and health.

> **Trap.** In Gardner, Pickett and Brewer (2000) the **rejected** students remembered **more** social events, not fewer. Students sometimes assume rejection impairs memory; here it **biased** memory towards social content.

### Self-esteem

The esteem need has generated by far the largest body of research. Everyone has some level of self-esteem, so it is easy to measure and correlate with almost anything. Schultz organises the findings under six headings.

#### High self-esteem

The research supports Maslow's claim that people **high in self-esteem** have more **self-worth and self-confidence**, and feel more **competent and productive**. They also function better in many situations.

- **Job seekers.** Among college students applying for jobs, those with **high self-esteem got more job offers** and were rated more favourably by recruiters (**Ellis & Taylor, 1983**).
- **Job loss.** People high in self-esteem **cope better with losing a job** (**Shamir, 1986**).
- **Self-perception.** They see themselves as significantly higher in **intellectual skills, agreeableness and morality**, and as more **extraverted**, than low-self-esteem people see themselves (**Campbell, Rudich, & Sedikides, 2002**).
- **Sport and anxiety.** Young adults in the **United States and Canada** with high self-esteem were much more likely to **play school sports** and had **lower anxiety and fewer defensive behaviours** (**Bowker, 2006; Pyszczynski, Greenberg, Solomon, Arndt, & Schimel, 2004**).
- **Unstable self-esteem and fame.** Women college students whose self-esteem **fluctuated** between high and low had a much **stronger desire to become famous** than those with stable self-esteem (**Noser & Zeigler-Hill, 2014**). Fame, on this reading, is a way of trying to fix an esteem need that will not stay satisfied.

#### Low self-esteem

Large research programmes in the **United States, Iceland, Canada, China, Norway and New Zealand** linked **low self-esteem** to a long list of problems: **anxiety, depression, smoking, dropping out of school, criminal convictions, financial problems, strong emotional reactions to bad outcomes, and difficulties at work** (Brown, 2010; Cai, Wu, & Brown, 2009; Donnellan, Trzesniewski, Robins, Moffitt, & Caspi, 2005; Jonsdottir, Arnarson, & Smari, 2008; Moksnes, Moljord, Espnes, & Byrne, 2010; Trzesniewski, Donnellan, Moffitt, Robins, Poulton, & Caspi, 2006).

Further findings:

- **Health and death anxiety.** In **Canada and China**, people with low self-esteem had **more physical health problems** and **more anxiety about death** (Routledge, Ostafin, Juhl, Sedikides, Cathey, & Jiangqun, 2010; Stinson et al., 2008).
- **Social adjustment.** College students low in self-esteem had significantly **more social problems** of adjustment and getting along with others (**Crocker & Luhtanen, 2003**).
- **Appearance.** In a study of **Canadian adolescents in grades 7, 9 and 11**, **more than one-third** said that how attractive they thought they were **determined their self-esteem**. Teenagers who were more concerned with their appearance had **lower** self-esteem, and there were **no differences between boys and girls** (**Seidah & Bouffard, 2007**).
- **Rejection.** When people were led to believe that the other members of a laboratory group had **deliberately excluded** them, those **low in self-esteem felt significantly more rejected** than those high in self-esteem (**Nezlek, Kowalski, Leary, Blevins, & Holgate, 1997**).
- **Failing self-esteem goals.** Failing to reach goals on which self-esteem rests, such as high marks or success at work, can increase **anger, shame, sadness and feelings of worthlessness** (**Crocker & Park, 2004**). Self-esteem can therefore have lasting effects; people low in it may think and act in self-defeating ways that reduce their quality of life (**Swann, Chang-Schneider, & McClarty, 2007**).
- **Social media.** College students with **lower self-esteem spent more time on Facebook** and other social networking sites (**Kalpidou, Costin, & Morris, 2011; Mehdizadeh, 2010; Vogel, Rose, Roberts, & Eckles, 2014**).
- **Self-esteem as a reward.** College students valued self-esteem boosts such as **praise or good grades** far more than eating a favourite food, drinking alcohol, spending time with a friend, getting paid, or having sex (**Bushman, Moeller, & Crocker, 2011; Salamon, 2011**). That is a striking result for Maslow's hierarchy: for these well-fed young people, an esteem reward outranked physiological ones.

#### Effects of self-esteem on other behaviours

Self-esteem also reaches into **political views and voting**.

- In **Belgium and the United States**, in a sample of **older adults whose average age was 71**, those with **higher self-esteem** tended to hold **more conservative political views**: with age, people became both more conservative and more pleased with themselves (**Van Hiel & Brebels, 2011**).
- In the **2008 US presidential primaries and election**, voters favoured the **candidates they judged to have more self-esteem** (**Ziegler-Hill & Myers, 2009**).

#### Stability of self-esteem over time

Self-esteem changes across the life span. It **rises during adolescence and adulthood**, **peaks at about age 60**, and then **declines** (Gentile, Twenge, & Campbell, 2010; Orth & Robins, 2014; Orth, Trzesniewski, & Robins, 2010). Research in **Taiwan** found a different pattern: self-esteem rose through childhood, adolescence and early adulthood but **changed very little after age 30** (**Huang, 2010b**). In **Germany**, high self-esteem went with two things: being **married** (usually a post-adolescent event) and reporting high **subjective well-being** (**Wagner, Lang, Neyer, & Wagner, 2014**).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition is internally inconsistent here. The **body text** says self-esteem rises through adolescence and adulthood and **peaks at about 60**. The **chapter summary** says self-esteem is **high in childhood, low in adolescence, rises in adulthood and falls in middle and old age**, a description carried over from earlier work. **Classic background:** the widely cited life-span curve (from large cross-sectional studies in the early 2000s) is high in childhood, a **dip in adolescence**, a gradual **rise through adulthood to a peak around the late 50s or 60s**, and a **decline in old age**. For an MCQ, "peaks at about age 60" is the 11th-edition figure; "Taiwan: little change after 30" is the exception.

#### Ethnic and cultural differences in self-esteem

- **Black and White Americans.** Black teenagers report **higher** self-esteem than White teenagers. Black Americans' self-esteem rises through adulthood but **falls more steeply than Whites' after 60** (**Shaw, Liang, & Krause, 2010**).
- **Ethnic identification.** Among Black women college students, self-esteem was much higher in those who **identified strongly with Black culture** (**Eaton, Livingston, & McAdoo, 2010**). But Black college students who felt **incompetent or inadequate despite evidence to the contrary** (an impostor-like feeling) were **high in stress and low in self-esteem** (**Peteet, Brown, Lige, & Lanaway, 2014**).
- **Across 50-plus countries.** Comparative studies in **more than 50 countries** found that **Japanese college students consistently scored lowest** in self-esteem (**Schmitt & Allik, 2005; Tafarodi, Shaughnessy, Yamaguchi, & Murakoshi, 2011; Yamaguchi et al., 2007**).
- **Asian Americans.** Among US college students, **Asian Americans** generally reported **lower** self-esteem than **European Americans**, but Asian-American students who felt **more socially connected in their dormitories** had higher self-esteem (**Fong & Mashek, 2014**).
- **Delinquency.** **High** self-esteem also appears to **correlate with delinquency**, as found in a sample of **Mexican-American teenage boys** (**Caldwell, Beutler, Ross, & Silver, 2006; Swenson & Prelow, 2005**). High self-esteem is not uniformly good.
- **Acculturation pressure.** Among **first-generation Mexican immigrants** to the US, those who felt the most **pressure to adopt American cultural practices** had **lower** self-esteem (**Kim, Hogge, & Salvisberg, 2014**).
- **Ethnic identity among immigrants.** In the **Netherlands**, immigrants from **Turkey and Morocco** who **identified more strongly with their ethnic background** had **higher** self-esteem (**Verkuyten, 2009**); similar results came from **Puerto Rican** immigrants in the US (**Lopez, 2008**).

The common thread across the ethnic findings is that a strong, valued group identity tends to protect self-esteem, whereas pressure to abandon it, or a sense of not measuring up despite one's record, erodes it.

> **Trap.** "High self-esteem is always associated with good outcomes" is **false** in this chapter: high self-esteem correlated with **delinquency** among Mexican-American teenage boys, and **unstable** self-esteem predicted a desire for fame.

### Self-actualisation

Research with the **POI** has found **higher self-actualisation scores** to be **positively** related to:

- emotional health
- creativity
- well-being after therapy
- academic achievement
- autonomy
- racial tolerance

and **negatively** related to:

- alcoholism
- institutionalisation for mental disturbance
- neuroticism
- depression
- hypochondriasis

All of these run in the directions Maslow's description of self-actualisers predicts. POI research on **women aged 19 to 55** also confirmed Maslow's view that self-actualisation develops **gradually over the life span**.

### Peak experiences

> **Key study, Hoffman, Kaneshiro, & Compton (2012).** Americans aged **40 to 65** described their peak experiences. The **three most frequent** involved **joyful experiences with other people**, a **sense of achievement**, and **personal growth**.

Cross-cultural comparisons found different emphases (**Ho, Chen, & Hoffman, 2012; Ho, Chen, Hoffman, Guan, & Iversen, 2013**):

| Group | Most typical peak experiences |
|---|---|
| Mainland Chinese | Experiences of **serenity** |
| Hong Kong Chinese | **Interpersonal joy** and **external achievement** |
| Brazil and Portugal | Reaching a **developmental landmark**, such as finishing college, getting married or getting a first job |

What these studies show is that peak experiences are reported by ordinary adults, not only by Maslow's handful of self-actualisers, and that culture shapes what kind of moment counts as a peak.

## 9.7 Self-Determination Theory

Schultz presents **self-determination theory (SDT)** as a **contemporary outgrowth of the core of Maslow's self-actualisation idea**. Developed by **Edward Deci and Richard Ryan** (Deci & Ryan, 2009, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2000), it holds that human beings are born with a **tendency** to pursue what interests them, to use and build their abilities and potential, and to master challenges. Put Maslow's language beside it and the family resemblance is obvious: an inborn push towards growth that flourishes when conditions allow.

### Evidence from many groups and cultures

Support has come from strikingly varied groups: **football players in Australia**, **teenagers in India and Nigeria**, and **older women in the United States**. In each, the people who scored **highest in self-determination** showed the **greatest improvement in overall behaviour and subjective well-being** (Deci, 2011; Podlog & Eklund, 2010; Sheldon, Abad, & Omoile, 2009; Stephan, Boiche, & LeScanff, 2010). More recent research in the **United States, Belgium, China, Peru, Australia, Mexico, Venezuela, the Philippines, Malaysia and Japan** has added support for the role of self-determination in fostering positive growth needs and self-actualisation (Chen et al., 2014; Church et al., 2013). The breadth matters: one standard criticism of Maslow is cultural bias, and SDT's cross-cultural record is part of the answer.

### Intrinsic motivation

Self-determination is fostered when a person is guided by **intrinsic motivation**: doing an activity **purely because the activity itself is interesting and challenging**. **Extrinsic motivation** is doing something **only for an external reward**, such as praise, a promotion, a pay rise or a better grade. A student who reads extra psychology because the questions fascinate her is intrinsically motivated; one who reads it only because it will be on the exam is extrinsically motivated.

The overlap with Maslow is direct. Intrinsic motivation, self-determination and Maslow's self-actualisation all concern **developing one's talents and abilities for the sake of inner satisfaction, not external reward**. Metamotivation in 9.3 is, in effect, intrinsic motivation at its fullest.

> **Classic background.** SDT's best-known finding, not covered in the 11th edition, is the **overjustification effect** (also called the undermining effect): giving people an **external reward** for an activity they already enjoy can **reduce** their intrinsic motivation for it, so that once the reward stops they do it less than before. Deci's experiments in the early 1970s, in which paid students later spent less free time on an interesting puzzle task than unpaid ones, are the classic demonstration. It is a frequent MCQ in its own right and fits Maslow's warning that growth comes from within.

### Three basic needs

SDT names **three basic psychological needs**. It is by satisfying them that a person reaches a state of **well-being**:

1. **Competence**: the need to feel able to **master difficult tasks**.
2. **Autonomy**: being free to choose what to do on the basis of **what one personally values, needs and finds interesting**.
3. **Relatedness**: feeling **closely bound to other people**.

Satisfying these needs, in young and old people alike and in cultures as different as the **United States, France and Russia**, has been **positively associated with high self-esteem, self-actualisation and psychological well-being** (Ferrand, Martinent, & Dumaz, 2014; Milyavskaya & Koestner, 2011; Ryan & Deci, 2000; Strizhitskaya & Davedyuk, 2014).

| SDT need | Closest Maslow idea |
|---|---|
| Competence | Esteem needs (feeling capable and adequate) |
| Autonomy | Self-actualisers' independence and resistance to enculturation; POI inner directedness |
| Relatedness | Belongingness and love needs |

> **Trap.** SDT has **three** needs: **competence, autonomy and relatedness**. Do not add "self-esteem" or "achievement", and do not confuse SDT with **McClelland's** three needs (achievement, power, affiliation) from motivation theory. SDT is attributed to **Deci and Ryan**, not to Maslow.

### Highlights: Research on Maslow's ideas

The book's Highlights box, printed in this section, condenses the self-esteem and SDT research into three short profiles. Put in one paragraph each:

**The high-self-esteem profile.** These people feel capable and productive, do better in the job market (more offers, better coping when a job is lost), are less prone to anxiety, depression and dropping out, relate well to others, are emotionally healthy and creative, often hold a strong ethnic identity, feel worthy and confident, and may be lighter users of Facebook and similar sites.

**The low-self-esteem profile.** Here the picture is the mirror image: a tendency towards depression, school dropout and criminal convictions, social difficulties and poorer health, lower well-being and quality of life, and heavier use of social media.

**The high-self-determination profile.** These people have generally met their needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness, score high on self-esteem and self-actualisation, and show the inborn drive to take on challenges and develop their abilities that SDT describes.

## 9.8 Reflections on Maslow's Theory

### Criticisms

The criticisms centre on Maslow's **research methods** and the **lack of experimental evidence** for his theory. Each one is worth understanding rather than just listing.

**1. The sample is too small and too indirect.** The theory rests on a small group of self-actualisers, **fewer than half of whom were interviewed in person**. A few dozen people, many of them historical figures known only through biographies, cannot support generalisations about the whole population.

**2. Data collection was inconsistent and vague.** Maslow **did not describe how he interpreted test results or analysed the biographical material**, and he **did not say precisely what led him to classify particular people as self-actualising**. Without that, nobody can repeat the study. (Schultz notes fairly that weak scientific method is **not unique to Maslow**; the same was said of Freud, Jung, Adler and others.)

**3. Selection by admiration.** Maslow chose as subjects **people he admired**, judged by his **own personal criteria** for self-actualisation. Those criteria were **not specified** at the time, and he later admitted that self-actualisation was **difficult to define precisely**. This creates an obvious circularity: he picked people because they had the qualities he admired, then "discovered" that self-actualisers have those qualities.

**4. The portrait may be Maslow's own ideal.** His list of characteristics comes **solely from his clinical interpretation** of the data and could easily have been shaped by his **personal philosophy and moral values**. The description of the self-actualiser may therefore be a picture of **Maslow's own ideal** of a worthy, emotionally healthy person, rather than a discovery.

**5. Key terms are loosely defined.** Critics found his use of **metaneeds, metapathology, peak experiences and self-actualisation** inconsistent and ambiguous.

**6. Why innate?** Critics asked on what grounds self-actualisation is assumed to be **innate**. Why could it not be **learned**, the product of a particular combination of childhood experiences?

> **Classic background.** Other textbooks add three criticisms that the 11th edition does not list: that the hierarchy reflects **Western, individualistic values** (in more collectivist cultures belonging may rank above esteem, and self-actualisation may be defined through the group); that the theory is **elitist**, since by Maslow's own estimate almost no one reaches the top; and that the **strict order** of the needs has not been consistently confirmed (Wahba & Bridwell, 1976, noted in 9.6). In a "which is NOT a criticism" question, the answer is usually something that is in fact a **strength**, such as "it focuses on psychological health" or "it has been widely applied in education and management".

**Table: Criticisms of Maslow and the reasoning behind them**

| Criticism | Why it holds |
|---|---|
| Small sample, fewer than half interviewed in person | Too few cases, and too indirect, to generalise to the population |
| Inconsistent, vague data collection | No account of how tests or biographies were interpreted; not replicable |
| Subjects chosen by personal admiration | Unstated criteria; circularity between selection and findings |
| Characteristics reflect Maslow's own values | List derived only from his clinical interpretation |
| Ambiguous concepts | Metaneeds, metapathology, peak experiences, self-actualisation used inconsistently |
| Innateness assumed, not shown | Self-actualisation might be learned from childhood experience |

### Maslow's defence

Maslow's reply was that although laboratory research did not widely support his theory, it had succeeded in **social, clinical and personal** terms. He argued that it matched most people's personal experience and gave them a structured way of making sense of their inner lives (1970b). In other words, he defended the theory by its usefulness and its fit with experience, not by experimental proof.

### Contributions and influence

Partly because of Maslow's **optimism and compassion**, his theory, and humanistic psychology in general, became **extremely popular in the 1960s and 1970s**. The movement acquired the trappings of a formal school of thought: **journals and organisations** were founded, and a **division of humanistic psychology** was established within the **American Psychological Association**.

> **Classic background.** The *Journal of Humanistic Psychology* and the Association for Humanistic Psychology were founded in the early 1960s with Maslow among the founders, and the APA's humanistic division is **Division 32** (the Society for Humanistic Psychology, which the chapter's weblink box mentions).

The concerns of humanistic psychology were reborn in the **positive psychology movement** (Chapter 14), whose leaders credit humanistic psychology as its **forerunner**. Maslow's legacy continues to influence **personality, social psychology, developmental psychology and organisational behaviour** (Kenrick, Griskevicius, Neuberg, & Schaller, 2010). Few theories have had such a broad impact outside psychology: **teachers, counsellors, business and government leaders, health-care professionals** and ordinary people coping with everyday problems have all found his ideas useful. In India the hierarchy is probably best known through management and HRD courses, where it is the standard starting point for discussions of employee motivation.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition credits Maslow's humanistic psychology as the **forerunner of positive psychology** (Seligman's movement, Chapter 14). It is a strength, not a criticism. And Maslow's defence was **social, clinical and personal success**, not laboratory confirmation.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **A change of sample is the whole theory.** Freud, Adler and Horney built theories out of patients; Maslow built his out of the healthiest people he could find. That single decision explains why his theory is about growth rather than conflict, why it is optimistic, and why its main weakness is the way he chose and studied those people.

2. **One ladder, two kinds of rung.** The five needs run from strongest to weakest, earliest to latest, most to least urgent. The bottom four are **deficit needs** (D-needs) whose frustration is a crisis; the top one is a **growth need** whose frustration is a quiet discontent. The same split reappears as **D-motivation versus metamotivation**, and as **neurosis versus metapathology**.

3. **A satisfied need does not motivate.** This simple rule explains why physiological needs matter little to the well-fed, why Graham and Balloun's subjects worried most about self-actualisation, and why a recession can push a person back down the ladder.

4. **Tension increase, not tension reduction.** Self-actualisers seek challenge and stimulation. This puts Maslow with **Allport** (Chapter 7) and against **Freud**, for whom the aim of behaviour is to discharge tension.

5. **Childhood matters, but it does not decide.** Love and security in the first two years, a balance of freedom and structure, and freedom from crippling sex-role training make self-actualisation possible. This echoes **Erikson's basic trust** (Chapter 6) and **Horney's childhood need for safety** (Chapter 5). Unlike Freud, Maslow insists we are not victims of that past.

6. **Adler everywhere.** Maslow's own childhood is a case of Adlerian **compensation** for inferiority; his account of early recollections is Adler's **style of life**; and he borrowed Adler's **social interest** directly as a characteristic of self-actualisers.

7. **From theory to research to new theory.** Maslow did almost no research in the ordinary sense, but his concepts launched large literatures on **self-esteem**, **belongingness**, **peak experiences**, the **POI**, and **self-determination theory**, and humanistic psychology became the forerunner of **positive psychology**.

8. **Looking ahead to Rogers.** Chapter 10 continues the humanistic approach. Rogers shares Maslow's optimism and his idea of an inborn actualising tendency, but builds his theory out of **therapy clients** (not admired exemplars) and centres it on the **self** and **unconditional positive regard**. Jung's **individuation** (Chapter 3), sometimes also called self-realisation, is an earlier idea of the same family.

## Worked example

*A 41-year-old engineer is referred by his general physician for "low mood without cause". He has a well-paid, secure job in a public-sector firm, a stable marriage, two children he is close to, and he is respected at work, where he was recently promoted. He describes feeling flat, cynical about his organisation ("nothing here is honest, nothing means anything"), and bored to the point of despair. As a student he wanted to do research on water purification for villages, and was offered a place in a doctoral programme at 26, but turned it down for the security of the job. When his wife suggests he apply now for a funded research post, he says it is too late, that he would make a fool of himself among younger scientists, and that he "should be grateful for what he has". He grew up in a home where his father insisted on order and obedience and treated any display of tenderness in a boy as weakness.*

Reason through it with the chapter's concepts.

**Where is he on the hierarchy?** His **physiological** and **safety** needs are well met (secure income, stable home). His **belongingness and love** needs are met (marriage, close children). His **esteem** needs are met both from others (promotion, respect) and, on the surface, from himself. On Maslow's rule that a satisfied need no longer motivates, none of these four is driving him now. The need that is active and unmet is **self-actualisation**. He is also at the right age for it to have emerged: Maslow says it does not appear until **midlife**, and the research says people over 36 are far more likely to self-actualise.

**Is this a deficit-need problem?** No. A frustrated **deficit** need would produce a crisis (hunger, insecurity, maladjustment from lack of love). What he describes is the characteristic picture of a frustrated growth need: Maslow said a person who has satisfied every other need but is not self-actualising will be **restless, frustrated and discontented**.

**What is the "low mood without cause"?** It fits **metapathology**: a formless malaise, depression and helplessness for which the person cannot name a source. The content of his complaints even matches specific rows of **Table 9.1**: cynicism and mistrust (frustrated **truth**), a sense that nothing means anything (frustrated **meaningfulness**), loss of zest (frustrated **aliveness**). His boredom also echoes Maslow's clients who were intelligent people in unchallenging jobs, and who improved when they took on activities that met the **needs to know and to understand**.

**Why did he not pursue it at 26, and why not now?** Two of Maslow's reasons for failing to self-actualise are visible. First, the **Jonah complex**: his reasons for refusing ("too late", "I'd look a fool", "be grateful") are the voice of a man who fears that stepping into his potential will put him in situations he cannot handle. It is a fear of growth, not of failure in his present role, where he is succeeding. Second, **courage**: self-actualisation requires giving up secure routines, and he chose the safe job over the calling. In the background is **childhood**: a father who demanded order and punished tenderness is Maslow's example of **sex-role training** that keeps part of a boy's nature from developing, and of over-control that produces an inhibited adult.

**Would a self-actualising version of him look different?** He would have a **mission outside himself** (water purification for villages is exactly the kind of cause Maslow means), would be **seeking tension** through challenge rather than avoiding it (**metamotivation**), would rely on his own values rather than on what is expected (**inner directedness** on the POI), and might report **peak experiences** in his work.

**What would you not say?** Not that he is "ungrateful", and not that his mood must be caused by an unmet lower need that you have not yet found. Maslow's framework predicts exactly this picture in a person whose lower needs are fully met. Nor would you assume he must be a self-actualiser just because he is successful and respected: success satisfies **esteem**, which is still a deficit need.

**A formulation in Maslow's terms:** a midlife man whose deficit needs are fully satisfied, showing metapathology from frustrated metaneeds (truth, meaningfulness, aliveness), held back from self-actualisation by a Jonah complex and by childhood training that inhibited parts of his nature. In SDT terms, his **competence** and **relatedness** needs are met but his **autonomy** need (acting on his own interests and values) is not. The direction of help is towards challenge and meaning, not towards further security.

## MCQ traps

1. **Order of the five needs (bottom to top):** physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, self-actualisation. Strongest at the bottom, weakest at the top.
2. **Sex is a physiological need** (bottom band of Fig 9.1), not a love need, although sex can express love.
3. **Instinctoid** = the needs have a hereditary component; the **behaviours** that satisfy them are **learned**.
4. **Emergence ages:** physiological and safety in **infancy**; belongingness and esteem in **adolescence**; self-actualisation not until **midlife**.
5. **Hypothetical satisfaction percentages:** physiological **85**, safety **70**, belongingness **50**, esteem **40**, self-actualisation **10**.
6. **Deficit (deficiency) needs** = lower needs, failure produces a crisis; **growth (being) needs** = higher needs, failure produces no crisis. D-motivation covers physiological, safety, love and **esteem**.
7. **Higher needs need better external conditions** (social, economic, political), and **can be postponed**.
8. **Order can reverse:** recession and job loss restore physiological and safety priority; martyrs, ascetics and artists self-actualise while frustrating lower needs; some put **esteem before love**.
9. **Safety needs** matter most for **infants and neurotic adults**; too much permissiveness makes children anxious.
10. **Failure to satisfy love** is a **fundamental cause of emotional maladjustment** (Maslow).
11. **Two forms of esteem:** from **self** (self-worth) and from **others** (status, recognition).
12. **"A first-rate soup is more creative than a second-rate painting"** (1987): self-actualisation is not limited to artists and intellectuals.
13. **Four conditions for self-actualisation:** freedom from social and self-imposed constraints; not distracted by lower needs; secure self-image and relationships (love and be loved); realistic knowledge of strengths and weaknesses.
14. **Cognitive needs:** **to know** and **to understand**; outside the five-need hierarchy but overlapping it; **to know is stronger**; emerge in **late infancy and early childhood**; impossible to self-actualise without them.
15. **Seven- and eight-level hierarchies** (adding cognitive, aesthetic, self-transcendence) are later textbook versions; Schultz 11th ed. keeps **five**.
16. **Metamotivation = B-motivation (Being)**; *meta-* means "beyond"; self-actualisers **increase** tension. **D-motivation** reduces tension and seeks a specific goal object.
17. **Metaneeds** = states of being (truth, goodness, beauty, justice, order, uniqueness, perfection, meaningfulness...); **18** in Table 9.1, from *The Farther Reaches of Human Nature* (**1971**). Older term: **B-values**.
18. **Metapathology** = formless illness from frustrated metaneeds (helpless, depressed, no identifiable cause); not the same as a frustrated deficit need.
19. **Self-actualisers are 1 percent or less** of the population; **1 in 3,000** college students.
20. **Peak experience** = intense ecstasy in which the **self is transcended**; akin to religious or mystical experience; can occur in almost any activity.
21. **Dedication to a cause outside oneself** is a **requirement** for self-actualisation (not optional).
22. **Social interest** among self-actualisers is borrowed from **Adler**.
23. Self-actualisers: **few but deep** friendships; **need privacy**; **individualistic ideas but not necessarily unconventional behaviour**; resist enculturation **without open rebellion**; can occasionally be **rude, even ruthless**.
24. **Text lists 11** characteristics; **Table 9.2 lists 12** (adds tolerance and acceptance of others); **summary** includes **democratic character structure**.
25. **Jonah complex** = fear that maximising potential will lead to situations one cannot cope with; fear of **growth**, not of failure.
26. **Childhood blocks:** sex-role training (boys taught to suppress tenderness), **overprotection**, and **excessive permissiveness**; ideal = **balance** of permissiveness and regulation; love and lower needs met in the **first two years**. Parallels **Erikson's trust** and **Horney's security**.
27. **Maslow's mother**, not his father, was never forgiven; he refused to attend her funeral. He reconciled with his father.
28. **Maslow's life:** born **1908** Brooklyn, oldest of **7**; married cousin **Bertha** at **20**; PhD **Wisconsin 1934**; postdoc with **Thorndike** at Columbia; IQ **195**; Brooklyn College until **1951**; Brandeis **1951 to 1969**; APA president **1967**; died **1970**.
29. **Models of self-actualisation:** **Ruth Benedict** (anthropologist) and **Max Wertheimer** (Gestalt psychologist). **1941** Pearl Harbor parade turned him to the study of the highest ideals.
30. **Primate research** at Wisconsin under **Harry Harlow** (Classic background; Schultz says only "dominance and sexual behaviour in primates").
31. **Humanistic psychology = Third Force** (psychoanalysis first, behaviourism second); transpersonal = fourth.
32. **Historical self-actualisers named in the 11th ed.:** Jefferson, Einstein, George Washington Carver, Harriet Tubman, Eleanor Roosevelt.
33. **Maslow's methods:** interviews, free association, projective tests, biographical analysis; often indirect. **Not** experimental, correlational or case-study.
34. **POI** = **Shostrom (1964, 1974)**; **150 pairs**; **2 major scales** (**time competence** = living in the present; **inner directedness** = relying on oneself) and **10 subscales**. Not Maslow's own test.
35. **Smartphone Basic Needs Scale** = **20 items**; US and **South Korean** students; **Kang & Jung (2014)**.
36. **Tay & Diener (2011):** more than **40,000** people in **123 countries**; basic needs before fulfilment and happiness.
37. **Graham & Balloun (1973):** supported the order; concern **rises** from lowest to highest need.
38. **Williams & Page (1989):** need satisfaction negatively related to neuroticism and depression; esteem stronger than belongingness in students. **Lester (1990):** Need Satisfaction Inventory vs **Eysenck Personality Inventory**.
39. **Over 36** far more likely to self-actualise (Taormina & Gao, 2013; Ivtzan et al., 2013).
40. **Gardner, Pickett & Brewer (2000):** chat-room exclusion led to **better** recall of social diary events. **Smith & Williams (2004):** negative feelings within **8 minutes**.
41. **Self-esteem peaks at about 60** (11th ed. text); **Taiwan:** little change after **30**; **Japan** lowest of 50-plus countries; **high self-esteem correlated with delinquency** in Mexican-American boys.
42. **Peak experiences, Americans 40 to 65:** joy with others, achievement, personal growth. Mainland Chinese **serenity**; Hong Kong **interpersonal joy and achievement**; Brazil and Portugal **developmental landmarks**.
43. **SDT (Deci & Ryan):** **competence, autonomy, relatedness**; tested in **India** and Nigeria among others. Not McClelland's achievement, power, affiliation.
44. **Maslow's defence:** success in **social, clinical and personal** terms, not lab evidence. He admitted it was "not research at all".

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Humanistic psychology** | Movement of the 1960s and 1970s that studied human strengths, conscious free will and the fulfilment of potential, in reaction against psychoanalysis and behaviourism |
| **Third Force** | Maslow's name for humanistic psychology, after psychoanalysis (first) and behaviourism (second) (Classic background) |
| **Hierarchy of five innate needs** | Maslow's ordering of inborn needs from strongest (physiological) to weakest (self-actualisation), which activates and directs behaviour |
| **Instinctoid needs** | Maslow's term for the innate needs of the hierarchy; they have a hereditary component, though the behaviours that satisfy them are learned |
| **Physiological needs** | Needs for food, water, air, sleep and sex; strongest, but play little role once satisfied |
| **Safety needs** | Needs for security, stability, order, and freedom from fear and anxiety; dominant in infants and neurotic adults |
| **Belongingness and love needs** | Needs to belong to a group and to give and receive love in close relationships |
| **Esteem needs** | Needs for self-esteem (self-worth) and for esteem from others (status, recognition) |
| **Self-actualisation** | The fullest development of the self; realising one's potentials, talents and abilities to the highest possible level |
| **Deficit (deficiency) needs** | The lower needs; failing to satisfy them produces an actual lack or crisis |
| **Growth (being) needs** | The higher needs; less necessary for survival but involve realising and fulfilling human potential |
| **Cognitive needs** | Innate needs to know and to understand, outside the five-need hierarchy; the need to know is the stronger |
| **Metamotivation (B-motivation)** | The motivation of self-actualisers: maximising personal potential rather than striving for a particular goal object; increases tension |
| **D-motivation** | Deficiency motivation: striving for a specific object to make up for something lacking; reduces tension |
| **Metaneeds** | States of growth or being (truth, goodness, beauty, justice and others) towards which self-actualisers evolve; also called B-values |
| **Metapathology** | Thwarting of self-development from failure to satisfy the metaneeds; a formless helplessness or depression |
| **Peak experience** | A moment of intense ecstasy, like a religious or mystical experience, in which the self is transcended |
| **Social interest** | Adler's term, adopted by Maslow, for self-actualisers' sympathy and empathy for all humanity |
| **Resistance to enculturation** | Self-actualisers' independence from social and cultural pressure, without open rebellion |
| **Democratic character structure** | Respect for others regardless of background; listed among self-actualisers' traits in the chapter summary |
| **Jonah complex** | The fear that maximising one's potential will lead to situations one cannot cope with |
| **Personal Orientation Inventory (POI)** | Shostrom's self-report measure of self-actualisation: 150 forced-choice pairs, 2 major scales and 10 subscales |
| **Time competence** | POI major scale: the degree to which a person lives in the present |
| **Inner directedness** | POI major scale: the degree to which a person relies on himself or herself, not others, for judgements and values |
| **Smartphone Basic Needs Scale** | Kang and Jung's (2014) 20-item self-report scale of how far smartphone use satisfies Maslow's needs |
| **Need Satisfaction Inventory** | Self-report questionnaire measuring how well a person satisfies Maslow's needs (used by Lester, 1990) |
| **Self-determination theory (SDT)** | Deci and Ryan's theory of an innate tendency to express interests, develop capacities and overcome challenges, through satisfying three basic needs |
| **Intrinsic motivation** | Doing an activity for the interest and challenge of the activity itself |
| **Extrinsic motivation** | Doing an activity only for an external reward such as praise, pay or grades |
| **Competence** | SDT need to feel able to master difficult tasks |
| **Autonomy** | SDT need to base one's actions on one's own interests, needs and values |
| **Relatedness** | SDT need to feel closely connected with other people |
| **Overjustification effect** | External rewards for an enjoyable activity reduce intrinsic motivation for it (Classic background) |

## Likely exam points

1. **Humanistic approach:** what it objected to in psychoanalysis (studying only the disturbed) and behaviourism (people as machines); "Third Force"; Maslow as founder and spiritual leader.
2. **Maslow's rationale:** study the fastest runner, that is the healthiest and most creative people, not patients.
3. **Life and theory:** unloving mother never forgiven (love need, environmental view of evil); felt ugly, compensated through athletics then books (Adler's compensation and style of life); Bertha and belonging; behaviourist primate work (Harlow, Classic background); first child and World War II end his behaviourism; Benedict and Wertheimer as models; 1941 parade.
4. **Key dates:** 1908 to 1970; PhD Wisconsin 1934; Thorndike postdoc; IQ 195; Brooklyn College to 1951; Brandeis 1951 to 1969; APA president 1967.
5. **The five needs in order** with their content, and Figure 9.1.
6. **Instinctoid needs:** innate needs, learned behaviours.
7. **Prepotency:** lower needs at least partly satisfied first; one need dominates at a time; a satisfied need does not motivate; the order can reverse.
8. **Six characteristics of needs:** lower is stronger; higher appear later (infancy, adolescence, midlife); higher can be postponed (deficit vs growth); higher promote growth, health and longevity; higher need better external conditions; declining percentages 85, 70, 50, 40, 10.
9. **Deficit vs growth needs** and which needs fall in each.
10. **Safety needs** in infants, children and neurotics; parenting implications of too much freedom.
11. **Love is not sex;** failure of love as a cause of maladjustment.
12. **Two forms of esteem.**
13. **Self-actualisation:** definition; restlessness without it; not limited to artists (soup example).
14. **Four conditions** for self-actualisation and the **non-traditional routes** (exceptions to the order).
15. **Cognitive needs:** to know and to understand; evidence (animal curiosity, risk for knowledge, healthy adults and mystery, bored clients); emergence in late infancy; outside but overlapping the hierarchy.
16. **Expanded seven- and eight-level hierarchies** (cognitive, aesthetic, transcendence) vs Schultz's five.
17. **Metamotivation vs D-motivation:** tension increase vs reduction; no goal object vs specific goal object.
18. **Metaneeds and metapathology** with Table 9.1 pairings.
19. **Characteristics of self-actualisers** (Table 9.2) and their human flaws; 1 percent or less.
20. **Peak experiences:** definition and whether they are necessary.
21. **Why so few self-actualise:** weakest need, unmet lower needs, faulty child-rearing (sex roles, overprotection, permissiveness), early love and security, Jonah complex, courage.
22. **Jonah complex** defined with the biblical source.
23. **Human nature:** free will, interaction of nature and nurture, uniqueness, not victims of childhood, growth, optimism; evil environmental but real.
24. **Assessment:** Benedict and Wertheimer; 1 in 3,000 students; several dozen cases (certain, partial, potential); named historical figures; interviews, free association, projective tests, biographies, indirect study.
25. **POI:** Shostrom, 150 pairs, time competence and inner directedness; POI correlates (positive with health, creativity, autonomy, racial tolerance; negative with alcoholism, neuroticism, depression, hypochondriasis); women 19 to 55.
26. **Smartphone Basic Needs Scale:** Kang & Jung (2014), 20 items, US and South Korea.
27. **Research on the hierarchy:** Williams & Page (1989); Lester (1990); Graham & Balloun (1973); Noltemeyer et al. (2012); Tay & Diener (2011, 40,000 people, 123 countries); over-36 finding.
28. **Belongingness research:** Gardner, Pickett & Brewer (2000); Smith & Williams (2004, 8 minutes); nostalgia; teenagers' health.
29. **Self-esteem research:** correlates of high and low self-esteem; politics and voting; life-span course (peak about 60; Taiwan after 30); ethnic and cultural differences (Japan lowest, ethnic identity protective, delinquency exception).
30. **Peak-experience research** across cultures.
31. **Self-determination theory:** Deci and Ryan; competence, autonomy, relatedness; intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation; cross-cultural support including India.
32. **Criticisms:** small and indirect sample, vague methods, selection by admiration with unstated criteria, characteristics reflecting his own values, ambiguous terms, innateness assumed; plus (Classic background) cultural bias and unconfirmed order.
33. **Maslow's defence and legacy:** social, clinical and personal success; 1960s and 1970s popularity; APA division; forerunner of positive psychology; influence on education, counselling, business, health care and government.
