# Chapter 5: Karen Horney: Neurotic Needs and Trends

**What this chapter is really about.** Freud built personality out of biology: instincts, libido, a sexual timetable of stages that every child passes through. Karen Horney kept Freud's conviction that childhood shapes the adult, and then removed almost everything biological from the explanation. In her account a child is not driven by sex or aggression but by a need to feel **safe**, and whether that need is met depends on one thing, how the parents treat the child. When parents fail to give real warmth, the child becomes resentful, cannot afford to show it, buries the resentment, and is left with **basic anxiety**, the sense of being alone and helpless in a hostile world. Everything else in the chapter is what people do about that feeling: the four childhood ways of protecting oneself, the ten **neurotic needs** those protections harden into, the three **neurotic trends** (moving toward, against and away from people) into which Horney later grouped the needs, and the **idealized self-image** the neurotic builds to paper over the conflict between those trends. Running alongside is her second great project, a **feminine psychology** that answered Freud's penis envy with **womb envy** and argued that women's feelings of inferiority are manufactured by culture, not anatomy.

Read the life closely. More than for most theorists, Horney's theory is an autobiography in technical language. A girl who felt less loved than her brother, who tried being the obedient daughter until about 8, and who then switched to ambition and rebellion, later described a child who first moves toward people and then against them. Indian papers ask exactly this kind of question: which life event fed which concept.

**How to read this chapter.** 5.1 is the life, and each event is tied to the idea it fed. 5.2 and 5.3 build the causal chain from the safety need to basic anxiety and the four self-protective mechanisms; you cannot follow anything later unless this chain is clear. 5.4 is the core: ten needs, three trends, the three personality types, and Horney's definition of conflict. 5.5 adds the idealized self-image, the tyranny of the shoulds and externalization. 5.6 is feminine psychology. 5.7 rates Horney on Schultz's six questions about human nature. 5.8 to 5.10 cover assessment, research and evaluation, which is where "which of the following is NOT..." questions come from. The single most common error on this chapter is mixing up the **10 needs** with the **3 trends**; Table 5.1 in 5.4 is the place to fix it permanently.

## Opening: The Neo-psychoanalyst Who Started with Women

Horney is the third defector from orthodox Freudian theory in Schultz's Neo-psychoanalytic section, after Jung and Adler. The part opener names what these theorists share despite their many disagreements: they all rejected Freud's view of instincts as the main movers of behaviour, they rejected his determinism, and they offered a more hopeful and more flattering picture of human nature. Horney's route out, though, was different from Jung's and Adler's. Jung and Adler had worked alongside Freud personally and broke with him face to face. Horney was never Freud's colleague. She was schooled in the official psychoanalytic doctrine of her day and left the fold through her writing, and the first issue on which she parted company was Freud's account of women.

She was, in the book's word, an early **feminist**, and her complaint was that psychoanalysis had been built around male development and then applied to women as an afterthought. Freud said women are driven by envy of the penis. Horney turned the argument round: men, she said, envy women's ability to give birth. Her well-known retort, "I know just as many men with womb envy as women with penis envy" (quoted in Cherry & Cherry, 1973, p. 75), did not endear her to Freud or to most male analysts of her day.

Two forces outside her own psychology pushed her theory away from Freud's. The first was geography. Horney did most of her theorising in the **United States**, a culture very different from Freud's Vienna, and she noticed that her American patients differed from her earlier German patients both in the kinds of neurosis they developed and in their ordinary, healthy personalities. If personality were mainly the unfolding of biological instincts, she reasoned, patients from two Western nations should not differ so much. Only the different social pressures they had grown up under could explain it. Her conclusion was that personality does not rest wholly on biology, as Freud claimed, and that cultures shape people. The second force was history. By the 1930s and 1940s attitudes towards sex and towards the roles of men and women had shifted a great deal, in Europe and still more in America, so the Victorian sexual climate that had coloured Freud's clinical material no longer described the people in Horney's consulting room.

Out of this came her central disagreements with Freud. Like Adler, she put **social relationships** at the centre of personality formation. She denied that sex is the governing force in personality, and she questioned three pillars of Freud's system: the **Oedipus complex**, the **libido**, and the **three-part structure** of id, ego and superego. In place of sexual and aggressive drives she put two needs, the need for **security** and the need for **love**. As the life story below shows, those were precisely the two things she felt she had been denied.

> **Trap.** Horney is a neo-psychoanalyst, not an anti-psychoanalyst. She kept the importance of early childhood, unconscious motivation, repression, and the clinical methods of free association and dream analysis. What she threw out was the **biological and sexual** foundation. A question that says "Horney rejected the importance of childhood" is wrong; one that says "Horney rejected the sexual basis of the Oedipus complex" is right.

> **Trap.** Older Indian keys often follow Hall and Lindzey, who grouped Horney with Adler, Erich Fromm and Harry Stack Sullivan as the "social psychological" theorists (sometimes called the socio-cultural neo-Freudians). Schultz places her in the Neo-psychoanalytic Approach with Jung and Adler. Both groupings point to the same idea: social forces, not instincts. Classic background: Hall and Lindzey's chapter on Adler, Fromm, Horney and Sullivan carried this "social psychological" label.

## 5.1 The Life of Horney (1885 to 1952)

### A Neglected Second-Born

Karen Danielsen was born in **1885** and grew up in a small village near **Hamburg, Germany**. She was the **second-born** child, and from early on she envied her older brother, **Berndt**. In the family's eyes he was the charming, good-looking, adored first-born; she believed she was the cleverer and livelier of the two, and her adolescent diaries record her pride at outshining him at school and at being the subject of more family stories. She also envied him for a reason that would matter later in her theory: he was a **boy**, and girls in that time and place were treated as lesser. Her diary admits that for a long time as a child she wished she were a boy, and she remembered envying the simple physical fact that he could urinate standing up (Horney, 1980, p. 252).

Notice what this memory is and is not. A Freudian would read it as penis envy, a biologically rooted wish for the male organ. Horney's own later reading of experiences like this was social: what a girl envies is the status and freedom the culture hands to boys. The same childhood fact sits at the root of both her own envy and her later argument against Freud.

Her **father** mattered even more. He was a sea captain, Norwegian by descent, already **50** when Karen was born, and her **mother** was a full **17 years** his junior. The two could hardly have been less alike: he was devout, overbearing and sullen; she was good-looking, lively and independent-minded. Because his voyages kept him away for months, the household had long calm stretches broken by stormy homecomings, when the parents' opposite natures produced constant rows. The mother made no effort to conceal that she would have liked to be a widow, and she let Karen know that the marriage had been a hedge against spinsterhood, not a love match.

Put yourself in the child's position. A daughter who hears that her parents' marriage was a loveless transaction, who watches them fight, and who believes the boy of the family is preferred, is a child for whom the home is not a reliable source of safety. That is exactly the situation Horney would later describe as the breeding ground of **basic anxiety**.

### A Search for Love

Through most of her youth Karen doubted that her parents wanted her and believed they loved Berndt more. At **16** she wrote in her diary a lament that she had been given everything beautiful in life except the one thing that mattered most, **love** (Horney, 1980, p. 30). She longed for her father's affection and attention, but he frightened her with his fierce gaze and his stern, exacting manner, and he often belittled her intelligence and her looks. She felt rejected by him.

This is the first link between life and theory. The most important childhood need, in Horney's system, is the **safety need**, and the thing that frustrates it is not some dramatic trauma but a steady **lack of genuine warmth and affection** from the parents. The chapter's epigraph states her view in one line: the basic evil is invariably a lack of real warmth and affection. She was describing her own home.

### Rebellion and Hostility

Karen's first strategy for keeping her mother's affection was to become the adoring, obedient daughter. **Until about the age of 8** she was a model child, clinging and compliant. It did not work: however good she was, she did not feel she was receiving enough love or security. So she changed tactics. She deliberately became **ambitious and rebellious**, deciding that if love and security were not on offer she would take her revenge for feeling unattractive and inadequate. Her own summary of the switch was that if she could not be beautiful she would be smart (Horney quoted in Rubins, 1978, p. 14).

As an adult she recognised how much **hostility** she had built up as a child, and her theory describes precisely this sequence: a lack of love in childhood produces anxiety and hostility. Her biographer Bernard Paris concluded that in all her psychoanalytic writing Horney was trying to make sense of herself and to find relief from her own difficulties (Paris, 1994, p. xxii).

The link to the theory is direct enough to memorise as a pair. The clinging, compliant little girl is the self-protective strategy of **being submissive**, and later the **compliant personality** that moves **toward** people. The ambitious girl who decides to win through intelligence is **attaining power**, and later the **aggressive personality** that moves **against** people.

### Still Searching for Love (adolescence)

Her diary at **14** is full of a schoolgirl passion for one of her teachers, and more such crushes on male teachers followed, with the muddle and misery typical of adolescence (Seiffge-Krenke & Kirsch, 2002). By **17** she had discovered sexuality, and what she later called her first real love affair was over within **two days**. The next man prompted a burst of agonised self-scrutiny that filled **76 pages** of the diary. From these episodes she concluded that being in love took away her anxiety and insecurity, at least for a time; love offered an escape (Sayers, 1991).

That conclusion is the seed of the **neurotic need for affection and approval**: love sought not for itself but as a painkiller for anxiety, which is exactly what makes a need neurotic in her later system.

### Marriage and Career

If her search for love kept failing, her search for a career went straight to its goal. At **12**, after a physician treated her kindly, she decided to become a **doctor**. She worked hard at school to qualify for medical study despite two obstacles: the medical profession's discrimination against women and her father's opposition. In **1906** she entered the medical school of the **University of Freiburg**, only **6 years** after the first woman had been admitted there, and grudgingly at that.

At medical school she met two men; she fell in love with one and **married the other**, **Oskar Horney**, then a doctoral student in political science who later became a successful businessman. She did very well in her studies and took her medical degree from the **University of Berlin in 1913**.

The early years of the marriage were a time of great distress. She had **three daughters** but felt crushed by unhappiness and a sense of oppression, and she complained of crying spells, stomach pains, chronic fatigue, compulsive behaviour, frigidity, and a longing for sleep and even for death. The marriage ended after **17 years**.

During and after the marriage she had a series of love affairs. Paris (1994, p. 140) describes the pattern: without a lover, or when an affair was collapsing, she felt lost, lonely, desperate and at times suicidal; inside a morbidly dependent relationship she despised herself for being unable to break free; and she traced her desperate need for a man to her unhappy childhood. When she saw that these attachments were not lifting her depression and other problems, she entered psychoanalysis.

Read that pattern again with the theory in mind and you have, almost word for word, the **compliant personality** of 5.4: someone whose security depends entirely on another person, who is terrified of rejection, and who needs one dominant partner to hold her life together.

### Horney's Psychoanalysis

Her analyst was **Karl Abraham**, one of Freud's most loyal followers. Abraham explained her troubles as an attraction to forceful men, which he read as a leftover of her childhood **Oedipal longing for her powerful father**. As evidence that she was ready to surrender herself to such patriarchal figures, he pointed to the fact that on her very first visit she had left her **handbag** in his office, a handbag being, in Freud's symbolism, a representation of the female genitals (Sayers, 1991, p. 88). The analysis failed. Horney decided that Freudian analysis had been of only minimal help, and she turned instead to **self-analysis**, which she practised for the rest of her life.

Two consequences follow for the theory. First, her rejection of the sexual reading of the Oedipus complex (5.6) began on her own couch: an interpretation that reduced her unhappiness to childhood sexual longing simply did not help her. Second, her confidence that people can understand and change themselves, which shows in her book *Self-Analysis* (1942) and in her **free-will** position in 5.7, grew out of her own experience of doing exactly that.

During her self-analysis she was strongly influenced by **Adler's** idea of **compensation for inferiority feelings**, and in particular by his remark that physical unattractiveness can be a source of inferiority. She concluded, in Paris's account, that her sense of being plain and her sense of inferiority as a woman had driven her to feel superior through **masculine protest**, that is, by excelling in a field dominated by men, as medicine then was (Paris, 1994, p. 63). She apparently believed that by studying medicine, and by being sexually promiscuous, she was behaving more like a man.

> **Trap.** **Karl Abraham** was Horney's Freudian analyst in Germany. **Abraham Maslow** is the American humanistic psychologist who later borrowed her ideas of the real self and self-realization (5.10). Same word, two different people, opposite roles in her story.

> **Trap.** **Masculine protest** is Adler's term, which Horney applied to herself. In Chapter 4 Schultz explains that the early Adler equated inferiority with weakness or femininity (reflecting women's low standing at the time) and called the compensation for it the masculine protest, an idea he later dropped in favour of striving for superiority. If an MCQ asks whose term it is, the answer is Adler, even though the example in this chapter is Horney.

### Still Searching for Love (the American years)

Her search for love went with her when she emigrated to the United States. Her most intense affair there was with another analyst, **Erich Fromm**, who was **15 years younger** than she was. When it ended after **20 years** she was deeply hurt. One event that helped break it up was that she persuaded Fromm to analyse her daughter **Marianne** (spelled Marriane in the 11th edition). Fromm helped the young woman understand her hostility towards her mother, and this gave Marianne the confidence to confront Horney for the first time (McLaughlin, 1998). In a footnote the book adds that in **2006**, at a commemoration of the **120th anniversary** of Horney's birth, Marianne described her mother as a private person who was never a good team player and never a family person (Eckardt, 2006, p. 3).

Horney's later relationships were with younger and younger men, many of them analysts whose training she was supervising, and she could be detached about them. Paris (1994) records her joking to a friend that she could not decide between marrying one young man and getting a cocker spaniel; she chose the dog. Here, in her own behaviour, is the third of her trends, **moving away** from people, sitting alongside the compliant longing for love. Her life displayed all three trends, which is perhaps why she could describe the conflict between them so precisely.

Her professional life in America ran from **1932 to 1952**, on the teaching staff of psychoanalytic institutes first in **Chicago** and then in **New York**. She helped to found two bodies, the **American Institute for Psychoanalysis** and the **Association for the Advancement of Psychoanalysis**, and in **1941** she launched the **American Journal of Psychoanalysis**. Over many years she was in demand as a lecturer, an author and a therapist. She died in 1952.

Classic background: Horney's main books, which exam questions sometimes name, are *The Neurotic Personality of Our Time* (1937), *New Ways in Psychoanalysis* (1939), *Self-Analysis* (1942), *Our Inner Conflicts* (1945, the book that introduced the three trends), *Neurosis and Human Growth* (1950, her fullest account of the idealized self and the "shoulds"), and the posthumous collection *Feminine Psychology* (1967). The 11th edition also cites her *Adolescent Diaries* (1980), written between the ages of 13 and 25, and *Final Lectures* (1987), given in the last year of her life.

## 5.2 The Childhood Need for Safety and Security

### Where Horney agreed and disagreed with Freud

On one big point Horney stood with Freud: the **early years of childhood** are decisive for the adult personality. Where she parted from him was on **how** those years do their work. For Freud, the child is pushed through a fixed biological sequence (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital), and conflicts such as the Oedipus complex are built into the human species. For Horney, there are **no universal developmental stages** and **no inevitable childhood conflicts**. What shapes the child is a **social** fact, the relationship between the child and the parents. Change the relationship and you change the outcome.

This is worth stating as a contrast you can write in two lines. Freud: biology sets the agenda, and society only modifies how it is expressed. Horney: the social relationship sets the agenda, and a child who is treated well need not pass through any neurotic conflict at all.

### The safety need

Horney held that childhood is ruled by the **safety need**, which she defined as the need for **security and freedom from fear** (Horney, 1937). The book's margin glossary calls it a higher-level need for security and freedom from fear. Whether an infant comes to feel secure and unafraid is, in her view, the deciding factor in whether personality develops normally.

The crucial point is what security depends on. It does not depend on the child's constitution, on whether feeding went smoothly, or on the resolution of a psychosexual stage. It depends **entirely on how the parents treat the child**. And the main way parents weaken or block a child's security is by **failing to show warmth and affection**.

Horney's own childhood fitted this description: her parents gave her very little warmth, and the book notes that she in turn behaved in the same cool way with her own three daughters, a sad illustration of how the pattern can pass from one generation to the next.

One consequence of her view surprises students. Experiences that most clinicians would list as traumatic, she thought, can be survived without lasting harm, whether it is being weaned **suddenly**, being **beaten now and then**, or even being exposed to sex **too early**, as long as the child feels **wanted and loved** and therefore secure. The damage, on her account, lies less in any particular event than in the emotional climate around it. A child who is smacked now and then by parents who clearly cherish him is safer, in her sense, than a child who is never touched by parents who are cold and indifferent.

### Ways of Undermining a Child's Security

Parents can undermine security, and so provoke **hostility** in the child, in a number of ways. The book lists them, and the list is examinable:

1. **Obvious preference** for one sibling over another (Horney's own complaint about Berndt).
2. **Unfair punishment.**
3. **Erratic behaviour**, so that the child cannot predict how the parent will react.
4. **Promises not kept.**
5. **Ridicule.**
6. **Humiliation.**
7. **Isolating the child from peers.**

Horney added an important observation about the quality of parental love: children can tell whether it is **genuine**. Showy demonstrations and hollow declarations of affection do not easily deceive them. A mother who says all the right things while plainly resenting the child does not buy that child's security with words.

A concrete example helps. Imagine a seven-year-old whose father showers praise on her elder brother's marks while dismissing hers, who is punished severely one day for something ignored the next, and who is promised a trip that never happens. Each of these is on Horney's list. None is a dramatic trauma, but together they teach the child that the people she depends on are unpredictable and do not really value her. The natural response is **resentment**, which Horney calls hostility.

### Repressing Hostility toward Parents

Here is the step on which the whole theory turns. The child is angry with the parents, but the child usually cannot afford to express that anger, and so she **represses** it. Horney identified **four reasons** for the repression, and each has its own inner logic, which she put into the child's mouth.

**1. Helplessness.** Horney laid great weight on the **helplessness of the infant**, whose survival depends wholly on the parents. If parents keep a child in an excessively **dependent** state, the child's sense of helplessness is encouraged, and the more helpless a child feels, the less she dares to oppose or rebel against them. The child's position amounts to: *I have to repress my hostility because I need you.*

**2. Fear.** Children are easily made afraid of their parents, through punishment, physical abuse, or quieter forms of intimidation. The more frightened the child, the deeper the hostility is pushed down. The logic here is: *I must repress my hostility because I am afraid of you.*

**3. Love.** This reason is paradoxical. Some parents keep telling the child how much they love her and how much they sacrifice for her, but the warmth behind the words is not real. The child senses that this is a poor substitute for genuine love and security, but it is the only love on offer. So she represses her hostility in case she loses even this unsatisfactory version. The logic: *I must repress my hostility for fear of losing whatever love I get.*

**4. Guilt.** Children are often made to feel **guilty** about any hostility or rebelliousness, made to feel unworthy, wicked or sinful for expressing or even privately holding resentment against their parents. The more guilt the child feels, the more deeply the hostility is buried. The logic: *I must repress my hostility because it makes me bad.*

| Reason for repression | What produces it | The child's implicit reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Helplessness** | Being kept excessively dependent | "I need you, so I cannot be angry with you." |
| **Fear** | Punishment, abuse, intimidation | "I am afraid of you, so I cannot be angry with you." |
| **Love** | Insincere declarations of love and sacrifice | "If I show anger I may lose even this love." |
| **Guilt** | Being made to feel wicked for resentment | "Being angry with you makes me a bad child." |

Whatever the reason, the result is the same. The hostility does not vanish; it is pushed out of awareness while the parental behaviour that caused it continues. This **repressed hostility** undermines the childhood need for safety, and it shows itself in the condition Horney called **basic anxiety**.

> **Trap.** The order of the causal chain is a favourite MCQ. It runs: parental lack of warmth (and the undermining behaviours) → **hostility** in the child → **repression** of that hostility (helplessness, fear, love, guilt) → **basic anxiety** → self-protective mechanisms → neurotic needs → neurotic trends. Hostility comes **before** basic anxiety; basic anxiety is the product of hostility that cannot be expressed.

> **Trap.** Classic background: many other textbooks (for example Feist and Feist) use the term **basic hostility** for the child's resentment towards neglectful or rejecting parents, and pair it with basic anxiety as two sides of the same early experience. The 11th edition of Schultz speaks simply of "hostility". Do not confuse the two terms: **basic hostility** is the resentment towards the parents; **basic anxiety** is the feeling of being alone and helpless that results when that resentment has to be buried. The chapter epigraph's phrase about the "basic evil" refers to the parents' lack of genuine warmth, which is the cause of both.

## 5.3 Basic Anxiety: The Foundation of Neurosis

### What basic anxiety is

Horney defined **basic anxiety** as an "insidiously increasing, all-pervading feeling of being lonely and helpless in a hostile world" (Horney, 1937, p. 89). Learn the definition with its three key ideas: it builds up **gradually and pervasively** rather than arising from a single event; its content is **loneliness and helplessness**; and the world it perceives is **hostile**. The margin glossary condenses it to a pervasive feeling of loneliness and helplessness that is the foundation of neurosis.

Basic anxiety is the **foundation on which all later neuroses develop**, and it is bound up inseparably with feelings of **hostility, helplessness and fear** (see Hjertass, 2009). Horney believed that however different people's outward ways of coping might be, the underlying feeling is much the same for everyone who has it: a sense of being small, insignificant, abandoned and in danger, in a world that seems ready to mistreat, deceive, attack, humiliate and betray (Horney, 1937, p. 92).

To see how the feeling generalises, go back to the seven-year-old in 5.2. She cannot show her anger at the father on whom she depends. But a child who has learned that the most important people in her life cannot be trusted does not keep that lesson confined to her father. It spreads into a general expectation about people: teachers, classmates and later colleagues and partners are all quietly assumed to be dangerous. That generalised expectation is basic anxiety.

> **Trap.** Compare Horney's anxieties with Freud's. Freud's **objective, neurotic and moral anxiety** are each a fear of a specific source: the real world, the id's impulses, or the superego's punishment. Horney's **basic anxiety** is a global sense of isolation and helplessness in a hostile world, produced socially by the parent-child relationship. If an option says basic anxiety arises from the conflict between id and superego, it is Freud's language wrongly attached to Horney.

### Four ways of protecting oneself

Horney identified **four** distinct strategies a child uses to keep basic anxiety at bay: winning **affection and love**, **submitting**, gaining **power**, and **withdrawing**. These are the **self-protective mechanisms** (sometimes called self-protective devices). They are the raw material from which the neurotic needs and trends later develop.

#### Securing Affection

The first strategy is to win love and affection from others, on the implicit reasoning *if you love me, you will not hurt me*. People can try to obtain affection in several ways: by doing whatever the other person wants, by **bribing** others, or even by **threatening** them into giving the affection. That last route shows that securing affection is not always gentle. A teenager who says "if you leave me I'll hurt myself" is trying to secure affection by threat.

#### Being Submissive

The second strategy is to comply with the wishes of others, either of one particular person or of everyone around. Submissive people avoid anything that might provoke others. They dare not criticise or offend in any way, they have to bury their own wishes, and they cannot even defend themselves against mistreatment, because defending themselves might anger the person mistreating them. Most submissive people see themselves as **unselfish and self-sacrificing**. Their implicit reasoning is *if I give in, I will not be hurt*. This, the book notes, describes Horney's own behaviour in childhood until the age of **8 or 9**.

The difference from the first strategy is subtle but real. Securing affection aims at being **loved**; submission aims at not being **hurt**. The affection-seeker wants something from the other person; the submissive person mainly wants to avoid the other person's displeasure.

#### Attaining Power

The third strategy is to gain **power** over others, which compensates for helplessness and brings security either through **success** or through a **sense of superiority**. The reasoning is *if I have power, no one can harm me*. This describes Horney's own childhood once she resolved to excel academically. Note the Adlerian flavour: power here is a compensation for an underlying feeling of weakness.

#### Withdrawing

The first three strategies share one feature: each copes with basic anxiety by **dealing with other people**, whether by winning them over, giving in to them, or dominating them. The fourth strategy is to **withdraw** from people. Horney stressed that the withdrawal is **psychological, not physical**: the person may still go to work, attend family functions and sit in a crowded room, but inwardly aims to become **independent of others**, relying on no one to satisfy either internal or external needs.

For internal, psychological needs, independence is achieved by becoming **aloof**: the withdrawn person no longer looks to others for emotional satisfaction. This involves **blunting** or minimising one's emotional needs. By giving those needs up, the person protects himself from being hurt by others. If you never need anyone's love, nobody can wound you by withholding it.

| Self-protective mechanism | Implicit reasoning | Horney's own life |
|---|---|---|
| **Securing affection** | "If you love me, you will not hurt me." | Being in love relieved her anxiety |
| **Being submissive** | "If I give in, I will not be hurt." | Compliant model child until about 8 or 9 |
| **Attaining power** | "If I have power, no one will harm me." | Academic ambition after age 8 |
| **Withdrawing** | "If I need no one, no one can hurt me." | Later emotional detachment from lovers |

### Characteristics of Self-Protective Mechanisms

Horney attributed several defining features to the four mechanisms.

**A single goal: defence, not happiness.** All four have one purpose, to defend against basic anxiety. They drive the person to seek **security and reassurance**, not **happiness or pleasure**. They are a protection against pain, not a pursuit of well-being. This is an important theoretical point. A person using these mechanisms is not trying to live well; he is trying not to feel afraid.

**Power and intensity.** Horney believed these mechanisms could be **more compelling than sexual or other physiological needs**. This is a direct reversal of Freud's priority. For Freud, sexual drives are the most powerful motivators; for Horney, the need to protect oneself from anxiety can outweigh them.

**An expensive price.** The mechanisms may reduce anxiety, but the usual cost is an **impoverished personality**. The person who relies on submission, for example, purchases safety at the price of never asserting a wish of his own; the withdrawn person buys invulnerability at the price of never being close to anyone.

**Incompatible combinations.** Neurotic people often pursue safety with **more than one** mechanism, and because the mechanisms point in incompatible directions this creates further problems. A person may be driven both to **attain power** and to **gain affection**: he wants to dominate people and to be loved by them. Or a person may want to **submit** to others and at the same time to have **power** over them. Such incompatibilities cannot be resolved, and they lead to still more severe conflicts. This is the first appearance of what Horney will later define, in 5.4, as **conflict**: the basic incompatibility of the neurotic trends.

> **Trap.** There are **four** self-protective mechanisms but only **three** neurotic trends. The extra one disappears because **securing affection** and **being submissive** both end up inside a single trend, **moving toward people** (the compliant personality). Power maps onto moving against people and withdrawal onto moving away from people.

## 5.4 Neurotic Needs

### From protective device to permanent drive

A self-protective mechanism starts as a child's strategy for getting through a frightening home. Horney's next step was to ask what happens when the strategy is used so often, for so long, that it stops being a strategy and becomes part of who the person is. Her answer: any of the four mechanisms can become so **permanent** a part of the personality that it takes on the properties of a **drive or need**, steering behaviour across situations. She listed **ten** such needs and called them **neurotic needs** because they are **irrational solutions** to one's problems. The margin glossary defines them as ten irrational defences against anxiety that become a lasting part of personality and affect behaviour.

### The ten neurotic needs

In the 11th edition's order:

1. **Affection and approval**
2. **A dominant partner**
3. **Power**
4. **Exploitation**
5. **Prestige**
6. **Admiration**
7. **Achievement or ambition**
8. **Self-sufficiency**
9. **Perfection**
10. **Narrow limits to life**

The ten needs are simply the four protective mechanisms grown into fixed form, and the book spells out the mapping:

| Self-protective mechanism | Neurotic need(s) it becomes |
|---|---|
| **Securing affection** | Affection and approval |
| **Being submissive** | A dominant partner |
| **Attaining power** | Power, exploitation, prestige, admiration, achievement or ambition |
| **Withdrawing** | Self-sufficiency, perfection, narrow limits to life |

Count them: 1 + 1 + 5 + 3 = 10. Five of the ten belong to the power cluster, which is the easiest way to remember where most of the needs lie.

Two needs on the list look out of place until you think about them. **Perfection** sits under withdrawal, not power, because the perfectionist protects himself by becoming unassailable: if there is nothing to criticise, no one can get at him. **Narrow limits to life** means restricting one's life to a small, safe compass, asking little, wanting little and attempting little, so that there is little to lose; it is withdrawal in its most constricted form.

Classic background: Horney's own labels were longer, and older texts sometimes use them. The needs appear there as, for example, the need for "a partner who will take over one's life", for "social recognition or prestige", for "personal admiration", for "personal achievement", for "self-sufficiency and independence", and for "perfection and unassailability". The order of the ten also varies from source to source, so learn the **grouping**, not the numbering.

### When an ordinary need becomes neurotic

Horney insisted that **everyone** shows these needs to some degree. All of us at some point want affection or pursue achievement, and none of the needs is abnormal in an everyday, passing sense. What turns a need **neurotic** is the way it is pursued: **intensely and compulsively**, as the **only** way of dealing with basic anxiety. Once that happens the need grows increasingly severe and then **tyrannical**, gradually taking over and dominating the personality (Hess & Hess, 2010).

Compare two students who both want top marks. The first enjoys the subject, works hard, and is disappointed but not shattered by a lower grade. The second cannot tolerate any rank below first, feels worthless after a single average result, sacrifices sleep, friendships and health for marks, and pursues the same pattern in sport, in arguments and in games with friends. The first has a normal need for achievement; the second has a **neurotic need for achievement or ambition**. The difference is not the goal; it is the compulsion, the indiscriminateness, and the anxiety underneath.

Horney added two further points:

- **Satisfying a neurotic need does not produce security.** It only lets the person escape the discomfort of anxiety for a while. The underlying basic anxiety is untouched. In medical language, the needs treat the **symptoms**, not the **cause**. The second student's first rank calms her for a day; then the anxiety returns, attached to the next examination.
- **Neurotic needs are pursued narrowly.** When a need is pursued only as a way of coping with anxiety, the person tends to fasten on **one** need and to seek its satisfaction **compulsively in every situation**, whether or not it fits.

> **Trap.** "Horney regarded the ten needs as abnormal in themselves" is false. The needs are universal; what makes them neurotic is **intensity, compulsiveness, indiscriminate use, and reliance on one need as the only solution** to basic anxiety.

---

### Neurotic Trends

In her later writings (Horney, **1945**), Horney reformulated her list. Working with patients, she concluded that the ten needs could be grouped into **three** clusters, each expressing a person's basic **attitude towards the self and towards others**. She called these three categories of directional movement the **neurotic trends**. The margin glossary defines them as three categories of behaviour and attitude towards oneself and others that express a person's needs, Horney's revision of the concept of neurotic needs.

Because the trends grow out of the self-protective mechanisms and elaborate them, you will recognise them at once. Like the needs, they are **compulsive**: the neurotic person is driven to behave in line with at least one of them. And they are displayed **indiscriminately**, in any and every situation.

The three trends are:

- **Moving toward people**: the **compliant personality**.
- **Moving against people**: the **aggressive personality**.
- **Moving away from people**: the **detached personality**.

**Table 5.1** Horney's ten neurotic needs grouped into her three neurotic trends

| Neurotic trend (direction of movement) | Personality type | Neurotic needs it contains | Self-protective mechanism it grows from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Moving toward people** | **Compliant** | Affection and approval; a dominant partner | Securing affection; being submissive |
| **Moving against people** | **Aggressive** | Power; exploitation; prestige; admiration; achievement | Attaining power |
| **Moving away from people** | **Detached** | Self-sufficiency; perfection; narrow limits to life | Withdrawing |

The book's own Table 5.1 sets the needs in one column and the trends in the other, labelling each group of needs as the components of the corresponding personality. The last column above is added from the text of 5.3 so that you can see the whole chain in one row.

> **Trap.** This is the single most tested distinction in the chapter. **Neurotic needs = 10** (the earlier formulation). **Neurotic trends = 3** (the later regrouping, Horney 1945). The trends are categories that **contain** the needs. An option that says "Horney's ten neurotic trends" or "Horney's three neurotic needs" is wrong in the 11th edition's terms. Horney's own terminology shifted between books, and some older sources use the words loosely, so if a question uses "trends" for ten items, read it as the needs.

> **Trap.** The three directions are **toward, against, away from**. Examiners swap the type labels: **compliant = toward**, **aggressive = against**, **detached = away**. A frequent distractor is "aggressive = away from people"; it is wrong.

### The Compliant Personality

The **compliant personality** is Horney's name for the cluster of attitudes and behaviour that make up the trend of moving **toward** people, with the need for affection and approval as its hallmark.

At the centre of this type is a craving for **affection and approval** that never lets up: the person wants above all to be loved, to be wanted and to be looked after. The craving is aimed at people in general, yet there is usually also **one dominant figure** in the background, a spouse or a close friend, whom the compliant person relies on to run his life, shield him and tell him what to do.

On the surface, compliant people are easy to like. They come across as unusually considerate, appreciative, responsive, understanding and sensitive to other people's needs. They work hard to live up to others' ideals and expectations, and others see them as unselfish and generous. But Horney noticed that this behaviour is also **manipulative**: compliant people use it, especially with their partners, to get what they need.

In their dealings with others they are **conciliatory**. They put their own wishes below other people's, readily accept blame, and defer to others; they are never assertive, critical or demanding. They do whatever the situation requires, as they read it, to win affection, approval and love.

Their attitude to **themselves** is one of consistent **helplessness and weakness**. Horney summed up their unspoken message as a plea to be protected and loved precisely because they are so weak and helpless. They therefore regard other people as **superior**, and even where they are competent they see themselves as **inferior**. Because their security rests entirely on how others treat them, they become **excessively dependent**, needing constant approval and reassurance. Any sign of rejection, whether real or imagined, terrifies them and makes them redouble their efforts to win back the affection of the person they think has rejected them.

Now the twist that makes Horney's analysis more than a description. The **source** of all this sweetness is **repressed hostility**. In her patients Horney found that compliant people had buried deep feelings of **defiance and vindictiveness**, and a wish to **control, exploit and manipulate** others, which is the exact opposite of what their behaviour displays. Because those hostile impulses must be kept out of sight, the compliant person becomes **subservient**, always trying to please and asking nothing for himself.

A clinical example: a young wife who agrees to every one of her husband's decisions, apologises when he is late, cannot say no to her in-laws, and becomes frantic if he seems cool towards her, may in therapy reveal intense, frightening resentment that she has never allowed herself to feel. Horney would say her niceness is not a sign of the absence of hostility but a defence against it.

> **Trap.** Compliant people are not simply "kind". In Horney's account their compliance is **compulsive** and **manipulative**, and its source is **repressed hostility**. If an MCQ asks what lies beneath the compliant personality's behaviour, the answer is repressed hostility (defiance, vindictiveness, a wish to control), not genuine altruism.

### The Aggressive Personality

The **aggressive personality** moves **against** people. The glossary defines it as the behaviours and attitudes associated with moving against people, such as a domineering and controlling manner.

The aggressive person takes for granted that **other people are enemies**. Life, as he sees it, is a struggle for survival in which the strong and the shrewd win, and so dominance, toughness and ruthlessness are what he values most. Underneath, his motive is **identical** to the compliant type's, the relief of basic anxiety; the strategy is simply reversed. Where the compliant person dreads rejection, the aggressive person **never lets any such fear show**. He presents himself as hard and commanding and pays no attention to what others feel.

To achieve the control and superiority that matter so much to him, the aggressive person must perform at a consistently **high level**. Excelling and being recognised gives him the satisfaction of having his superiority confirmed by others. Driven to outdo everyone, he judges every relationship by **what he will gain from it**. He makes no effort to appease others; instead he argues, criticises, makes demands and does whatever is needed to gain and keep superiority and power. He drives himself hard to be the best.

One consequence is that aggressive people can rise **very high** in their occupations. The catch is that they get **no intrinsic satisfaction** from the work: a job, like a friendship or a marriage, is valued only for what it delivers, as an **instrument** for winning superiority, never for its own sake.

To an observer, such a person seems self-assured and unafraid to push his own case. Horney's point is that the confidence is a front. What drives him is the same **insecurity, anxiety and hostility** that drives the compliant type.

A familiar example is the manager who treats meetings as contests, cannot let a subordinate's idea stand without improving on it, measures colleagues only by their usefulness, and is outwardly very successful, yet takes no pleasure in the work and becomes enraged at any hint that someone else is ahead.

> **Trap.** Review Question 7 in the book asks which type is more likely to be successful in a career. The answer from the text is the **aggressive** type, because it drives itself to excel and be recognised, though the work brings it no intrinsic satisfaction. Detached people may develop their own resources well but avoid competition.

### The Detached Personality

The **detached personality** moves **away** from people and keeps an **emotional distance**. The glossary defines it as the behaviours and attitudes associated with moving away from people, such as an intense need for privacy.

Detached people must not love, hate, or cooperate with others, or become involved with them in any way. To make this complete detachment workable they try to become **self-sufficient**. If they are to function without others, they must depend on their own resources, and those resources have to be well developed.

**Privacy** matters to them with something close to desperation. They want to be alone as much as they can manage, and even a shared experience as harmless as listening to music together can unsettle them. Because independence is everything, they bristle at anyone who tries to **persuade, pressure or put them under obligation**, and they steer clear of every kind of constraint: timetables and schedules, long-term commitments such as marriage or a mortgage, and, Horney noted, sometimes even the pressure of a belt or a necktie.

Detached people also need to feel **superior**, but not in the aggressive type's way. They cannot compete openly for superiority, because competing would mean getting involved with others. So they believe their greatness should be **recognised automatically**, without any struggle or effort on their part. One expression of this superiority is the feeling of being **unique**, different from and apart from everyone else.

They **suppress or deny all feelings towards other people**, especially love and hate, because intimacy would lead to conflict, and conflict must be avoided. Because their emotions are so constricted, detached people put great weight on **reason, logic and intelligence**.

An example: a brilliant research scholar who lives alone, refuses invitations, resents his supervisor's deadlines as intolerable interference, feels privately that his talent ought to be obvious without his having to publicise it, and approaches every personal question with cool intellectual analysis.

> **Trap.** Both the aggressive and the detached types need to feel **superior**. The difference is the route: the aggressive type **competes and fights** for superiority; the detached type expects it to be **recognised automatically**, without effort or involvement, and experiences it as uniqueness.

### Horney's three types and Adler's styles of life

The book points out a resemblance to Adler's theory (Chapter 4):

| Horney's neurotic trend | Adler's style of life |
|---|---|
| Compliant personality (toward) | **Getting** type |
| Aggressive personality (against) | **Dominant** or **ruling** type |
| Detached personality (away) | **Avoiding** type |

Schultz presents this as another example of how Adler's ideas influenced later accounts of personality, and it fits Horney's life: she read Adler during her self-analysis. Adler's fourth style, the **socially useful** type, has no counterpart among Horney's neurotic trends, which makes sense, since Adler's fourth type is the healthy one and Horney's trends are all neurotic patterns.

### The Dominance of One Trend

In the neurotic person, Horney found, **one** of the three trends is **dominant**, while the other two are present to a lesser degree. A predominantly aggressive person, for example, also has some need for compliance and for detachment.

The dominant trend is the one that governs the person's behaviour and attitudes towards others. It is the way of acting and thinking that works best for that person in controlling basic anxiety, and so any departure from it feels threatening. For this reason the other two trends must be **actively repressed**, which creates further problems. Whenever there is a sign that a repressed trend is pushing for expression, the person experiences **conflict**.

Think of the aggressive manager above. Suppose a colleague he respects is warm towards him, and he feels a pull to be liked, a stirring of the compliant trend. For him that pull is dangerous, because it threatens the strategy that keeps his anxiety at bay. He may react by being even more cutting, crushing the compliant impulse before it can surface.

### Conflict

In Horney's system **conflict** has a precise meaning: the **basic incompatibility of the three neurotic trends**, and this conflict is the **core of neurosis**. The glossary entry is exactly that, the basic incompatibility of the neurotic trends.

Everyone, normal or neurotic, suffers some conflict among these fundamentally irreconcilable ways of relating. The difference between normal and neurotic people lies in the **intensity** of the conflict, which is much greater in the neurotic. Neurotic people have to fight to keep the non-dominant trends from showing. They are **rigid and inflexible**, meeting every situation with the behaviour and attitudes of the dominant trend whether or not they suit it.

> **Trap.** Horney's "conflict" is not Freud's conflict between id, ego and superego. For Horney, conflict is between **ways of relating to people** (toward, against, away), and it is social in origin. Also note: conflict is present in **everyone**; neurosis is a matter of **degree** (intensity and rigidity), not of having conflict versus not having it.

### The Expression of All Three Trends

A person who is **not** neurotic draws on whichever trend the **situation calls for**: assertive in one setting, yielding in another, withdrawn in a third. Nothing forces a choice between them, because in a healthy personality the three directions **coexist and blend** rather than compete. The mark of normality, then, is **flexibility**: attitudes and behaviour shift to fit changing circumstances.

An everyday example: a healthy lawyer argues hard against opposing counsel in court (moving against), comforts and defers to an elderly parent in the evening (moving toward), and spends a quiet Sunday alone reading (moving away), without any of these feeling forced or threatening. Her neurotic counterpart would argue with her parent, demand recognition from her family, and find a quiet Sunday unbearable, or would comfort her opponent in court; whatever her dominant trend, she would apply it everywhere.

| | Normal person | Neurotic person |
|---|---|---|
| Number of trends used | All three, as the situation demands | One dominant; the other two repressed |
| Flexibility | Flexible, adaptive | Rigid, inflexible, indiscriminate |
| Conflict among trends | Present but mild | Intense; the core of neurosis |
| Integration | Trends integrated harmoniously | Trends at war |

## 5.5 The Idealized Self-Image

### Normal and neurotic self-images

Everyone, Horney argued, normal or neurotic, builds a **picture of the self**, and that picture may or may not match reality. She knew the difficulty from the inside. At **21** she wrote in her diary that she still felt chaotic inside, comparing herself to her own face, which seemed to her a shapeless mass that only took form from the expression of the moment, and calling the search for one's self the most agonising of all (Horney, 1980, p. 174).

A **normal** person's self-image rests on an **honest stock-taking**: what I can do, what I might become, where I fall short, what I am aiming at, and how I actually get on with people. Such an image does two jobs. It gives the personality a sense of **unity and integration**, and it provides a framework from which to approach other people and oneself. If we are to realise our full potential, the state Horney called **self-realization**, our self-image must accurately reflect our **true self**.

**Neurotics** are in a different position. Because they live with conflict between incompatible ways of relating (5.4), their personalities are marked by **disunity and disharmony**. They build an **idealized self-image** for the same reason normal people build a self-image, to **unify** the personality. But the attempt is doomed, because their image does not rest on a realistic assessment of strengths and weaknesses. It rests on an **illusion**, an unreachable ideal of **absolute perfection**.

The margin glossary captures the distinction in one entry: for normal people, the self-image is an idealized picture of oneself built on a **flexible, realistic** assessment of one's abilities; for neurotics, it is based on an **inflexible, unrealistic** self-appraisal.

> **Trap.** In the 11th edition, **both** normal and neurotic people have an idealized self-image. The difference is not whether the image exists but whether it is **realistic and flexible** (normal) or **illusory and rigid** (neurotic). An option saying "only neurotics form an idealized self-image" contradicts the book's glossary.

### Tyranny of the Shoulds

In trying to reach their impossible ideal, neurotics fall into what Horney called the **tyranny of the shoulds**. Whatever role they occupy (student, husband or wife, parent, lover, employee, friend, son or daughter) they insist to themselves that they **should** be the flawless, best-ever version of it. Since the **real** self strikes them as intolerable, they believe they have to live up to the illusory idealized image, in which they appear in a glowing light: virtuous, honest, generous, considerate and brave.

To do this they **deny their real selves** and try to become what they think they should be, what they need to be in order to match the idealized image. The effort is bound to fail. They can never reach the unrealistic image, and they end up in a state of **self-hatred**, unable to forgive themselves or anyone else (Kerr, 1984).

In the book's glossary the term has two parts: the goal (reaching an idealized self-image that cannot be reached) and the method (setting aside the true self and acting according to what one believes one ought to do).

Here is what this looks like in a patient. A 30-year-old doctor tells her therapist that she should never lose her temper with patients, should always be available to her parents, should keep her house spotless, should be the most published person in her department, and should never feel tired. Each "should" is part of an idealized image of a tireless, saintly, brilliant woman. Each ordinary lapse (an irritable moment, a missed call home, a rejected paper) is experienced not as a normal human limit but as proof of failure, and she hates herself for it. Horney's point is that the "shoulds" are not her real values; they are commands issued by an image she has constructed to hold an anxious, divided personality together.

> **Trap.** The tyranny of the shoulds is **not** Freud's superego, although both produce guilt-like demands. The superego is an internalised parental and social conscience, formed in the phallic stage. Horney's shoulds come from the **idealized self-image**, a self-made illusion built to resolve the conflict between neurotic trends.

Classic background: in *Neurosis and Human Growth* (1950) Horney developed this idea at length, and some other textbooks (for example Feist and Feist) present the idealized self-image as a wider package that includes the neurotic **search for glory**, **neurotic claims** and **neurotic pride**, with the tyranny of the shoulds as one part of the search for glory. Schultz's 11th edition covers only the shoulds and externalization.

### The Neurotic's Self-Image

The idealized self-image of a neurotic does not correspond to reality, but to the person who created it, it seems **real and accurate**. Other people can easily see through the false picture; the neurotic cannot. He believes in the incomplete and misleading portrait of himself. The idealized self-image is a model of what the neurotic thinks he **is, can be, or should be**.

Compare the two kinds of self-image directly:

| | Realistic self-image (normal) | Idealized self-image (neurotic) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Realistic appraisal of abilities, weaknesses, goals | Illusion; an unattainable ideal of absolute perfection |
| Quality | Flexible and dynamic; changes as the person develops | Static, inflexible, unyielding |
| What it contains | Strengths, growth and self-awareness | A glorified picture (virtuous, honest, generous, brave) |
| Function | A **goal** to strive towards, which both reflects and leads the person | A **fixed idea** that blocks growth and demands rigid obedience to its rules |
| Effect on self-worth | Reality-based self-worth | Only an illusory sense of worth |
| Relation to true self | Reflects the true self; enables self-realization | Alienates the person from the true self |
| Effect on conflict | Unifies the personality | Becomes one more element in the conflict |

Several points in the table need explaining. The neurotic has little real self-confidence, because of insecurity and anxiety, and the idealized self-image cannot correct this, because it does not allow its owner to notice and work on his actual shortcomings. It provides only an **illusory** sense of worth and draws the neurotic further from his **true self**.

There is a cruel irony in its function. The idealized self-image was built to **reconcile** incompatible modes of behaviour. Instead of solving that problem, it becomes **one more element in the conflict** and adds to a growing sense of futility. And it is fragile. The slightest crack in the idealized picture threatens the false sense of superiority and security the whole structure was meant to provide, and very little is needed to bring it down. Horney's image for it was a **house filled with dynamite**, always on the edge of self-destruction.

### Externalization

One way neurotics try to defend themselves against the inner conflict created by the gap between the idealized and the real self is **externalization**: **projecting the conflicts onto the outside world**. Externalization may ease the anxiety for a while, but it does nothing to narrow the gap between the idealized image and reality.

Externalization has two parts. The person tends to experience his conflicts **as though they were happening outside** him, and he portrays **external forces as their source**. For example, a neurotic who hates himself for falling short of his idealized image may project that hatred onto other people or onto institutions, and come to believe that the hatred is coming at him from them rather than from within.

Put as a glossary-style definition: externalization is a defence in which the conflict created by the mismatch between idealized and real self is thrown outward onto the world.

A clinical example: a postgraduate student whose idealized image is of a brilliant, effortless scholar fails a qualifying examination. Rather than face the gap between that image and his real preparation, he becomes convinced that the department is biased against him, that the examiners despise him, and that the whole system is out to crush talent like his. The contempt he feels is his own self-contempt, relocated outside him. For a while he feels better, because the enemy is now external; but nothing has changed in his relationship to his real self, and the next setback will need another external villain.

> **Trap.** **Externalization** vs **tyranny of the shoulds** (the book's Review Question 11). The shoulds turn the conflict **inwards**: the person drives himself to meet the idealized image and hates himself for failing. Externalization pushes the conflict **outwards**: the person experiences it, and his self-hatred, as coming from the world. Both arise from the same gap between the idealized and real self; they deal with it in opposite directions.

> **Trap.** Externalization resembles Freud's **projection**, and the book defines it with the word "projecting". The difference is scope. Freud's projection attributes one's own unacceptable impulse (say, hostility) to someone else. Horney's externalization relocates the whole **inner conflict between idealized and real self** onto the outside world, and casts outside forces as its cause.

## 5.6 Feminine Psychology

### 1922: Horney takes on Freud

Horney's quarrel with Freud over women started early. Her work on a **feminine psychology** dates from **1922**, when she read a paper on the subject at an **international psychoanalytic congress**, the **first woman** ever to do so. The venue was **Berlin**, and the man in the chair was **Freud** himself.

The glossary defines **feminine psychology**, as Horney used it, as a revision of psychoanalysis to take in the psychological conflicts built into the traditional ideal of womanhood and women's roles.

Her sharpest criticism was aimed at **penis envy**. She argued that Freud had derived it from **inadequate evidence**, namely his clinical interviews with **neurotic women**, and that he described and interpreted it from a strictly **male point of view**, in a time and place where women were treated as second-class citizens. In Freud's account women were victims of their anatomy, permanently envious and resentful of men for having a penis. Freud also concluded that women had **poorly developed superegos**, a result of incompletely resolved Oedipal conflicts, and **inferior body images**, because they believed they were really castrated men.

Notice the methodological shape of her objection. It is not only "Freud is wrong"; it is "Freud generalised to all women from a biased sample (neurotic patients), interpreted through a biased lens (male), in a biased culture (women's subordination)". That is the same kind of criticism that later research methodologists would make of the case-study method.

### Womb Envy

Horney's counter-argument was that **men envy women** for their capacity for **motherhood**. Her position drew partly on the **pleasure she said she had experienced in childbirth**. In her male patients she found what she called **womb envy**, and she reported that when an analyst who had worked for a long time with women began to analyse men, the intensity of men's envy of pregnancy, childbirth and motherhood came as a real surprise (Horney, 1967, pp. 60 to 61).

The glossary definition: the envy a male feels towards a female because she can bear children and he cannot; Horney's response to Freud's concept of penis envy in females.

Horney then used the idea to explain how men behave. A man's contribution to producing a child is tiny, so, she argued, men turn their envy into something else (**sublimation**) and push themselves to excess in the one arena left to them, **achievement at work** (Gilman, 2001). The envy, and the resentment that comes with it, also leak out **unconsciously** in conduct that runs women down and keeps them subordinate: denying women equal rights, minimising their chances to contribute to society, and downgrading their efforts to achieve. In this way men preserve their supposedly natural superiority. Beneath such typical male behaviour, Horney argued, lies a **sense of inferiority** that comes from womb envy.

Look at the structure of the argument. It is Freud's own logic turned around. Freud said women envy what men have and compensate for the lack; Horney said men envy what women have and compensate through achievement and through keeping women down. Note also how Adlerian it is: an inferiority feeling, compensated by striving for superiority.

**Horney did not deny that women feel inferior.** Many women, she agreed, did believe themselves inferior to men. What she questioned was Freud's claim that this feeling has a **biological basis**. Her explanation was social: a woman who has been **treated** as worth less in a culture run by men will come to feel worth less. When discrimination of a social, economic and cultural kind has gone on for generations, women's low opinion of themselves is no mystery and needs no anatomy to explain it.

> **Trap.** The correct statement is "Horney accepted that many women felt inferior but attributed it to **cultural** discrimination, not anatomy". The distractors are "Horney denied that women felt inferior" (wrong) and "Horney agreed that women's inferiority feelings are biologically based" (wrong).

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Horney's **womb envy** with Melanie Klein's notion of envy. Classic background: Klein, the object-relations analyst, wrote of the infant's envy of the mother's breast (in *Envy and Gratitude*, 1957), an envy present in infants of both sexes in the first months of life. Horney's womb envy is an envy felt by **males** of women's capacity to bear children. Horney and Klein are both early women analysts and both broke with parts of Freud's account, which is why their names get swapped.

### The Flight from Womanhood

Inferiority feelings produced by culture can lead a woman to **disown her own femininity** and to harbour an **unconscious** wish to have been born male. Horney called this the **flight from womanhood** (Horney, **1926**), and she held that it can lead to **sexual inhibitions**.

Part of the sexual fear involved comes, she suggested, from **childhood fantasies** about the difference in size between the adult penis and a young girl's vagina. These fantasies centre on vaginal injury and the pain of forced penetration. The result is a conflict between an **unconscious wish to have a child** and a **fear of intercourse**. If the conflict is strong enough, it can produce emotional disturbances that show in the woman's relationships with men: such women **distrust and resent men** and **reject their sexual advances**.

> **Trap.** The flight from womanhood (1926) is **not** penis envy under a new name. Freud's penis envy is a biologically driven wish for the male organ. Horney's flight from womanhood is a **denial of femininity** driven by **culturally produced** inferiority feelings, with an added element of childhood fear about intercourse. Note the date: 1926 for the flight from womanhood, 1922 for the Berlin congress paper.

### The Oedipus Complex

Horney also disagreed with Freud about the **Oedipus complex**. She did not deny that **conflicts between children and parents** exist, but she did not believe they had a **sexual** origin. Removing sex from the Oedipus complex, she reinterpreted it as a **conflict between dependence on one's parents and hostility towards them**.

You already have the pieces from 5.2. Parental behaviour that frustrates the safety need produces hostility. At the same time the child **depends** on the parents, so expressing the hostility is impossible; the child's position is *I have to repress my hostility because I need you*. The hostile impulses remain and generate basic anxiety. Horney observed that the outcome can look just like what Freud called the Oedipus complex, in the form of a child who clings passionately to one parent and is jealous of the other (Horney, 1939, p. 83).

So her explanation of Oedipal feelings lies in **neurotic conflicts arising from parent-child interactions**. These feelings are **not based on sex or other biological forces**, and they are **not universal**. They develop **only when parents act to undermine the child's security**.

A worked contrast. A five-year-old boy hangs on to his mother and is jealous and hostile towards his father. Freud: this is the universal phallic-stage Oedipus complex, a sexual desire for the mother and rivalry with the father, resolved by castration anxiety and identification. Horney: look at the family. Perhaps the father is harsh and unpredictable, so the boy resents him but cannot show it; the boy clings to the mother as his only source of safety. Change the father's behaviour and the "complex" need never form.

> **Trap.** Horney's reinterpreted Oedipus complex has two features examiners test: it is **not sexual** (dependence versus hostility), and it is **not universal** (it appears only when parents undermine security). An option saying Horney "denied that children ever have conflicts with their parents" is wrong; she denied the sexual origin and the universality, not the conflict.

### What Did Freud Say about Horney?

Freud never answered Horney's challenge directly, and he did not change his concept of the Oedipus complex. But in a barely disguised reference to her work he wrote that one should not be surprised if a woman analyst who had not been sufficiently convinced of the strength of her own wish for a penis also failed to give that factor its due weight in her patients (Freud, 1940). In other words, he explained her disagreement as a symptom of her own unanalysed penis envy, a move that made the theory impossible to challenge from inside it. Of Horney personally, Freud is reported to have said, "She is able but malicious" (quoted in Blanton, 1971, p. 65). Horney was bitter that Freud would not recognise the legitimacy of her views.

> **Trap.** Freud's comment "able but malicious" was about **Horney**, and it is reported in **Blanton (1971)**. The 1940 allusion to a "woman analyst" is from Freud's own writing (Freud, 1940). Neither is a direct published reply to her papers; the book says Freud did not respond directly.

### Motherhood or Career?

As an early feminist, Horney was concerned about the psychological **conflicts in defining women's roles**, and she contrasted the **traditional** ideal of womanhood with a more **modern** view (Horney, 1967). In the traditional scheme, promoted and endorsed by most men, a woman's role was to **love, admire and serve her man**. Her identity was merely a reflection of her husband's.

Horney argued that women must find **their own identity**, as she had done, by developing their abilities and pursuing **careers**. The clash between the traditional and the modern roles creates conflicts that some women still struggle to resolve. Writing at the height of the women's movement in the 1980s and drawing on Horney's work, the feminist scholar **Westkott (1986, p. 14)** described modern women as pulled between making themselves attractive to men and pursuing their own goals; the competing aims produce contradictory behaviour (seductive against aggressive, deferential against ambitious), and women are left torn between love and work and dissatisfied with both.

The book notes that combining marriage, motherhood and career remains as hard for some women in the 21st century as it was for Horney in the 1930s. Her own decision to develop her abilities and concentrate on her work gave her great satisfaction, yet she went on searching for security and love all her life. The theory and the life, once again, mirror each other: a career won, love never found.

### Cultural Influences on Feminine Psychology

Horney recognised the impact of **social and cultural forces** on personality, and she noted that different cultures and social groups see women's roles differently. It follows that there can be **many different feminine psychologies**, not a single one dictated by anatomy. She illustrated this by contrasting American women with German women, both with certain **Pueblo Indian** women, and a New York society woman with a farmer's wife in **Idaho**, concluding that particular cultural conditions produce particular qualities in women just as they do in men (Horney, 1939, p. 119).

To show how far culture can shape women's lives, Schultz turns to **traditional Chinese** society. From the **first millennium B.C.** onwards, women there ranked below men, and the ranking was underwritten by a cosmology: the universe was thought to be made of two opposed yet interdependent principles, **yin and yang**. **Yang** stood for the male element and included everything vital, positive, strong and active; **yin** stood for the female element and included everything dark, weak and passive. Over time these came to form a **hierarchy** in which men were superior and women inferior.

The idea became part of the teaching of **Confucius (551 to 479 B.C.)**, whose philosophy was China's ruling ideology for centuries. Under it, women's conduct was tightly prescribed: a good woman was **obedient and submissive**, **respectful**, **chaste** and **self-denying**. Even the language encoded her place, since the Chinese word for woman meant, literally, **"inside person"**, someone whose world ended at the walls of the house. Loo (1998, p. 180) describes the ideal: a respectable woman was neither seen nor heard and was never to be free of male authority, owing obedience to her **father** at home, to her **husband** after marriage, and to her **eldest son** if widowed. Men were warned against listening to women in case disaster followed, and the self-will and ambition admired as heroic in a man were condemned as wicked in a woman.

Set this beside the widely accepted views of women's place in contemporary American society, and in a rapidly changing China, and Horney's position becomes easy to accept: the feminine psyche is **influenced, even determined, by cultural forces**.

An Indian reader will recognise the same three-stage formula of father, husband and son in the well-known *Manusmriti* verse placing a woman under male protection at each stage of life. Classic background: this parallel is not in Schultz, but it makes Horney's point with a local example, which can be useful in an interview answer on culture and gender.

## 5.7 Questions about Human Nature

Schultz rates every theorist on six issues. For Horney the overall verdict is clear from the first line of the book's section: her image of human nature is **considerably more optimistic than Freud's**. Each of the six positions follows from one core belief, that **biology does not condemn us** to conflict, anxiety, neurosis or a universal pattern of personality. Neurosis, when it occurs, comes from **social forces in childhood**, and social forces can be changed.

### Free will versus determinism

Horney comes down on the side of **free will**. She believed we can **consciously shape and change our personality**. Her confidence in our capacity for growth was so strong that she made **self-analysis** a part of her therapeutic approach as well as of her own life; in her book ***Self-Analysis* (Horney, 1942)** she argued that people can help resolve their own problems. The book states the conclusion directly: on free will versus determinism, Horney argued for free will, and we can all shape our lives and achieve self-realization.

The reason is personal as well as theoretical. Horney's own analysis with Karl Abraham failed, and she went on to understand herself through a lifetime of self-analysis. Her theory generalises her experience: if she could do it, so can others.

### Nature versus nurture

Horney is primarily on the side of **nurture**, specifically the **social environment** of the family. Neurotic behaviour results from **social forces in childhood**: parent-child relationships either satisfy or frustrate the safety need, and frustration leads to neurosis. She rejected Freud's biological instincts and found the differences between her German and American patients, and between women in different cultures, too large to be explained by biology. There is one innate element, however. Each of us has an **innate potential for self-realization**, so the **urge to grow** is part of our nature. The best summary is: the **drive towards growth is innate**, but **whether it is fulfilled or blocked depends on the social environment**.

### Importance of the past versus the present

Horney gives weight to **both**. Childhood matters, because the thwarting of the safety need in early life is the only thing that can obstruct our development. But she held that human nature is **flexible** and is **not cast into unchangeable forms in childhood**; each of us keeps the capacity to grow, and so **adult experiences may be as important as those of childhood**. The Life-Span part opener in Schultz makes the same point: Adler and Horney both held that personality can keep changing well beyond childhood. Her clinical technique fits this view, since she examined a patient's present attitudes and conflicts before asking about the early years (5.8).

### Uniqueness versus universality

Horney stresses **uniqueness**. In the book's words, to Horney each person is unique. She rejected universal developmental stages and inevitable childhood conflicts (5.2), denied that Oedipal feelings are universal (5.6), and argued that there can be many feminine psychologies shaped by different cultures. The three neurotic trends are general categories, but each person's mix of them, and each person's history, is individual.

### Equilibrium versus growth

For the healthy person, Horney's answer is **growth**. **Self-realization**, the fullest development and use of one's potential, is our **ultimate and necessary goal** in life. Our inborn abilities and potential will flower as naturally and inevitably as an **acorn grows into an oak tree**, unless the childhood need for safety and security is frustrated. Notice the contrast within her theory: the **neurotic** is stuck in a defensive, equilibrium-like pattern, driven by the self-protective mechanisms to seek **security rather than happiness** (5.3), whereas the **healthy** person moves towards growth.

### Optimism versus pessimism

Horney is **optimistic**. Biology does not doom us to conflict and neurosis. **Neuroses and conflicts can be avoided** if children are raised with **love, acceptance and trust**. People retain the capacity to change throughout life, and they can even analyse and help themselves.

### Summary table

| Issue | Horney's position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Free will** | We can consciously shape and change our personality; *Self-Analysis* (1942) |
| Nature vs nurture | Mainly **nurture** (social forces), with an **innate** urge to self-realization | Neurosis comes from parent-child relationships, not instincts; growth potential is inborn |
| Past vs present | **Both** | Childhood safety need is crucial, but personality stays flexible and adult experience may matter as much |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Uniqueness** | Each person is unique; no universal stages; Oedipal feelings not universal; many feminine psychologies |
| Equilibrium vs growth | **Growth** | Self-realization is the ultimate goal (the acorn and the oak); only neurotics are stuck in defensive security-seeking |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimism** | Neurosis is avoidable with love, acceptance and trust; people can change and analyse themselves |

> **Trap.** Horney is often assumed to be a pessimist because she wrote so much about neurosis and conflict. The 11th edition rates her as **considerably more optimistic than Freud**. The writing about neurosis describes what goes wrong when the safety need is frustrated, not what must happen.

## 5.8 Assessment in Horney's Theory

### The analyst-patient relationship: constructive friendliness

Horney's assessment methods were essentially Freud's, **free association** and **dream analysis**, but she modified both. The most basic difference between her technique and Freud's lay in the **relationship between analyst and patient**. She thought Freud played too **passive** a role and was too **distant and intellectual**. Analysis, in her view, should be an "exquisitely cooperative enterprise" between patient and therapist (Horney quoted in Cherry & Cherry, 1973, p. 84).

She kept a **couch** in her office but did not use it with every patient. She adopted an attitude she called **constructive friendliness**. In practice this meant finding out, by trial and error, whether a particular patient worked better lying down or sitting up, and encouraging patients to feel free to sit, lie down, walk about, or do whatever they wished during the session (Horney, 1987, p. 43).

Think about why this follows from her theory. If neurosis grows out of a childhood in which the person never felt safe with the people in charge of him, then a cold, silent, all-knowing analyst risks recreating the very situation that caused the problem. A warmer, cooperative stance allows the patient to experience, perhaps for the first time, a relationship with an authority figure in which he is not in danger.

> **Trap.** **Constructive friendliness** is Horney's term for her therapeutic attitude. It is not Rogers's "unconditional positive regard" (a later, humanistic concept), although the two are sometimes confused because both reject the detached Freudian analyst.

### Free Association

In using free association, Horney did not follow Freud in trying to **probe the unconscious**. She believed patients could easily **distort or conceal** parts of their inner lives, or **misrepresent** their feelings about events they remembered. So she concentrated instead on what she could actually observe: the patient's **visible emotional reactions towards her**. These, she believed, revealed the patient's attitudes towards other people in general. She did not dig for presumed **infantile sexual fantasies**, and she asked about the patient's **early years only after** assessing **present** attitudes, defences and conflicts.

She also had a particular model of how the material was organised. Each attitude or feeling, she believed, results from a deeper attitude that existed before it, which in turn results from a still deeper one, and so on. Through free association the analyst gradually uncovers the patient's early experiences and emotions, layer by layer, in a process like **peeling an onion**.

An example: a patient keeps apologising to Horney and anxiously asks whether she is boring her. Horney would treat this visible reaction as the starting point: the patient seems to expect rejection from her, and probably from everyone. Beneath that lies a deeper attitude (perhaps a conviction that she is only acceptable when useful to others), and beneath that, eventually, the early experience of a parent whose affection was conditional. The order of work is from the present surface towards the past core, the opposite of Freud's starting point in infantile sexuality.

| | Freud's free association | Horney's free association |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Probe the unconscious; uncover repressed, often sexual, material | Understand present attitudes, defences and conflicts |
| Main data | The content of the patient's associations | The patient's **visible emotional reactions to the analyst** |
| Childhood material | Central from the start; infantile sexual fantasies | Explored **only after** present attitudes are evaluated |
| Model | Digging down to repressed instinctual wishes | **Peeling the onion**: each attitude rests on a deeper earlier one |
| Analyst's stance | Passive, distant, interpretive | Cooperative, **constructive friendliness** |

### Dream Analysis

Horney believed **dream analysis** could reveal a person's **true self**, and she saw dreams as **attempts to solve problems**, either constructively or neurotically. Dreams can show a set of attitudes different from those of the person's self-image, which makes them especially useful for exposing the gap between the idealized and the real self.

She did **not** offer a list of **universal dream symbols**. Instead she insisted that each dream be understood within the context of the **patient's own conflict**. She focused on the dream's **emotional content**, concluding that the safest key to understanding a dream lies in the feelings the patient has **within** the dream (Horney, 1987, p. 61).

Example: a man with a strongly aggressive trend dreams that he is lost in a crowd and desperately searching for his mother. Horney would not translate the crowd or the mother into fixed symbolic equivalents. She would ask how he felt in the dream (frightened, small, longing to be cared for) and note that these feelings contradict his tough self-image, suggesting a repressed compliant trend pushing for expression.

> **Trap.** "Horney used a dictionary of universal dream symbols" is false. Freud, too, used some fixed symbols (the handbag in Abraham's interpretation is an example of that tradition), and Jung relied on archetypal symbols. Horney's rule was: no universal symbols; read the dream through the patient's **conflict** and the **feelings** in the dream.

### Self-Report Inventories

**Horney herself did not use psychological tests.** Later researchers, however, built several self-report inventories on parts of her theory.

**The CAD.** A **35-item** self-report inventory named after the three trends, **Compliant, Aggressive and Detached**, was devised to measure Horney's three neurotic trends (**Cohen, 1967**).

**The HCTI.** A second questionnaire for the same three trends is the **Horney-Coolidge Type Indicator (HCTI)**, which has **57** self-report items. Research with **children** and with **college students** confirmed the HCTI as a **valid** measure of the compliant, aggressive and detached types (Coolidge, Moor, Yamazaki, Stewart, & Segal, 2001; Coolidge, Segal, & Estey, 2010).

Other studies of college students' HCTI responses found **sex differences**: **men** tended to score higher on the **aggressive** and **detached** scales, and **women** scored higher on **compliance**. The research also linked Horney's three types to various personality disorders and personality dimensions. For example, **aggression and detachment correlated highly with psychoticism**, while **compliance was associated with neuroticism** (Coolidge et al., 2001; Shatz, 2004; for further support see Coolidge, Segal, Benight, & Danielian, 2004). Psychoticism and neuroticism are dimensions from Eysenck's theory, which appears later in the book.

**The Horney-Coolidge Tridimensional Inventory.** More recently, a new version of the HCTI was developed for use with **children and adolescents aged 5 to 17** (Coolidge, Segal, Estey, & Neuzil, 2011).

| Instrument | Authors (as cited) | Items | What it measures / key finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CAD** (Compliant-Aggressive-Detached) | Cohen, 1967 | **35** | The three neurotic trends |
| **HCTI** (Horney-Coolidge Type Indicator) | Coolidge et al., 2001; Coolidge, Segal, & Estey, 2010 | **57** | The three trends; valid in children and college students; men higher aggressive and detached, women higher compliant |
| **Horney-Coolidge Tridimensional Inventory** | Coolidge, Segal, Estey, & Neuzil, 2011 | (not given) | New version of the HCTI for ages **5 to 17** |

> **Trap.** The numbers are the MCQ: **CAD = 35 items, Cohen 1967**; **HCTI = 57 items, Coolidge et al. 2001**; **Tridimensional Inventory = ages 5 to 17, 2011**. And the correlations go **aggression and detachment → psychoticism**, **compliance → neuroticism**; examiners reverse them.

## 5.9 Research on Horney's Theory

### The case study method and its limits

Horney's own research method was the **case study**, so her approach, her data and her interpretations are open to the same criticisms Schultz has already made of Freud, Jung and Adler. The case study's built-in weaknesses apply to her just as much: the data are not systematically recorded, cannot be replicated, come from a small and unrepresentative group of patients, and are interpreted by the same person who collected them.

One specific problem makes this worse. Horney **objected to taking verbatim notes** during sessions. She doubted that an analyst could give a patient wholehearted, receptive attention while anxiously writing everything down (Horney, 1987, p. 30). As with Freud, Jung and Adler, then, there are **no complete records** of her analytic sessions or of the data she gathered in them.

In her defence, she **tried to be rigorous and scientific** in her clinical observations. She formulated hypotheses, tested them in the therapeutic situation, and maintained that her data were tested in the same way that scientists in other fields test theirs. Whether therapy sessions can count as tests in that sense is exactly what critics doubt.

Because Horney left no controlled studies, the research below was done by **later psychologists**, who operationalised her concepts and tested them.

### Neurotic Trends

#### Caspi, Elder and Bem: thirty years of follow-up

**Caspi, Elder and Bem (1987, 1988)** studied Horney's three trends after renaming them in behavioural terms:

- **Moving against people** became **ill-tempered**.
- **Moving away from people** became **shy**.
- **Moving toward people** became **dependent**.

They compared the behaviour of children of each type in **late childhood** with their behaviour **30 years later**, looking for continuities.

- **Ill-tempered children**, both **boys and girls**, tended to become **ill-tempered adults**. They were prone to **divorce** and to **downward occupational mobility**, that is, ending up in lower-status jobs than they might have been expected to hold.
- For **shyness** and **dependence**, outcomes **depended on sex**. Shy boys grew into **aloof** men whose **marriages and jobs were unstable**; shy girls grew up with **none of these difficulties**.
- Dependent boys turned into men who were **pleasant, at ease socially, warm and generous**, with **lasting marriages and steady careers**; dependent girls showed the **reverse** pattern (Caspi, Bem, & Elder, 1989).

What does this show? First, that Horney's three ways of relating to people are **stable** enough across three decades to predict adult life outcomes, which supports the idea that they are enduring features of personality. Second, that the **consequences** of a trend depend on the **culture's gender expectations**. In the period these participants grew up in, shyness was more acceptable in girls than in boys, so it cost the boys more. That gender-specific pattern is itself consistent with Horney's broader claim that cultural forces shape what a personality pattern means.

> **Trap.** Get the sexes right. **Ill-tempered: both sexes** carried it into adulthood (divorce, downward mobility). **Shy: problems for boys only.** **Dependent: good outcomes for boys, the opposite for girls.** The follow-up was **30 years**, from late childhood.

#### Moskowitz and Schwartzman: aggressive and withdrawn children

**Moskowitz and Schwartzman (1989)** looked at two of the trends, **moving against** (aggressive) and **moving away** (detached or withdrawn). They took measures of aggressive and withdrawn children aged **7 to 13** and compared them with the same children's behaviour **5 to 7 years later**. At follow-up, the highly **aggressive** children were doing **poorly at school** and showed **psychiatric problems**, while the **withdrawn** children held **distorted, unfavourable views of themselves**. Because the childhood classification forecast these later outcomes, the authors judged that Horney's types have **predictive value**.

#### The CAD and career choice

Research with the **CAD** found that **college students** preparing for careers in the **helping professions**, such as **nursing and social work**, scored **higher on compliance** than students considering careers in **business or science**. **Business students**, on a more competitive path, scored **higher on aggression**. **Science students** scored **highest on detachment**. These results fit Horney's descriptions of the three trends (Cohen, 1967; Rendon, 1987): people who need to move toward others choose work that involves caring for them; people who need to move against others choose competitive work; people who need to move away choose work centred on ideas and reason rather than relationships.

#### Aggression and road accidents in Iran

In **Iran**, high scorers on **aggression** turned out to have been involved in **significantly more car accidents** than low scorers, a result few would find surprising (Haghayegh & Oreyzia, 2009).

| Study | Participants and design | Main finding |
|---|---|---|
| Caspi, Elder & Bem (1987, 1988); Caspi, Bem & Elder (1989) | Ill-tempered, shy and dependent children; late childhood vs **30 years later** | Ill-tempered (both sexes) → ill-tempered adults, divorce, downward mobility; shy boys → aloof, unstable; shy girls fine; dependent boys → warm, stable; dependent girls opposite |
| Moskowitz & Schwartzman (1989) | Aggressive and withdrawn children aged **7 to 13**; follow-up **5 to 7 years** later | Aggressive → low school achievement, psychiatric problems; withdrawn → inaccurate, negative self-images; types predictive |
| Cohen (1967); Rendon (1987) | College students' CAD scores by intended career | Helping professions highest compliance; business higher aggression; science highest detachment |
| Haghayegh & Oreyzia (2009) | Adults in Iran | High aggression → significantly more car accidents |

### Feminine Psychology

Some research bears **indirectly** on Horney's feminine psychology, and it cuts both ways. In the Freud chapter, Schultz described a classic **dream study** that **supported** Freud's concept of **penis envy** (**Hall & Van de Castle, 1965**). That study **fails to support** Horney's challenge to penis envy. On the other hand, research that **refutes** Freud's claims that women have **inadequately developed superegos** and **inferior body images** can be taken as **support** for Horney's views.

> **Trap.** The evidence on feminine psychology is **mixed**. **Hall and Van de Castle (1965)** count **against** Horney (they supported penis envy). Research refuting women's weak superegos and inferior body images counts **for** her. If asked which study fails to support Horney, the answer is Hall and Van de Castle.

### The Tyranny of the Shoulds

**Berg, Janoff-Bulman and Cotter (2001)** asked **college students** to recall **three things** they had done during the **week before** the study, and to say whether they had done each one because they felt they **should** or **ought** to, or because they had **genuinely wanted** to. Students who had done more things because they wanted to, rather than because they felt they should, scored **significantly higher on general life satisfaction** than those whose behaviour was driven mainly by what they believed they ought to do.

The link to Horney is straightforward. A life governed by "shoulds" is, in her terms, a life lived for the idealized self-image at the expense of the real self, and she predicted it would be unsatisfying. The study gives that prediction an ordinary, measurable form.

### Neurotic Competitiveness

Horney saw **neurotic competitiveness** as a major feature of contemporary culture, and defined it as an **indiscriminate need to win at all costs**. She compared the attitude of such a person to that of a **jockey** in a race, to whom the only thing that matters is whether he is ahead of the others (Horney, 1937, p. 189). The glossary defines neurotic competitiveness in the same words: an indiscriminate need to win at all costs.

Researchers built self-report inventories to measure it: the **Hyper-competitive Attitude Scale (HCA)** and the **Neurotic Competitiveness Inventory (NCI)** (Deneui, 2001; Ryckman, Thornton, & Butler, 1994). Items are statements about how winning makes one feel, for instance that beating others in competition increases one's sense of personal power, and respondents rate each on a **five-point scale** running from "never true of me" to "always true of me".

The findings:

- People scoring **high in competitiveness** were also **high in narcissism, neuroticism, authoritarianism, dogmatism and mistrust**, and **low in self-esteem and psychological health**.
- People who **deliberately avoided** competition showed **higher neuroticism** and a **lower drive to prove themselves** in competitive situations (Ryckman, Thornton, & Gold, 2009). Avoiding competition, in other words, is not the healthy alternative to hyper-competitiveness; it is another neurotic pattern.
- **Hyper-competitive men** were also found to be **hyper-masculine** or "macho", holding the belief that women are sex objects who deserve neither respect nor consideration.
- When **American** and **Dutch** college students were compared, the American students came out **more hyper-competitive**, which points to a **cultural** component in this trait (Dru, 2003; Ryckman, Hammer, Kaczor, & Gold, 1990; Ryckman, Thornton, & Butler, 1994; Ryckman, Thornton, Gold, & Burckle, 2002).

The book concludes that these findings support Horney's description of the neurotic competitive personality. The cross-cultural result also fits her wider claim that American culture in particular breeds this kind of competitiveness.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition cites the HCA and NCI together with **Deneui (2001)** and **Ryckman, Thornton and Butler (1994)** without saying which authors built which scale, so do not memorise a pairing from the book. What you should hold: **HCA and NCI** measure neurotic competitiveness; high scorers are **narcissistic, neurotic, authoritarian, dogmatic, mistrustful, low in self-esteem**; **Americans > Dutch**; and **avoiders of competition are also more neurotic**.

### Two Types of Competitiveness

Later researchers distinguished **two kinds** of competitiveness:

- **Competing to win (CW)**: competing in order to **dominate others**.
- **Competing to excel (CE)**: competing in order to **surpass one's own personal goals**.

Among **high school students**, **CE** was linked to **high self-esteem** and **low depression**. In general, **teenage boys** scored **higher on CW** than teenage girls. But **girls** who did score **high on CW** showed **more depression and loneliness** and had **fewer close friends** than girls low on this drive to win (Hibbard & Buhrmester, 2010).

This distinction refines Horney. Not all competitiveness is neurotic: competing against your own standard (CE) looks healthy, while competing to beat and dominate others (CW) is closer to her jockey, and it seems especially costly for girls, for whom it runs against cultural expectations.

### Highlights: Research on Horney's Ideas

The book's Highlights box condenses the research into two lists.

High scorers on the **aggressive** trend:

- tend to have weaker **school** performance;
- are at risk of **mental health** problems;
- choose **business** majors over the **helping professions** more often.

High scorers on **neurotic competitiveness** typically show:

- high **neuroticism**;
- **narcissism**;
- an **authoritarian** outlook;
- poor **self-esteem**.

## 5.10 Reflections on Horney's Theory

### Contributions of Horney's Theory

Schultz opens with a frank admission: Horney's contributions, though impressive, are **less well known and less recognised within psychology** than those of Freud, Jung and Adler. Yet her work attracted a **large public following**, partly because of her personal qualities. A former student, **Clemmens (1987, p. 108)**, remembered her as conveying a sense of wholeness and certainty, total dedication, and a conviction that her ideas were worth sharing with colleagues and students because knowing them would help people in need.

The same qualities show in her **books**, which are written in a style that people without professional analytic training can follow. Her theory has a **commonsense appeal**, and many readers find it fits their own personality or that of a relative or friend. Anyone who has known a clinging, people-pleasing friend, a bullying boss and a cold, self-sufficient loner has met her three trends.

Further contributions:

- Her ideas may be **more relevant to problems in present-day American culture** than those of Freud, Jung or Adler.
- Many personality researchers regard her **neurotic trends** as a valuable way of **categorising deviant behaviour**.
- Other psychologists have taken over four of her emphases: **self-esteem**, the childhood **need for safety and security**, **basic anxiety** as a root of disturbance, and the **idealized self-image**.
- She shaped the thinking of **later theorists**, notably **Erik Erikson** and **Abraham Maslow**. **Maslow** used her concepts of the **real self** and **self-realization**, and her **basic anxiety** resembles Erikson's **basic mistrust**.

To see the influence concretely: Maslow's idea of self-actualization carries Horney's self-realization, the inborn push to develop one's real potential, into humanistic psychology. Erikson's first crisis, **trust versus mistrust** in infancy, describes the same early experience of whether the world can be relied on that Horney called basic anxiety when it goes badly.

> **Trap.** Influence questions: **Maslow** took the **real self** and **self-realization**; **Erikson's basic mistrust** parallels **basic anxiety**. Do not reverse them (Erikson did not take self-realization; Maslow did not take basic mistrust).

### Criticisms of Horney's Theory

Horney was trained in orthodox Freudian theory, and she paid tribute to Freud for providing the foundation and the tools for her work. But her theory departed from psychoanalysis in several ways, and the criticisms reflect that.

1. **From the Freudians: too little biology, sex and unconscious.** Loyal Freudians saw the gaps at once: she **played down biological instincts**, and she gave **sexuality and the unconscious** far less weight than Freud did. This is a criticism from a particular standpoint; a non-Freudian might count the same features as strengths.
2. **Less complete and less consistent than Freud's.** Her theory has been criticised as **not as fully or consistently developed** as Freud's. Some argued that because Freud's model was so elegantly and precisely constructed, Horney would have done better to **reject it outright and start afresh** rather than try to rework it along different lines. The point is that a theory patched onto Freud's framework inherits its vocabulary (repression, unconscious, Oedipus) while denying its foundations, which makes it less tidy than either Freud's system or a fully new one.
3. **Too shaped by middle-class American culture.** Her observations and interpretations were said to be **too heavily influenced by the middle-class American culture** in which she developed much of her theory. Schultz adds a fair qualification: as the book shows throughout, **all** personality theorists are shaped by the class, culture and time in which they worked. The criticism is valid, but it is not unique to Horney.
4. **Methodological weaknesses.** From 5.9: her method was the **case study**, with the weaknesses that implies, and she kept **no verbatim records** of sessions.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a criticism of Horney?" Correct criticisms: underplaying **instincts, sexuality and the unconscious** (the Freudian complaint); **less complete and consistent** than Freud's theory; **too influenced by middle-class American culture**; **case-study data without verbatim records**. Not criticisms (distractors): "overemphasis on sexuality", "too pessimistic", "too biological", "ignored culture", "relied on laboratory experiments". Each of these is the opposite of what Horney did.

### Renewed Interest in Horney's Ideas?

Largely because of the **women's movement that began in the 1960s**, Horney's books drew renewed attention. Her writings on **feminine psychology and sexuality** may be her **most influential** contribution, still valued by scholars of women's role in society well over **50 years after her death** (see Gilman, 2001; Miletic, 2002). One biographer, **Quinn (1987, p. 211)**, judged that these papers alone would have earned Horney an important place in the history of psychoanalysis.

Her influence also lives on institutionally. The **Karen Horney Clinic** and the **Karen Horney Psychoanalytic Institute**, a training centre for analysts, both in **New York City**, carry on her work. The clinic, established in **1945**, continues to treat people with **mild to moderate neurotic problems** at **moderate cost** (Paul, 2010). A small but loyal group of followers continues her work, much of it published in the **American Journal of Psychoanalysis**, the journal she founded in 1941.

> **Trap.** Dates that look alike: **1941** American Journal of Psychoanalysis founded; **1945** Karen Horney Clinic established (and also the year of the three trends, Horney 1945); **1960s** women's movement revives interest; **1922** Berlin congress paper; **1926** flight from womanhood.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **One causal chain runs through the whole theory.** Parents' lack of genuine warmth (and the seven undermining behaviours) → **hostility** in the child → **repression** of hostility (helplessness, fear, love, guilt) → **basic anxiety** → four **self-protective mechanisms** → ten **neurotic needs** → three **neurotic trends** → **conflict** among the trends → an **idealized self-image** built to unify the divided personality → the **tyranny of the shoulds** and **externalization** as attempts to live with the gap. If you can draw this chain from memory, you can answer almost any question on the chapter.
2. **Social, not biological.** Every step is social. The safety need is satisfied or frustrated by parents; neurosis is avoidable; the Oedipus complex is a dependence-hostility conflict that is neither sexual nor universal; women's inferiority feelings come from discrimination; there are as many feminine psychologies as cultures. This is the line that separates Horney from Freud.
3. **Security, not pleasure.** Freud's people pursue pleasure and tension reduction driven by instincts. Horney's neurotics pursue **safety** and **reassurance**, and her healthy people pursue **self-realization**. The self-protective mechanisms are a defence against pain, not a search for happiness.
4. **Normal and neurotic differ in degree and flexibility, not in kind.** Everyone has the ten needs, the three trends, some conflict and an idealized self-image. The neurotic is the person for whom one need or trend has become compulsive, indiscriminate and rigid, and whose self-image is unrealistic and fixed.
5. **The life is the theory.** Second-born and less loved than Berndt; compliant till about 8, then ambitious; love as an anaesthetic for anxiety; dependent affairs; a failed Freudian analysis; lifelong self-analysis; Adler's compensation; a career in a man's profession; pleasure in childbirth. Each maps onto a concept (see the table at the end of 5.1).
6. **Where she sits among the theorists.** From **Freud** she kept childhood, repression, the unconscious, free association and dream analysis, and dropped instincts, libido, stages and the id-ego-superego structure. With **Adler** she shares the social emphasis, compensation for inferiority, and three types that parallel the getting, ruling and avoiding styles of life. Like **Jung** she trusted an inner drive towards wholeness (self-realization) and rejected the sexual Oedipus complex, though her reasons were social rather than mystical. She fed forward into **Erikson** (basic anxiety and basic mistrust) and **Maslow** (real self, self-realization), and her emphasis on the analyst's warmth anticipates later humanistic therapy.
7. **The research is post-Horney.** Her own data are case studies without verbatim records. The evidence comes from later inventories (CAD, HCTI, HCA, NCI) and longitudinal studies (Caspi et al.; Moskowitz & Schwartzman), which broadly support the trends, the tyranny of the shoulds and neurotic competitiveness, with mixed evidence on feminine psychology.

## Worked example

*Ms P., a 29-year-old bank officer, comes to a hospital outpatient clinic with low mood, tearfulness and sleeplessness after her fiancé broke off their engagement. In the first session she apologises repeatedly for "wasting your time", asks twice whether the psychologist is getting bored, and agrees with every suggestion before it is finished. She describes herself as "the person everyone can depend on". She is the elder of two children; her parents, she says, "always made it clear that my brother was the important one". Her father was strict and unpredictable, praising her one day and mocking her marks the next; her mother often told her how much she had given up for the children. As a child she was "the good girl who never answered back". At work she cannot refuse extra tasks, yet she was recently reprimanded for an angry outburst at a junior colleague, which shocked her. She says she "should be a perfect daughter, a perfect fiancée and the best officer in the branch", and that since the break-up she feels everyone at the bank is secretly laughing at her. She reports a recurring dream of shouting at her father while he walks away, in which she feels "furious, then terrified".*

**What frustrated the safety need?** Three of Horney's seven undermining behaviours are present: **obvious preference** for a sibling (the brother), **erratic behaviour** (praise and mockery unpredictably), and **ridicule** (of her marks). The mother's speeches about sacrifice suggest affection that was **not genuine**, which Horney said children can detect.

**Why did she not rebel?** At least three of the four reasons for repressing hostility apply. **Fear** of the unpredictable father; **love**, in the paradoxical sense, since the mother's sacrificial declarations were the only affection available and anger might cost even that; and probably **guilt**, being the "good girl" for whom answering back was wicked. The repressed hostility produced **basic anxiety**, a pervasive feeling of being alone and helpless in a hostile world.

**Which self-protective mechanisms and needs?** **Securing affection** and **being submissive**, hardened into the neurotic needs for **affection and approval** and for **a dominant partner** (the fiancé, on whom her security rested). Her inability to refuse extra work and her constant apologising are the compulsive, indiscriminate quality that makes a need neurotic.

**Which trend is dominant?** **Moving toward people**: the **compliant personality**. She sees others as superior, herself as weak, defers and takes blame, and is terrified by rejection; the broken engagement is exactly the rejection the compliant type fears most.

**What is the angry outburst?** The **repressed aggressive trend** (moving against people) pushing for expression. Horney held that beneath compliance lie defiance, vindictiveness and a wish to control. The outburst shocks her because it violates the dominant trend; this is **conflict** in Horney's sense, the basic incompatibility of the trends, felt with neurotic intensity.

**What is the "perfect daughter, fiancée and officer"?** An **idealized self-image** based on illusion, and the **tyranny of the shoulds** that comes with it. Each failure (the break-up, the reprimand) cracks the "house filled with dynamite", producing self-hatred.

**What is "everyone is laughing at me"?** **Externalization**: her own self-contempt, from the gap between the idealized and real self, is experienced as coming from colleagues.

**How would Horney assess and treat her?** With **constructive friendliness**, not analytic distance; the couch optional. In **free association** she would start from the **visible emotional reactions to the therapist** (the apologising, the fear of boring her), which reveal her attitude to people in general, and only later peel back towards the childhood layers. In **dream analysis** she would use no symbol dictionary but attend to the **feelings in the dream**: fury, then terror, the exact sequence of hostility followed by the fear that forces its repression. A researcher could add the **HCTI**, on which she would be expected to score high on compliance (which the HCTI research links to neuroticism).

**What should you not conclude?** Not that she has an unresolved sexual Oedipal attachment to her father: Horney would read the father-centred dream as a **dependence-versus-hostility** conflict, not a sexual one. Not that her compliance is genuine altruism. And not that she is doomed: Horney's optimism and free-will position imply that, with a relationship in which she feels safe and with self-understanding, she can integrate all three trends flexibly and move towards **self-realization**.

## MCQ traps

1. Horney lived **1885 to 1952**; born Karen **Danielsen** near **Hamburg**; second-born, envied brother **Berndt**; father a **50-year-old Norwegian ship's captain**, mother **17 years younger**.
2. Model, compliant child **until about 8** (submissive "until 8 or 9"), then ambitious and rebellious: the compliant-then-aggressive sequence of her own theory.
3. Decided at **12** to be a doctor; entered **Freiburg** medical school in **1906**, 6 years after the first woman; degree from **Berlin, 1913**; married **Oskar Horney**, three daughters, marriage of **17 years**.
4. Her Freudian analyst was **Karl Abraham** (handbag = female genitals); not Abraham **Maslow**, who borrowed her real self and self-realization.
5. **Erich Fromm**: 15 years younger, affair of 20 years, analysed her daughter Marianne; Horney taught in Chicago and New York **1932 to 1952** and founded the **American Journal of Psychoanalysis** in **1941**.
6. Safety need = **security and freedom from fear** (Horney, 1937); depends **entirely** on parental treatment; weaning, occasional beatings, even premature sexual experience can be survived if the child feels **loved**.
7. Seven undermining behaviours: sibling preference, unfair punishment, erratic behaviour, broken promises, ridicule, humiliation, isolation from peers.
8. Four reasons for repressing hostility: **helplessness, fear, love, guilt**; "I need you" = helplessness (also the dependence in her Oedipus reinterpretation).
9. Order: hostility → repression → **basic anxiety**; basic anxiety is not the first link.
10. Basic anxiety = "insidiously increasing, all-pervading" feeling of being **lonely and helpless in a hostile world** (Horney, 1937, p. 89); foundation of **all** neuroses.
11. **Four** self-protective mechanisms (affection, submissiveness, power, withdrawal) but **three** trends; affection and submission merge into moving toward; withdrawal is **psychological, not physical**.
12. Self-protective mechanisms seek **security, not happiness**, and can be **more compelling than sexual needs**; cost: an impoverished personality.
13. **Ten neurotic needs**, later grouped into **three neurotic trends** (Horney, **1945**).
14. Power cluster has **five** needs (power, exploitation, prestige, admiration, achievement); detached has three (self-sufficiency, **perfection**, narrow limits); compliant has two. Perfection is **detached**, not aggressive.
15. Needs become neurotic through **compulsive, intense, indiscriminate** pursuit as the **only** solution; they treat symptoms, not the cause (Hess & Hess, 2010).
16. **Toward = compliant, against = aggressive, away = detached.**
17. Compliant behaviour springs from **repressed hostility** (defiance, vindictiveness, wish to control).
18. The **aggressive** type is most likely to be career-successful, but work gives **no intrinsic satisfaction**; the **detached** type expects superiority to be recognised **automatically**.
19. Horney-Adler: compliant = **getting**, aggressive = **dominant/ruling**, detached = **avoiding**; no Horney match for Adler's socially useful type.
20. **Conflict** (Horney) = basic incompatibility of the three trends, the **core of neurosis**; present in everyone, more **intense** in neurotics, who keep one trend dominant and repress the other two.
21. **Both** normal and neurotic people have an idealized self-image (realistic and flexible vs illusory and rigid); the neurotic one is a **house filled with dynamite**.
22. Tyranny of the shoulds ends in **self-hatred** (Kerr, 1984); **externalization** projects the idealized-real conflict outward.
23. **1922**: first woman to present a feminine-psychology paper at an international psychoanalytic congress, **Berlin**, chaired by **Freud**.
24. **Womb envy**: men envy childbearing and **sublimate** it through work achievement (Gilman, 2001); based partly on her pleasure in childbirth.
25. Horney **accepted** that women felt inferior; she rejected the **biological** explanation.
26. **Flight from womanhood** = **1926**; denial of femininity, unconscious wish to be male, sexual inhibitions.
27. Horney's Oedipus complex: **dependence vs hostility**, **not sexual**, **not universal** (Horney, 1939).
28. Freud: "able but malicious" (**Blanton, 1971**); veiled jibe about a woman analyst's own penis envy (**Freud, 1940**); he never changed his view.
29. **Westkott (1986)**: modern women torn between love and work. **Loo (1998)**: Chinese woman obeyed father, husband, eldest son; **Confucius 551 to 479 B.C.**; woman = "inside person"; **yang male, yin female**.
30. Assessment: free association and dream analysis, modified; **constructive friendliness**; an "exquisitely cooperative enterprise"; couch optional.
31. Free association: **present first**, visible reactions to the analyst, **onion peeling**. Dreams: **no universal symbols**; feelings in the dream are the safest clue (Horney, 1987).
32. **CAD**: 35 items, **Cohen 1967**; **HCTI**: 57 items, **Coolidge et al. 2001**; Tridimensional Inventory: ages **5 to 17**, **2011**.
33. HCTI: men higher **aggressive and detached**, women higher **compliant**; aggression and detachment → **psychoticism**, compliance → **neuroticism**.
34. **Caspi, Elder & Bem**: ill-tempered, shy, dependent; **30 years**; shy girls fine, dependent girls the opposite of dependent boys.
35. **Moskowitz & Schwartzman (1989)**: ages **7 to 13**, follow-up **5 to 7** years; aggressive → low achievement and psychiatric problems; withdrawn → negative self-images.
36. CAD careers: helping = compliant, business = aggressive, science = detached; **Iran** (Haghayegh & Oreyzia, 2009): aggression → car accidents.
37. **Hall & Van de Castle (1965)** fails to support Horney (it supported penis envy).
38. **Berg, Janoff-Bulman & Cotter (2001)**: three things in the past week; "want to" beats "should" for life satisfaction.
39. Neurotic competitiveness = **win at all costs**, the **jockey** (Horney, 1937); HCA and NCI; five-point scale; **Americans > Dutch**; competition avoiders also more neurotic.
40. **CW** (win, dominate) vs **CE** (excel, personal goals); CE → high self-esteem, low depression; high-CW girls lonelier and more depressed (Hibbard & Buhrmester, 2010).
41. Influence: **Maslow** (real self, self-realization), **Erikson** (basic mistrust ≈ basic anxiety); **Karen Horney Clinic 1945**, mild to moderate neurosis at moderate cost.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Safety need** | The child's higher-level need for security and freedom from fear; its satisfaction depends entirely on how the parents treat the child |
| **Basic anxiety** | A gradually growing, pervasive feeling of being lonely and helpless in a hostile world; the foundation of all neurosis |
| **Hostility** (basic hostility, in other texts) | The child's resentment of parents whose behaviour undermines security; repressed out of helplessness, fear, love or guilt |
| **Basic evil** | The parents' lack of genuine warmth and affection, named in the chapter epigraph as the root of the problem |
| **Self-protective mechanisms** | The four childhood defences against basic anxiety: securing affection, being submissive, attaining power, withdrawing |
| **Neurotic needs** | Ten irrational defences against anxiety that become a permanent part of personality and steer behaviour |
| **Neurotic trends** | Three categories of attitude and behaviour towards self and others (toward, against, away) into which Horney later grouped the needs |
| **Compliant personality** | The pattern of moving toward people: compulsive need for affection, approval and a dominant partner, masking repressed hostility |
| **Aggressive personality** | The pattern of moving against people: domineering, controlling, driven to excel and win superiority |
| **Detached personality** | The pattern of moving away from people: emotional distance, self-sufficiency and an intense need for privacy |
| **Conflict** | In Horney's usage, the basic incompatibility of the three neurotic trends; the core of neurosis |
| **Idealized self-image** | A person's picture of the self: realistic and flexible in normal people, illusory, perfectionist and rigid in neurotics |
| **Real (true) self** | The self as it actually is, with its genuine potentials; the basis of self-realization when the self-image reflects it |
| **Self-realization** | The innate drive to develop and use one's potential to the full; the ultimate goal of life |
| **Tyranny of the shoulds** | Trying to live up to an unattainable idealized self-image by denying the true self and acting as one believes one should |
| **Externalization** | Defending against the gap between idealized and real self by experiencing the conflict as located in, and caused by, the outside world |
| **Feminine psychology** | Horney's revision of psychoanalysis to include the conflicts built into the traditional ideal of womanhood and women's roles |
| **Womb envy** | A male's envy of the female capacity to bear children; Horney's reply to penis envy |
| **Flight from womanhood** | A woman's denial of her femininity and unconscious wish to be male, rooted in culturally produced inferiority; can lead to sexual inhibition (1926) |
| **Masculine protest** | Adler's term for compensating for inferiority felt as weakness or femininity; Horney applied it to her own choice of medicine |
| **Self-analysis** | Understanding and changing oneself without an analyst; Horney's lifelong practice and the title of her 1942 book |
| **Constructive friendliness** | Horney's warm, cooperative therapeutic attitude, in place of Freud's distant, passive stance |
| **Neurotic competitiveness** | An indiscriminate need to win at all costs |
| **CAD** | Cohen's (1967) 35-item self-report inventory of the Compliant, Aggressive and Detached trends |
| **HCTI** | Horney-Coolidge Type Indicator; a 57-item self-report inventory of the three trends (Coolidge et al., 2001) |
| **Horney-Coolidge Tridimensional Inventory** | Newer version of the HCTI for children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 (2011) |
| **HCA / NCI** | Hyper-competitive Attitude Scale and Neurotic Competitiveness Inventory; self-report measures of neurotic competitiveness |
| **Competing to win (CW)** | Competing in order to dominate others; linked to depression and loneliness in high-scoring girls |
| **Competing to excel (CE)** | Competing in order to surpass one's own goals; linked to high self-esteem and low depression |
| **Yin / yang** | Traditional Chinese female (dark, weak, passive) and male (vital, positive, strong, active) elements, later ranked as a gender hierarchy |

## Likely exam points

2. Her analysis with **Karl Abraham**, its failure, and her turn to lifelong **self-analysis**.
3. **Adler's influence**: compensation, unattractiveness, masculine protest; parallel of the three types with getting, ruling and avoiding.
4. American vs German patients as the origin of her **social-cultural** emphasis.
