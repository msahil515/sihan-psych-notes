# Chapter 12: B. F. Skinner: Reinforcement Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** Every theorist in the first eleven chapters went looking for personality somewhere inside the person. Freud found it in instincts and repressed wishes, Jung in a collective unconscious, Adler in inferiority feelings and striving, Horney in basic anxiety, Erikson in an ego working through crises, Allport, Cattell and Eysenck in traits, Maslow and Rogers in needs and a push towards self-actualisation, Kelly in personal constructs. With Chapter 12 the book opens a new part, **the behavioural approach**, and the first thing to understand is that this approach refuses the whole search. It does not offer a different picture of the inside of the person; it says the inside is the wrong place to look.

The part opener traces the idea to **John B. Watson**, the founder of **behaviourism**. Watson's psychology dealt with nothing except **overt behaviour**, meaning how people respond to stimuli in the world around them. It modelled itself on the natural sciences: tightly controlled experiments, with the stimulus and the response each measured as precisely as possible. That programme became enormously popular in the 1920s and stayed a dominant force in psychology for **more than 60 years**. Watson excluded conscious and unconscious forces alike, for a methodological reason rather than a metaphysical one. Whatever goes on inside an organism, human or animal, in the gap between the stimulus arriving and the response coming out cannot be seen, manipulated or measured in a laboratory, and what cannot be experimented on cannot be part of a science. Classic background: Watson launched the movement with his 1913 paper "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It".

Follow that reasoning through and the familiar vocabulary of personality disappears. A behaviourist chapter contains no anxiety, no drives, no motives, no needs and no defence mechanisms, because every one of those is an internal process inferred rather than observed. What is left is a very lean definition: **personality is an accumulation of learned responses to stimuli**, and the word refers only to what can be objectively observed and manipulated. **B. F. Skinner** represents this approach in the book. He carried Watson's position forward, dismissed any supposed internal force or process as irrelevant, and concerned himself solely with overt behaviour and the external stimuli that shape it. He worked out his ideas on rats and pigeons in the laboratory, not on patients in a clinic, and yet, in one of the ironies of the history of psychology, those ideas became some of the most clinically useful in the book through **behaviour modification**. The epigraph Schultz chose sums up the attitude: "It is the environment which must be changed."

So the chapter is about one big idea and its consequences. **Behaviour is controlled by its consequences.** What follows a response (Skinner's word is **reinforcement**) decides whether that response becomes more or less likely. Everything else in the chapter, the Skinner box, the four schedules of reinforcement, shaping, superstitious behaviour, self-control, token economies, the clinical and industrial programmes, and Skinner's view of free will, is that one idea worked out in a different setting.

**How to read this chapter.** Module 12.1 states Skinner's stance (the "empty organism") and why he studied animals. Module 12.2 is the life, and the life explains a lot: a rule-bound childhood, a boy who built machines and trained animals, a failed novelist whose "dark year" ended when he decided to study behaviour scientifically. Module 12.3 is the vocabulary everything else depends on: **respondent versus operant** behaviour, conditioning, reinforcement and extinction. Module 12.4 puts a rat in the Skinner box and arrives at Skinner's definition of personality. Module 12.5, the four **schedules of reinforcement**, is the most heavily examined module; learn each schedule with its rule, its effect on responding and the book's everyday example. Modules 12.6 to 12.8 cover shaping, superstitious behaviour and self-control. Module 12.9 is the applications module, and it contains the single most-missed distinction in the chapter: **negative reinforcement is not punishment**. Modules 12.10 to 12.13 are the standard closing set: human nature, assessment (functional analysis), research (the single-subject method) and evaluation (including instinctive drift and the rise of cognitive psychology).

## 12.1 Rats, Pigeons, and an Empty Organism

Skinner did not give us a theory of personality that can be laid side by side with Freud's or Rogers' and compared point by point. Strictly speaking he offered no personality theory at all, and none of his research was specifically about personality. His aim was larger and plainer: to account for **all behaviour**, not just the part we call personality, in purely factual, descriptive terms. Psychologists, he insisted, must confine themselves to facts, meaning to what can be seen, manipulated and measured in the laboratory. In practice that meant attending only to the **overt responses** a subject makes. Psychology, on his definition, is the science of behaviour, of what an organism does.

That makes Skinner's work the **antithesis** of the psychoanalytic, trait, life-span, cognitive and humanistic approaches you have studied. It is not just that he studied different things. His **subject matter**, his **methods** and his **aims** all differed from theirs.

### Why Skinner rejected inner causes

Most personality theorists explain behaviour by looking inside the person. The causes, motives and drives that steer our development and our actions are, on their accounts, located within each of us. Skinner made no appeal to internal or subjective states at all. Unconscious influences, defence mechanisms, traits and other inner driving forces cannot be observed, he argued, and so they have no place in a scientific psychology. In his eyes they had no more scientific standing than the old theological idea of the **soul**.

It is important to read this precisely. Skinner **did not deny that internal forces exist**. He denied that they are **useful for science**. A useful way to see his point is to imagine a student who fails to submit her assignments. A trait theorist says she is "low in conscientiousness"; a psychoanalyst might speak of unconscious resistance to authority. Skinner would ask how we know she is low in conscientiousness, and the answer is: because she fails to submit assignments. The "inner cause" is simply a renaming of the behaviour it is supposed to explain, so it adds nothing. What does add something is finding out what happens just before and just after the behaviour: what she gets (time with friends, escape from a dreaded task) when she does not submit, and what she gets when she does.

He applied the same reasoning to **physiological** processes. These are not overtly observable either, so they too were set aside as irrelevant to a science of behaviour. In a frequently quoted remark he said that the inside of the organism is irrelevant whether one thinks of it as the site of physiological processes or as the home of mental activity (quoted in Evans, 1968, p. 22). He saw no need to look inside for some form of inner activity. Human beings, for Skinner, are **"empty organisms"**: not literally empty, but containing nothing that can explain behaviour in scientific terms.

> **Trap.** Two easy-to-miss distinctions. (1) Skinner rejected inner causes as **scientifically useless**, not as **non-existent**; an option saying "Skinner denied that thoughts and feelings exist" is wrong. (2) The empty-organism stance covers **physiology as well as mind**. Skinner is not a biological reductionist who swaps the unconscious for the brain; he sets both aside and looks only at stimuli, responses and consequences. Classic background: Skinner called his position **radical behaviourism** (the 11th edition uses the phrase "radical behaviorist position" in its evaluation). Radical behaviourists treat private events such as thinking as behaviour that happens to be observable only to one person, never as the cause of public behaviour.

> **Trap.** "Skinner's theory of personality" is a phrase you will meet in exam papers, but Schultz is explicit that Skinner did **not** offer a personality theory and that his research did not deal specifically with personality. He offered an account of behaviour in general; personality, for him, is simply a name for a person's pattern of behaviour (module 12.4).

### Where are the people?

Skinner also parted company with the other theorists over **whom** he studied. Freud, Horney and the other clinicians built their theories from emotionally disturbed patients; others, such as Allport, worked mainly with normal or average adults; Maslow went further and based his theory on the healthiest, most accomplished people he could find. Skinner's behavioural approach, by contrast, was built from research on **rats and pigeons**, even though its ideas were later applied to people.

What could a pigeon possibly teach us about human personality? The answer lies in what Skinner was interested in. He was not interested in childhood memories or adult feelings; he was interested in **how organisms respond to stimuli**, and responding to stimuli is something animals do very well, sometimes better than people do. Skinner accepted that human behaviour is more complex than animal behaviour, but he argued that the difference is one of **degree, not of kind**: the fundamental processes are the same. And since every science must move from the simple to the complex, the more elementary processes should be studied first. Animal behaviour is simpler, so he started there.

> **Trap.** "Differences in degree, not in kind" is Skinner's justification for animal research, and examiners like it. A distractor such as "Skinner believed animal and human behaviour are governed by fundamentally different laws, so he studied animals only as a convenience" reverses his reasoning. His case for animals rests on the claim that the laws are the **same**.

Despite this laboratory focus, the practical reach of his work on human behaviour has been very wide. **Behaviour therapy** techniques derived from his research are used in clinical settings with a range of conditions, including psychoses, intellectual disability (the book uses the older label "mental retardation") and autism. His behaviour-modification methods are also used extensively in schools, businesses, correctional institutions and hospitals. Module 12.9 walks through the main examples.

## 12.2 The Life of Skinner (1904 to 1990)

Skinner would have disliked the idea that his biography "explains" his theory, since that sounds like an appeal to inner causes. But the way Schultz tells the story fits his own framework rather well: a set of environmental histories (rules and their consequences, machines that behave predictably, animals that can be trained, a career that failed and one that was rewarded) that produced a man who saw people as lawful systems. Read the life with that lens, and tie each event to the idea it fed. Classic background: his full name was **Burrhus Frederic Skinner**.

### A strict childhood

Skinner was born in **Susquehanna, Pennsylvania**, the elder of two sons; his younger brother died at the age of **16**. His parents were hard-working people who laid down clear rules of proper conduct and kept laying them down. Skinner summed up his upbringing by saying he was taught to fear God, the police, and what other people would think (Skinner, 1967, p. 407). His mother held to her strict standards without exception, and her method of control was a disapproving "tut tut".

The rest of the family joined in. His grandmother made sure he grasped the punishments of Hell by pointing to the glowing coals in the parlour stove. His father contributed lessons on the fate of criminals: he showed the boy the county jail and took him to a lecture describing life in a notorious New York state prison.

Skinner's autobiography returns again and again to the effect of these childhood warnings on his adult conduct. One example he gives: visiting a cathedral as a grown man, he was careful not to step on the gravestones set into the floor, because as a child he had been told over and over that walking on them was improper. The rule had long outlived the reasons for it, and it still controlled his feet.

From experiences like these Skinner drew a conclusion about himself that became a conclusion about everyone: his adult behaviour had been determined by the rewards and punishments, the **reinforcements**, he had received as a child. His later description of people as complex systems behaving in lawful ways (Skinner, 1971, p. 202) is, in Schultz's reading, a direct reflection of his own early life.

> **Trap.** The childhood details are examined as links, not as trivia. The "tut tut", the stove coals, the jail visit and the cathedral gravestones all point to one idea: **behaviour is shaped by its consequences and by rules backed by consequences**. If a question asks which early experience prefigured Skinner's emphasis on reinforcement and control, choose the strict, rule-and-consequence upbringing, not (for example) his brother's death, which the book does not link to the theory.

### Machines and animals

Two childhood interests foreshadow the rest of his career. The first was **machines**. As a boy he spent hours building mechanical devices: wagons, see-saws, merry-go-rounds, catapults, model aeroplanes, and even a steam cannon that fired plugs of potato and carrot over the neighbours' houses. He also laboured over a perpetual-motion machine, which, as Schultz drily notes, failed perpetually. A boy who loves machines learns that if you know how a device is built and what goes into it, you can predict exactly what it will do. Skinner later treated people in much the same way, as systems whose output follows lawfully from their inputs.

The second interest was **animals**. He kept turtles, snakes, toads, lizards and chipmunks as pets. At a county fair he was captivated by a troupe of performing pigeons that raced across the stage, hauled a toy fire engine up to a "burning" building and pushed a ladder against its wall, after which one pigeon in a fireman's hat climbed up to an upper window and "rescued" another pigeon stranded there. The adult Skinner went on to do the same kind of thing as a scientist: he trained pigeons to play **Ping-Pong** and to **guide a missile** to its target, taught his daughters' cat to play the piano, and taught the family beagle to play hide and seek. Classic background: the missile work was a Second World War project (often called **Project Pigeon**) in which pigeons pecking at a target image were meant to steer a guided bomb; it was never used in combat.

### A dark year

Skinner did not set out to be a psychologist. He majored in **English** at **Hamilton College** in upstate New York and graduated expecting to become a **novelist**. He set up a study in the attic of his parents' home in **Scranton, Pennsylvania**, and sat down to write. The result was a disaster. He read, listened to the radio, played the piano and built model ships while he waited for inspiration, and inspiration never arrived. He thought about consulting a psychiatrist, but his father told him it would be a waste of money. At **22** he was a failure at the only thing he had wanted to do.

Skinner later called this period his **dark year**, and Schultz points out that it is what **Erikson** (Chapter 6) would call an **identity crisis**. During college he had carefully built an occupational identity as a writer; when it collapsed it took his sense of self-worth with it. He moved from Scranton to **Greenwich Village** in New York City, but he could not write there either. Worse, by his own account, several women rejected his declarations of love, and he was so distressed that he **branded one woman's initial on his arm**, where it stayed for years (Skinner, 1983).

> **Trap.** The "dark year" is the year **after college**, spent trying and failing to write fiction, at about age **22**. It is not the later **period of depression** in his **40s** (next subsection), which he resolved by writing *Walden Two*. Examiners pair these two low points precisely because students merge them. Note also the cross-chapter link: Schultz explicitly labels the dark year an Eriksonian identity crisis, and the "new identity" that ended it is the behaviourist one.

### A new identity

Just when he seemed to have run out of hope, Skinner found an identity that suited him and that he kept for the rest of his life. His reasoning is revealing. He decided that writing had failed **him** (not that he had failed at writing), and that he would study human behaviour by the methods of **science** instead of the methods of **fiction**. He read **Pavlov** and **Watson** and committed himself to behaviourism, both as a set of ideas and as a way of living. For the time being, at least, his self-image and identity were secure.

He entered **Harvard in 1928** to study psychology, although he had never taken a single psychology course, and he completed his **Ph.D. in three years**. Choosing behaviourism meant turning his back on everything he had tried to draw on as a writer: feelings, emotions, inspiration. A historian of psychology, Mindess (1988), spelled out the contrast. A career in poetry and fiction depends on processes inside the psyche, such as inspiration, intuition, free association, the stream of consciousness, the unconscious, fantasy and feeling. A career promoting behaviourism dismisses that whole inner domain as "pre-scientific" (Skinner's favourite word for it) and attends instead to observable behaviour and to the operations needed to record, predict and control it. Schultz concludes that in Skinner's work psychic processes appear **only as objects of derision**.

Put the dark year and the new identity together and you have one of the cleanest life-to-theory links in the book. The man who could not make inspiration come by waiting for it built a psychology in which inspiration, and every other inner state, plays no explanatory part, and in which what matters is what you can arrange from the outside.

### A period of depression

Supported by postdoctoral fellowships, Skinner stayed at Harvard until **1936**. He then taught at the **University of Minnesota** and **Indiana University**, and returned to **Harvard in 1947**. In his **40s** he went through a period of **depression**, and he resolved it by returning to his failed identity as a writer. He poured his emotional and intellectual discontent into the main character of a novel, **_Walden Two_** (Skinner, 1948), letting that character voice his personal and professional frustrations.

The book is still in print and has sold **more than two million copies**. It describes a **utopian community** in which every aspect of life is governed by **positive reinforcement**, the basic principle of Skinner's whole system (Altus & Morris, 2009). Adams (2012) has suggested that *Walden Two* anticipates major themes of the more recent **positive psychology**, which Schultz covers in Chapter 14.

> **Trap.** *Walden Two* is a **novel** (1948) about a society run on **positive reinforcement**, not on punishment or coercion. It is not Skinner's first scientific book (that is *The Behavior of Organisms*, 1938) and not his controversial non-fiction argument about freedom (that is *Beyond Freedom and Dignity*, 1971). Classic background: the title echoes Thoreau's *Walden*.

### Quirky, conscientious, and somewhat neurotic

Early in his career Skinner agreed to be tested with both the **Rorschach** and the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)**, the two classic projective techniques. In **2012** a group of psychologists in **Norway** examined those old protocols and drew conclusions about his personality (Gronerod, Overskeid, & Hartmann, 2013; Koren, 2013; Overskeid, Gronnerod, & Simonton, 2012). There is an irony worth noticing: the man who rejected projective tests as unscientific had his personality assessed by them decades later. They rated him:

- **high in conscientiousness** and **high in openness to experience**,
- **somewhat extraverted**,
- **neurotic**,
- **quirky** and **driven** in his work, with **strained social relationships** and a **lack of liveliness**.

The family view was warmer. One of his daughters described him as a wonderful father who spent a great deal of time with his children (Freeman, 2013).

Well into his **80s** Skinner kept working with enthusiasm and discipline. He managed his own habits as he would manage a laboratory animal's, recording his daily work output and calculating the average time he spent per published word (**2 minutes**). He had become a living example of his own definition of human beings as complex systems behaving in lawful ways. He once told a friend that he was cited in the psychology literature more often than Freud; asked whether that had been his aim, he replied, "I thought I might make it" (quoted in Bjork, 1993, p. 214).

Classic background: the **air crib** (also called the **baby tender**) is a famous episode the 11th edition does not mention. In the mid-1940s Skinner built an enclosed, glass-fronted, temperature- and humidity-controlled crib for his younger daughter, Deborah, and described it in a popular magazine article. Persistent rumours later claimed she had been raised "in a Skinner box" and harmed by it; these are false, and the crib was not an operant-conditioning experiment. If an exam asks, the air crib is an example of Skinner's habit of redesigning the **environment** to improve behaviour and comfort, the same habit that produced *Walden Two*.

### How the life feeds the theory

| Event in Skinner's life | Idea it fed |
|---|---|

## 12.3 Reinforcement: The Basis of Behaviour

Skinner's system is simple to state, even though it rests on thousands of hours of carefully controlled experiments. Its central claim is that **behaviour can be controlled by its consequences**, that is, by what happens after the behaviour. Skinner believed that a rat or a person could be trained to do almost anything, and that the kind of reinforcement following a behaviour is what determines whether that behaviour occurs. Follow the logic one step further and you reach the claim that made Skinner famous and controversial: **whoever controls the reinforcers can control behaviour**, in a society just as an experimenter does in a laboratory.

To make sense of that claim you need Skinner's basic division of behaviour into two kinds: **respondent** and **operant**.

### Respondent behaviour

**Respondent behaviour** is a response made to, or **elicited by**, a specific stimulus. The simplest examples are **reflexes**. Tap the tendon just below the kneecap and the lower leg jerks forward; the stimulus (the tap) comes first and the response (the jerk) follows automatically. Other examples are blinking when a puff of air hits the eye, the pupil narrowing in bright light, or salivating when food is placed in the mouth. Behaviour of this reflex kind is **unlearned**: it happens automatically and involuntarily, and nobody has to be trained or conditioned to produce it.

#### Conditioning

Above simple reflexes sits respondent behaviour that has been **learned**. This learning is called **conditioning**, and its essence is the **substitution of one stimulus for another**: a response that used to be elicited only by stimulus A comes to be elicited by stimulus B as well. The idea comes from the Russian physiologist **Ivan Pavlov**, working in the **early 1900s**. **John B. Watson** later took Pavlov's conditioning over as the **basic research method of behaviourism**.

Pavlov stumbled on the phenomenon while studying digestion in dogs. He noticed that his dogs salivated at stimuli that should have been neutral, such as the sound of their keeper's footsteps. Until then salivation had been produced by only one stimulus, the sight of food. Intrigued, he set out to study the effect systematically. He sounded a bell shortly before feeding a dog. To begin with, the dog salivated only when the food arrived, not when the bell rang, because the bell meant nothing to it. After the bell and the food had been paired a number of times, however, the dog began to salivate **at the sound of the bell**. It had been conditioned to respond to the bell. Its salivary response had shifted from the food to what had previously been a neutral stimulus.

Classic background: the standard Pavlovian labels, which Schultz does not use but exam papers do, are the **unconditioned stimulus** (UCS: the food), the **unconditioned response** (UCR: salivation to food), the **conditioned stimulus** (CS: the bell, once it has acquired its effect) and the **conditioned response** (CR: salivation to the bell). Other textbooks call this whole process **classical** or **Pavlovian conditioning**; Schultz calls it conditioning of respondent behaviour, and Skinner's own term for it was **respondent conditioning**. These are one process under three names.

A clinical example shows why this matters for personality. A child who was frightened during a hospital stay may later feel her heart race at the smell of antiseptic, even in a perfectly safe setting. The smell has been paired with fear; the fear response has been transferred to the smell. Nothing the child **does** produces this; the response is drawn out of her by the stimulus.

#### Reinforcement

Schultz uses Pavlov's experiment to make a point about **reinforcement**. In the book's account the dogs would not have learned to respond to the bell unless they had been rewarded, and here the reward was food. From such findings Pavlov formulated a basic law of learning: **a conditioned response cannot be established without reinforcement**. Reinforcing a response strengthens it and makes it more likely to be repeated. The glossary definition follows the same line: **reinforcement** is strengthening a response by adding a reward, which raises the probability that the response will occur again.

> **Trap.** In Pavlov's procedure the "reinforcement" (the food) arrives after the bell **whether or not the dog salivates**. Strictly, what makes classical conditioning work is the **pairing** of two stimuli; the reinforcement does not depend on the animal's response. In operant conditioning (below) the reinforcer arrives **only if** the animal makes the response. Schultz uses the word "reinforcement" for both, so an option stating "conditioning will not occur without reinforcement" is correct for this book; but an option saying "in classical conditioning the reward is contingent on the response" is wrong. Classic background: this difference in contingency is the standard way to separate the two kinds of conditioning.

#### Extinction

A conditioned response, once established, does not maintain itself. Take a dog that reliably salivates whenever the bell rings. Now the experimenter keeps ringing the bell but stops bringing the food. The dog hears the bell and nothing follows: no food, no reward. With each further unaccompanied ring, salivation becomes weaker and less frequent, until the dog does not salivate to the bell at all. This process is **extinction**: the response has been wiped out because the reinforcement that maintained it is no longer provided.

A large body of research shows that **the more reinforcement given during training, the more resistant the conditioned response is to extinction** (Shull & Grimes, 2006). Resistance only delays the end, though; sooner or later extinction occurs. Classic background: an extinguished response often reappears briefly after a rest period without any new training, which is called **spontaneous recovery**; it shows that extinction suppresses a response rather than erasing it.

> **Trap.** Extinction is not forgetting and is not punishment. **Extinction** = the response weakens because reinforcement is **withheld** (bell, no food). **Forgetting** = weakening through disuse with no trials at all. **Punishment** (module 12.9) = an aversive stimulus is **applied** after the response. In clinical work, deliberately ignoring a child's tantrums is an extinction procedure, not a punishment.

### Operant behaviour

Respondent behaviour, then, depends on reinforcement and is tied directly to a physical stimulus: each response is elicited by a particular stimulus. For Skinner it was the **less important** of the two kinds. We are conditioned to react to many stimuli in our surroundings, but a great deal of behaviour cannot be explained that way. Much of what people do seems spontaneous and cannot be traced back to any particular stimulus. Such behaviour is **emitted** rather than elicited. It looks like **acting**, in a way that seems voluntary, rather than **reacting** involuntarily to a stimulus one has been conditioned to.

This is **operant behaviour**: behaviour that is emitted spontaneously or voluntarily and that **operates on the environment** to change it. The name is the definition. Two features set it apart from respondent behaviour, and both are examined.

1. **What controls it.** The **nature and frequency** of operant behaviour are determined or modified by the **reinforcement that follows** it. The controlling event comes **after** the response, not before.
2. **What it does to the world.** Respondent behaviour has **no effect on the environment**. In Pavlov's experiment the dog's salivation changed nothing: the bell still rang and the food still came. Operant behaviour, by contrast, **acts on the environment and changes it**.

A child in a classroom illustrates both. When she raises her hand, nothing in the room "elicited" that particular movement; she emitted it. If the teacher calls on her and praises the answer, the environment has changed as a result of her act (she now has attention and approval), and that consequence makes hand-raising more likely next time. If the teacher ignores her every time, hand-raising will gradually extinguish. Compare her eye-blink when a book is slammed on the desk: elicited, involuntary, and it changes nothing around her.

| | Respondent behaviour | Operant behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| How it is produced | **Elicited** by a specific prior stimulus | **Emitted** spontaneously; no identifiable eliciting stimulus |
| Voluntary or involuntary | Involuntary, automatic (reflex-like) | Appears voluntary |
| What controls it | The stimulus that comes **before** | The reinforcement that comes **after** |
| Effect on the environment | None | Operates on the environment and changes it |
| Learning procedure | Conditioning: substitution of one stimulus for another (Pavlov; adopted by Watson) | Operant conditioning (Skinner) |
| Example | Knee jerk; dog salivating to a bell | Rat pressing a lever for food; a child raising her hand to be called on |
| Importance for Skinner | Less important | The basis of most human and animal behaviour |

> **Trap.** Watson versus Skinner. Both rejected inner causes, and Schultz presents Skinner as continuing Watson's approach. But **Watson** built behaviourism on **Pavlov's** stimulus-substitution conditioning, in which a prior stimulus elicits a response. **Skinner** made **operant** behaviour the centre, in which a response is emitted and then strengthened or weakened by its **consequences**. An option saying "Skinner's main contribution was to extend Pavlov's classical conditioning to humans" describes Watson's programme, not Skinner's. Classic background: the idea that consequences stamp in responses goes back to E. L. Thorndike's **law of effect** (around 1900), from his studies of cats escaping from puzzle boxes; Skinner's apparatus and his concept of reinforcement developed that tradition.

Classic background: textbooks of learning add a vocabulary of **kinds of reinforcers** that the 11th edition of Schultz does not use but that Indian papers often test. A **primary reinforcer** satisfies a biological need without any learning (food for a hungry rat, water for a thirsty one). A **secondary** or **conditioned reinforcer** is a stimulus that has acquired reinforcing power by being paired with a primary reinforcer (the click that precedes the food pellet, a parent's smile, praise). A **generalised reinforcer** is a conditioned reinforcer that has been paired with many different primary reinforcers, so that it works whatever the person's current state of deprivation; **money**, **attention**, **approval** and the **tokens** of a token economy (module 12.9) are the standard examples. Keep the distinction from **positive versus negative** reinforcement, which is about **adding versus removing** a stimulus and is covered with punishment in module 12.9.

## 12.4 Operant Conditioning and the Skinner Box

The clearest way to understand **operant conditioning** is to watch it happen. Skinner's apparatus for this is the **operant-conditioning apparatus**, better known as the **Skinner box**: a small enclosure with a lever (or bar) on one wall and a mechanism that drops a food pellet into a tray when the lever is pressed.

The photograph shows how bare the arrangement is. A single rat stands on its hind legs on a floor of metal bars inside a small metal-framed box, its forepaws raised against the side wall where the response mechanism is mounted. There is nothing else to see, and that is the point: the box strips the environment down to one response that can be counted (pressing) and one consequence the experimenter controls (food). Classic background: many operant chambers use a barred floor of this kind because it can deliver a mild electric shock in escape and avoidance experiments, and the modern laboratory name for the whole device is the **operant chamber**.

### Following the rat through the box

Follow one rat through the process step by step.

1. **Random, emitted behaviour.** A food-deprived rat is placed in the box. At first its behaviour is spontaneous and unsystematic: it moves about, sniffs, pokes its nose into corners, explores. None of this is elicited by any particular stimulus. The rat is emitting behaviour, not reacting.
2. **The first lever press.** At some point in this random activity the rat happens to press the lever on the wall, and a pellet of food drops into the tray. The rat's behaviour has **operated on the environment** and changed it: the environment now contains food. The food is a **reinforcer** for lever-pressing.
3. **The rate rises.** The rat begins to press the lever more often. Each press brings more food, which means more reinforcement, so it presses more often still. Its behaviour is now **under the control of the reinforcers**. It is no longer random: the rat spends most of its time pressing and eating.
4. **Prediction and control.** Put the rat back in the box the next day and we can **predict** what it will do. We can also **control** its lever-pressing by presenting the reinforcers, withholding them, or delivering them at a different rate.
5. **Extinction.** If food stops coming, lever-pressing extinguishes, just as respondent behaviour does when its reinforcement is withdrawn. A behaviour that no longer works, in the sense that it no longer brings a reward, eventually stops.

The conclusion Skinner drew is the theme of the whole chapter: **the person who controls the reinforcers controls the subject's behaviour**. The glossary definition of **operant conditioning** makes the mechanism explicit: it is the procedure by which **changing the consequences of a response changes the rate at which that response occurs**. Notice the dependent variable. Skinner measured **response rate**, how often the behaviour occurs per unit time; he did not ask how the rat felt or what it expected.

### From the Skinner box to the real world

Operant conditioning is not a laboratory curiosity. A wide range of animals have been trained to perform a great many behaviours this way (Gillaspy, Brinegar, & Bailey, 2014; Poling, Weetjens, Cox, Beyene, Bach, & Sully, 2010; Tomina & Takahata, 2010; Vaughan, de Passille, Stookey, & Rushen, 2014):

- **dogs** trained to sniff out drugs or explosives;
- **giant African rats** in **Mozambique** trained to detect buried **land mines**;
- **lobsters** taught to grasp a bar with their claws to obtain food;
- **whales and dolphins** taught to perform tricks at marine parks such as SeaWorld;
- **calves** taught to urinate only in designated places.

> **Trap.** The land-mine rats are **giant African rats in Mozambique**, and the "even a lobster can be conditioned" finding appears in the chapter's Highlights box. Options that move the rats to another country or swap the lobster for a crab are the usual distortions.

### Operating on the environment

Skinner believed that **most human and animal behaviour is learned through operant conditioning**. Consider how babies learn. An infant at first produces a stream of random, spontaneous behaviours. Only some of them are reinforced by parents, siblings or other caregivers, with food, hugs, toys and the like. As the infant grows, the behaviours that were positively reinforced, those the parents approved of, persist; those the parents disapproved of are **extinguished** or dropped.

The rat in the box works on the same principle. Behaviours that work (pressing the lever brings food) are performed often; behaviours that do not work are not repeated. There is a two-way traffic here, and it is worth stating it carefully because it reappears in Skinner's view of free will (module 12.10): **the organism's behaviour operates on the environment, and the environment, in the form of reinforcement, operates on the organism's behaviour.**

The power of this two-way traffic is what impressed Skinner. In a 1953 book he compared operant conditioning to a sculptor shaping a lump of clay (Skinner, 1953, p. 91; classic background: the book is *Science and Human Behavior*). If the organism needs the reinforcer badly enough, there is almost no limit to how far its behaviour can be moulded, and Schultz's list of possible sculptors runs from the laboratory to the state: the experimenter holding a food pellet, the owner training a puppy with biscuits, the mother who smiles, the boss who pats an employee on the back, and the government that makes promises. The list is deliberately mixed, from laboratory to politics, because Skinner believed the same principle runs through all of them.

### Personality: a collection of operant behaviours

Here at last is Skinner's answer to the question this book asks of every theorist. From infancy onwards we emit a great many behaviours, and those that are reinforced grow stronger and settle into patterns. **Personality, for Skinner, is simply that pattern or collection of operant behaviours.** It is not a structure behind the behaviour; it is the behaviour.

The same move removes the mystery from abnormality. What other psychologists call **neurotic or abnormal behaviour** was, for Skinner, nothing more than the **continued performance of undesirable behaviours that have somehow been reinforced**. A clinical example makes the point concrete. A college student complains of "social anxiety" and avoids every class presentation. A psychoanalyst might look for an unconscious conflict over exposure. A Skinnerian looks at consequences: each time she skips a presentation, the dread she was feeling disappears, and that relief strengthens skipping (in module 12.9 you will see that this is **negative reinforcement**). Her friends may also offer sympathy and attention when she avoids, which adds positive reinforcement. The "anxious personality" is the pattern of avoidance that these consequences have built and are maintaining. Change the consequences and you change the pattern.

> **Trap.** For Skinner, neurotic behaviour has **no inner cause** such as conflict, anxiety or repressed material. It is undesirable behaviour that has been **reinforced**. A statement like "Skinner explained neurosis as the result of anxiety from unresolved conflicts" mixes him up with Freud or Horney.

Having shown that behaviour could be changed by **continuous reinforcement**, a reinforcer after every single response, Skinner turned to a new question: what happens to behaviour if the **rate** at which it is reinforced is varied? That question produced the schedules of reinforcement.

## 12.5 Schedules of Reinforcement

Outside the laboratory, Skinner pointed out, behaviour is almost never reinforced every time it happens. Schultz's examples make the point quickly: babies are not picked up and cuddled every time they cry; a baseball star does not hit a home run every time at bat; the person bagging groceries at the supermarket is not tipped for every bag; and your favourite band does not win a Grammy for every new song. Yet all of these behaviours persist even though they are rewarded only now and then.

The laboratory discovery that started this line of research was simple. Skinner noticed that his rats kept pressing the bar at a fairly steady rate even when not every press was reinforced. He therefore set out to test different **reinforcement schedules**, the patterns or rates at which reinforcers are provided or withheld, to see how well each one controls behaviour. He tested four.

The four schedules come from crossing two questions. **First, what does reinforcement depend on?** Either on the passage of **time** (an **interval** schedule) or on the **number of responses** (a **ratio** schedule). **Second, is the requirement constant?** Either it is the same every time (**fixed**) or it varies around an average (**variable**). Two questions with two answers each give four schedules. If you remember the two questions, you can reconstruct the whole module.

### Fixed interval

On a **fixed-interval (FI)** schedule, the reinforcer is delivered for the **first response made after a fixed period of time has passed**. The period might be 1 minute, 3 minutes or any other fixed length. The **number of responses** made during the interval makes no difference. Whether the rat presses 3 times or 20 times a minute, the food arrives only when the set time has elapsed **and** the correct response is then made.

Many real situations run on this schedule. A job that pays a salary **once a week or once a month** is a fixed-interval arrangement. You are not paid for the number of items you make or the number of sales you close (the number of responses); you are paid for the hours, days or weeks that pass.

Skinner's research produced three findings about this schedule:

1. **The shorter the interval, the higher the response rate.** As the interval between reinforcers lengthened, the rate of responding fell.
2. **How often reinforcers appeared also affected how quickly the response could be extinguished.** A rat that had been reinforced **continuously** stopped responding **sooner**, once reinforcement was cut off, than a rat that had been reinforced **intermittently**. (The source sentence for this point is garbled in the 11th edition, but its sense is clear and matches the standard finding described below.)
3. **It works in applied settings.** The fixed-interval schedule has proved highly effective in real-world situations, including **special education classrooms**, where it has been used to reduce **disruptive behaviour** (Tomlin & Reed, 2012).

Classic background: the second finding is known as the **partial reinforcement extinction effect**: behaviour reinforced only some of the time is more resistant to extinction than behaviour reinforced every time, because the switch to "no reinforcement at all" is harder for the organism to detect. Fixed-interval responding also has a characteristic shape when plotted cumulatively, the **FI scallop**: responding drops just after each reinforcer and accelerates as the next one becomes due (think of students who study little just after an exam and cram as the next one approaches).

### Fixed ratio

On a **fixed-ratio (FR)** schedule, the reinforcer comes only after a **set number of responses**. The experimenter might reinforce every 10th or every 20th press. Unlike the fixed-interval schedule, the delivery of reinforcement now **depends on how often the subject responds**: the rat gets nothing until it has made the required number of presses. The consequence is that this schedule produces a **faster rate of responding than the fixed-interval schedule**.

The higher rate holds for people too. Under **piece-rate** pay, what you earn depends on how much you produce: the more items, the more money. A salesperson working on **commission** is in the same position, since income depends on the number of products sold. Compare a salesperson on a **weekly salary**, who earns the same amount whatever she sells; she is on a fixed-interval schedule, and the piece-rate or commission worker, on a fixed-ratio schedule, has a much stronger reason to keep responding.

Classic background: fixed-ratio responding typically shows a brief **post-reinforcement pause** after each reinforcer, followed by a rapid, steady run of responses to the next one.

### Variable interval

Everyday life does not always offer such regular arrangements. Sometimes reinforcement comes on a variable basis. On a **variable-interval (VI)** schedule, the time that must pass before a response is reinforced keeps changing: the reinforcer might become available after 2 hours the first time, after 1 hour 30 minutes the next, and after 2 hours 15 minutes the third. Schultz's example is a day spent **fishing**. If the angler is rewarded at all, it is on a variable-interval basis, because the schedule is set by the random arrival of fish nibbling at the bait, not by how many times the line is cast.

Classic background: variable-interval schedules typically produce a **steady, moderate** rate of responding without pauses, since a reinforcer could become available at any moment. Repeatedly checking for a message you are expecting is a common illustration (not one from the book).

### Variable ratio

On a **variable-ratio (VR)** schedule, reinforcement depends on an **average number of responses** between reinforcers, but the actual number **varies widely** around that average. Skinner found that this schedule produces **high and stable response rates**, a fact, Schultz remarks, that the people who run gambling casinos can confirm with pleasure.

**Slot machines, roulette wheels, horse races and state lotteries** all pay off on a variable-ratio schedule, which makes it an extremely effective way to control behaviour. The next payout might come on the next play or after fifty more, and the only way to get it is to keep playing.

Two general conclusions close the module. First, **variable schedules produce enduring behaviour that resists extinction**. Second, **most everyday learning comes from variable-interval or variable-ratio schedules**, not from fixed ones. Skinner's schedule research shows that schedules can be used to control, modify and shape behaviour, whether you are in charge of rats, salespeople or assembly-line workers, or trying to train a pet or a child.

| Schedule | Reinforcement depends on | Fixed or variable | Effect on responding (book) | Book's everyday example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed interval (FI)** | First response after a set **time** | Fixed | Shorter interval gives higher rate; slower than FR | Salary paid weekly or monthly; used to reduce disruptive behaviour in special education classes |
| **Fixed ratio (FR)** | A set **number** of responses (e.g. every 10th or 20th) | Fixed | Faster responding than FI | Piece-rate pay; salesperson on commission |
| **Variable interval (VI)** | First response after a **time** that varies (e.g. 2 h, 1 h 30 min, 2 h 15 min) | Variable | Enduring, resistant to extinction | A day spent fishing |
| **Variable ratio (VR)** | An **average number** of responses, with wide variation | Variable | High and stable rates; enduring, resistant to extinction | Slot machines, roulette, horse races, state lotteries |

> **Trap.** Map the examples, not the words. **Salary** = fixed **interval** (time). **Commission / piece-rate** = fixed **ratio** (responses). **Fishing** = variable **interval**. **Gambling** = variable **ratio**. A common distractor calls a monthly salary "fixed ratio" because the amount is fixed; the schedule label refers to what the reward **depends on** (time), not to the size of the reward.

> **Trap.** "Which schedule is most resistant to extinction?" Schultz says **variable schedules** (both VI and VR) resist extinction and that VR gives **high and stable** rates. Classic background: the standard learning-textbook answer to "highest rate and most resistant to extinction" is **variable ratio**, and to "lowest overall rate" is **fixed interval**. **Continuous reinforcement** (every response reinforced) gives the **fastest learning** but the **fastest extinction**. Classic background: the definitive laboratory work is Ferster and Skinner's *Schedules of Reinforcement* (1957).

> **Trap.** Schultz's review question asks about a child who is allowed an ice-cream cone for good behaviour "only occasionally". The book does not print an answer. The reinforcement is irregular, so it is a **variable** schedule; it is **variable ratio** if the treat depends on an unpredictable number of good acts, and **variable interval** if it depends on unpredictable stretches of time. Read the stem for which of the two is being described.

## 12.6 The Shaping of Behaviour

In Skinner's original experiment the operant behaviour, pressing a lever, is something a laboratory rat will almost certainly do sooner or later while it wanders about exploring the box. The probability of that response is high, so an experimenter with enough patience simply waits for it and then reinforces it.

Most interesting behaviour is not like that. Animals and people perform many complex operant behaviours that would almost **never** occur by chance in the ordinary course of events. A pigeon will not spontaneously turn a full circle and then peck a small key; a toddler will not spontaneously produce a correctly pronounced sentence. If reinforcement can only strengthen a behaviour after it has occurred, how can an experimenter, or a parent, condition a behaviour that is unlikely ever to occur on its own?

### Successive approximation

Skinner's answer was the method of **successive approximation**, also called **shaping** (Skinner, 1953). The trick is not to wait for the final behaviour but to reinforce **anything that resembles it a little**, and then to keep raising the standard.

His demonstration used a pigeon that was to learn to peck at one particular spot in its cage. The chance that it would peck that exact spot on its own was low, so Skinner proceeded in stages:

1. At first the pigeon was given food whenever it merely **turned towards** the designated spot.
2. Next, food was withheld until the pigeon made **some movement**, however slight, **towards** the spot.
3. Then only movements that brought it **closer** to the spot were reinforced.
4. After that, the pigeon was reinforced only when it **thrust its head towards** the spot.
5. Finally, reinforcement came only when its **beak touched the spot**.

It sounds slow, but Skinner conditioned pigeons this way in **less than 3 minutes**.

The name of the method describes the procedure. The organism is reinforced as its behaviour comes, in **successive** (consecutive) stages, to **approximate** the final behaviour wanted. Two ingredients are essential. Each stage must be **reinforced** until it is reliable, and then the reinforcement for that stage must be **withdrawn** so that only a closer approximation pays off. Withholding reinforcement from the old version is exactly what pushes the organism to vary its behaviour, and the variations that come closer are the ones that get reinforced.

Skinner suggested that children learn the complex behaviour of **speaking** in just this way. Infants spontaneously produce meaningless sounds, and parents reinforce the babbling by smiling, laughing and talking back. After a while parents become selective, reacting more warmly to sounds that resemble real words. As the process continues, parental reinforcement is given only for correct use and proper pronunciation. Language in infancy is therefore acquired, on Skinner's account, by **differential reinforcement in stages**: reinforcing some versions of a behaviour and not others, with the standard rising over time. The glossary definition uses this very example: in successive approximation, behaviour such as learning to speak is reinforced only as it comes to approximate the final desired behaviour.

To see how you would use the method yourself, take one of the book's review questions: training a dog to walk in a circle. You would first reward the dog for turning its head to one side; once that is reliable, reward only a step in that direction; then only a quarter turn, then a half turn, then three-quarters, and finally only a complete circle. At each point you stop rewarding the old, easier version.

> **Trap.** Shaping is **not** the same as reinforcing the final behaviour more often. It is the **reinforcement of closer and closer approximations** to a target behaviour that the organism does not yet perform. If a stem describes reinforcing "small steps towards" a goal, the answer is shaping (successive approximation), not a schedule of reinforcement. Classic background: clinicians use the same procedure to build speech in non-verbal children with autism and to rebuild self-care in long-stay patients, and it is the logic behind breaking a task into graded steps.

### Shaping the behaviour of a person in five minutes

Skinner once shaped the behaviour of a well-known psychoanalyst, **Erich Fromm**, whose remarks during a lecture irritated him. In Skinner's own account (Skinner, 1983, pp. 150-151), Fromm seemed to have an opinion on almost everything, and when he began arguing that **people are not pigeons**, Skinner decided to act. He passed a note to a colleague announcing that he would shape a chopping motion of Fromm's **left hand**. Fromm gestured a great deal as he spoke. Each time his left hand rose, Skinner looked straight at him; each time he brought it down, Skinner nodded and smiled. Within **five minutes**, by Skinner's account, Fromm was chopping the air so energetically that his wristwatch kept sliding down over his hand.

The anecdote is a shaping demonstration, and it is worth noticing which reinforcers Skinner used: attention, a nod and a smile, the social reinforcers that every speaker responds to. It is also a pointed joke, since Fromm was being shaped like a pigeon at the very moment he was arguing that people are not pigeons, and he was apparently unaware of it. That last point matters for the theory: the behaviour changed **without the person knowing why**, which is just what Skinner would expect if behaviour is controlled from outside rather than by conscious choice.

> **Trap.** The person Skinner shaped was **Erich Fromm**, a psychoanalyst, and the behaviour was a **chopping motion of the left hand**, achieved in about **five minutes** using looks, nods and smiles. Do not confuse this with the pigeon demonstration (less than **3 minutes**, pecking a spot).

## 12.7 Superstitious Behaviour

Life outside the laboratory is not as tidy as a Skinner box. Sometimes we are reinforced **by accident** just after we have done something. That behaviour did not produce the reinforcement and played no part in causing it, but because the two happened together, the behaviour may be repeated in similar situations.

Schultz gives two examples from American football. An offensive lineman for the **Tampa Bay Buccaneers** was having an awful season early in his career. He asked his roommate to swap beds so that he could sleep nearer the bathroom, and straight away his play improved. For the rest of his career he insisted on the bed closest to the bathroom door in every motel the team stayed in. The second example is an **NFL kicker** who hugged the goal posts before every game. He had done it once just before a successful kick, and because it had "worked" he kept doing it; he told a reporter that he wanted the posts to know he loved them and to beg them to stay still when he kicked.

You will recognise the pattern closer to home: the student who once topped a test while using a particular pen and now carries the same "lucky" pen to every exam, or the cricket fan who will not change seats while the team is batting well. In each case one chance pairing of an act with a good outcome has turned the act into a habit.

### How to shape superstitious behaviour

Skinner called this phenomenon **superstitious behaviour** and produced it in the laboratory. A hungry pigeon was placed in the operant-conditioning apparatus and given food **every 15 seconds** on what Schultz calls a **fixed-interval** schedule. The pigeon is bound to be doing **something** when each pellet arrives: turning, raising its head, strutting, hopping, or standing still. Whatever it happens to be doing at the moment of reward is reinforced.

Skinner found that **a single reinforcement** was enough to make the pigeon repeat the accidentally reinforced behaviour more often for a while. That, in turn, raised the chance that the next pellet would arrive while the pigeon was doing the same thing, which strengthened it further. With **short intervals** between reinforcers, superstitious behaviours were learned **quickly**. Like the footballers' rituals, the pigeon's behaviours had **no functional relationship** to the reinforcers. The connection was **unintentional**, a coincidence that the reinforcement process cannot tell apart from a real cause. The glossary definition captures it: superstitious behaviour is persistent behaviour whose relationship to the reinforcement received is **coincidental, not functional**.

In humans, Schultz adds, such behaviours may last a lifetime and need only **occasional reinforcement** to keep them going. Link this to module 12.5: an occasional, unpredictable payoff is an intermittent schedule, and intermittently reinforced behaviour is especially resistant to extinction. A ritual that is followed by success even once in a while will survive many failures.

Classic background: the laboratory demonstration was reported in Skinner's 1948 paper on "superstition" in the pigeon. Strictly, the food in that study was delivered at fixed times **whatever the bird did** (what learning texts now call a **fixed-time** or response-independent schedule), which is precisely why any behaviour that happened to coincide with food was strengthened. Schultz's chapter summary says superstitious behaviour arises when reinforcement is presented on a **fixed- or variable-interval** schedule.

### Superstitions in professional baseball

When **major league baseball players in the United States and Japan** were surveyed, **74 percent** of them admitted to some form of superstitious behaviour. **American** players were **more superstitious than Japanese** players, which suggests that **cultural differences** may influence how much people engage in such actions (Burger & Lynn, 2005).

> **Trap.** The numbers and the direction: **74 percent** of players admitted superstitions; **Americans more than Japanese** (Burger & Lynn, 2005). Note also the key word in the definition: the link between behaviour and reinforcer is **coincidental, not functional**. An option saying superstitious behaviour "is maintained because it actually produces the reinforcer" is wrong by definition.

## 12.8 The Self-Control of Behaviour

Skinner's position, as you know by now, is that behaviour is controlled and modified by variables **outside** the organism. Nothing inside us, no process, drive or other internal activity, determines what we do. So it may seem surprising that Skinner wrote about **self-control** at all.

He did, but on his own terms. Although external stimuli and reinforcers shape and control behaviour, we have some ability to exercise what Skinner called self-control, which he described as **acting to alter the impact of external events**. He did **not** mean acting under the direction of some mysterious "self" or inner will. He meant that, to a degree, we can **control the external variables that control us**. The glossary definition says exactly this: self-control is the ability to exert control over the variables that determine our behaviour. He described **four techniques**.

### Stimulus avoidance

In **stimulus avoidance** we remove ourselves from, or remove from our surroundings, a stimulus that is controlling our behaviour. If your roommate is so noisy that you cannot study for tomorrow morning's exam, you can go to the library; you have taken yourself away from an external variable that was affecting your behaviour. In the same way, by staying away from a person or situation that makes you angry, you reduce that person's or situation's control over what you do. Schultz's clinical example: a person with an alcohol problem can avoid the stimulus that controls the drinking by **not keeping liquor at home**.

### Self-administered satiation

In **self-administered satiation** we cure ourselves of a bad habit by **overdoing** the behaviour until it loses its appeal. A smoker who wants to quit can **chain-smoke** for a while, inhaling until disgusted, uncomfortable or sick enough to stop. Schultz notes that this technique has been used successfully in **formal therapy programmes** designed to eliminate smoking.

### Aversive stimulation

**Aversive stimulation** as a self-control technique means arranging **unpleasant consequences** for failing to change. Schultz's example: people who want to lose weight announce their intention to friends in person, or to a much wider audience on Facebook or other social networking sites. If they then fail to keep the resolution, they face the unpleasant consequences of public failure, embarrassment and criticism, and the prospect of those consequences helps hold them to the plan.

### Self-reinforcement

In **self-reinforcement** we **reward ourselves** for behaving well. Schultz's example is a teenager who commits to achieving a certain grade point average, or to looking after a younger brother or sister, and then rewards himself or herself with concert tickets or new clothes on meeting the goal.

The common thread, and the crucial point for Skinner, is that **external variables still shape and control behaviour**. Self-control does not replace external control with inner willpower; it is a way of using our own actions to **modify the effects** of those external forces: changing which stimuli we meet, what a behaviour feels like, and what consequences follow it.

| Technique | What the person manipulates | Book's example |
|---|---|---|
| **Stimulus avoidance** | Which **stimuli** are present | Leaving a noisy roommate for the library; not keeping liquor at home |
| **Self-administered satiation** | The behaviour itself, **overdone** until aversive | Chain-smoking until sick, used in programmes to stop smoking |
| **Aversive stimulation** | **Unpleasant consequences** for not changing | Announcing a weight-loss goal publicly, so failure brings embarrassment |
| **Self-reinforcement** | **Rewards** for desirable behaviour | Teenager buys concert tickets or clothes after meeting a grade or child-care goal |

> **Trap.** Skinner's self-control is **not** willpower, ego strength or an autonomous self. It is the person **rearranging external variables**. A distractor like "Skinner proposed that self-control comes from an inner self that overrides reinforcement" contradicts the whole chapter. Note also the look-alike: **self-administered satiation** (overdo the behaviour until it is unpleasant) versus **aversive stimulation** (arrange an unpleasant consequence such as public embarrassment for failing). Cross-chapter: Bandura (Chapter 13) also uses the term self-reinforcement, but in a cognitive framework with internal standards; Skinner's version is simply a person delivering a reinforcer to himself or herself.

### Benefits of self-control

Later research links self-control to a wide range of good outcomes.

- **Tangney, Baumeister, and Boone (2004)**, a large-scale study of **American college students**: those scoring high on a self-control measure had **better grades**, **higher psychological adjustment**, and **greater self-acceptance and self-esteem**. They also had **better interpersonal skills and family relationships** and **lower levels of anger** than students low in self-control.
- **Hofmann, Luhmann, Fisher, Vohs, and Baumeister (2014)**: high self-control is related to **life satisfaction**, **happiness**, and **avoiding frequent conflict**.
- **Wills et al. (2007)**, a study of **African-American children** with an **average age of 11.2 years**: children whose parents were **more nurturing and more involved** in their upbringing had **higher self-control** than children of less nurturing, less involved parents. Notice how a Skinnerian would read this: involved parents provide more consistent consequences, so the children have more practice in managing them.

A researcher has observed that self-control has become a "hot topic". Baumeister (quoted in Weir, 2012, p. 36) argued that most of the problems troubling people in modern society have self-control failure at their core: he listed addiction, overeating, crime, domestic violence, sexually transmitted disease, prejudice, debt, unwanted pregnancy, poor performance at school and work, failure to save money, and failure to exercise.

> **Trap.** Average age **11.2 years** in the Wills et al. (2007) study, and the predictor was **nurturing, involved parenting**. The college-student study is **Tangney, Baumeister, and Boone (2004)**; the life-satisfaction and happiness study is **Hofmann et al. (2014)**. The Highlights box summarises the first as: college students high in self-control get better grades, are better adjusted psychologically and have higher self-esteem.

## 12.9 Applications of Operant Conditioning

Psychologists have taken Skinner's operant-conditioning techniques out of the laboratory and used them to change human behaviour in **clinical, business and educational** settings. The general name for this work is **behaviour modification**, a form of therapy that applies the principles of reinforcement to bring about desired changes in behaviour. It has been used successfully with children and with adults, with the mentally healthy and the mentally disturbed, and with individuals as well as with groups.

### Token economy programmes

The classic application of behaviour modification is the **token economy**, a technique in which people earn **tokens** for desirable behaviours and can later **exchange** them for objects or privileges they value.

#### The pioneering study

In the pioneering study, **Ayllon and Azrin (1968)** treated a ward of **female psychotic patients** in a **state mental institution** as one giant Skinner box. Classic background: the programme ran at Anna State Hospital in Illinois, and the 1968 report was their book *The Token Economy*. These were patients whom conventional treatments could no longer help. They had been institutionalised for a long time and could not look after themselves.

In this setting of apparent hopelessness, the patients were offered the chance to do jobs on the ward, jobs normally done by paid hospital attendants, and to be paid for them in **tokens**. The tokens worked like money, which is where the term "token economy" comes from. Just like people outside the hospital, the patients could spend their earnings on goods and privileges that made life better.

What could the tokens buy? Small purchases included sweets, cigarettes, lipstick, gloves and newspapers. Privileges included watching a film on the ward, walking around the hospital grounds, and moving to a better room. At the top of the price list, each costing **100 tokens**, were an **escorted trip into town** and a **private meeting with a social worker**. Seeing the **psychologist** privately was much cheaper, only **20 tokens**, a detail Schultz clearly enjoys.

What did the patients have to do to earn tokens? Basic self-care earned **one token per activity**: bathing at the scheduled time, brushing their teeth, making their beds, combing their hair and dressing properly. Work paid better: a spell of work in the hospital **kitchen or laundry** could earn as many as **10 tokens**, and so could helping to clean the ward, running errands, or taking other patients out for walks.

These tasks look trivial, but before the programme began these patients had been regarded as helpless and aimless, unable to do almost anything for themselves. The conditioning worked **dramatically**:

- the patients **groomed themselves** and **cleaned their surroundings**;
- they kept themselves **busy at a range of tasks**;
- they began **interacting socially**, with one another and with staff;
- some even took on **responsibility for patient care**;
- their **self-esteem improved markedly** and they became **less dependent**.

Notice something about the last result. Skinner's framework has no use for "self-esteem" as an inner cause, yet the outcome is reported in those terms. The behaviourist reading is that the patients' observable behaviour changed, and the change was large enough that observers described it as improved self-esteem; the token economy did not target self-esteem directly.

#### Later token economy research

Token programmes have since worked in many institutional settings as a way to **reduce problem behaviours**:

- **DePhilippis, Quintieri, Noble, Reyes, and Akundi (2008)**: among **cognitively impaired patients in a psychiatric hospital**, **aggressive behaviours fell by 79 percent** after a **nine-month** token economy programme.
- **Ogasahara, Hirono, and Kato (2013)**: a token economy proved highly effective in changing the behaviour of **autistic children in Japan**.

Classic background: tokens are a textbook example of a **generalised conditioned reinforcer** (module 12.3). A token has no value in itself; it becomes reinforcing because it can be exchanged for many different backup reinforcers, so it works regardless of what a particular patient happens to want at that moment. That is also why tokens can be given **immediately** after the behaviour, even when the backup reward (a trip to town) has to wait.

#### The token economy online

The approach has also been taken **online**, in a study of **heavy smokers** (Dallery, Glenn, & Raiff, 2007). For **four weeks**, the smokers video-recorded themselves at home **twice a day** and used a **web camera** to provide a **carbon monoxide** sample, which was sent electronically to the smoking clinic. (Carbon monoxide in the breath is an objective sign of recent smoking, so the clinic could verify the behaviour rather than rely on the smoker's word.) Participants earned **vouchers** for lowering their carbon monoxide level over a **four-day period** and then for keeping it at a level typical of a non-smoker. The vouchers could be exchanged for items bought over the Internet. The technique worked: participants showed **significant decreases in carbon monoxide** and **sustained abstinence** for the whole study period.

#### What happens when the tokens stop?

Schultz adds a caution to these impressive results. **Reinforcement must continue** if the changes are to last. When tokens are no longer given, the reinforced behaviours usually **revert to their original state** (Kazdin & Bootzin, 1972; Repucci & Saunders, 1974). This is just extinction (module 12.3) at the level of a hospital ward. The way round it is to hand the job of reinforcing over to the person's natural environment. If caregivers are **trained to reward desirable behaviour** with smiles, praise, hugs and other signs of affection, behaviours conditioned in the institutional token economy are **more likely to continue at home** (Kazdin, 1989).

> **Trap.** Who, where, result, limitation. **Ayllon and Azrin (1968)**; **female psychotic patients** on a ward of a **state mental institution**; tokens for self-care (1 each) and ward work (up to 10); most expensive privileges **100 tokens** (escorted trip to town, private meeting with a social worker), psychologist only **20**; results: self-care, work, social interaction, responsibility for patient care, better self-esteem, less dependence. **Limitation**: gains usually **disappear when tokens stop** (Kazdin & Bootzin, 1972) unless caregivers continue with social reinforcers (Kazdin, 1989). **79 percent** reduction in aggression is **DePhilippis et al. (2008)**, a different study.

### Behaviour modification programmes

Beyond hospital wards, behaviour modification programmes have been applied successfully in **education**, improving **academic performance** and **social behaviour in classrooms** and reducing **behavioural, emotional and developmental problems** (Lang & Rispoli, 2015).

Operant techniques have also been applied in **business and industry**. Programmes at major manufacturers, financial institutions and government agencies have **reduced absenteeism, lateness and abuse of sick-leave privileges** and **improved job performance and safety**. The techniques can also be used to teach **low-level job skills**. The reinforcers used at work include **pay**, **job security**, **recognition from supervisors**, **perks and status** within the company, and **opportunities for personal growth**.

What these programmes leave out is as characteristic as what they include. **No attempt is made to deal with supposed anxieties, repressed traumas or unconscious motives.** The work consists of three steps: identify the **overt behaviour** to be changed, define the **appropriate reinforcers**, and determine the **best rate of presenting** them. A company that wants fewer accidents does not explore its workers' attitudes to risk; it rewards safe behaviour, for example with recognition or a bonus for each accident-free month, and measures what happens.

### Punishment and negative reinforcement

Most operant-conditioning applications use **positive reinforcement**, not **punishment**. The token-economy patients were never punished for failing to behave appropriately; they were reinforced when their behaviour changed for the better. Skinner held that **punishment is ineffective** for changing behaviour from undesirable to desirable or from abnormal to normal, and that **positive reinforcement of desirable behaviour is far more effective**.

His reasons, given in an interview reported by Goleman (1987), can be summarised in three points. Punishment works **immediately** but produces **no long-term results**. It provokes one of three reactions, all unwanted: an **urge to escape**, a desire to **counterattack**, or a **stubborn apathy**. And these are exactly the bad effects seen wherever punishment is relied on, in **prisons** and **schools** alike. A child who is beaten for lying usually learns to lie more skilfully, or to avoid the person who beats him, rather than learning to tell the truth.

The glossary definition of **punishment** is the **application of an aversive stimulus after a response, in an attempt to make that response less likely to recur**.

#### Negative reinforcement is not punishment

Schultz states this plainly, and it is the distinction examiners most often test. A **negative reinforcer** is an **aversive or noxious stimulus whose removal is rewarding**. **Negative reinforcement** is the **strengthening of a response by the removal of an aversive stimulus**. In a laboratory or classroom, an operant situation can be arranged in which an unpleasant stimulus, such as a loud noise or an electric shock, **continues until the subject makes the desired response**. Just as with positive reinforcement, the environment changes as a consequence of the behaviour; here the change is that the noxious stimulus goes away.

Schultz's everyday example: a person may stop smoking to escape a spouse's or colleague's nagging. The nagging is the aversive stimulus, and it should stop once the desired behaviour (not lighting up at home or in the office) appears. Because not-smoking makes the nagging go away, not-smoking is strengthened. That is reinforcement, because a behaviour is being **strengthened**; it is negative, because the strengthening comes from **taking something away**.

Skinner **opposed** using noxious stimuli to change behaviour, for two reasons the book gives: their consequences are **less predictable** than those of positive reinforcement, and negative reinforcement **does not always work**, whereas positive reinforcement is **more consistently effective**.

The four possibilities can be laid out in one grid. The rows ask whether a stimulus is **added** or **removed**; the columns ask whether the behaviour becomes **more** or **less** likely.

| | Behaviour becomes **more** likely (reinforcement) | Behaviour becomes **less** likely (punishment) |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus **added** | **Positive reinforcement**: a pleasant stimulus follows the response (token for making the bed; food pellet for a lever press) | **Punishment** in Schultz's sense: an aversive stimulus follows the response (a scolding or smack after a tantrum) |
| Stimulus **removed** | **Negative reinforcement**: an aversive stimulus is taken away when the response occurs (nagging stops when the person stops smoking; shock stops when the rat presses) | Classic background: **negative punishment**, also called response cost or time-out (a pleasant stimulus is taken away, such as losing tokens or television time) |

Classic background: learning textbooks name the top-right cell **positive punishment** and the bottom-right cell **negative punishment**; Schultz uses only the single word "punishment", defined as applying an aversive stimulus. In the same tradition, negative reinforcement is divided into **escape** learning (the aversive stimulus is already present and the response ends it, as in the book's loud-noise example) and **avoidance** learning (the response prevents the aversive stimulus from arriving at all).

> **Trap.** **Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment.** "Negative" means **removal** of a stimulus, not "bad" or "unpleasant for the learner"; "reinforcement" always means the behaviour is **strengthened**. Punishment **weakens** behaviour by **applying** an aversive stimulus. Test it on the nagging example: the behaviour that increases is not-smoking, and it increases because the nagging is **removed**, so it is negative reinforcement. The nagging itself, if it were delivered after each cigarette to stop smoking, would be **punishment** of smoking. Many Indian keys include a distractor "negative reinforcement is a form of punishment"; it is always wrong.

> **Trap.** "Which did Skinner favour?" **Positive reinforcement.** He judged **punishment ineffective** (immediate effect, no long-term change; escape, counterattack or apathy) and **opposed negative reinforcement** as less predictable and less reliable than positive reinforcement. Behaviour modification in Schultz's summary **positively reinforces desirable behaviour and ignores undesirable behaviour**; ignoring is extinction, not punishment.

## 12.10 Questions about Human Nature

Skinner's answers to Schultz's six questions follow directly from his system, so the trick is to derive them rather than memorise them.

**Nature versus nurture.** Here Skinner's position is unambiguous: **nurture**. People are primarily **products of learning**, shaped far more by external variables than by genetic factors. Everything in modules 12.3 to 12.9 is about how the environment builds behaviour. (Module 12.13 shows that this very claim was challenged by the Brelands' evidence of instinctive drift.)

**Importance of the past versus the present.** Schultz has to infer Skinner's position, since he did not address it as such. Childhood experiences would seem to matter more than later ones, because our **basic behaviours are formed in childhood**. But that does not mean adult behaviour is fixed. Whatever was learned in childhood can be **modified**, and new behaviour patterns can be acquired **at any age**; the success of behaviour modification programmes with long-institutionalised adults (the token economy) is the proof. So childhood is important because it is where most reinforcement history begins, not because it sets personality in stone as it does for Freud.

**Uniqueness versus universality.** **Uniqueness.** Since we are shaped by experience, and since everyone's experiences differ, particularly in childhood, no two people will behave in exactly the same way. Notice the subtlety: the **laws** of behaviour (reinforcement, extinction, schedules) are universal, but the **histories** they operate on are unique, so the products are unique. This is also why Skinner preferred to study individual subjects rather than group averages (module 12.12).

**Equilibrium versus growth (the ultimate goal of life).** Skinner did **not address** the issue of an ultimate and necessary goal. He said nothing about overcoming inferiority (Adler), reducing anxiety or tension (Freud, Horney) or striving for self-actualisation (Maslow, Rogers), because all such motives assume internal, subjective states that he did not accept. Insofar as his work contains a life goal, it is **societal, not individual**. In *Walden Two* and elsewhere he described his idea of an ideal society, and he argued that individual behaviour should be directed towards the kind of society that has the **greatest chance of survival**.

**Free will versus determinism.** **Determinism**, as strongly as any theorist in the book. Skinner believed people function like **machines**, in lawful, orderly, predetermined ways. He rejected every suggestion of an inner being or **autonomous self** that decides on a course of action or chooses to act freely and spontaneously. His message is the same from his scholarly papers to his novel: **behaviour is controlled by reinforcers**. One consequence follows, and Schultz states it bluntly: in a sense it is **pointless to blame or punish people** for their actions. On this view a dictator who orders the killing of thousands, or a serial killer who murders a dozen people, is no more responsible than a driverless car that rolls down a hill. All of them behave in lawful, predictable ways controlled by external variables.

**Optimism versus pessimism.** Does all this leave us with a pessimistic picture of people as helpless, passive robots? Schultz says **no**; that is not Skinner's complete view. Although behaviour is controlled by external stimuli and reinforcers, we are not victims. **We are controlled by our environment, but we design that environment.** Buildings, cities, consumer goods, factories, media and government institutions are human products; so are social systems, languages, laws, customs and recreations. We constantly change our environment, often to our benefit, and when we do so we are **both controller and controlled**: we design the culture that controls us, and we are products of that culture. Skinner wrote that we may not be free agents but can still do something about our lives by rearranging the controls that influence our behaviour, and that his aim was not to change people but to change the world they live in (quoted in Bjork, 1993, pp. 16, 233). Add his lifelong belief that operant conditioning offered a technique for **improving human nature and the societies people design**, and the overall stance is best described as **optimistic**, in a qualified, social-engineering sense.

> **Trap.** The free-will answer is **determinism**, without qualification for the individual act. The "controller and controlled" argument is **not** a concession of free will; it says that our behaviour of designing environments is itself controlled. What it does do is rule out a **pessimistic** reading. Schultz's summary of Skinner's image of human nature lists: **determinism, uniqueness, the importance of the environment, and the design of a society that maximises the chances of survival**.

| Issue | Skinner's position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Determinism** | People function like machines in lawful, predetermined ways; no autonomous self; blaming the individual is pointless |
| Nature vs nurture | **Nurture** | People are products of learning, shaped by external variables more than by genes |
| Past vs present | **Childhood important, but change possible at any age** (inferred) | Basic behaviours form in childhood; behaviour modification shows adults can relearn |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Uniqueness** | Everyone's reinforcement history, especially in childhood, is different |
| Equilibrium vs growth | **Not addressed**; any goal is **societal** | Inner motives (tension reduction, self-actualisation) are rejected; aim is a society with the best chance of survival |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimistic, in a qualified sense** | We are controlled by the environment but design it; operant techniques can improve people and societies |

## 12.11 Assessment in Skinner's Theory

Skinner did not use the assessment techniques favoured by the other theorists. There was no place in his work for **free association**, **dream analysis** or **projective techniques**. Since he was not dealing directly with personality, he had no real interest in assessing it. He **did**, however, assess **behaviour**.

### Functional analysis

Behaviour modification cannot begin without assessment. First, the specific behaviours, both the **desirable and the undesirable** ones, must be identified. Second, the **environmental factors that act as reinforcers**, and that can be manipulated to change behaviour, must be identified as well. No behaviour can be modified properly without this prior assessment. Skinner's approach to it is called **functional analysis**, and it looks at **three aspects** of a behaviour:

1. the **frequency** of the behaviour;
2. the **situation** in which the behaviour occurs;
3. the **reinforcement** associated with the behaviour.

Until all three have been evaluated, a behaviour modification programme cannot be planned or carried out.

Schultz's worked example is a functional analysis for **cigarette smokers** who want to quit. The smokers keep an accurate record of **how many cigarettes** they smoke each day (frequency) and the **situations** in which they smoke: in a particular place or at particular times? With other people or alone? After meals, or while driving? And what are the **reinforcers**? Most smokers smoke more in the presence of certain stimuli. Identifying those stimuli matters because **changing the stimuli should change the smoking**. (This connects straight back to self-control: once a smoker knows that coffee breaks with colleagues are the trigger, stimulus avoidance becomes a concrete plan.)

Classic background: modern behaviour therapists often summarise the same idea as an **ABC analysis**: the **A**ntecedents (situation), the **B**ehaviour (with its frequency), and the **C**onsequences (reinforcers).

> **Trap.** Functional analysis has **three** parts, in the book's order: **frequency, situation, reinforcement**. It assesses **behaviour, not personality**. Distractors add "the unconscious motive behind the behaviour" or "the person's traits"; neither belongs.

Schultz describes **three approaches** to assessing behaviour: **direct observation**, **self-reports** and **physiological measurements**.

### Direct observation of behaviour

Many behaviours can be assessed by **watching them happen**. Usually **two or more observers** do the watching, to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Schultz's example is a classic report by **Hawkins, Peterson, Schweid, and Bijou (1966)**. A mother sought help for her **4-year-old son**, whose behaviour was considered unruly. **Two psychologists observed mother and child at home** to establish the nature and frequency of the boy's undesirable behaviours, when and where they occurred, and what reinforcers he received for them.

They identified **nine undesirable behaviours**, including **kicking, throwing things, biting and pushing a sibling**. They also saw what was maintaining them: the mother **reinforced the boy by giving him toys or food when he misbehaved**. She meant to stop the misbehaviour; in fact she was rewarding it, and so strengthening it. The observation phase took **16 hours**, but without it the psychologists would not have known exactly which behaviours to target or which reinforcers the child had come to expect.

With that information a programme could be planned. The psychologists told the mother to use **attention and approval** as reinforcers whenever the child behaved well and **never to reward** him when he showed any of the nine undesirable behaviours. The frequency of the undesirable behaviours recorded during observation served as a **baseline** against which behaviour during and after treatment could be compared.

Read this case as a template of the whole Skinnerian method: observe and count; find the reinforcer that is maintaining the problem (here, inadvertently, the parent); withdraw it from the problem behaviour (extinction) and give a better reinforcer to the desired behaviour (positive reinforcement); measure against baseline.

> **Trap.** Hawkins et al. (1966): **4-year-old boy**, **two** psychologists, **home** observation, **nine** undesirable behaviours, **16 hours** of observation, the mother was **unknowingly reinforcing** the misbehaviour with toys or food. The treatment combined **positive reinforcement of good behaviour** (attention, approval) with **non-reinforcement of bad behaviour**, not punishment.

### Self-reports of behaviour

A second approach is the **self-report**, gathered through **interviews and questionnaires**. Here the person observes his or her own behaviour and reports on it. A questionnaire might, for instance, measure how frightened someone is in situations such as **driving a car**, **going to the dentist** or **speaking in public**. In format, questionnaires that assess behaviour look much like the self-report inventories used to assess personality; the difference is in what they ask about (what the person does in specific situations, not what kind of person he or she is).

### Physiological measurements of behaviour

A third approach records **physiological** responses such as **heart rate, muscle tension and brain waves**. These measures show the bodily effects of different stimuli, and they can also be used to **check the accuracy** of information obtained by other methods. For example, someone too embarrassed to admit, in an interview or on a questionnaire, that being in a lift frightens her might still show a change in heart rate or muscle tension when lifts are mentioned.

There is an apparent tension here worth noticing. Module 12.1 said Skinner set physiological processes aside as irrelevant to explanation. Here physiology reappears, but only as a **measure** of a response to a stimulus, not as a **cause** of behaviour. The heart-rate change is treated as one more observable response.

Whichever technique is used, the focus stays on **what people do**, not on **what might have motivated them** to do it. The final aim is to **modify behaviour, not to change personality**.

## 12.12 Research on Skinner's Theory

Skinner's research methods differed from those of mainstream experimental psychology as sharply as his assessment methods differed from those of other personality theorists. The usual procedure in experimental psychology is to study **large groups** of animal or human subjects and to compare their **average** responses statistically. Skinner preferred the **intensive study of a single subject**.

His arguments ran as follows. Data on the average performance of a group are of little value when you have to deal with a **particular case**; a science built on averages tells us little about the unique individual. **Valid and replicable results** can be obtained **without statistical analysis**, provided that enough data are collected from a single subject under **well-controlled** experimental conditions. Using large groups forces the experimenter to deal with **average behaviour**, and the resulting data cannot reflect any **individual's** responses or the **individual differences** between subjects.

Classic background: this approach is known as the **experimental analysis of behaviour**, and it is the origin of the **single-case (N = 1) designs** used in clinical research today, in which one client's behaviour is measured repeatedly across a baseline phase and a treatment phase. The Hawkins et al. (1966) baseline in module 12.11 is an example of the same logic.

Skinner and his followers carried out **thousands** of operant-conditioning experiments on topics including **reinforcement schedules, language acquisition, the shaping of behaviour, superstitious behaviour and behaviour modification**. Schultz's verdict is that the results have been **highly supportive** of Skinner's ideas.

> **Trap.** Skinner favoured the **intensive study of a single subject** and argued that **statistical comparison of group averages** obscures the individual. Do not confuse this with Allport's idiographic approach (Chapter 7): both champion the individual case, but Allport studied a person's unique traits (for example through letters and personal documents), whereas Skinner studied one organism's **response rate** under tightly controlled reinforcement conditions. Also note the verdict: Skinner's ideas have **strong empirical support**. "Lack of empirical support" is **not** a criticism of Skinner.

### Highlights: what research on Skinner's behaviourism has found

The chapter's Highlights box condenses the evidence into six findings:

- the more reinforcement given during training, the **more resistant** the conditioned response is to **extinction** (Shull & Grimes, 2006);
- operant conditioning can **shape most forms of behaviour** in humans and animals;
- **even a lobster** can be conditioned;
- **American** major league baseball players show **more** superstitious behaviour than **Japanese** players (Burger & Lynn, 2005);
- college students who score **high in self-control** get **better grades**, are **better adjusted** psychologically and have **higher self-esteem** (Tangney et al., 2004);
- token economy programmes have **reduced aggressive acts** by cognitively impaired patients by as much as **79 percent** (DePhilippis et al., 2008).

## 12.13 Reflections on Skinner's Theory

### Criticisms

Skinner's approach has been criticised on several grounds. Learn each with the reason it has force.

1. **Determinism.** Anyone who opposes a deterministic view of human beings finds a great deal to dislike in Skinner. His position leaves no room for choice and, taken literally, none for responsibility (recall the driverless car in module 12.10).
2. **The humanistic objection.** **Humanistic psychologists** (Maslow, Rogers; Chapters 9 and 10), who believe people are more complex than machines, rats or pigeons, object to Skinner's image of human nature. They argue that focusing **exclusively on overt behaviour** ignores uniquely human qualities such as **conscious free will**.
3. **The subjects and the simplicity of the situations.** Skinner made sweeping claims and predictions about human behaviour and society, about **social, economic, religious and cultural** matters, with considerable confidence. But, critics ask, can we really generalise from a **pigeon pecking at a disc** to a person living in the real world? The distance between the two seems too great for broad generalisations, and many aspects of human behaviour cannot be meaningfully reduced to the level at which Skinner did his research. This criticism has force precisely because Skinner himself justified animal research by claiming differences "in degree, not in kind"; if the differences are in kind (language, culture, planning), the extrapolation fails.
4. **A narrow dependent variable.** Schultz's chapter summary adds Skinner's **lack of interest in any aspect of behaviour other than response rate**.
5. **The failure to consider what sets humans apart.** The same summary lists the failure to consider the human qualities that distinguish us from rats and pigeons.

#### Instinctive drift: the Brelands' challenge

The most damaging empirical challenge came from inside Skinner's own camp. Skinner's belief that **all behaviour is learned** was challenged by **two of his former students**, **Keller and Marian Breland** (Classic background: their first names are not given in the 11th edition). They became professional animal trainers and conditioned **more than 6,000 animals of 38 different species** to perform in **television commercials and tourist attractions**. Their animals included pigs, raccoons, chickens, hamsters, porpoises, whales and cows.

The Brelands found that their animals showed a tendency they called **instinctive drift**: the animals **substituted instinctive behaviours for the behaviours that had been reinforced**, even when the instinctive behaviours got in the way of receiving food.

Their best-known example involved **pigs and raccoons** trained to pick up a coin, carry it some distance, and drop it into a toy bank (a piggy bank, naturally), with food given after a certain number of coins had been deposited. The animals learned the sequence quickly. But after performing it well for a while, they began to do things the trainers had not reinforced. The **pigs** would stop on the way to the bank, **bury the coin and root it up again with their snouts**. The **raccoons** spent long periods **handling and rubbing the coins**, with the familiar washing-like movements of their species. At first this was funny, but it slowed the act down and made the show look badly flawed to the audience; as the account of the episode puts it, "Commercially, it was a disaster" (Richelle, 1993, p. 68).

What had happened was that **instinctive behaviour** (the pig's rooting in the soil, the raccoon's rubbing of its paws as if washing) took **precedence over learned behaviour**, even though it **delayed the reinforcement** (the food). The Brelands published their findings in an article titled **"The Misbehavior of Organisms"** (Breland & Breland, **1961**), a parody of the title of Skinner's landmark book ***The Behavior of Organisms*** (**1938**). Skinner reportedly was not pleased (Gillaspy, 2009).

Why does instinctive drift matter? Because it attacks the **nurture** answer in module 12.10. If reinforcement could shape any behaviour, the reinforced coin-depositing should have stayed stable. Instead, each species drifted back towards its own evolved food-handling behaviour. Biology sets limits on what can be conditioned and how stable the conditioning will be.

> **Trap.** **Instinctive drift** = the substitution of **instinctive** behaviours for **reinforced** ones, even at the cost of reinforcement. It was reported by **Breland and Breland (1961)**, two of Skinner's **former students**, in "The **Misbehavior** of Organisms", a play on Skinner's *The **Behavior** of Organisms* (1938). Numbers: **more than 6,000 animals**, **38 species**. Examples: **pigs** burying and rooting the coin, **raccoons** "washing" it. It challenges Skinner's claim that **all behaviour is learned**.

#### How Skinner responded to critics

Mostly he did not. Skinner **ignored most criticisms** of his work. Of one critic's book review he told an interviewer that he had read a little of it, seen that the reviewer had missed the point, and decided he had better uses for his time than correcting such misunderstandings (quoted in Rice, 1968). Asked how he coped with being misunderstood so often, he said: "I find that I need to be understood only three or four times a year" (quoted in Blackman, 1995, p. 126).

### Contributions and recognition

None of this diminishes Skinner's influence. He was a **potent force in 20th-century American psychology** and shaped the field perhaps more than any other individual.

- **Journals.** The ***Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior***, founded in **1958**, publishes research on the behaviour of **individual subjects**. The ***Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis***, founded in **1968**, publishes work on **behaviour-modification** techniques.
- **Awards.** The **American Psychological Foundation** gave him its **Gold Medal**, and the **American Psychological Association** gave him its **Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award** (**1958**), whose citation praised the depth of his influence on the development of psychology and on promising younger psychologists. He also received the **U.S. National Medal of Science**.
- **Books.** His first book on behaviourism, ***The Behavior of Organisms: An Experimental Analysis*** (Skinner, **1938**), has been described as one of the few books that genuinely changed the nature of the field (Thompson, 1988).
- **Fame.** *Time* magazine put him on its cover and called him the most famous American psychologist in the world. The book that turned him into a public celebrity was ***Beyond Freedom and Dignity*** (**1971**), a controversial **best seller**. According to his biographer Bjork (1993, p. 192), for a short while every national and big-city talk show wanted him: within a month millions of Americans had read or heard about him and the book, letters, phone calls and visitors swamped him, and strangers in restaurants asked to shake his hand, as if he were a film or television star.

> **Trap.** Dates that are tested together: *The Behavior of Organisms* **1938**; *Walden Two* **1948**; APA Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award **1958**; *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior* **1958**; *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis* **1968**; *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* **1971**; "The Misbehavior of Organisms" **1961**. Classic background: other well-known Skinner books not cited in this chapter are *Science and Human Behavior* (1953) and *Verbal Behavior* (1957).

### Current status

Skinner's **radical behaviourist** position is still applied in laboratory, clinical and organisational settings, but its dominance has been challenged by the **cognitive movement** in psychology, which began in the **1960s**. Skinner himself conceded that his kind of psychology had lost ground to the cognitive approach. Other psychologists agreed; Baars (1986) wrote that Skinnerian behaviourism had fallen out of favour with most active researchers and was often spoken of in the past tense.

Classic background: a landmark in that shift was Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, which argued that children's language cannot be explained by reinforcement and shaping alone, a direct challenge to the account of language learning in module 12.6. Within this book, the next chapter shows the shift in miniature: **Bandura** (Chapter 13) keeps reinforcement but adds observational learning and cognitive processes that Skinner excluded.

Despite the advances of cognitive psychology, Skinner's position **remains influential** in many areas, from **classrooms** to **assembly lines**, from **Skinner boxes** to **treatment programmes for behaviour disorders**. Skinner believed that in operant conditioning he had given us a technique for **improving human nature and the societies people design**.

> **Trap.** "Which of the following is NOT a criticism of Skinner?" The genuine criticisms in Schultz are: **determinism**; the humanistic objection that he **ignores conscious free will** and uniquely human qualities; the **simple subjects and situations** and the leap from pigeons to people; the **narrow focus on response rate**; and the **instinctive drift** challenge to "all behaviour is learned". Things that are **not** criticisms: lack of empirical support (support is **considerable**); over-reliance on unconscious or inner constructs (he **rejected** them); reliance on case studies of disturbed patients (he used **animals** in controlled experiments); lack of practical application (behaviour modification is **widely used**).

## How the chapter fits together

Five through-lines run through every module. If you can trace each, you understand Skinner rather than just remembering him.

1. **Consequences, not causes inside.** The empty organism (12.1), operant behaviour (12.3), the Skinner box (12.4), personality as a collection of operants and neurosis as reinforced undesirable behaviour (12.4), functional analysis (12.11): each is the same move, replacing an inner explanation with a description of what the behaviour produces.
2. **Whoever controls the reinforcers controls the behaviour.** It appears first as a laboratory fact (the rat), then as an everyday fact (salaries, commissions, casinos), then as a clinical tool (token economies, Hawkins et al.'s mother), and finally as a social programme (*Walden Two*, "change the world in which they live").
3. **The rate and pattern of reinforcement matter as much as its presence.** Continuous reinforcement teaches fast and extinguishes fast; intermittent and especially variable schedules produce persistent behaviour. The same fact explains gambling (12.5), superstitions that survive many failures (12.7), and why token-economy gains fade when tokens stop (12.9).
4. **Positive reinforcement over aversive control.** Skinner rejected punishment as ineffective and negative reinforcement as unreliable. His applied work, his novel and his advice to parents all rely on rewarding what you want and not rewarding what you do not want.
5. **Determinism, but the environment is designable.** People are controlled, but people also build the environments that control them, which is why Skinner could be a strict determinist and still an optimist about society.

**Where Skinner sits in the book.** He is the polar opposite of the **psychoanalytic** theorists (Freud, Jung, Adler, Horney): no unconscious, no defences, no instincts, no anxiety as a cause, and no clinical case studies. He is equally opposed to the **trait** theorists (Allport, Cattell, Eysenck), since a trait is an inferred inner disposition, and to the **humanists** (Maslow, Rogers), who make conscious experience and a drive to grow central and who criticised him for ignoring free will. Against **Kelly** (Chapter 11), who said that how a person construes events is what matters, Skinner says that only the events and their consequences matter. He shares with **Erikson** only the borrowed label: Schultz calls his dark year an identity crisis. He shares with **Allport** a preference for studying the individual rather than group averages, for completely different reasons. And he sets up the next chapter: **Bandura** (Chapter 13) begins from reinforcement but argues that people learn by observing others and regulate themselves through cognitive processes, bringing back the inner variables Skinner excluded, in line with the cognitive movement that displaced radical behaviourism. *Walden Two*, finally, is linked by Adams (2012) to the **positive psychology** of Chapter 14.

## Worked example

*A 7-year-old boy, Aarav, is brought to a child guidance clinic by his mother. Every evening when she tells him to start his homework he screams, throws his books and kicks the furniture. The tantrums last up to half an hour. Some evenings she holds firm; on others, worn out, she lets him skip the homework, and occasionally she gives him a chocolate "to calm him down". She says he "has a bad temper, like his grandfather" and asks whether the problem is in his personality. In the clinic interview you are asked how a Skinnerian psychologist would understand and treat the case.*

**Step 1: Reject the inner explanation.** "A bad temper" inherited from his grandfather is a trait label, and for Skinner a trait label simply renames the behaviour it claims to explain (module 12.1). The question is not what is inside Aarav but what happens just before and just after his tantrums.

**Step 2: Functional analysis.** Assess the three aspects (module 12.11). **Frequency**: how many tantrums per week, how long. **Situation**: they occur when homework is demanded, in the evening, at home, with the mother. **Reinforcement**: what follows the tantrum? Ideally two observers watch at home, as in Hawkins et al. (1966), to record a **baseline**.

**Step 3: Identify the reinforcers maintaining the problem.** Three are visible. (a) When he is let off homework, an aversive task is **removed**: the tantrum is being **negatively reinforced**. (b) The chocolate is a pleasant stimulus **added** after the tantrum: **positive reinforcement**. (c) The attention of the whole family during the scene may be a further positive reinforcer. Like the mother in Hawkins et al., Aarav's mother is unintentionally rewarding the very behaviour she wants to stop.

**Step 4: Explain why the tantrums are so persistent.** The mother gives in only **sometimes**, after an unpredictable number of tantrums. That is an intermittent, **variable** schedule (closest to **variable ratio**), the kind that produces durable behaviour that **resists extinction** (module 12.5). A child who has learned that the sixth or tenth tantrum sometimes pays off will keep going through the first five.

**Step 5: Notice that the mother's behaviour is also being conditioned.** When she gives in, the screaming stops. Her giving-in is strengthened by the **removal of an aversive stimulus**: she is being **negatively reinforced** too. This is the point most students miss, and it is why "just be firm" advice fails; the treatment has to change her consequences as well as his.

**Step 6: Plan the programme.**
- **Extinction of tantrums.** Tantrums no longer lead to escape from homework, chocolate or extra attention. Warn the mother that the behaviour will take time to fade (Classic background: a temporary increase, called an **extinction burst**, is common at first), and that giving in even once puts the child back on an intermittent schedule.
- **Positive reinforcement of the desired behaviour.** Praise and a **token** (a star on a chart) for starting homework, exchangeable at the weekend for an outing he values: a small token economy.
- **Shaping.** Aarav cannot go from half-hour tantrums to an hour of quiet work. Reinforce **successive approximations**: sitting down at the table, then 5 minutes of work, then 10, then 20, raising the requirement each week (module 12.6).
- **No punishment.** Smacking or shouting may stop a tantrum immediately but, on Skinner's account, has no lasting effect and invites escape, counterattack or sullen apathy (module 12.9).
- **Fading.** When the tokens are withdrawn, gains usually disappear unless they are replaced by natural reinforcers, so the mother is trained to keep up praise and affection for homework (Kazdin, 1989).

**Step 7: Answer her question.** Is the problem in his personality? Skinner would say his "personality" just **is** this pattern of reinforced operant behaviours, and that the pattern will change when the consequences change. The aim is to modify behaviour, not to restructure personality; success is measured against the baseline frequency of tantrums.

**What a Skinnerian would not do:** look for repressed hostility, interpret his play, give a projective test, or attribute the tantrums to an inherited temperament. Note, finally, the limits a critic would raise: this approach says nothing about what Aarav thinks or feels about homework, which is exactly where Bandura (Chapter 13) and the cognitive approach would add something.

## MCQ traps

1. **Skinner offered no personality theory.** Schultz states that he did not offer a personality theory and that his research did not deal specifically with personality; his aim was to explain **all behaviour** descriptively.
2. **Empty organism** = nothing inside that can explain behaviour scientifically. Skinner rejected inner forces as **useless for science**, not as non-existent; physiological processes were set aside too.
3. **Animal research justification:** human and animal behaviour differ **in degree, not in kind**; science proceeds from simple to complex.
4. **Born 1904 in Susquehanna, Pennsylvania**; died **1990**; elder of two sons; brother died at **16**.
5. **Dark year** = after graduating in **English** from **Hamilton College**, failing as a novelist in his parents' attic in **Scranton** at **22**; Schultz calls it an Eriksonian **identity crisis**. Not the **depression in his 40s**, which he resolved by writing ***Walden Two*** (**1948**).
6. **Harvard 1928**, no prior psychology course, **Ph.D. in three years**; Harvard postdoctoral fellowships until **1936**; then **Minnesota** and **Indiana**; back to Harvard **1947**.
7. **Rorschach and TAT protocols** analysed by **Norwegian** psychologists in **2012**: high conscientiousness and openness, somewhat extraverted, neurotic, quirky, driven.
8. **Two minutes per published word**: Skinner's own record of his output, an example of his view of people as lawful systems.
9. **Respondent behaviour is elicited** by a specific stimulus (knee jerk); **operant behaviour is emitted** and operates on the environment.
10. **Respondent behaviour has no effect on the environment**; operant behaviour **changes** it. The salivating dog changes nothing.
11. **Conditioning** (of respondent behaviour) = **substitution of one stimulus for another**; Pavlov, early **1900s**; adopted by **Watson** as behaviourism's basic method.
12. **Watson vs Skinner:** Watson built on Pavlov's stimulus-response conditioning; Skinner made **operant** behaviour, controlled by **consequences**, the centre. Skinner thought respondent behaviour **less important**.
13. **Pavlov's law (as Schultz states it):** a conditioned response **cannot be established without reinforcement**.
14. **Extinction** = weakening by **withholding** reinforcement; **more reinforcement in training gives more resistance** to extinction (Shull & Grimes, 2006). Extinction is not punishment.
15. **Operant conditioning** (glossary) = changing the **consequences** of a response changes the **rate** at which it occurs. Skinner's dependent variable was **response rate**.
16. **Personality** for Skinner = a **pattern or collection of operant behaviours**; neurotic behaviour = **reinforced undesirable behaviour**.
17. **Land-mine rats** were **giant African rats in Mozambique**; "even a **lobster** can be conditioned".
18. **Continuous reinforcement** = every response reinforced; fastest learning, **fastest extinction**.
19. **Fixed interval** = first response after a set **time**; **salary**; shorter intervals give higher rates; used to cut disruptive behaviour in **special education** (Tomlin & Reed, 2012).
20. **Fixed ratio** = set **number** of responses (every 10th or 20th); **piece-rate** and **commission**; faster responding than FI.
21. **Variable interval** = varying **time**; **fishing**.
22. **Variable ratio** = varying **number** around an average; **slot machines, roulette, horse races, lotteries**; high, stable rates; variable schedules **resist extinction**; most everyday learning is VI or VR.
23. **Shaping = successive approximation**; pigeon pecking a spot in **under 3 minutes**; children's **speech** by differential reinforcement of babbling.
24. **Erich Fromm** was shaped to make a **chopping motion with his left hand** in about **five minutes**, by looks, nods and smiles.
25. **Superstitious behaviour** = **coincidental, not functional** link to reinforcement; pigeon fed every **15 seconds**; a **single** reinforcement can start it; maintained in humans by occasional reinforcement.
26. **Burger & Lynn (2005):** **74 percent** of US and Japanese major league baseball players admitted superstitions; **Americans more** than Japanese.
27. **Self-control** = controlling the **external variables** that control us, not an inner self. **Four** techniques: **stimulus avoidance, self-administered satiation, aversive stimulation, self-reinforcement**.
28. **Satiation** = overdo the habit (chain-smoke) until it is aversive; **aversive stimulation** = arrange unpleasant consequences (public announcement of a diet).
29. **Self-control studies:** **Tangney, Baumeister & Boone (2004)** college students (grades, adjustment, self-esteem, less anger); **Hofmann et al. (2014)** happiness and life satisfaction; **Wills et al. (2007)** African-American children aged **11.2** on average, **nurturing, involved parents**.
30. **Token economy pioneers: Ayllon & Azrin (1968)**, **female psychotic patients**, state mental institution; **100 tokens** for a trip to town or social-worker meeting; psychologist only **20**; up to **10** tokens per work period.
31. **79 percent** fall in aggression among cognitively impaired psychiatric patients after a **nine-month** token programme: **DePhilippis et al. (2008)**, not Ayllon & Azrin.
32. **Online token economy for smokers:** **Dallery, Glenn & Raiff (2007)**; **four weeks**; carbon monoxide via **web camera**; vouchers.
33. **When tokens stop**, behaviour usually **reverts** (Kazdin & Bootzin, 1972); it persists at home if caregivers keep reinforcing socially (Kazdin, 1989).
34. **Negative reinforcement is NOT punishment.** Negative reinforcement **strengthens** a response by **removing** an aversive stimulus (nagging stops when smoking stops); punishment **applies** an aversive stimulus to **weaken** a response.
35. **Skinner on punishment:** works immediately, **no long-term results**; produces **escape, counterattack or stubborn apathy** (quoted in Goleman, 1987). He also **opposed negative reinforcement** as less predictable. He favoured **positive reinforcement**.
36. **Functional analysis** = **frequency, situation, reinforcement**; assesses behaviour, not personality.
37. **Three assessment methods:** direct observation, self-report, physiological measures. **Hawkins et al. (1966)**: **4-year-old** boy, **nine** behaviours, **16 hours**, mother reinforcing misbehaviour with toys or food.
38. **Research method:** intensive study of a **single subject**, no need for statistics; group averages hide the individual.
39. **Instinctive drift:** **Breland & Breland (1961)**, "The **Misbehavior** of Organisms", former students, **6,000+** animals, **38** species; pigs rooting coins, raccoons "washing" them.
40. **Dates:** *The Behavior of Organisms* **1938**; *Walden Two* **1948**; *JEAB* **1958**; APA Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award **1958**; *JABA* **1968**; *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* **1971**; cognitive movement from the **1960s**.
41. **Human nature:** determinism, nurture, uniqueness, childhood important but change possible at any age, no individual goal (a society with the best chance of **survival**), not pessimistic ("controller and controlled").
42. **"NOT a criticism of Skinner":** lack of empirical support, reliance on the unconscious, reliance on disturbed patients, lack of practical application. All four are false as criticisms.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Behaviourism** | Watson's approach to psychology that studies only overt behaviour and the external stimuli it responds to, excluding conscious and unconscious processes |
| **Behavioural approach** | The part of the book represented by Skinner: personality as learned responses to stimuli, with no inner forces |
| **Empty organism** | Skinner's view that nothing inside a person (mental or physiological) can explain behaviour scientifically |
| **Radical behaviourism** | Skinner's form of behaviourism (term used in the evaluation); classic background: private events are treated as behaviour, not as causes |
| **Respondent behaviour** | A response elicited by a specific environmental stimulus, such as a reflex; unlearned at its simplest |
| **Conditioning** | Learned respondent behaviour in which one stimulus comes to substitute for another (Pavlov's bell for food) |
| **Reinforcement** | Strengthening a response by adding a reward, so that the response becomes more likely to be repeated |
| **Extinction** | Eliminating a behaviour by withholding the reinforcement that maintained it |
| **Operant behaviour** | Behaviour emitted spontaneously or voluntarily that acts on the environment and changes it |
| **Operant conditioning** | The procedure in which changing the consequences of a response changes the rate at which the response occurs |
| **Skinner box** | The operant-conditioning apparatus: a chamber with a lever that delivers food, used to study response rates |
| **Continuous reinforcement** | Reinforcing every occurrence of a response |
| **Reinforcement schedules** | Patterns or rates of providing or withholding reinforcers |
| **Fixed-interval schedule** | Reinforcer given for the first response after a set time has passed (salary) |
| **Fixed-ratio schedule** | Reinforcer given after a set number of responses (piece-rate, commission) |
| **Variable-interval schedule** | Reinforcer given for the first response after a time that varies (fishing) |
| **Variable-ratio schedule** | Reinforcer given after a number of responses that varies around an average (gambling) |
| **Successive approximation (shaping)** | Acquiring complex behaviour by reinforcing responses only as they come closer and closer to the final desired behaviour, as in learning to speak |
| **Differential reinforcement** | Reinforcing some versions of a behaviour and not others, with the standard rising over time; how shaping proceeds |
| **Superstitious behaviour** | Persistent behaviour whose relation to the reinforcement received is coincidental, not functional |
| **Self-control** | The ability to exert control over the external variables that determine our own behaviour |
| **Stimulus avoidance** | Self-control by removing oneself from, or removing, a stimulus that controls one's behaviour |
| **Self-administered satiation** | Self-control by overdoing a habit until it becomes unpleasant |
| **Aversive stimulation** | Self-control by arranging unpleasant consequences for failing to change |
| **Self-reinforcement** | Self-control by rewarding oneself for desirable behaviour |
| **Behaviour modification** | Therapy that applies the principles of reinforcement to bring about desired changes in behaviour |
| **Token economy** | Behaviour modification in which tokens, exchangeable for valued objects or privileges, are given for desirable behaviours |
| **Positive reinforcement** | Strengthening a behaviour by presenting a pleasant stimulus after it |
| **Negative reinforcement** | Strengthening a response by removing an aversive stimulus |
| **Negative reinforcer** | An aversive or noxious stimulus whose removal is rewarding |
| **Punishment** | Applying an aversive stimulus after a response to make the response less likely to recur |
| **Functional analysis** | Assessing a behaviour's frequency, the situation in which it occurs and the reinforcers associated with it |
| **Direct observation** | Assessment by trained observers (usually two or more) watching and counting behaviour in its setting |
| **Self-report** | Assessment through interviews or questionnaires in which people report their own behaviour |
| **Physiological measurement** | Assessment through heart rate, muscle tension or brain waves, also used to check other reports |
| **Baseline** | The pre-treatment frequency of a behaviour, against which change during and after treatment is judged |
| **Single-subject research** | Skinner's method of studying one organism intensively under controlled conditions instead of averaging groups |
| **Instinctive drift** | The substitution of instinctive behaviours for behaviours that had been reinforced |
| **Dark year** | Skinner's year of failure as a novelist after college, described by Schultz as an identity crisis |
| ***Walden Two*** | Skinner's 1948 novel about a utopian society run on positive reinforcement |
| **Primary reinforcer** | Classic background: a reinforcer that satisfies a biological need without learning (food, water) |
| **Secondary (conditioned) reinforcer** | Classic background: a stimulus that becomes reinforcing through pairing with a primary reinforcer (praise, a click) |
| **Generalised reinforcer** | Classic background: a conditioned reinforcer linked to many primary reinforcers (money, tokens, attention) |
| **Negative punishment (response cost, time-out)** | Classic background: weakening a behaviour by removing a pleasant stimulus |
| **Air crib (baby tender)** | Classic background: the enclosed, climate-controlled crib Skinner built for his younger daughter in the 1940s; not an operant experiment |

## Likely exam points

1. **The behavioural approach and its roots:** Watson, overt behaviour only, dominant for more than 60 years from the 1920s; no anxiety, drives, motives, needs or defences; personality as an accumulation of learned responses.
2. **The empty organism** and why Skinner rejected inner causes: unobservable, so useless to science (like the soul); not denied to exist; physiology set aside as well.
3. **Why rats and pigeons:** behaviour differs in degree, not kind; simple before complex; yet huge clinical, educational and industrial application.
4. **Life-to-theory links:** strict rule-and-consequence childhood; machines and animals; the dark year at 22 as a failed novelist (identity crisis); new identity through Pavlov and Watson; Harvard 1928; *Walden Two* as a cure for depression in his 40s.
5. **Skinner's personality as assessed by projective tests** (Norwegian re-analysis, 2012): high conscientiousness and openness, somewhat extraverted, neurotic, quirky, driven.
6. **Respondent vs operant behaviour:** elicited vs emitted; stimulus before vs consequence after; no effect vs changes environment; examples.
7. **Pavlov's experiment,** conditioning as stimulus substitution, the role of reinforcement, and **extinction** (resistance increases with training reinforcement).
8. **Watson vs Skinner:** shared rejection of inner states; Pavlovian vs operant emphasis.
9. **The Skinner box** step by step and **Figure 12.1**; whoever controls reinforcers controls behaviour; response rate as the measure.
10. **Operant conditioning in the real world:** sniffer dogs, land-mine rats in Mozambique, lobsters, marine-park animals, calves; infants learning through reinforced and extinguished behaviours.
11. **Personality as a collection of operant behaviours;** abnormal behaviour as reinforced undesirable behaviour.
12. **The four schedules** with definitions, effects and examples; salary (FI), commission (FR), fishing (VI), gambling (VR).
13. **Continuous vs intermittent reinforcement** and resistance to extinction; variable schedules most durable; most everyday learning on VI or VR.
14. **Shaping by successive approximation:** the pigeon in under 3 minutes; language learning by differential reinforcement; training a dog to walk in a circle.
15. **The Fromm anecdote** as shaping of human behaviour without awareness.
16. **Superstitious behaviour:** the footballers, the 15-second pigeon, coincidental vs functional relationship, Burger & Lynn (2005) 74 percent and the US-Japan difference.
17. **Four self-control techniques** with examples, and why self-control is not an inner self.
18. **Self-control research:** Tangney et al. (2004), Hofmann et al. (2014), Wills et al. (2007), Baumeister's list of self-control failures.
19. **Token economy (Ayllon & Azrin, 1968):** patients, tokens, prices, behaviours, results.
20. **Later token research:** DePhilippis et al. (2008) 79 percent; autistic children in Japan (Ogasahara et al., 2013); online smoking programme (Dallery et al., 2007); reversal when tokens stop and the role of caregivers.
21. **Behaviour modification in education and industry:** outcomes, reinforcers used, and the exclusion of inner causes.
22. **Positive reinforcement vs negative reinforcement vs punishment,** with the nagging-spouse example, and why Skinner rejected punishment and opposed aversive control.
23. **Human nature:** determinism, nurture, uniqueness, childhood with lifelong modifiability, societal survival as the only goal, "controller and controlled", the driverless-car argument about responsibility.
24. **Functional analysis** (frequency, situation, reinforcement) with the smoking example.
25. **Direct observation, self-report and physiological measures;** Hawkins et al. (1966) in full.
26. **Single-subject method** and Skinner's case against group averages and statistics.
27. **Highlights box** findings.
28. **Criticisms:** determinism, humanistic objection (free will), simple subjects and situations, focus on response rate only.
29. **Instinctive drift:** Breland & Breland (1961), the pigs and raccoons, the parody title, and why it challenges "all behaviour is learned".
30. **Recognition and influence:** *JEAB* (1958), *JABA* (1968), APA award (1958), National Medal of Science, *Time* cover, *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938), *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* (1971).
31. **Current status:** challenged by the cognitive movement from the 1960s (Baars, 1986), but still influential in classrooms, industry and treatment programmes; bridge to Bandura.
