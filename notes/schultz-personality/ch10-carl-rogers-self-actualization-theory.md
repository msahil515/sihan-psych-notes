# Chapter 10: Carl Rogers: Self-Actualization Theory

**What this chapter is really about.** Rogers is the second of the two humanistic theorists in Schultz (Maslow, Chapter 9, is the first), and he is the one whose ideas walked out of the book and into almost every counselling room in the world. His theory can be stated in one breath. Every living organism has a single built-in push to grow, the **actualisation tendency**. In human beings part of that push is directed at the **self**, the picture each of us carries of who we are. A child needs love and approval (**positive regard**) so badly that, when the love comes with strings attached (**conditional positive regard**), the child swallows the strings and turns them into **conditions of worth**: "I am acceptable only when I am like this." From then on the person judges experience not by whether it helps growth but by whether it keeps approval. The gap that opens between the self-concept and actual experience is **incongruence**; it is felt as **anxiety** and kept at bay by **denial** and **distortion**. The cure follows directly from the cause. If conditional regard produced the damage, a relationship that offers **unconditional positive regard**, together with **empathy** and **genuineness**, lets the person drop the conditions, admit the denied experience and resume growing towards the ideal Rogers called the **fully functioning person**. That is why the therapy and the personality theory cannot be learned separately: the therapy is the theory turned into a way of sitting with someone.

**How to read this chapter.** The short opening section sets out Rogers's starting assumptions (conscious, rational, present-centred, subjective). Section 10.1, the life, is unusually important here because Rogers himself said the theory grew out of his own loneliness, his religious rebellion and his work with delinquent children; read it with the table at its end, which ties each event to an idea. Sections 10.2 to 10.4 are the core theory, in a strict logical order: the motive (actualisation), the world it operates in (the experiential field), and the developmental story (positive regard, conditions of worth, incongruence). If you can retell 10.4 as a chain of cause and effect, the rest of the chapter is easy. Section 10.5 describes the healthy end point, 10.6 gives Rogers's position on Schultz's six human-nature questions, 10.7 covers assessment (the therapy itself, encounter groups, and tests other people built), 10.8 walks through the research, including the **Q-sort** and the case of **Mrs. Oak**, and 10.9 weighs it up. The commonest exam errors in this chapter are about look-alike terms (positive regard, positive self-regard, unconditional and conditional regard; actualisation and self-actualisation; three different meanings of "congruence") and about who devised the Q-sort. Each is flagged where it arises.

## Opening: The Person at the Centre

Schultz places Rogers in the part of the book called **The Humanistic Approach**, alongside Maslow. The movement flourished in the **1960s and 1970s** and set itself against the two forces that then dominated American psychology. Psychoanalysis, the humanists complained, built its picture of human nature from the disturbed side of people alone; behaviourism, by attending only to overt behaviour, reduced people to conditioned machines. Humanistic psychology starts instead from human strengths, conscious free will and the fulfilment of potential, and it describes people as active, creative beings oriented towards growth. Rogers made this outlook the working basis of the relationship between therapist and client.

*Classic background:* for this reason humanistic psychology is often called the **"third force"** in psychology, after psychoanalysis and behaviourism.

Rogers is best known for a form of psychotherapy that went through three names. It was first called **non-directive** therapy, then **client-centred** therapy, and finally **person-centred therapy**. It has produced a very large research literature and is still widely used in counselling (Cain, 2014; Kirschenbaum, 2009).

Two features of how Rogers built his theory matter for the exam.

**First, the source of the theory was the consulting room, not the laboratory.** Rogers did not derive his ideas from experiments. He derived them from his work with people in distress, whom he preferred to call **clients** rather than patients. How he understood the therapy situation therefore tells you how he understood human nature. Take the final name of his therapy literally: in *person-centred* therapy the power to change and improve personality is located **in the person**. The client, not the therapist, directs the change; the therapist's job is to **facilitate** it (Bozarth, 2012). A doctor treating an infection decides what is wrong and prescribes a remedy; a Rogerian counsellor assumes the client already holds both the diagnosis and the remedy and needs the right conditions to find them.

**Second, the assumptions are almost the mirror image of Freud's.** Rogers saw people as **rational beings** governed by their **conscious perception** of themselves and their world. He gave unconscious forces and other Freudian explanations very little weight. He accepted that childhood experiences colour how we see ourselves and our surroundings, but he denied that past events control present behaviour; **current feelings and emotions** have the larger effect on personality.

Because the conscious present is what counts, Rogers held that personality can be understood **only from the person's own point of view**, through his or her **subjective experience**. He worked with reality as each individual consciously perceives it, and he stressed that this perceived reality need not match objective reality. A student who is sure that her supervisor despises her will act on that conviction, whatever the supervisor actually feels. The chapter summary calls this a **phenomenological approach**.

Finally, Rogers proposed **one single, innate, overriding motive**: the inborn tendency to **actualise**, to develop all our abilities and potentials as fully as possible. The ultimate goal is to actualise the self and become what he called a **fully functioning person**. This hopeful, humanistic picture was received enthusiastically not only in psychology but in education and in research on family life.

> **Trap.** Freud proposed two classes of instincts (life and death); Adler a striving for superiority; Horney a need for safety; Maslow a hierarchy of needs. Rogers proposed **one** master motive, the actualisation tendency. An option that credits Rogers with "a hierarchy of needs" or with "several innate drives" is describing Maslow or Freud.

> **Trap.** Indian keys and older textbooks use all three names of the therapy. **Non-directive** (the earliest), **client-centred** (the middle and most common name in older keys) and **person-centred** (the final name, used in the 11th edition) refer to the same approach at different stages of its development. They are not three rival schools.

## 10.1 The Life of Rogers (1902 to 1987)

Rogers is one of the clearest cases in the book of a theorist who said, in so many words, that his ideas came out of his own life. Almost every major concept in 10.2 to 10.7 has a recognisable source in the story below, and Indian interview panels like to ask for exactly those links.

### Relying on His Own Experience

Rogers was born in **1902 in Oak Park, Illinois**, a suburb of Chicago, the **fourth of six children**. His parents' religion was extremely strict. The household prized moral conduct and hard work and **suppressed every display of emotion**. Looking back, Rogers described their fundamentalist teaching as holding him in a tight grip through childhood and adolescence, and he saw that he had been made to live by their view of the world rather than his own. The children all understood the list of forbidden things: they were never to dance, play cards, go to the cinema, smoke, drink or show any sexual interest (Rogers, 1967, p. 344).

Schultz adds an observation that is easy to miss. The parents did not rule by harshness alone; they exerted their influence in **subtle and loving ways**, which is exactly the manner Rogers himself later adopted in his non-directive counselling. The style of influence survived even though its content was rejected. Those beliefs would soon become the target of his rebellion.

The theoretical significance is direct. A child who must keep feelings hidden, and who earns approval by conforming to rules he did not choose, is the textbook case of what Rogers later called **conditional positive regard** and **conditions of worth** (10.4). The emotional suppression of the household is the opposite of the fully functioning person's openness to all feeling (10.5).

#### An Older Brother and a Lonely Childhood

Outside the family Rogers had almost no social life. He came to believe that his parents **favoured an older brother**, and the two were intensely competitive. He described himself as **shy, solitary and dreamy**, often absorbed in fantasy. A biographer wrote that he carried bitter memories of being the regular target of his brother's jokes while receiving little joy or warmth from his mother (Milton, 2002, p. 128).

To escape the loneliness he **read constantly**, anything at all, even dictionaries and encyclopaedias. Solitude taught him to rely on **his own resources and his own experience**, his private view of the world. That habit stayed with him for life and, as Schultz puts it, became the foundation of his personality theory. In later years Rogers himself recognised how much his loneliness had shaped both his personality and his theory. He traced his interest in interviewing and therapy to it directly: therapy offered him a socially acceptable way of getting genuinely close to other people and so of satisfying the hunger for closeness he had felt as a boy (Rogers, 1980, p. 34).

Two ideas grow from this soil. Reliance on his own experience becomes the doctrine that **experience is the highest authority** (10.3) and that personality must be understood from the inside. The hunger for closeness and approval becomes the universal **need for positive regard** (10.4), which he placed at the centre of child development.

#### Moths and Bizarre Fantasies

When Rogers was **12**, the family moved to a **farm 30 miles from Chicago**, and country life woke his interest in **science** in two ways. He became fascinated by a species of moth he found in the woods, and over many months he watched, caught and bred them. He also became interested in farming, which his father ran on **modern scientific lines**. Reading about agricultural experiments, the teenager came to value the logic of the scientific method: **control groups**, **isolating a single variable** for study, and **statistical analysis** of the data. Schultz notes how unusual that was for an adolescent.

This is the root of a side of Rogers students often forget. The man who said experience was the highest authority was also the first therapist to record his sessions for research and to measure self-concept change with correlations (10.8). The farm boy who learned about control groups is the same person who, thirty years later, compared clients' self-ratings before and after therapy.

His emotional life, meanwhile, stayed in turmoil, though he never fully explained its nature. He wrote that his fantasies in this period were **"definitely bizarre"** and would probably have been labelled schizoid by a diagnostician, adding that luckily he never met a psychologist (1980, p. 30).

#### Finding Freedom in China

Rogers went to the **University of Wisconsin** to study **agriculture**, following his parents, two older brothers and a sister, who had all attended it. After his **sophomore (second) year** he abandoned agriculture and began preparing for the **ministry**. In his **junior (third) year** he was chosen to attend an **international Christian student conference in Beijing, China**. The trip lasted **6 months**, and during it he wrote home that his philosophy of life was changing: his religious views had moved from **fundamentalist to liberal**.

*Classic background:* the conference was held in **1922**, when Rogers was 20, and was organised by the World Student Christian Federation. The diary he kept on the trip has since been published.

Breaking free of his parents' outlook grieved them, but it gave him **emotional and intellectual independence**. He later wrote that he now felt able to think his own thoughts, reach his own conclusions and take his own stands (1967, p. 351). The liberation, and the confidence and sense of direction that came with it, strengthened his conviction that **every human being must learn to rely on his or her own experiences, ideas and beliefs**. Schultz also opens the theory section (10.2) by noting that it was on this trip that Rogers grasped the importance of an **autonomous self** in his own development.

The independence was expensive. Rogers was **hospitalised for 5 weeks with ulcers**, possibly brought on by stress, and then spent **a year recovering at the family farm** before going back to college. Read in the language of his own theory, the break with his parents was the painful dismantling of conditions of worth he had absorbed in childhood: growth, as he later said of the child learning to walk (10.2), involves struggle and pain.

### A Unique Approach to Counseling

In **1924** Rogers graduated from Wisconsin, **married a childhood friend**, and enrolled at **Union Theological Seminary** in New York to train as a clergyman. After **two years** he moved across the street to **Teachers College of Columbia University** to study **clinical and educational psychology**. He received his **Ph.D. in 1931** and joined the **Child Study Department of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children in Rochester, New York**, where his work was **diagnosing and treating delinquent and underprivileged children**.

*Classic background:* his wife was Helen Elliott. Union was a liberal seminary; Rogers later recalled that he and some fellow students won permission to run a seminar of their own with no instructor setting the agenda, and that this experience of self-directed inquiry helped carry several of them, Rogers included, out of religious work. The episode prefigures both the non-directive method and the leaderless encounter group (10.7).

The Rochester years matter more than any other job he held, because they produced the research on delinquent children (the **self-insight** studies of Kell and McNeil, 10.2) that turned Rogers's attention from the child's environment to the child's view of himself.

*Classic background:* Rogers's first book, *The Clinical Treatment of the Problem Child* (1939), came out of this work. At Rochester he also met social workers trained in the "relationship therapy" of Otto Rank, Freud's former associate, whose emphasis on the client's own will and on the therapeutic relationship itself influenced the early non-directive method.

In **1940** he moved from clinic to university as **professor of psychology at Ohio State University**. There he began to formulate his person-centred approach to counselling emotionally disturbed people, and he worked to bring clinical psychology into the mainstream of academic psychology.

*Classic background:* his book *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942), written at Ohio State, introduced **non-directive counselling** and included what is often cited as the first complete, word-for-word published transcript of a course of psychotherapy, made from sound recordings (the case of "Herbert Bryan"). It is the first sign of the recording method described in 10.8.

From **1945 to 1957** he was at the **University of Chicago**, teaching and building its **Counseling Center**.

#### A Breakdown and Therapy

At Chicago Rogers found himself unable to help a **severely disturbed client**, and he became so distressed that he fell ill himself, with what was then called a **nervous breakdown**. His self-confidence collapsed. He wrote of being utterly convinced that he was inadequate as a therapist, worthless as a person and without a future in psychology (1967, p. 367).

He and his wife left Chicago for their **cabin in upstate New York**, where he stayed in seclusion for **6 months**. When he was well enough to return to the university he also **entered therapy himself**, and discovered how deep his insecurity ran. He realised he had believed that **nobody could ever love him, even if they liked what he did** (quoted in Milton, 2002, p. 131).

Look closely at that sentence, because it is the whole theory of 10.4 in Rogers's own life. "They might like what I did" is approval for performance, which is **conditional positive regard**. "No one could ever love me" is the absence of **unconditional positive regard**, acceptance of the person regardless of performance. A man raised on the first without the second had built a self-concept on conditions of worth, and when he failed at the thing he was valued for (helping a client), the self-concept gave way.

#### Finally Finding Himself

The therapy apparently worked. Rogers came out of it newly able to **give and receive love** and to form **deep emotional relationships**, including with his clients. The cure, in other words, came from the kind of accepting relationship he would make the core of his therapy.

From **1957 to 1963** he taught at the **University of Wisconsin**, publishing many articles and books that carried his personality theory and therapy to a wide audience. Schultz draws an important contrast here. Throughout his academic career Rogers's clinical work was mostly with **college students** at counselling centres: **young, intelligent, highly verbal people facing problems of adjustment rather than severe emotional disorders**. That population was very different from the patients seen by Freudians or by clinical psychologists in private practice. A theory built on bright, articulate students with everyday adjustment difficulties is likely to see people as rational, conscious and basically healthy; a theory built on severely disturbed Viennese patients is likely to see them as driven and conflicted. (This contrast answers the common question of how Rogers's clinical experience differed from Freud's.)

In **1964** Rogers became a resident fellow at the **Western Behavioral Sciences Institute** in **California**, where he tried to apply his person-centred philosophy to international conflict, such as tension between **Protestants and Catholics in Northern Ireland** and between **Jews and Arabs in the Middle East**. He was **president of the American Psychological Association (APA) in 1946**, and he received both of the APA's major awards, the **Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award** and the **Distinguished Professional Contribution Award**.

*Classic background:* he received the scientific award in 1956 (in its first year) and the professional award in 1972, which makes him one of very few psychologists to hold both. He died in 1987.

> **Trap.** Rogers went to a **theological seminary** (Union, New York) before psychology, not to medical school. Freud, Adler and Jung were physicians; Horney was a physician; Rogers, like Allport and Maslow, was an academic psychologist. Psychoanalytic training required a medical degree, and this difference becomes part of the explanation for his therapy's rapid spread after World War II (10.9).

> **Trap.** Keep the dates and places straight: born **1902**, Oak Park; farm at **12**; China in his **junior year** (1922, 6 months); graduated **1924**; Union Theological Seminary, then Teachers College, Columbia; **Ph.D. 1931**; Rochester (child guidance); **Ohio State 1940**; **Chicago 1945 to 1957** (Counseling Center, breakdown); **Wisconsin 1957 to 1963**; **California 1964**; **APA president 1946**. The breakdown happened at **Chicago**, after failing to help a severely disturbed client, not during his student years (the student-years illness was **ulcers**).

## 10.2 The Self and the Tendency toward Actualization

Two of the ideas that hold Rogers's theory together, the self and the drive to actualise, did not begin as theory. The first came from his own experience in China, where he discovered how much his development depended on having an **autonomous self**. It was then confirmed, against his own expectations, by his early research on children.

The early research went like this. In the **1930s**, at Rochester, Rogers devised a method for judging whether a child's behaviour was **healthy and constructive** or **unhealthy and destructive**. He studied each child's background and had the child **rated on factors he expected to shape behaviour**:

- family environment
- health
- intellectual development
- economic circumstances
- cultural influences
- social interactions
- level of education

Every one of these is **external**, part of the child's surroundings. Rogers also included one possible **internal** influence, the child's **self-understanding or self-insight**, which he defined as **accepting oneself and reality and taking responsibility for oneself**. At this stage he still believed the external factors mattered more for personality.

### Self-Insight

About **ten years later**, **William Kell**, one of Rogers's students, used these ratings to try to **predict the later behaviour of delinquent children**. Rogers expected that **family environment** and **social interactions**, both external, would correlate most strongly with delinquency. He was wrong. The best predictor of the children's later behaviour was **self-insight**.

Rogers did not take the news well. He could not believe that family environment was so weakly related to later delinquency, and he admitted that he was not ready to accept the result, so the study was **shelved** (1987, p. 119). **Two years later, Helen McNeil repeated the study with a different group of children** and found much the same thing: a person's level of self-insight was the **single most important predictor** of behaviour.

Faced now with two sets of data pointing the same way, Rogers accepted the finding and, thinking it over, saw what it implied. If a child's **attitude towards the self** predicted behaviour better than the external circumstances everyone assumed were decisive, then counsellors and social workers had been working on the wrong things. Standard practice was to change the environment: remove a child from a threatening home and place him or her in foster care. The research suggested that the target should instead be the child's **self-insight**.

The realisation reshaped Rogers's career. He later explained that it made him decide to develop a psychotherapy aimed at increasing **self-understanding, self-direction and personal responsibility**, rather than at changing the social environment, and to put more weight on studying the **self and how it changes** (Rogers, 1987, p. 119). From then on the self was the core of his theory, as it had already become the core of his life.

Consider how this looks in a modern juvenile-justice setting. Two fourteen-year-olds come from equally chaotic homes. One can say, "I got into fights because I was angry about what was happening at home, and I need to find other ways to handle that." The other says that everything is somebody else's fault. Kell's and McNeil's data predict that the first child, who accepts himself and his situation realistically and takes responsibility for himself, has the better outlook, whatever the home.

> **Trap.** The self-insight studies were run by Rogers's **students**, **William Kell** first and then **Helen McNeil** (replication, two years later, different subjects), using Rogers's rating method. Rogers **predicted the opposite** of what they found (he expected family environment and social interactions to win) and initially **shelved** Kell's study. An option saying "Rogers's research confirmed his prediction that family environment best predicts delinquency" is wrong on both counts.

### Actualizing Tendency

Rogers held that people are motivated by an **innate tendency to actualise, maintain and enhance the self**. Read that sentence carefully, because Schultz immediately places it inside something larger. The drive towards **self-actualisation** is **one part** of a broader **actualisation tendency**, which covers **all our physiological and psychological needs**.

At the most basic level the actualisation tendency **keeps the organism alive**. By attending to requirements such as **food, water and safety**, it provides for sustenance and survival. It is already at work **before birth**: Rogers believed it **begins in the womb**, promoting growth by guiding the **differentiation of the physical organs** and the **development of physiological functioning**. It is responsible for **maturation**, the genetically programmed development of the body's parts and processes, from the growth of the foetus through to the appearance of **secondary sex characteristics at puberty**. All these changes are written into our genes, but it is the actualisation tendency that carries them through.

Genetic programming does not mean that development is automatic or easy. For Rogers, growth involves **struggle and pain**. His example is a child taking its first steps. The child falls and gets hurt; staying at the crawling stage would hurt less. Yet most children keep trying, fall again, cry, and carry on, because the **tendency to actualise is stronger than the urge to regress** merely because growing up is hard. The same pattern appears in adults: a person recovering from a stroke who keeps working at physiotherapy despite pain and frustration, or a shy student who forces herself to present in class, is showing the actualising tendency overriding the pull towards the safer, smaller option.

It helps to see the logic of the two levels.

| | Actualisation tendency | Self-actualisation (the self-actualising tendency) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | The **whole organism**: all physiological and psychological needs | The part of the actualisation tendency that concerns the **self** |
| When it starts | **In the womb** | Once the **self** has differentiated in infancy (10.4) |
| What it does | Maintains the organism (food, water, safety), drives **maturation** of organs and functions up to puberty | Develops and enhances the self-concept; aims at becoming a **fully functioning person** |
| Example | Growth of the foetus; appearance of secondary sex characteristics | A young adult developing her own values and talents |

*Classic background:* in his formal 1959 statement of the theory, Rogers distinguished the organism's actualising tendency from a **self-actualising tendency**, its expression in the part of experience organised as the self. When the self has been built on conditions of worth, the two can pull in different directions: the organism moves towards what would really enhance it while the self-concept moves towards what keeps approval. That split is another way of describing the incongruence of 10.4.

> **Trap.** Schultz's margin glossary defines the actualisation tendency as the basic motive to actualise, maintain and enhance **the self**, but the text makes it broader than self-actualisation: self-actualisation is **part of** the actualisation tendency, which also covers biological maintenance and maturation. If an item asks which is the more inclusive concept, the answer is the **actualisation tendency**. Note too that Schultz uses "actualizing tendency" (the heading) and "actualization tendency" (the glossary) interchangeably; they are the same concept.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Rogers's actualisation with Maslow's self-actualisation (Chapter 9). For Maslow, self-actualisation is the **top level of a hierarchy**, which emerges only when lower needs are satisfied. For Rogers, the actualisation tendency is the **one master motive**, operating from the womb onwards and covering the "lower" survival needs themselves. Maslow's ideal person is the **self-actualiser**; Rogers's is the **fully functioning person**.

### Organismic Valuing Process

How does the actualisation tendency steer behaviour from moment to moment? Through what Rogers called the **organismic valuing process**, which he regarded as the governing process **throughout the life span**. Through it we evaluate every experience by **how well it serves the actualisation tendency**.

- Experiences we perceive as **promoting** actualisation are judged **good and desirable** and given a **positive value**.
- Experiences we perceive as **hindering** actualisation are judged **undesirable** and given a **negative value**.

These evaluations shape behaviour because we **repeat desirable experiences** and **avoid undesirable ones**. The chapter summary puts it simply: experiences that promote actualisation will be sought, and experiences that hinder it will be avoided.

The infant is the clearest illustration because nothing has yet interfered with the process. A baby who is held warmly relaxes and seeks more of it; a baby who tastes something bitter spits it out and turns away. Neither needs to be taught; the organism values what enhances it. In an adult the process shows up as a felt sense of rightness or wrongness: a student who has spent a semester in a course her parents chose may notice that she feels drained and flat in it but alive and absorbed when she volunteers at a counselling helpline. For Rogers, that bodily, felt evaluation is information from the organismic valuing process, and the healthy person listens to it.

Notice what the valuing process implies. It is an **internal** standard of value, built into the organism. In 10.4 you will meet its rival, an **external** standard taken over from other people, the **conditions of worth**. Much of Rogers's account of psychological trouble is the story of how the external standard displaces the internal one.

> **Trap.** The organismic valuing process judges experience by whether it **serves actualisation**. Conditions of worth judge experience by whether it **wins positive regard** from others. The first is innate and growth-directed; the second is learned and approval-directed. When an item describes someone choosing what "feels right for my growth" versus what "will make my parents proud", it is contrasting these two valuing systems.

## 10.3 The Experiential World

Rogers's theory takes place inside what he called the **experiential world** (the chapter summary also calls it the **experiential field**): the world as it is experienced by the person. It is the **frame of reference**, the context, within which growth happens. Every day each of us is exposed to an enormous number of sources of stimulation, some trivial and some important, some threatening and some rewarding. Rogers wanted to know how we perceive this many-sided world and how we react to it.

His answer was that **the reality of our environment depends on our perception of it**, and our perception may not coincide with objective reality. Two people can react to the same event in completely different ways; you might judge a roommate's behaviour very differently from how someone several decades older would judge it. Perceptions also **change with time and circumstances**: what you think is acceptable behaviour for college students now will probably look different to you at **70**.

A clinical example shows why this matters. Two candidates sit the same entrance interview. The examiner frowns while reading. One candidate perceives the frown as hostility and freezes; the other perceives it as concentration and carries on. Objectively the frown is one event. Psychologically there are two different events, and each candidate behaves according to the one she perceives. For Rogers, a therapist who wants to understand either candidate must understand the frown **as she saw it**, not as a neutral observer would have described it.

The experiential world is not fixed. As the **actualisation tendency** drives the infant's growth, the experiential world **widens**. Infants meet more and more sources of stimulation and respond to them **as they subjectively perceive them**. Our experiences become **the only basis for our judgements and behaviour**. Rogers put this in a single famous sentence: "Experience is, for me, the highest authority" (1961, p. 23). As development proceeds, the experiential world becomes sharper and more differentiated, and this eventually leads to the **development of the self** (10.4).

Connect this back to 10.1. A lonely boy who learned to rely on his own reading and his own private view of things became the theorist who made personal experience the final court of appeal.

*Classic background:* older textbooks, including Hall and Lindzey's, and many older Indian keys call the experiential field the **phenomenal field**, borrowing a term from the phenomenological psychology of the 1940s. In the 19 propositions of *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951), Rogers began by saying in effect that each person lives in a continually changing private world of experience of which he or she is the centre, and that the person reacts to this field as it is perceived. "Phenomenal field", "experiential field", "experiential world" and "internal frame of reference" all point to the same idea.

> **Trap.** The experiential world is the environment **as perceived**, not the objective environment. If an item asks what Rogers meant by "reality" for an individual, the answer is the **perceived** world (the phenomenal or experiential field). Likewise, when Schultz defines incongruence (10.4), the "experiential world" in the definition is "the environment as we perceive it". Rogers's approach to personality is therefore **phenomenological**.

> **Trap.** The experiential world **broadens and changes with age**; it is not laid down once in childhood. This is why Rogers rated the present above the past (10.6): what matters is how a person perceives things now.

## 10.4 The Development of the Self in Childhood

This is the section on which the whole chapter turns. It is best learned as a causal chain, so here is the chain first, and then each link in turn:

1. The **self** separates out from the rest of the infant's experience.
2. As it does, the infant develops a **need for positive regard** (love, acceptance, approval).
3. If regard is given **unconditionally**, the child grows freely.
4. If regard is given **conditionally**, the child internalises the conditions as **conditions of worth**, and **positive self-regard** becomes available only when the conditions are met.
5. The child then starts valuing experience by whether it wins approval, not by whether it serves growth. Experiences that contradict the self-concept are **denied** or **distorted**, producing **incongruence**, which is felt as **anxiety**.
6. A child who received **unconditional** regard needs no such defences, lives **congruently**, and moves towards becoming a **fully functioning person**.

### The emergence of the self

As infants' experiential field becomes more complex through widening contact with other people, **one part of their experience becomes differentiated from the rest**. This part, marked out by the words **"I", "me" and "myself"**, is the **self** or **self-concept**. Forming it means telling apart what belongs directly and immediately to oneself from the people, objects and events that lie outside oneself. The baby who discovers that the hand moving in front of its face is *its own* hand, and that the voice in the next room is *not*, is building this boundary.

The self-concept is more than a description of what we are now. Schultz defines it as our image of **what we are, what we should be, and what we would like to be**. It therefore contains the seeds of the distinction, used throughout the research in 10.8, between the **perceived self** (how I see myself) and the **ideal self** (the person I would most like to be).

Ideally the self is a **consistent pattern, an organised whole**, and all its parts strive for consistency. That striving has consequences for behaviour. Take someone who is troubled by aggressive feelings and has chosen to deny them. Such a person **cannot afford to act aggressively in any obvious way**, because doing so would contradict a self-concept built on the belief that they should not be aggressive. The self-concept, in other words, starts to police behaviour and perception. Hold that idea; it reappears as incongruence.

### Positive Regard

As the self emerges, the infant develops a need for what Rogers called **positive regard**: **acceptance, love and approval from other people**, above all from the **mother** in infancy. Rogers thought the need was **probably learned**, though he said its origin was not important. What matters is that it is **universal** (everyone has it) and **persistent** (it lasts throughout life).

Receiving positive regard is satisfying; failing to receive it, or having it withdrawn, is frustrating. Because positive regard is so central to personality development, **infant behaviour is steered by how much affection and love is given**. If the mother does not provide positive regard, the infant's innate tendency towards actualisation and the development of the self-concept will be **hampered**.

There is a crucial perceptual twist. Infants do not distinguish between disapproval of what they **did** and disapproval of who they **are**. They take **parental disapproval of their behaviour as disapproval of their newly forming self**. If this happens often, they stop striving for actualisation and development and instead behave in whatever ways will **earn positive regard from others**, even when those behaviours are **inconsistent with their own self-concept**. This is the first point at which the need for approval begins to override the actualising tendency.

#### Unconditional Positive Regard

Even a child who receives plenty of love will sometimes be punished for particular behaviours. The question is what happens to the love when that occurs. If the parent's positive regard for the child **continues despite the child's undesirable behaviour**, the condition is called **unconditional positive regard**. Rogers meant that the mother's love is **given freely and fully**; it is **not conditional on, or dependent on, the child's behaviour**.

The everyday version: a four-year-old breaks a vase in a tantrum. A parent offering unconditional positive regard may still be cross, may still remove a privilege, and may say firmly that throwing things is not allowed. What the child does not experience is a withdrawal of love: the message is "what you did was wrong" and not "you are bad and I love you less."

> **Trap.** Unconditional positive regard is **not** approval of all behaviour and is **not** permissiveness. Schultz explicitly allows that specific behaviours may still be punished; what is unconditional is the **regard for the person**. An option defining UPR as "never disciplining a child" or "approving everything the client does" is wrong. In therapy the same applies: the therapist accepts the client as a person without endorsing every action.

The need for positive regard has a **reciprocal** quality. When we see that we are satisfying **someone else's** need for positive regard, our own need is satisfied in turn. Giving acceptance is itself rewarding, which is part of why loving relationships are self-sustaining.

Because satisfying this need matters so much, especially in infancy, we become **highly sensitive to other people's attitudes and behaviour**. We read their feedback, approval or disapproval, and use it to **refine our self-concept**. In forming the self-concept, therefore, we **internalise the attitudes of other people**. A child who is repeatedly told "you are so careful with your little brother" comes to see herself as a careful, responsible person; a child who repeatedly hears "you are hopeless at maths" comes to see himself that way.

#### Positive Self-Regard

Over time, positive regard comes to originate **more from within us than from other people**. Rogers called this condition **positive self-regard**. It becomes **as strong as the need for positive regard from others** and can be satisfied **in the same way**. Schultz's example: children who receive affection, approval and love when they are happy will come to produce positive self-regard **whenever they behave happily**. In effect, we learn to **reward ourselves**.

Positive self-regard can be defined as **a feeling of contentment with oneself**, and it is associated with **positive mental health** (Leising, Borkenau, Zimmermann, Roski, Leonhardt, & Schutz, 2013). Like positive regard, it is **reciprocal**: people who receive positive regard and develop positive self-regard are in turn able to give positive regard to others. This is why, in the research in 10.8, self-accepting mothers turn out to be more accepting of their children.

| Term | Where the regard comes from | Is it conditional? | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive regard** | Other people (especially the mother in infancy) | Either | A universal, persistent, probably learned need for acceptance, love and approval |
| **Unconditional positive regard** | Other people | **No**: given regardless of behaviour | Ideal parenting; the therapist's core attitude; behaviour can still be corrected |
| **Conditional positive regard** | Other people | **Yes**: given only for desirable behaviour and attitudes | Produces conditions of worth |
| **Positive self-regard** | **Oneself** (internalised) | Mirrors whatever the person received | Contentment with oneself; we learn to reward ourselves |

> **Trap.** Positive **regard** comes from **others**; positive **self-regard** comes from **within**, after others' attitudes have been internalised. Both are **reciprocal**. A frequent wrong option reverses them or calls positive self-regard "innate". Positive self-regard develops **out of** positive regard; it is the developmental sequence from outside to inside.

### Conditions of Worth

Conditions of worth grow out of this sequence, in which **positive regard leads to positive self-regard**. Schultz says that positive self-regard is Rogers's version of the **Freudian superego** and that it comes from **conditional positive regard**.

Recall that **unconditional** positive regard means the parents love and accept the infant without conditions, whatever the child does. **Conditional positive regard is the opposite.** Parents do not respond to everything an infant does with positive regard. Some behaviours **annoy, frighten or bore** them, and for those they may withhold affection or approval. The infant learns that parental affection **has a price**: it depends on behaving in certain acceptable ways. The child comes to understand that sometimes he or she is **prized** and sometimes not.

Schultz's example is deliberately small. If a parent shows irritation **every time the infant drops something out of the cot**, the child learns to **disapprove of himself or herself** for doing it. External standards of judgement have become **internal and personal**. In a sense, children now **punish themselves as their parents did**. They develop self-regard **only in situations that won parental approval**, and the self-concept formed in this way comes to act as a **parental surrogate**, a parent installed inside the child.

The result is **conditions of worth**. Children come to believe that they are **worthy only under certain conditions**, namely the ones that first earned parental positive regard and then personal positive self-regard. Having absorbed their parents' norms and standards, they judge themselves worthy or unworthy, good or bad, **on the terms their parents set**.

A familiar Indian version: a girl whose parents light up when she ranks first and go quiet when she ranks fifth. Nobody is cruel to her, but she learns the rule perfectly. By sixteen she does not need anyone to say it; she feels worthless after a 92 because "92 is not me". Her parents' approval has become her own condition of worth.

The research evidence on what this does to adolescents is specific.

> **Study: Assor and Tai (2012).** Adolescents whose **mothers used conditional regard** to reward academic achievement and to punish its absence developed **unstable, erratic feelings of self-worth**. After good grades they behaved in **self-aggrandising** ways; after poor grades they felt **shame** and played down or devalued their sense of their own worth. The self-esteem of these teenagers had become hostage to their marks, exactly as the concept of conditions of worth predicts. (Schultz's text says the mothers used "conditional positive self-regard"; the sense is clearly conditional positive regard given by the mothers.)

The consequence for development is the heart of Rogers's argument. Children who live by conditions of worth learn to **avoid certain behaviours** and **no longer function freely**. Because they must vet their behaviour and attitudes so carefully, and hold back from certain actions, they are **prevented from fully developing or actualising the self**. They **limit their own growth** by living within the boundaries their conditions of worth draw.

> **Trap.** Which Rogerian concept is "similar to the Freudian superego"? Schultz's **margin glossary** and **chapter summary** both say **conditions of worth**. The running text says positive self-regard is Rogers's version of the superego and derives from conditional positive regard. The two statements fit together: the superego-like element is positive self-regard that has been made **conditional**, which is exactly what conditions of worth are. In an MCQ with one answer, choose **conditions of worth**. Note the difference from Freud: the superego in Freud forms through resolution of the Oedipus complex, whereas conditions of worth form through ordinary experiences of **conditional regard** from infancy onwards.

> **Trap.** The direction of cause runs: **conditional positive regard** (from parents) → **conditions of worth** (internalised) → **restricted self-regard and blocked actualisation**. Unconditional positive regard produces **no** conditions of worth. An item asking "what produces conditions of worth?" wants **conditional positive regard**, not "lack of any positive regard".

### Incongruence

Children learn not only to inhibit unacceptable **behaviours** but also, sometimes, to **deny or distort unacceptable ways of perceiving** their experiential world. When certain experiences are perceived inaccurately, the person risks becoming **estranged from his or her true self**.

Here is the key step. Once conditions of worth are in place, we learn to evaluate experiences, accepting or rejecting them, **not by what they contribute to our overall actualisation tendency** (the organismic valuing process of 10.2) **but by whether they will bring positive regard from others**. The result is **incongruence** between the **self-concept** and the **experiential world**, the environment as we perceive it. In the glossary's terms, incongruence is a **discrepancy between a person's self-concept and aspects of his or her experience**.

Experiences that are incongruent with, or incompatible with, the self-concept become **threatening**, and the threat is felt as **anxiety**. Schultz's example: suppose your self-concept includes the belief that you **love all humanity**. When you meet someone towards whom you feel **hatred**, you are likely to become anxious, because hating does not fit your picture of yourself as a loving person.

To keep the self-concept intact, the hatred must be dealt with. Rogers named two ways of doing it:

- **Denial**: the threatening experience is kept out of awareness altogether. "I don't hate him; I don't feel anything about him."
- **Distortion**: the experience is admitted, but in a changed form that no longer threatens the self. "I'm not angry; I'm just concerned about the harm he does to others", or "He deserves it, so it isn't really hatred."

Either way, the person **defends against the anxiety** that comes with the threat, but at a cost. A part of the experiential field is **closed off**, and the result is a **rigidity** in some of the person's perceptions. The more of experience that has to be defended against, the less of reality the person can see, and the less of their organism they can use.

A clinical illustration. A young doctor whose self-concept is "I am a calm, caring professional who never resents patients" begins to feel intense irritation towards a demanding patient. The feeling is incongruent with the self-concept, so it arrives as a vague, free-floating anxiety and a dread of ward rounds. If she **denies** it, she simply cannot say what is wrong; if she **distorts** it, she may explain the dread as "tiredness" or decide the patient is "manipulative and needs firm handling", which lets the anger out in a form that fits her self-image. A Rogerian therapist would aim to create conditions safe enough for her to say, eventually, "I resent him, and I hate that I do", at which point the anxiety has something to attach to and the self-concept can widen to include it.

*Classic background:* in his 1959 formal theory Rogers added two refinements often found in older texts. Threat can be registered **before full awareness**, a process he called **subception** (a term he took from the perception research of Lazarus and McCleary); this is why the anxiety arrives without the person knowing what it is about. And if incongruence becomes very great and a strongly threatening experience breaks through suddenly, defence can fail and the self-structure can **break down into disorganisation**, the Rogerian account of an acute breakdown. Rogers's own collapse at Chicago (10.1) fits this description.

> **Trap.** Rogers's account of **anxiety** is not Freud's. For Freud, anxiety signals danger from **instinctual impulses** (neurotic anxiety), the **superego** (moral anxiety) or the outside world (objective anxiety), and it is managed by a long list of **unconscious** defence mechanisms. For Rogers, anxiety is the response to an experience that is **incongruent with the self-concept**, and the defences are just two, **denial and distortion** of perception. An option attributing "reaction formation" or "sublimation" to Rogers is borrowing from Freud.

### Congruence and Emotional Health

The positive side of the same principle: a person's level of **psychological adjustment and emotional health** depends on the **degree of congruence**, or compatibility, **between the self-concept and experience**.

Psychologically healthy people can perceive **themselves, other people and events much as they really are**. They are **open to new experiences** because nothing threatens their self-concept. Having been given **unconditional positive regard** as children, they never had to take **conditions of worth** inside themselves, so nothing obliges them to **deny or distort** what they perceive.

They feel **worthy under all conditions and in all situations** and can **use all of their experience**. They are free to develop and actualise every facet of the self, moving towards the goal of becoming a **fully functioning person** and living what Rogers called **"the good life"**.

> **Trap.** "Congruence" has **three** uses in this chapter, and examiners mix them up. (1) In the **personality theory**, congruence is the match between the **self-concept and experience**; its absence (incongruence) causes anxiety. (2) In the **research** (10.8), it is operationalised as the correlation between **perceived self and ideal self** on the Q-sort. (3) In the **therapy** (10.7), it is a quality of the **therapist**: being **genuine**, so that what the therapist outwardly shows matches what he or she actually feels. Read each question to see which one it means.

## 10.5 Characteristics of Fully Functioning Persons

For Rogers, the **fully functioning person** is the most desirable outcome of **psychological development and of social evolution** (Rogers, 1961). The glossary gives the definition: the fully functioning person is **Rogers's term for self-actualisation**, the development of **all facets of the self**. It is what a person becomes when the actualising tendency has not been blocked by conditions of worth, or when therapy has removed the blocks. Rogers listed several characteristics, which Schultz collects in a table.

**Table 10.1** Characteristics of fully functioning persons (Schultz's seven, in his order)

| # | Characteristic | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Awareness of all experience** | Nothing is denied or distorted; open to negative as well as positive feelings |
| 2 | **Freshness of appreciation for all experiences** | Each moment is lived fully, as new, rather than observed from a distance |
| 3 | **Trust in one's own behaviour and feelings** | Guided by one's own organismic reactions rather than others' opinions or social codes |
| 4 | **Freedom of choice, without inhibitions** | A sense of personal power; the future depends on one's own actions |
| 5 | **Creativity and spontaneity** | Flexible, adaptive, seeks challenge; does not need predictability or security |
| 6 | **Continual need to grow** | Keeps striving to make the most of one's potential |
| 7 | **In a state of actualising** | Always in process; never a finished, "actualised" product |

Each deserves a closer look, because the descriptions are where MCQs are set.

### Fully functioning persons are aware of all experiences

**No experience is distorted or denied**; all of it reaches the self. There is **no defensiveness**, because there is nothing to defend against: nothing threatens the self-concept. Fully functioning people are open to **positive feelings** such as **courage and tenderness** and to **negative feelings** such as **fear and pain**. In this sense they are **more emotional** than other people: they accept a **wider range** of positive and negative emotions and feel them **more intensely**.

This is the direct opposite of incongruence (10.4). The doctor in the earlier example who can say "I resent this patient" without feeling her identity is in danger is, in that respect, functioning fully.

> **Trap.** Fully functioning does **not** mean calm, controlled or free of negative emotion. Rogers's fully functioning person feels **fear and pain**, as well as courage and tenderness, **more intensely** than others, because none of it is screened out. An option describing the fully functioning person as "emotionally detached" or "free of anxiety-provoking feelings because they are suppressed" is wrong.

### Fully functioning persons live fully and richly in every moment

Every experience is potentially **fresh and new**. Experiences **cannot be predicted or anticipated**; they are **participated in fully rather than merely observed**. A fully functioning person at a family wedding is at the wedding, not mentally rehearsing what relatives will think of her job.

*Classic background:* in *On Becoming a Person* (1961) Rogers called this quality **existential living**.

### Fully functioning persons trust in their own organism

By this phrase Rogers meant that fully functioning people **trust their own reactions** rather than being steered by **other people's opinions**, by a **social code**, or by their **intellectual judgements**. Behaving in a way that **feels right** is a good guide to behaving in a way that will prove **satisfying**.

This is often misread as anti-intellectualism. Rogers did **not** say that fully functioning people ignore information from their own intellect or from others. He meant that **all data are accepted as congruent** with the self-concept. Because nothing is threatening, all information can be **perceived, evaluated and weighed accurately**, and the decision about what to do comes from considering **all the experiential data** together. The person is **unaware of making these calculations**, precisely because self-concept and experience are congruent, so the decisions **look more intuitive and emotional than intellectual**.

An analogy: an experienced clinician who "just knows" that a quiet patient is at risk is not ignoring evidence; she is integrating a great deal of it so fluently that the integration is not felt as reasoning. Rogers's fully functioning person does this with life decisions.

*Classic background:* Rogers called this **organismic trusting**. It is the adult form of the organismic valuing process of 10.2, now working without interference from conditions of worth.

> **Trap.** "Trust in one's own organism" does **not** mean ignoring the intellect or other people's views. All information, including intellectual and social information, is **taken in and weighed**; it simply does not threaten, so the result feels intuitive. Review questions in Schultz ask about the role of the intellect precisely because of this point.

### Fully functioning persons feel free to make choices without constraints or inhibitions

This freedom brings a **sense of power**. Such people know that what happens to them next rests on **their own actions**, rather than on their past, their current circumstances or the people around them. Neither inner pressure nor outside pressure **compels** them into a single way of acting.

*Classic background:* Rogers's term was **experiential freedom**. It is the personal basis for his free-will position in 10.6.

### Fully functioning persons are creative and live constructively and adaptively as environmental conditions change

Closely linked with **creativity** is **spontaneity**. Fully functioning people are **flexible** and actively **seek new experiences and challenges**. They do **not require predictability, security or freedom from tension**. Where a person living by conditions of worth clings to the familiar because the unfamiliar might expose them, the fully functioning person treats change as material for growth.

### Fully Functioning Persons are in a State of Actualizing

Rogers chose the word **actualising**, not **actualised**, for the fully functioning person. "Actualised" would imply a **finished or static personality**, which was not his meaning at all. Self-development is **always in progress**. In his often-quoted phrase, being fully functioning is "a direction, not a destination" (1961, p. 186). If striving and growth stop, the person loses **spontaneity, flexibility and openness**. The same emphasis on continuing change is built into the title of his best-known book, *On **Becoming** a Person* (1961).

> **Trap.** Schultz's review questions ask whether the fully functioning person can be called "self-actualised". The answer is **no**: Rogers deliberately said **actualising**, a continuing process, because "actualised" implies a finished state. "A direction, not a destination" (1961). Compare Allport's "becoming" (Chapter 7), which makes the same point.

### Being fully functioning is not always easy

Living this way means **continually testing, growing, striving and using all of one's potential**, a way of life full of **complexity and challenge**. Rogers did **not** describe fully functioning people as **happy, blissful or contented**, although at times they may be. A better description of their personality, he thought, is **enriching, exciting and meaningful**.

> **Trap.** The fully functioning person is **not defined by happiness**. Rogers explicitly avoided "happy", "blissful" and "contented" as descriptions; the right words are **enriching, exciting, meaningful**. This is a common "which of the following is NOT a characteristic" item.

> **Trap.** The number of characteristics differs between sources. Schultz's Table 10.1 lists **seven**. Rogers's own essay on "the good life" in *On Becoming a Person* concentrates on three (openness to experience, existential living, organismic trusting), and many secondary texts list five (adding experiential freedom and creativity). Answer according to the list the question is plainly using; if the question cites Schultz, use the seven.

## 10.6 Questions about Human Nature

Rogers's picture of human nature is one of the most optimistic in the book. Schultz rates him on the six standard issues as follows.

**Free will versus determinism: free will.** Rogers's position is clear. Fully functioning people have **free choice in creating their selves**; **no aspect of personality is predetermined** for them. This follows from the experiential freedom described in 10.5. A person bound by conditions of worth is less free in practice, but the capacity for choice remains and therapy can release it.

**Nature versus nurture: environment (nurture) is given prominence.** Here students often stumble. The **actualisation tendency is innate**, so there is a biological base. But Rogers held that the **actualising process itself is influenced more by social than by biological forces**. Whether the tendency flourishes or is stunted depends on the regard a child receives, the conditions of worth absorbed, and later relationships, including therapy. On Schultz's scale, then, Rogers gives prominence to the environment.

**Importance of the past versus the present: the present.** **Childhood experiences have some influence** on personality development, but **experiences later in life have more**. Our **present feelings** matter more to personality than the events of our childhood. This is why the person-centred therapist explores what the client is feeling now, in the session, rather than reconstructing a case history.

**Uniqueness versus universality: some universality, with room for uniqueness.** Rogers recognised a **universal** element in personality when he described characteristics that all fully functioning people share. But Schultz infers from his writing that there is scope for **uniqueness in the ways these characteristics are expressed**. Two fully functioning people are both open, trusting and creative, but the content of one person's creativity (music, say) may be utterly different from the other's (bringing up children, running a clinic).

**Equilibrium versus growth: growth.** The **ultimate and necessary goal of life is to become a fully functioning person**. Our outlook is **progressive rather than regressive**, directed towards **growth rather than stagnation**. We seek **challenge and stimulation** instead of the security of the familiar. This contrasts with Freud's tension-reduction model, in which the goal of behaviour is a return to equilibrium.

**Optimism versus pessimism: strongly optimistic.** A theorist who credits people with the **ability, motivation and responsibility** to understand and improve themselves obviously sees them in a positive light. Rogers believed we have a **basically healthy nature** and an **innate tendency to grow** and fulfil our potential. He never lost this optimism: in an interview at **85**, he said that his work with individuals and groups continually reinforced his positive view of human nature (1987, p. 118).

In Rogers's view we are **not doomed to conflict** with ourselves or with society. We are **not ruled by instinctive biological forces**, nor **controlled by events of the first 5 years of life** (both direct rejections of Freud). We experience the world **openly rather than defensively**. Emotional disturbances can happen, but they are **uncommon**. Through person-centred therapy people overcome their difficulties by drawing on their **inner resources**, the innate drive to actualise.

Rogers did not claim that people never behave badly. In *On Becoming a Person* he acknowledged that, out of defensiveness and inner fear, individuals can act with great cruelty, destructiveness, immaturity and antisocial hurtfulness; yet he described it as one of the most refreshing parts of his experience to work with such people and discover in them, as in everyone, **strongly positive directional tendencies** (Rogers, 1961, p. 27). The badness, on this view, is the product of defence, not of human nature.

Finally, the striving to become fully functioning **benefits society as well**. As more people in a culture become self-actualising, the **improvement of society** will naturally follow. This belief lay behind Rogers's later work on international conflict (10.1).

| Issue | Rogers's position | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | **Free will** | Fully functioning people freely create their selves; no aspect of personality is predetermined |
| Nature vs nurture | **Environment (nurture) given prominence** | The actualisation tendency is innate, but the actualising process depends more on social than on biological forces |
| Past vs present | **Present** | Childhood has some impact, but later experience and present feelings matter more |
| Uniqueness vs universality | **Some universality, with uniqueness in expression** | Fully functioning people share characteristics but express them in individual ways |
| Equilibrium vs growth | **Growth** | Life's necessary goal is to become fully functioning; we seek challenge, not the security of the familiar |
| Optimism vs pessimism | **Optimistic** | Human nature is basically healthy; disturbance is uncommon and comes from defence; personal growth improves society |

> **Trap.** On nature versus nurture, Rogers is rated as giving prominence to the **environment**, even though the actualisation tendency is **innate**. Students who reason "innate tendency, therefore nature" lose the mark. Similarly, on uniqueness versus universality he is **not** a pure uniqueness theorist like Allport; Schultz says he recognised a **universal** quality (shared characteristics) with room for unique expression.

> **Trap.** Rogers versus Freud on human nature (a standard review question): Freud saw people as driven by unconscious instincts, shaped decisively by the first 5 years, in permanent conflict with society, and pessimistically; Rogers saw them as conscious and rational, shaped more by the present, not doomed to conflict, and optimistically. Freud's goal is tension reduction; Rogers's is growth.

## 10.7 Assessment in Rogers's Theory

For Rogers there was only one legitimate way to assess personality: through the person's **subjective experiences**, the events of the person's life **as he or she perceives them and accepts them as real**. This follows from 10.3. If reality for each person is the perceived world, then any assessment that ignores the person's perceptions measures the wrong thing.

Rogers believed that his clients (he **never called them "patients"**) had the ability to **examine the roots of their own problems** and to **redirect the personality growth** that had been held back by some **incongruence between the self-concept and experience**. The assessor's role is therefore not to diagnose from outside but to help the client see from inside.

### Person-Centered Therapy

#### What the therapist does

In **person-centred therapy** Rogers explored the client's **feelings and attitudes towards the self and towards other people**. He **listened without preconceptions**, trying to understand the client's experiential world **as the client saw it**. The glossary definition captures the stance: person-centred therapy is the approach in which **the client is assumed to be responsible for changing his or her own personality**.

The **only predetermined belief** the person-centred therapist brings is the client's **inherent value and worth**. Clients are **accepted as they are**. The therapist offers them **unconditional positive regard**, makes **no judgements about their behaviour** and gives **no advice** about how to behave. **Everything centres on the client**, including responsibility for changing behaviour and for re-evaluating relationships.

Rogers saw a major advantage here. His approach does not depend on a **predetermined theoretical structure**, such as Freudian psychoanalysis, into which the therapist has to fit the patient's problem. A psychoanalyst listening to a young man's account of fights with his boss may already be listening for the father behind the boss; a Rogerian is listening only for what the young man feels and means.

Here is a brief illustration of the style, described rather than scripted. A client says she has come because she "should" be happy with her new job and isn't. A directive counsellor might ask about the job's demands or suggest ways to cope with them. A person-centred counsellor responds to the feeling inside the statement, reflecting back something like her sense of being stuck between what she is supposed to feel and what she does feel. Nothing is interpreted, nothing is advised; the client is helped to hear herself. Over several sessions she may notice that "should" is her father's voice, a condition of worth, and begin to sort out what she actually wants.

*Classic background:* the techniques usually taught under Rogers's name, **reflection of feeling**, restatement, clarification and **active listening**, are ways of communicating empathy. Rogers himself warned against treating them as mechanical tricks; they work only when they carry real understanding.

#### Why it is called person-centred: the three names

The therapy's names track its development. *Classic background:* it was introduced as **non-directive counselling** in *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942), renamed **client-centred therapy** in *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951), and from the 1970s Rogers increasingly spoke of a **person-centred approach**, signalling that the same attitudes applied in education, groups, families and international relations, not only in therapy. Schultz's explanation of the final name (see the opening) is that the capacity for change is **centred within the person**, with the therapist as facilitator.

#### The therapist conditions

Schultz reports the conditions under the research in 10.8, but they belong here as the working core of the therapy. Person-centred therapy is more likely to succeed when clients **perceive** their therapists as:

1. **Empathic and understanding**,
2. Showing **unconditional positive regard** for them, and
3. Showing **congruence**, that is, being **genuine** in the therapeutic relationship (Cain, 2013).

*Classic background:* these are the three **"core conditions"**, and they come from Rogers's famous 1957 paper on **the necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic personality change**. That paper actually lists **six** conditions:

| # | Rogers's 1957 condition | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Two persons are in **psychological contact** | A relationship exists |
| 2 | The client is in a state of **incongruence**, vulnerable or anxious | The client is hurting |
| 3 | The therapist is **congruent** (integrated, genuine) in the relationship | The therapist is real, not playing a role |
| 4 | The therapist experiences **unconditional positive regard** for the client | Acceptance without conditions |
| 5 | The therapist experiences **empathic understanding** of the client's internal frame of reference and tries to communicate it | Understanding from the inside |
| 6 | The client **perceives**, at least to a minimal degree, the therapist's empathy and unconditional positive regard | The client must feel it |

Conditions 3, 4 and 5 are the therapist's "core conditions". Condition 6 explains why Cain's (2013) wording stresses what clients **perceive**: an accepting therapist whose acceptance never reaches the client has not met the conditions. The logic is the developmental theory run backwards. **Conditional** regard created the incongruence; **unconditional** regard, delivered by a genuine and empathic person, removes the need to deny and distort, so the client's self-concept can expand to include the experience it had shut out.

> **Trap.** Know the **three core conditions**: **empathy** (empathic understanding), **unconditional positive regard** (acceptance, prizing, warmth), and **congruence** (genuineness). "Interpretation", "transference analysis", "advice", "diagnosis" and "confrontation" are not among them. Also note: **congruence** here is a quality of the **therapist**, not the client's self-ideal match (see the trap at the end of 10.4).

> **Trap.** Older Indian keys may ask for the number of conditions. **Six** is the 1957 "necessary and sufficient" list; **three** is the core-conditions list (therapist conditions only). Schultz names the three.

#### The limits Rogers admitted

Although Rogers regarded person-centred therapy as the **only worthwhile approach to personality assessment**, he did not think it was infallible. He recognised three limitations.

1. **It reaches only what is conscious.** Because the therapist focuses on subjective experience, he or she learns only about events the client **consciously expresses**. Experiences outside awareness remain hidden.
2. **Inference is risky.** If the therapist tries to infer too much about these non-conscious experiences, the inferences may reflect the **therapist's own projections** more than the client's actual experience.
3. **It depends on communication.** What the therapist learns depends on the client's **ability to communicate**. All communication is imperfect, so the therapist will inevitably see the client's world **imperfectly and incompletely**.

Within these limits, Rogers argued, person-centred therapy gives a **clearer view of a person's experiential world than any other form of assessment or therapy**.

#### Why he rejected psychoanalytic techniques

Rogers **opposed free association, dream analysis and case histories**. His reason was not that they produced false information but that they produced **dependence**. Such techniques make the therapist an expert with an aura of **authority**, who seems to know all about the client. They take **personal responsibility** away from clients, who may conclude that the therapist will solve their problems and that all they need do is sit back and follow the expert's instructions. That would undercut the very capacity (self-direction) the therapy exists to restore.

> **Trap.** Rogers rejected **free association, dream analysis and case histories** because they make the client **dependent** on the therapist and remove the client's **responsibility**, not because they are "unscientific". This "why" is a favourite question, and Schultz's chapter summary repeats it.

> **Trap.** The person-centred clinical interview differs from the psychoanalytic one on almost every dimension: the client (not the analyst) directs the content; the focus is on **present conscious feelings**, not repressed childhood material; the therapist **reflects** rather than **interprets**; there is **no predetermined theoretical structure**; and the client is a **"client"**, an equal, not a "patient" being treated.

### Encounter Groups

Having shown that person-centred therapy could help individuals who were out of touch with their feelings and closed to experience, Rogers wanted to bring that enhanced psychological health to **larger numbers of people**. He developed a **group technique** in which people could learn more about themselves and about how they related to, or **encountered**, one another. He called it the **encounter group** (Rogers, 1970).

The format, which is directly examinable:

| Feature | Encounter groups |
|---|---|
| Group size | **8 to 15** people |
| Total time | Typically **20 to 60 hours**, spread over several sessions |
| Structure | **No formal structure or agenda** at the start |
| Person in charge | A **facilitator**, not a leader in the usual sense |
| Facilitator's job | To create an atmosphere in which members can express themselves and focus on **how others perceive them**; to make it easier for members to gain **self-insight** and become more **fully functioning** |
| Rogers's expectation | **Most (though not all)** participants would become more fully functioning |

A typical early session is uncomfortable precisely because nothing is organised. Members wait for the facilitator to take charge; when she does not, frustration surfaces, and the group starts dealing with it. Gradually people drop the polite masks they use in ordinary life and begin to tell each other honestly how they come across, which is the "encounter".

Not all psychologists agreed that the groups worked. A **large-scale analysis** of studies of encounter groups (**Faith, Wong, & Carpenter, 1995**) found results **comparable to those of traditional psychotherapies**. It also found that **larger groups meeting more frequently** produced **more favourable outcomes** than smaller groups meeting less often.

Encounter groups are **less popular today** than when Rogers himself was promoting them, but some of his followers still run them as a way of helping people develop their potential and become more fully functioning.

*Classic background:* the encounter group was one branch of the 1960s **human potential movement**. Its forerunner was the **T-group** (training group) or sensitivity-training group, developed in the late 1940s from Kurt Lewin's work and the National Training Laboratories in the United States. Rogers's version, the "basic encounter group", put more weight on personal growth and less on learning about group dynamics.

> **Trap.** In Faith, Wong and Carpenter (1995), **larger** groups that met **more often** did **better**, not smaller groups. And the outcomes were **comparable to** (not better than, not worse than) traditional psychotherapy. The person who runs the group is a **facilitator**, not a leader or therapist in the directive sense.

### Psychological Tests

Rogers **neither used nor developed** psychological tests to assess personality. Other psychologists, however, have built tests to measure aspects of the experiential world.

- **The Experience Inventory (Coan, 1972)** is a **self-report questionnaire** that tries to measure **openness or receptivity to experience**, one of the characteristics of the fully functioning person.
- **The Experiencing Scale (Gendlin & Tomlinson, 1967)** measures **self-trust**. Its method is unusual: the person being assessed **does not answer test items directly**. Instead he or she talks freely about anything at all, and the **recorded comments are later rated** for the degree of self-trust they show. Raters look, for example, at how far the person treats his or her **feelings as an important source of information** for deciding how to behave, or how far the person **denies that personal feelings influence decisions**.

The Experiencing Scale has been used alongside person-centred therapy.

> **Study: Klein, Malthieu, Gendlin, and Kiesler (1969).** Clients who **improved most** during therapy showed an **increase in self-trust** from before to after therapy. Those who improved **little** showed **little or no increase**. And people with **less severe** emotional disorders showed **more** self-trust than those with **more severe** disorders. Self-trust, one of the marks of the fully functioning person, thus tracked both therapeutic progress and the severity of disturbance.

*Classic background:* Eugene Gendlin was Rogers's student and colleague at Chicago and later developed the experiential technique known as **focusing**, built on the idea that change comes when clients attend to their bodily felt sense of a problem. The journal named in 10.9, *Person-Centered & Experiential Psychotherapies*, carries both traditions.

| Measure | Author (year) | What it measures | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Experience Inventory** | **Coan (1972)** | Openness to experience | Self-report questionnaire |
| **Experiencing Scale** | **Gendlin & Tomlinson (1967)** | Self-trust | Ratings of freely spoken, recorded comments |
| **Q-sort** (10.8) | **Stephenson (1953)**; used by Rogers | Self-concept (perceived and ideal self) | Sorting self-descriptive statements |

> **Trap.** Rogers devised **no** tests. The Experience Inventory is **Coan's** (openness); the Experiencing Scale is **Gendlin and Tomlinson's** (self-trust, rated from speech, not a questionnaire); the Q-sort is **Stephenson's**. An option crediting Rogers with any of them is wrong.

## 10.8 Research on Rogers's Theory

### Rogers's attitude to research, and the recorded session

Rogers rated **person-centred interviews**, which depend on clients' self-reports, above **experimental methods**. In his view the more orthodox scientific approaches told us less about personality than his clinical approach did. He once said, "I never learned anything from research" (quoted in Bergin & Strupp, 1972, p. 314), adding that most of his research had simply confirmed what he already felt to be true.

That remark is misleading if taken alone, because Rogers's actual record is that of an energetic researcher. He did not use laboratory methods to **collect** data about personality, but he did use them to **verify and confirm** his clinical observations. And he was enthusiastic about **research on the therapy session itself**, an idea many clinicians resisted as a **violation of privacy**.

His decisive contribution was a procedure that was **radical at the time** (Goldfried, 2007): he **recorded and filmed therapy sessions** so that researchers could study the **client-therapist interaction**. Before this, the only data from therapy were the **therapist's reconstructions after the event**, notes written up some time after the session ended. Such notes suffer from **memory distortion** over time, and a written record also misses the client's **emotional state and body language**. A facial expression or a tone of voice can sometimes say more than the words. With recordings, **everything became available for study**.

Rogers described the recording as a **microscope** for examining the "molecules of personality change" (1974, p. 120). He **always obtained the client's permission** to record, and he found that the presence of the equipment **did not interfere** with the course of therapy.

> **Trap.** Rogers's innovation was **recording (and filming) therapy sessions** for research, replacing therapists' after-the-fact notes. He was not the inventor of the case-study method (Freud) or the Q-sort (Stephenson). And despite saying he "never learned anything from research", he was a pioneer of **psychotherapy process and outcome research**. An item asking for the "microscope" metaphor wants "molecules of personality change" (Rogers, 1974).

### Evaluating Person-Centered Therapy

Rogers and his colleagues also studied **how the self-concept changes during a course of therapy**. They analysed recorded sessions with both **qualitative and quantitative** techniques in the scientific tradition (despite Rogers's claim not to be a scientist), applying **rating scales** and **content analyses** of what clients said, to track changes in the self-concept.

#### The Q-sort technique

Much of this research used the **Q-sort technique**, a procedure devised by **William Stephenson (1953)**. The client is given a large set of statements about the self and **sorts them into categories ranging from "most descriptive of me" to "least descriptive of me"**. The Q-sort is therefore a way of **defining the client's self-image empirically**. The glossary calls it a **self-report technique for assessing aspects of the self-concept**.

The statements are simple first-person descriptions covering feelings, social behaviour and maturity, for instance a statement about feeling helpless or one about enjoying solitude.

The Q-sort can be used in several ways. The one Rogers made famous is the **double sort**:

1. The client first sorts the statements to describe the **perceived self**, "me as I am now".
2. The client then sorts the **same statements** to describe the **ideal self**, "the person I would most like to be".
3. Using the **correlational method**, the researcher computes how closely the two sorts agree. A **high positive correlation** means the perceived self is close to the ideal self (congruence in the research sense); a correlation **near zero** means no relationship between how the person sees herself and how she wants to be; a **negative** correlation would mean the person sees herself as the opposite of her ideal.
4. Repeating the double sort **before and after therapy** shows how much the self-concept has changed.

*Classic background:* in the standard procedure the client must place a fixed number of cards in each pile, so that the piles form an approximately **normal distribution** (few cards at the extremes, many in the middle); this "forced" distribution stops people from calling every statement highly characteristic. Stephenson's broader **Q-methodology** correlated whole sorts with each other (persons or self-descriptions) rather than test scores across many people, which is the more usual "R" approach.

> **Trap.** The Q-sort was **devised by William Stephenson (1953)**. **Rogers used it**; he did not invent it. This is one of the most frequently tested facts in the chapter. Also do not confuse the **Q-sort** with **Cattell's Q-data** (Chapter 8), which is simply questionnaire data, one of Cattell's three data sources (L-, Q- and T-data).

#### The case of Mrs. Oak

Rogers also measured how far the self-concept changed between the period before therapy and the period after it. His best-known example is a client he called **"Mrs. Oak"** (Rogers, 1954).

> **Case: Mrs. Oak (Rogers, 1954).** At the start, the correlation between her **perceived self and ideal self** was **+.36**. **A year after therapy** it had risen to **+.79**. To Rogers, this showed that her perceived self had become **much more congruent with her ideal self**, and he concluded that the dramatic change reflected an **increase in emotional health**.

Her choice of statements changed in a meaningful way. Before therapy she saw herself as **dependent and passive** and felt **rejected by other people**. After therapy she believed she was **much more like the self she wanted to be**; she felt **more secure, less afraid, and better able to relate to others**.

**Table 10.2** Mrs. Oak's Q-sort description of her perceived self before therapy and 12 months after (summary)

Schultz's table sets out, in two columns, six Q-sort statements Mrs. Oak chose as most like her perceived self before therapy and six she chose 12 months after it. The items themselves are not reproduced here; the table below summarises what the two columns show.

| | Before therapy | 12 months after therapy |
|---|---|---|
| What the table lists | Six statements she placed as most characteristic of herself | Six statements she placed as most characteristic of herself |

Two examples, paraphrased: before therapy she endorsed a statement to the effect that she had to shield herself with excuses and rationalising; afterwards she endorsed one saying that she expressed her emotions freely. The pair is a neat illustration of the theory: **distortion** (rationalising) has given way to **openness to experience**. (Schultz's source for the table is Rogers's chapter on Mrs. Oak in Rogers and Dymond's edited volume *Psychotherapy and Personality Change*, 1954.)

#### Butler and Haigh (1954)

> **Study: Butler and Haigh (1954).** These associates of Rogers measured the **discrepancy between perceived self and ideal self in 25 clients**. The discrepancy **decreased** over time during and after therapy. **Before therapy** the **average correlation** between perceived and ideal self was **.01**, effectively no relationship. **After therapy** it was **+.31**.

Note the contrast with Mrs. Oak. Her single case went from **+.36 to +.79**; the group of 25 clients went from **.01 to +.31**. Both show movement in the predicted direction, but the group figures are lower at both ends.

*Classic background:* the Chicago studies reported in Rogers and Dymond (1954) were among the first outcome studies in psychotherapy to use control procedures, including a **waiting period** before therapy against which change during therapy could be compared, a design that reflects the young Rogers's lessons about control groups on the farm (10.1).

> **Trap.** Memorise the two pairs separately. **Mrs. Oak (Rogers, 1954): +.36 → +.79**, one client, a year after therapy. **Butler and Haigh (1954): .01 → +.31**, **25** clients, average correlations. Examiners swap them.

#### The therapist conditions and the Sexual Satisfaction Q-Sort

More recent research has found that person-centred therapy is more likely to succeed when clients **perceive** their therapists as (1) **empathic and understanding**, (2) showing **unconditional positive regard**, and (3) showing **congruence**, being **genuine** in the relationship (Cain, 2013). (These core conditions are discussed fully in 10.7.)

The Q-sort method has also been extended to new areas. A **Sexual Satisfaction Q-Sort** asks people to sort **63 statements** about their behaviours, feelings and experiences during sexual satisfaction. Early findings point to **four categories**, and most people's sorts fall into one or more of them: experiences that are **partner focused**, **orgasm focused**, **emotional and masculine**, or **relational and feminine** (McClelland, 2014a, 2014b).

#### Conditional Positive Regard

> **Study: Assor, Roth, and Deci (2004).** Research on **three generations of women** and on **college students** showed that parents' use of **conditional positive regard** did **succeed** in producing the behaviours the parents wanted. But self-reports from children of such parents revealed **poor coping skills, fluctuating self-esteem, low self-worth, a feeling that their parents disapproved of them, and resentment towards their parents**. Children whose parents did **not** use conditional regard reported **none** of these negative effects.

The finding is worth stating precisely, because it is exactly what Rogers predicted. Conditional regard is not ineffective as a way of controlling behaviour; it works. The cost is paid in the child's self-regard. A father who withdraws warmth whenever his son skips tuition may well get perfect attendance, along with a son whose self-worth rises and falls with his father's mood.

#### Organismic Valuing Process

There is **some evidence** supporting Rogers's concept of the **organismic valuing process**. Studies also suggest that **positive self-regard may be less prevalent in a collectivist culture such as Japan** than in an **individualistic culture such as the United States** (Heine, Lehman, Markus, & Kitayama, 1999; Joseph & Linley, 2005; Sheldon, Arndt, & Houser-Marko, 2003).

The cultural finding raises a real question about generality. Rogers's ideal of a self that trusts its own organism above social codes and others' opinions fits an individualistic culture well. In cultures that value harmony with the family and group, a strong drive for positive self-regard may be neither as common nor as obviously healthy. (Schultz's Highlights box puts it as "positive self-regard may be more important in individualistic cultures".)

*Classic background:* Sheldon, Arndt and Houser-Marko's (2003) study, titled as a search for the organismic valuing process, reported that people's goal choices tended to drift over time towards goals that are better for well-being, which they interpreted as support for an innate valuing tendency.

### Openness to Experience

> **Study: Chodorkoff (1954).** This classic study tested Rogers's claim that **fully functioning people are open to all experience**, whereas psychologically unhealthy people **put up defences** against experiences that threaten their self-image. **College students** completed a Q-sort describing themselves. Separately, **clinicians** prepared a Q-sort description of each student, based on a range of data including responses to the **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** and the **Rorschach inkblot test**. On the basis of these clinical measures the students were divided into **good-adjustment and poor-adjustment groups**.
>
> **Perceptual defence** was then measured by comparing reactions to **neutral words** (such as *table*) with reactions to supposedly **threatening words** (such as *penis*). **All** subjects were **slower to perceive threatening words** than neutral ones, but the effect was **more marked in the poorly adjusted group**. The good-adjustment group showed **significantly less perceptual defence**. In addition, the **closer the agreement** between a student's self-description and the clinicians' description, the **better adjusted** the student was.

Two findings, both supporting Rogers. First, the well adjusted defend less against threatening material: they let it into awareness sooner, which is openness to experience measured at the level of perception. Second, the well adjusted see themselves **as others see them**: their self-concept matches the evidence, which is congruence measured from the outside.

*Classic background:* in perceptual-defence experiments of this period, words were usually flashed for very brief, gradually increasing exposures (with a tachistoscope) and the exposure needed to recognise each word was recorded; a higher recognition threshold for threatening words counted as defence. This is the laboratory counterpart of Rogers's **denial**, and of subception (10.4).

> **Trap.** In Chodorkoff (1954) **all** subjects showed some perceptual defence; the difference was one of **degree**, greater in the **poorly adjusted**. And better adjustment went with **closer agreement between self-description and clinicians' description**, not with a more flattering self-description.

### Acceptance of Self

> **Study: Medinnus and Curtis (1963).** A study of **56 mothers** examined the link between their **self-acceptance** and how far they **accepted their children as they actually were** rather than as they wished them to be. The rationale came from Rogers: someone who sees and accepts her own nature realistically, so that her **perceived and ideal selves match**, should find it easier to **accept other people**, her children included, for what they actually are. Self-accepting mothers differed significantly from those who were not: they were **more accepting of their children's nature**, and the **child's degree of self-acceptance depended partly on the mother's** self-acceptance.

This is the reciprocity of positive regard and positive self-regard (10.4) in action: a mother who has positive self-regard can give unconditional regard, and her child, receiving it, develops self-acceptance in turn.

#### Racial identity

> **Study: Pedrotti and Edwards (2010).** Among **multiracial adults in the United States**, those with a **high degree of self-acceptance about their racial identities** showed **stronger positive growth** than those with less self-acceptance about their racial identities.

#### Parental behaviour

A cluster of studies links parents' behaviour to children's self-esteem, creativity and authenticity, all in the directions Rogers's theory predicts.

| Study | Who | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Baldwin (1949)** | Parents and children | Parents who **accepted their children unconditionally** and used **democratic** child-rearing had children with **higher self-esteem and greater emotional security** than parents who did not accept their children and were **authoritarian** |
| **Coopersmith (1967)** | Parents of children with high and low self-esteem | Parents of **high**-self-esteem children showed **affection** and used **reward rather than punishment**; parents of **low**-self-esteem children were more **aloof, less loving** and more likely to **punish** |
| **Harrington, Block, & Block (1987)** | Adolescents | Those whose parents gave **unconditional positive regard** and let them express themselves without restraint developed **greater creative potential** |
| **Harter, Marold, Whitesell, & Cobbs (1996)** | Adolescents | Those with **unconditional** regard were **more confident and hopeful** about getting support from others in future and acted **more consistently with their own interests and talents**; those with **conditional** regard lacked this confidence and hope and acted **more often against their true selves** to win parental support and approval |
| **Nolan (2008)** | High school students | Those who received **unconditional positive regard from their teachers** scored **higher in positive self-esteem** |

The Harter study is the closest to the theory's core. Teenagers who had to earn their parents' approval did what conditions of worth predict: they behaved in ways that **contradicted their own sense of who they were** in order to get support. Rogers would call this living by an external valuing process; later writers have called it "false-self" behaviour. Note also that Nolan's finding extends unconditional positive regard beyond parents to **teachers**.

#### Age

> **Study: Shallcross, Ford, Floerke, and Mauss (2013).** **Growing older** was found to **increase** the level of self-adjustment and to **decrease** feelings of **anger and anxiety**.

### Emotional Adjustment

Several studies support Rogers's proposal that **incongruence between perceived self and ideal self** indicates **poor emotional adjustment**. Researchers have concluded that **the greater the discrepancy**, the higher the levels of **anxiety, insecurity, self-doubt, depression, social incompetence** and other psychological disorders.

A large **inconsistency between perceived and ideal self** also goes with **low self-actualisation and low self-esteem**. Schultz cites Achenbach and Zigler (1963), Mahoney and Hartnett (1973), Gough, Fioravanti, and Lazzari (1983), Moretti and Higgins (1990), and Straumann, Vookles, Berenstein, Chaiken, and Higgins (1991). And people with a large perceived-ideal discrepancy were **rated by others** as **awkward, confused and unfriendly** (Gough, Lazzari, & Fioravanti, 1978), so the incongruence is visible from outside, not just felt from within.

*Classic background:* the Moretti and Higgins and the Strauman (Schultz spells it Straumann) studies come from E. Tory Higgins's **self-discrepancy theory**, which refines Rogers's single perceived-ideal gap into separate gaps. In that theory a gap between the actual self and the **ideal** self (who I hope to be) is linked especially to **dejection and depression**, while a gap between the actual self and the **ought** self (who I feel I should be) is linked especially to **agitation and anxiety**.

#### Temperament and unrealised potential: Ford (1991)

Rogers held that **failure to realise our innate actualisation tendency can lead to maladjustment**. One researcher tested this using the inherited temperaments proposed by **Buss and Plomin**: **Emotionality, Activity and Sociability (EAS)**.

> **Study: Ford (1991).** The participants were **male and female college students**. Using the **EAS Temperament Survey**, the students' **parents** were asked to **recall** their children's temperaments when the children were **very young**. These childhood temperament profiles were then compared with the students' **current self-perceptions** on the same three temperaments. The results supported Rogers: **the greater the discrepancy between childhood potential and adult realisation, the greater the level of maladjustment**.

The logic: temperament in early childhood is treated as an index of innate potential; the adult's current self-perception is treated as what has been realised. A child who was highly sociable but who grew into an adult who sees himself as withdrawn has, on this reasoning, failed to actualise part of his nature, and Ford found such people more maladjusted.

> **Trap.** Ford's study used **Buss and Plomin's EAS** dimensions (**E**motionality, **A**ctivity, **S**ociability), with **parents' recall** of early temperament set against **students' current self-perceptions**. The discrepancy measured was between **childhood potential** and **adult realisation**, not between perceived self and ideal self.

### Highlights: Research on Rogers's Ideas

Schultz's Highlights box summarises the research. In brief, research on Rogers's approach has found that:

- **Positive self-regard** may matter **more in individualistic cultures** (Heine et al., 1999, and others).
- **Fully functioning people are open to all experiences** (Chodorkoff, 1954).
- A **child's self-acceptance** depends in part on the **mother's self-acceptance** (Medinnus & Curtis, 1963).
- Children whose parents **accept them unconditionally** have **high self-esteem** (Baldwin, 1949; Coopersmith, 1967).
- People with **incongruence between perceived and ideal self** are **poorly adjusted emotionally** and have **low self-esteem and self-actualisation** (Achenbach & Zigler, 1963, and others).
- **Failing to realise our innate potential** can lead to **maladjustment** (Ford, 1991).

## 10.9 Reflections on Rogers's Theory

### Criticisms

Rogers insisted that the **only way to explore personality is through person-centred therapy**, examining a person's **subjective experiences** by listening to the client's **self-reports**. The criticisms follow from that insistence.

1. **He ignored what the client is not aware of.** Critics argue that Rogers neglected factors outside the client's conscious awareness that could nonetheless influence behaviour. Rogers himself conceded the point in principle (10.7: the therapist learns only what the client consciously expresses), but he built the whole method around conscious report anyway. A psychoanalyst would say that the most important material is exactly what the client cannot report.
2. **Self-reports can be distorted.** People may distort accounts of their subjective experience: **repressing** some events, **elaborating or inventing** others, to hide their true nature and present an **idealised self-image**. The irony is sharp. Rogers's own theory says that incongruent people **deny and distort** experience to protect the self-concept, so the very clients who most need help are, by his account, the least able to report accurately on themselves. A Q-sort filled out by such a person measures the defended self, not necessarily the real one.

The chapter suggests further points of evaluation that examiners sometimes add to the list. Rogers's clinical sample was mostly **young, bright, verbal college students with adjustment problems** (10.1), which raises doubts about how far the theory applies to severely disturbed patients or to other populations. The cultural research on positive self-regard (10.8) raises the question of whether a theory centred on an independent, self-trusting self fits collectivist cultures equally well. And Rogers claimed not to be a scientist and not to learn from research, while depending heavily on quantitative methods (correlations, rating scales, Q-sorts) to support his claims.

*Classic background:* other textbooks commonly add that some central concepts, notably the actualising tendency and the organismic valuing process, are **difficult to define operationally and test**, and that the theory says relatively little about how specific behaviours develop.

> **Trap.** The criticisms Schultz names are (1) **ignoring unconscious factors** and (2) **relying on self-reports that may be distorted** (repression, invention, an idealised self-image). "Rogers ignored the self" or "Rogers produced no research" are **not** criticisms of Rogers; the self was his core concept and he stimulated a large research literature. "Which is NOT a criticism of Rogers?" items are often built on these.

### The Influence of World War II on Person-Centered Therapy

Rogers's therapy caught on quickly, and Schultz explains why with a piece of social history. Its rapid acceptance was helped by conditions in the United States at the **end of World War II (1945)**. **Veterans** returning from overseas needed help readjusting to civilian life. That created a demand for **psychologists** and for a counselling technique that could be **learned and put into practice quickly**.

Training in traditional **psychoanalysis** required a **medical degree** and a long period of specialisation. Person-centred therapy, by contrast, was described by one analyst as simple, informal and brief, requiring little training (DeCarvalho, 1999, p. 142).

**More than 400 college counselling centres** based on Rogers's teachings were set up after the war under the auspices of the **Veterans Administration** to help returning veterans adjust. Client-centred therapy received an enormous boost, and **hundreds more colleges** established their own counselling centres. Rogers later acknowledged that the stresses of post-war America had **catapulted his method and his name** to worldwide prominence as the leader of a new approach to understanding and treating personality (see Barrett-Lennard, 2012; McCarthy, 2014).

> **Trap.** The rapid spread of person-centred therapy is credited by Schultz to **World War II (1945)**, **returning veterans**, the **Veterans Administration** and **more than 400 college counselling centres**, and to the fact that, unlike psychoanalysis, it needed **no medical degree** and **little training**. It is not attributed to the 1960s encounter movement, which came later.

### Acceptance of Rogers's Therapy

Rogers's therapy has since been applied widely, both as a **treatment for emotional disturbance** and as a way of **enhancing self-image**. In **business** it has been used to train **managers**. In the **helping professions** it is used to train **clinical psychologists, social workers and counsellors**.

Psychotherapists of many orientations have also adopted some of Rogers's **core concepts** in their own work with clients, so the person-centred approach remains influential in counselling and psychotherapy (see Bohart, 2014; Hazler, 2011; Kirschenbaum & Jourdan, 2005; Patterson & Joseph, 2007). Almost every modern training programme, whatever its school, teaches empathic listening and a non-judgemental stance as foundations of the therapeutic alliance; that is Rogers's legacy, often without his name attached.

The institutional numbers are examinable:

- **More than 200 training centres**, mostly in **Europe**, promote Rogers's form of therapy.
- **Several dozen journals** are devoted to research on and applications of his ideas (Murdock, 2008).
- The journal ***Person-Centered & Experiential Psychotherapies*** began publication in **2002** and remains influential.

### Rogers's Personality Theory

Rogers's **personality theory** has been **less influential than his psychotherapy**, but it too has received wide recognition, particularly for its emphasis on the **self-concept**. Rogers himself did **not** believe he had influenced academic or scientific psychology. Even so, his theory and therapy opened up three research fields:

- what actually happens in **psychotherapy**,
- the **interaction between client and therapist**, and
- the **self-concept**.

The way psychology defines the self, in theory and in measurement alike, owes a great deal to him. Modern self-esteem research, self-discrepancy theory and the study of the therapeutic alliance all descend in part from him.

Schultz closes with a portrait of the man's range. Rogers's background combined **clinic, lecture hall and laboratory**. He drew on extensive experience with emotionally disturbed clients and on the intellectual stimulation of colleagues and students, and he attracted large numbers of followers who continue to test his ideas in the clinic and the laboratory.

> **Trap.** Schultz judges Rogers's **therapy more influential than his personality theory**, and records that Rogers **did not think** he had influenced academic psychology. The theory's main lasting contribution is to research on the **self-concept**. An option claiming that Rogers's personality theory "eclipsed his therapy in influence" is wrong.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the chapter. If you can trace each one from the life to the research, you understand Rogers rather than just remembering him.

1. **One motive, two valuing systems.** The single **actualisation tendency** drives everything, and it is steered by the innate **organismic valuing process**. The trouble starts when a second, learned valuing system, **conditions of worth**, takes over. Almost every clinical idea in the chapter (incongruence, anxiety, denial, distortion, the fully functioning person, therapy) is about which of the two valuing systems is in charge.
2. **From outside to inside.** Positive regard from others becomes positive self-regard within; conditional regard becomes conditions of worth; parental judgement becomes a "parental surrogate" in the self-concept. Development, for Rogers, is the internalisation of how one was treated.
3. **The cause dictates the cure.** If conditional regard produced incongruence, then a relationship of **unconditional positive regard, empathy and genuineness** should undo it. The therapy is not a separate invention bolted onto the theory; it is the developmental theory run in reverse, with the therapist providing what the parents did not.
4. **Subjective experience is the data.** The experiential world (10.3) is the person's reality; therapy listens to it (10.7); research measures it through self-sorts and recorded talk (10.8); and the main criticism is that this data can be distorted (10.9).
5. **Congruence in three places.** Self-concept and experience (theory), perceived self and ideal self (research), the therapist's inner feeling and outer expression (therapy). The research tests the theory with the second sense, and the therapy produces change through the third.
6. **The life is in the theory.** An emotion-suppressing home, a lonely boy relying on his own experience, a revolt in China, research that overturned his expectations, a breakdown that exposed his belief that he could be liked but not loved, and recovery through an accepting relationship: Rogers lived each stage of his own theory before he wrote it down.

### Rogers and the other theorists in Schultz

| Theorist | Point of contact with Rogers | Key difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Freud** (Ch. 2) | The superego is paralleled by **conditions of worth**; both describe defence against threatening experience | Freud: unconscious instincts, first 5 years decisive, many defences, pessimism. Rogers: conscious self, present decisive, two defences (denial, distortion), optimism |
| **Adler** (Ch. 4) | Both see people as striving forward and as shaped by how they perceive their situation | Adler's striving starts from **inferiority feelings**; Rogers's actualising tendency is a positive push present from the womb |
| **Horney** (Ch. 5) | Horney's **real self versus idealised self** and her "tyranny of the shoulds" parallel Rogers's self-concept, ideal self and conditions of worth; both trace trouble to a lack of genuine warmth in childhood (Horney's basic anxiety, Rogers's conditional regard) | Horney's idealised self-image is a neurotic product; Rogers's ideal self is part of every normal self-concept, and only a large gap signals trouble |
| **Allport** (Ch. 7) | Conscious, present-centred, optimistic; personality as "becoming" | Allport built a trait psychology and studied healthy adults mainly through personal documents; Rogers built a therapy |
| **Maslow** (Ch. 9) | Fellow humanist; both make actualisation the goal of life | Maslow: a **hierarchy** of needs, self-actualisation at the top and reached by few. Rogers: a **single** tendency at work from the womb; the ideal is the **fully functioning person**, who is always "actualising" |
| **Kelly** (Ch. 11) | Both are phenomenological: behaviour follows from how the person construes or perceives the world. (Schultz notes that Kelly joined Ohio State in the post-war years as Rogers's replacement.) | Kelly's emphasis is cognitive (personal constructs); Rogers's is emotional and self-evaluative |
| **Skinner** (Ch. 12) | Both rate the **environment** above heredity, and both studied behaviour change empirically | See below |

### The Rogers and Skinner contrast

Schultz's suggested reading by Evans (1975) sets Rogers's humanistic view against **B. F. Skinner's** behaviourist view, and the contrast is a favourite in Indian interviews because the two men stand at opposite poles of Schultz's questions about human nature.

| Issue | Rogers | Skinner |
|---|---|---|
| What psychology studies | Subjective, conscious experience; the self | Observable behaviour; no need for an inner self |
| Cause of behaviour | The person's perceptions and the actualising tendency | Environmental contingencies of reinforcement |
| Free will vs determinism | Free will; the person creates the self | Determinism; freedom is an illusion |
| Role of the helper | Facilitator who provides conditions for self-directed growth | Designer of contingencies that shape behaviour |
| Method | Phenomenological; self-report, recorded sessions, Q-sort | Experimental analysis of behaviour, mostly with animals |

*Classic background:* the two debated the control of human behaviour face to face at the American Psychological Association convention in **1956**, and the exchange was published the same year in *Science*. Skinner argued that behaviour is already controlled by the environment and should be controlled deliberately and well; Rogers argued that the person's capacity for self-direction must be preserved and that the values guiding any control cannot themselves come from science.

> **Trap.** Rogers and Skinner both give the **environment** prominence over heredity in Schultz's ratings, so "nature vs nurture" does **not** separate them. **Free will vs determinism** does: Rogers, free will; Skinner, determinism.

## Worked example

*Arjun, 23, a final-year engineering student, comes to his college counselling centre complaining of exam anxiety, poor sleep and "a constant feeling that I'm faking it". He is preparing, at his parents' wish, for a competitive government-service exam. He describes his parents as "very supportive": they were delighted when he topped his school and "went quiet" for days after a bad result. He plays the tabla well and lights up when he talks about teaching it to children on weekends, but calls it "just a hobby, a waste of time really". When his father phones he gets headaches, though he insists, "I'm not angry with him at all. He only wants the best for me." He asks the counsellor to tell him whether he should keep preparing for the exam.*

Reason through it with the chapter's concepts, in the order a Rogerian would.

**What is the core problem, in Rogers's terms?** **Incongruence** between Arjun's **self-concept** ("I am a dutiful son who wants what my parents want, and music is unimportant") and his **experience** (resentment towards his father, flatness in engineering, absorption and joy in music). The self-concept includes an **ideal self** built around the exam; his perceived self increasingly fails to match it, which he feels as "faking it".

**Where did the incongruence come from?** From **conditional positive regard**. His parents' warmth arrived with top marks and disappeared after failures. Over the years he internalised the pattern as **conditions of worth**: he is worthy only when he achieves what they value. His **positive self-regard** is therefore available only under those conditions, and the self-concept now acts as a **parental surrogate**, judging him exactly as they did. This mirrors Assor and Tai (2012), whose adolescents' self-worth swung with their grades under conditional maternal regard, and Assor, Roth and Deci (2004), whose children of conditional parents complied but showed fluctuating self-esteem and resentment.

**Why is he anxious, and why can't he say about what?** Experiences that contradict the self-concept (anger at his father, the wish to teach music) are **threatening** and are felt as **anxiety**. Because they are kept out of clear awareness, the anxiety is free-floating: it attaches to the exam, to sleep, to the phone calls.

**Which defences is he using?** Both of Rogers's two. **Denial**: "I'm not angry with him at all." **Distortion**: the resentment surfaces in a disguised form (headaches when his father calls), and the valued experience of music is admitted only in a devalued form ("just a hobby, a waste of time"). Each keeps the self-concept intact at the cost of closing off part of his **experiential field**.

**What has happened to his organismic valuing process?** It is still working: his felt aliveness when teaching tabla is exactly the kind of evaluation by which the organism marks an experience as promoting actualisation. But it has been overruled by the external valuing system, conditions of worth, which judges experience by whether it will win approval.

**What would a person-centred counsellor do, and not do?** She would provide the three core conditions: **unconditional positive regard** (accepting Arjun whether he takes the exam or not, whether he is angry or not), **empathy** (understanding from inside his frame of reference how it feels to be the good son who is quietly suffocating), and **congruence** (being genuine rather than playing an expert). She would **not** answer his request to tell him what to do, because that would put her in the position of the parents and make him dependent; responsibility for the decision stays with him. She would **not** interpret his headaches as displaced Oedipal hostility, take a detailed case history or ask him to free-associate. She would reflect the feeling in what he says, for example the contrast between how he speaks about the exam and how he speaks about music, and let him hear it.

**What change would the theory predict?** As the relationship removes the threat, Arjun should become able to admit the denied experience ("I am angry, and I love music, and I am still a good son"). His self-concept widens, conditions of worth lose force, and his **ideal self** may itself be revised so that it is genuinely his. Movement is towards the **fully functioning person**: more open to negative as well as positive feelings, more willing to **trust his own organism** in deciding, freer in choice. Rogers would not promise that he will be happier; he would expect his life to become more **meaningful** and his own.

**How could the change be measured?** A **Q-sort** (Stephenson's technique, as Rogers used it) before and after counselling: Arjun sorts self-statements once for his **perceived self** and once for his **ideal self**. The theory predicts a low perceived-ideal correlation at intake, rising after therapy, as in **Mrs. Oak (+.36 to +.79)** and **Butler and Haigh's 25 clients (.01 to +.31)**. Session recordings rated on the **Experiencing Scale** should show rising **self-trust**, which Klein and colleagues (1969) found among clients who improved most.

**What would a critic say?** First, everything the counsellor knows comes from Arjun's **self-report**, which is exactly what an incongruent person distorts; his Q-sort at intake may describe his defended self. Second, a psychoanalyst would argue that the headaches point to **unconscious** conflict that conscious report will never reach. Third, Arjun's family is embedded in a relatively collectivist culture, where research (Heine et al., 1999) suggests positive self-regard may be less prevalent; a goal framed as independent self-trust may sit awkwardly with his values. A Rogerian reply is that the approach imposes no goal: if Arjun, freely and congruently, chooses the exam for reasons he owns, that too is actualisation.

## MCQ traps

1. **One master motive**: Rogers's **actualisation tendency**, not a hierarchy of needs (Maslow) or two instinct classes (Freud).
2. **Self-actualisation is part of the actualisation tendency**, which covers all physiological and psychological needs, starts **in the womb** and drives **maturation** up to the secondary sex characteristics at puberty.
3. **Child learning to walk**: the actualising tendency is **stronger than the urge to regress**; growth involves **struggle and pain**.
4. **Organismic valuing process** judges experience by whether it serves actualisation; **conditions of worth** judge it by whether it brings approval.
5. **Kell, then McNeil (two years later, different subjects)**: **self-insight** was the best predictor of delinquent behaviour. Rogers had predicted **family environment and social interactions**, and he **shelved** Kell's study at first.
6. **Self-insight** = acceptance of self and reality plus a sense of responsibility for oneself; the other seven rated factors were **external**.
7. Therapy names in order: **non-directive → client-centred → person-centred**. Same approach, not rival schools.
8. Rogers called them **clients**, never patients; encounter groups have **facilitators**, not leaders.
9. **Experience is the highest authority** (Rogers, 1961); reality = the **perceived** experiential (phenomenal) field.
10. **Self-concept** = what we are, what we **should be** and what we **would like to be**; defined by "I, me, myself".
11. **Positive regard**: from others, especially the **mother**; **probably learned** (Rogers said the source did not matter); **universal and persistent**; **reciprocal**.
12. **Unconditional positive regard** allows **punishment of specific behaviours**; what is unconditional is regard for the **person**.
13. **Positive self-regard** comes from **within**, after internalising others' attitudes; defined as **contentment with oneself** (Leising et al., 2013); also **reciprocal**.
14. "Similar to the Freudian superego": **conditions of worth** (glossary and summary). Text: positive self-regard is Rogers's version of the superego, derived from **conditional** positive regard.
15. The self-concept formed under conditional regard acts as a **parental surrogate**; example: parent annoyed every time the infant **drops an object from the cot**.
16. **Incongruence** = discrepancy between **self-concept and experience**; felt as **anxiety**; defended by **denial and distortion** (only two defences). Example: "I love all humanity" versus hatred of one person.
17. **Three congruences**: self-concept/experience (theory), perceived/ideal self (Q-sort research), therapist's genuineness (therapy).
18. **Fully functioning person** = Rogers's term for self-actualisation; **actualising, not actualised**; "**a direction, not a destination**" (1961, p. 186); *On Becoming a Person* (**1961**).
19. Fully functioning people are **more emotional**: they feel **fear and pain** as well as courage and tenderness, **more intensely**.
20. Fully functioning people are **not** described as happy, blissful or contented, but as **enriching, exciting, meaningful**.
21. **Trust in one's own organism** does **not** mean ignoring intellect or others; all data are weighed, so decisions **look** intuitive.
22. **Table 10.1** lists **seven** characteristics; Rogers's own "good life" essay stresses three; many texts list five.
23. Human nature: **free will**; **environment given prominence** (though the tendency is innate); **present over past**; **universality with unique expression**; **growth**; **optimism** (still optimistic in an interview at **85**, 1987).
24. Rogers rejected **free association, dream analysis and case histories** because they make the client **dependent** and remove **responsibility**.
25. Rogers's admitted limits of person-centred assessment: only **conscious** material; risk of **therapist projection**; dependence on the client's **communication**.
26. **Core conditions** (Cain, 2013, as **perceived** by clients): **empathy, unconditional positive regard, congruence (genuineness)**. Rogers's 1957 paper lists **six** necessary and sufficient conditions.
27. **Encounter groups** (Rogers, 1970): **8 to 15** people, **20 to 60 hours**, **no formal structure**, a **facilitator**.
28. **Faith, Wong, and Carpenter (1995)**: encounter-group outcomes **comparable** to traditional psychotherapy; **larger, more frequent** groups did **better**.
29. Rogers **used and developed no tests**. **Experience Inventory (Coan, 1972)** = openness; **Experiencing Scale (Gendlin & Tomlinson, 1967)** = self-trust, rated from **recorded free talk**.
30. **Klein, Malthieu, Gendlin, and Kiesler (1969)**: most-improved clients showed rising self-trust; less severe disorders, more self-trust.
31. **Q-sort devised by William Stephenson (1953)**; Rogers **used** it. Not Cattell's Q-data.
32. **Mrs. Oak (Rogers, 1954)**: perceived-ideal correlation **+.36 → +.79**, a year after therapy.
33. **Butler and Haigh (1954)**: **25 clients**, average correlation **.01 → +.31**.
34. Rogers's innovation (radical, Goldfried, 2007): **recording and filming sessions**; "**molecules of personality change**" (1974); client permission always obtained; recording did not impede therapy.
35. "**I never learned anything from research**" (quoted in Bergin & Strupp, 1972), yet he pioneered therapy research.
36. **Chodorkoff (1954)**: clinicians' Q-sorts drew on the **TAT and Rorschach**; **all** subjects slower on threatening words (e.g. *penis* vs *table*), **more so** the poorly adjusted; better adjustment = closer self/clinician agreement.
37. **Medinnus and Curtis (1963)**: **56 mothers**; self-accepting mothers more accepting of children; child's self-acceptance partly depends on mother's.
38. **Assor, Roth, and Deci (2004)**: **three generations of women** and college students; conditional regard **worked** behaviourally but cost coping, self-esteem stability, self-worth, and bred resentment.
39. **Baldwin (1949)** = unconditional acceptance + **democratic** rearing → higher self-esteem; **Coopersmith (1967)** = affection and **reward rather than punishment**.
40. **Harrington, Block, and Block (1987)** = **creativity**; **Harter et al. (1996)** = acting against the **true self** under conditional regard; **Nolan (2008)** = **teachers'** unconditional regard.
41. **Ford (1991)**: **Buss and Plomin's EAS** (Emotionality, Activity, Sociability); parents' recall vs students' current self-perception; bigger gap, more maladjustment.
42. Positive self-regard **less prevalent in Japan** (collectivist) than the US (Heine, Lehman, Markus, & Kitayama, 1999).
43. **Sexual Satisfaction Q-Sort** (McClelland, 2014): **63 statements**, **four** categories.
44. **Post-WWII (1945)**: **400+** college counselling centres under the **Veterans Administration**; therapy "simple, informal, and brief" (DeCarvalho, 1999); psychoanalysis needed a **medical degree**.
45. **200+ training centres, mostly in Europe**; *Person-Centered & Experiential Psychotherapies* from **2002**.
46. Life: born **1902, Oak Park**; fourth of six; China conference in **junior year** (6 months); **Union Theological Seminary** then **Teachers College, Columbia**; **Ph.D. 1931**; **Rochester** Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children; **Ohio State 1940**; **Chicago 1945 to 1957**; **APA president 1946**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Actualisation (actualizing) tendency** | The single innate motive to actualise, maintain and enhance the organism and the self; covers all physiological and psychological needs and begins in the womb |
| **Self-actualisation** | The part of the actualisation tendency directed at developing and enhancing the self |
| **Maturation** | Genetically programmed development of the body's parts and processes, carried through by the actualisation tendency |
| **Organismic valuing process** | The lifelong, innate process of judging experiences by whether they promote or hinder actualisation and growth |
| **Self-insight** | Acceptance of oneself and reality together with a sense of responsibility for oneself; the best predictor in the Kell and McNeil studies |
| **Experiential world (experiential field)** | The world as the person perceives and experiences it; the frame of reference for growth |
| **Phenomenal field** | Older term (Hall and Lindzey, older keys) for the experiential field |
| **Phenomenological approach** | Understanding personality from the person's own subjective viewpoint |
| **Self / self-concept** | The part of experience differentiated as "I, me, myself"; one's image of what one is, should be and would like to be |
| **Perceived self** | How a person currently sees himself or herself (first Q-sort) |
| **Ideal self** | The person one would most like to be (second Q-sort) |
| **Positive regard** | Acceptance, love and approval from other people |
| **Unconditional positive regard** | Acceptance granted regardless of the person's behaviour; the person-centred therapist's attitude towards the client |
| **Conditional positive regard** | Approval, love or acceptance given only when the person shows desired behaviours and attitudes |
| **Positive self-regard** | The state in which we grant ourselves acceptance and approval; contentment with oneself |
| **Conditions of worth** | The belief that one is worthy of approval only when showing approved behaviours and attitudes and avoiding disapproved ones; similar to the Freudian superego |
| **Incongruence** | A discrepancy between a person's self-concept and aspects of his or her experience; produces anxiety |
| **Congruence** | Correspondence between self-concept and experience; in therapy, the therapist's genuineness |
| **Denial** | Keeping a threatening, incongruent experience out of awareness |
| **Distortion** | Admitting a threatening experience in an altered form that fits the self-concept |
| **Subception** | (Classic background) registering threat before full conscious recognition |
| **Fully functioning person** | Rogers's term for self-actualisation: developing all facets of the self; always "actualising" |
| **The good life** | Rogers's phrase for the way of living of the fully functioning person |
| **Person-centred therapy** | Rogers's therapy, in which the client is assumed to be responsible for changing his or her personality; earlier called non-directive and client-centred |
| **Non-directive counselling** | The earliest name of Rogers's approach; the counsellor does not direct content or give advice |
| **Client-centred therapy** | The middle name of Rogers's approach, still common in older texts |
| **Empathy (empathic understanding)** | Understanding the client's experience from inside his or her frame of reference and communicating it |
| **Genuineness** | The therapist being real, with outward expression matching inner feeling; therapist congruence |
| **Core conditions** | Empathy, unconditional positive regard and congruence, the therapist qualities linked to successful therapy |
| **Encounter groups** | A group technique in which people learn about their feelings and how they relate to (encounter) one another |
| **Facilitator** | The non-directive person who runs an encounter group by creating a safe atmosphere, not by leading |
| **Experience Inventory** | Coan's (1972) self-report measure of openness to experience |
| **Experiencing Scale** | Gendlin and Tomlinson's (1967) measure of self-trust, rated from recorded free speech |
| **Q-sort technique** | A self-report technique for assessing aspects of the self-concept by sorting statements from most to least descriptive; devised by Stephenson (1953) |
| **Perceptual defence** | Slower recognition of threatening than neutral stimuli, used as a laboratory index of defensiveness (Chodorkoff, 1954) |
| **EAS temperaments** | Buss and Plomin's inherited temperaments: Emotionality, Activity, Sociability (used by Ford, 1991) |

3. **Organismic valuing process**: experiences that promote actualisation are valued positively and sought; those that hinder it are valued negatively and avoided.
