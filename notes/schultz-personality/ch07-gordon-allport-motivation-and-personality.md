# Chapter 7: Gordon Allport: Motivation and Personality

**What this chapter is really about.** Every theorist in the first six chapters of Schultz came out of psychoanalysis or argued with it from the inside: Freud, then Jung, Adler, Horney and Erikson, each keeping the clinic, the unconscious and the shadow of childhood somewhere near the centre. Allport is the first real break. He was trained in an experimental department, not a consulting room, and he built a theory around the healthy, conscious adult who is pulled forward by plans and purposes rather than pushed from behind by buried conflicts. Three ideas carry the whole theory. First, personality is made of **traits**, real and measurable tendencies inside the person, and the traits that matter most are the ones peculiar to one individual (his **personal dispositions**). Second, adult motives can cut loose from the childhood situations that first produced them; this is **functional autonomy**, the idea for which Allport is best known and most often examined. Third, the core of the person is a self he renamed the **proprium**, which grows in seven steps from infancy to adolescence and, if childhood goes well, yields a mature adult whom he described with six criteria. Allport also matters historically: he is the man who made personality an ordinary university subject, with a textbook, a course and a research programme.

**How to read this chapter.** It opens with the short history of the trait idea that Schultz uses to introduce this part of the book (Hippocrates' humours, Sheldon's body types), because Allport's version is easiest to understand against those older, fixed typologies. Section 7.1 sets Allport against Freud, and 7.2 shows how much of the theory you can already see in his life, especially his rivalry with his brother and his single, bruising meeting with Freud. Sections 7.3 to 7.5 are the core theory (definition, traits, motivation) and are the most examined part; 7.6 and 7.7 cover development and the healthy adult. Section 7.8 gives his position on Schultz's six human-nature questions. Sections 7.9 and 7.10 are about method: how Allport assessed people (letters, values tests) and the large modern literature on expressive behaviour and facial expression that grew out of his work. Section 7.11 weighs it all up. If you can explain functional autonomy with an example, list the cardinal, central and secondary dispositions in order, and recite the seven proprium stages with their ages, you hold the high-yield centre of the chapter.

## Opening: From Humours and Body Types to Traits

Schultz places Allport at the head of a part of the book he calls **The Genetics Approach**, which also contains the next chapter on the factor-analytic trait theorists (Cattell, Eysenck and the Five-Factor model). Before Allport is introduced, the part opener sketches what a trait is and how old the idea is.

A **trait** in everyday language is a distinguishing quality of a person. We use trait words constantly, and we tend to pick out the single most striking feature when we sum someone up: your cousin is "so stubborn", a classmate is "always anxious", a colleague is "very generous". The habit is easy, it feels like common sense, and that commonsense appeal is part of the reason the trait approach has survived for so long.

### Hippocrates and the four humours

The oldest trait classification in Western thought belongs to the Greek physician **Hippocrates (460 to 377 B.C.)**, more than two thousand years before any theory in this book. He sorted people into four kinds, which Schultz labels **happy, unhappy, temperamental and apathetic**, and he attributed the differences to the balance of internal bodily fluids called **humours**. The important conceptual point is where Hippocrates located the cause: these were **constitutional** traits, fixed by the body's biology, not acquired through experience or learning.

*Classic background:* the four humours were blood, yellow bile, black bile and phlegm, and the later physician Galen (2nd century A.D.) gave the matching temperaments the names still used today: **sanguine** (blood; cheerful, the "happy" type), **choleric** (yellow bile; quick to anger, the "temperamental" type), **melancholic** (black bile; gloomy, the "unhappy" type) and **phlegmatic** (phlegm; sluggish, the "apathetic" type). Eysenck, in the next chapter, maps these four ancient temperaments onto the quadrants formed by his introversion-extraversion and neuroticism dimensions.

> **Trap.** The four-humour *theory* is credited to Hippocrates; the four temperament *names* (sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic) are usually credited to Galen. An item asking "who proposed the humoral theory of personality" wants Hippocrates. Schultz himself uses only the plain-English labels happy, unhappy, temperamental and apathetic.

### Sheldon's body types

In the 1940s the American physician **William Sheldon (1899 to 1977)** proposed a typology built on **physique** (Sheldon, 1942). He described three body builds and claimed that each went with its own temperament:

| Body type | Build | Temperament Sheldon linked to it |
|---|---|---|
| **Endomorphic** | Soft, rounded, heavy | Sociable, relaxed, affectionate, even-tempered |
| **Mesomorphic** | Muscular, athletic | Energetic, competitive, aggressive, bold |
| **Ectomorphic** | Thin, delicate, lightly built | Inhibited, apprehensive, intellectual, introverted, self-conscious |

The figure sets three outline drawings side by side: a heavy, round-bellied endomorph described as sociable and easy-going, a broad-shouldered mesomorph described as energetic and combative, and a narrow, slight ectomorph described as shy, tense and intellectual. Schultz's caption makes the key evaluative point: the research evidence has **not supported** Sheldon's typology. He includes it as a historical example of an attempt to classify personality by traits, not as a live theory.

*Classic background:* Sheldon's temperament components were named **viscerotonia** (endomorph), **somatotonia** (mesomorph) and **cerebrotonia** (ectomorph), and he rated each person from 1 to 7 on all three physique components rather than putting people into one pure box. Before him, the German psychiatrist **Ernst Kretschmer** (1921) had linked a **pyknic** (stocky) build to manic-depressive illness and an **asthenic** or **leptosomic** (thin) build to schizophrenia, with an **athletic** type as well. Indian papers sometimes pair Kretschmer and Sheldon in the same question.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse the sets. Sheldon: endomorph, mesomorph, ectomorph (1942). Kretschmer: pyknic, athletic, asthenic (1921). Hippocrates: four humours. Jung's introvert and extravert (Chapter 3) are attitudes, not body types.

### What Sheldon and Hippocrates share, and what Allport changes

Both older schemes treat traits as **largely fixed**: constant and unvarying whatever situation the person is in, and rooted in the body. Allport kept the idea that traits are real and partly inherited, but he changed almost everything else. His traits interact with situations (one of his five trait characteristics is that traits vary with the situation), they are shaped by environment and culture as well as by heredity, and above all they describe **unique individuals** rather than sorting people into a handful of types. Schultz says the trait approach that Allport started has become central to personality psychology, and the next chapter shows how later theorists turned it into a measurement science.

> **Trap.** A **type** is a category you either belong to or do not; a **trait**, in Allport's sense, is a dimension measured on a continuum on which everyone can be placed (his glossary definition says exactly this). Hippocrates and Sheldon are type theorists in spirit; Allport is a trait theorist.

## 7.1 Allport Brings Personality into the Classroom and the Psychology Lab

Allport's career ran for more than forty years, and its first achievement was institutional rather than theoretical. When he started, psychoanalysis and the theories descended from it were regarded as clinical speculation, outside **mainstream scientific (academic) psychology**. Personality as a formal, systematic subject was not accepted by the psychology establishment until Allport published **Personality: A Psychological Interpretation in 1937**. The book was an instant success and became a classic. Schultz credits it with two things at once: it pulled personality into the academic mainstream, and it laid out a theory of personality in which **traits** were the main building blocks.

### Allport challenges Freud

Allport disagreed with Freud on three specific points, and exam questions are usually built on one of them.

1. **The role of the unconscious.** Allport did not accept that unconscious forces run the personality of normal, mature adults. Healthy people, he argued, act rationally and consciously, and they know and control much of what drives them. The unconscious becomes important only in neurotic or disturbed people.
2. **Historical determinism.** Freud held that the past, especially early childhood, determines the present. Allport rejected this for healthy adults: we are not captives of childhood conflicts and old experiences, and we are steered more by the present and by how we see our future. His best-known line on the point contrasts people, who are "busy leading their lives into the future", with a psychology that spends its effort "tracing them into the past" (Allport, 1955, p. 51).
3. **Studying the abnormal.** Freud saw normal and abnormal as points on one continuum, so data from patients could illuminate everybody. Allport saw a sharp break: the abnormal personality functions at an infantile level. The proper data for a theory of personality, he believed, come from emotionally healthy adults. Neurotics, children and animals should not be compared with normal adults, because there is no functional similarity between child and adult, abnormal and normal, or animal and human.

| Issue | Freud | Allport |
|---|---|---|
| Unconscious | Dominates everyone's personality | Important only for neurotic or disturbed people; healthy adults are largely conscious and rational |
| Past vs present | Historical determinism: childhood determines the adult | Healthy adults are guided by the present and their view of the future |
| Normal vs abnormal | A continuum; patients reveal general laws | A clear distinction; the abnormal personality is infantile |
| Proper data | Patients in analysis | Emotionally healthy adults only; not neurotics, children or animals |

> **Trap.** Allport did **not** deny that the unconscious exists. He restricted its importance to neurotic or disturbed behaviour. An option saying "Allport rejected the concept of the unconscious altogether" is wrong; "Allport held that the unconscious is important mainly in neurotics" is right.

### Allport makes each person unique

The second distinctive feature of the theory is its insistence on **uniqueness**. Science normally aims at general constructs and laws that apply to everyone. Allport opposed this for personality: personality, in his view, is not general or universal but **particular and specific to each individual**, defined by that person's own pattern of traits. This one commitment explains a great deal of what follows: his preference for studying single cases, his renaming of individual traits as personal dispositions, and the main criticism later made of him (that you cannot generalise from a theory built around one person at a time).

## 7.2 The Life of Allport (1897 to 1967)

Allport said very little about his childhood, which fits a theory claiming that healthy adults are not shaped by childhood events. What he did reveal, though, lines up closely with the theory he later built, and Indian interviewers like to ask exactly that link.

### Restrictions, morality, and no bright colours

Allport was born in **Montezuma, Indiana**, the **youngest of four sons**. His mother was a schoolteacher. His father had been a salesman who decided late to become a doctor, and the family was so short of money while he studied medicine in Baltimore that he supported them by bringing drugs across from Canada and selling them. When the authorities came after him, he slipped away, moved the family to Indiana and set up a medical practice there; Allport believed his own birth was the first case his father handled.

Home life was governed by his mother's devout religion. Smoking, drinking, dancing and card games were forbidden, and so were bright colours, eye-catching clothes and jewellery of any kind. Allport remembered her as strict, with firm ideas of right and wrong (Nicholson, 2003). The link to the theory is indirect but real: a household organised around conscious moral rules and self-discipline is the kind of setting in which deliberate values and a "unifying philosophy of life" (one of his six criteria of maturity, 7.7) look like the natural core of a person. His lifelong interest in social ethics also came from his parents, as the college years show.

### Isolation and rejection

He was too young to play with his older brothers and was also cut off from other children. He later described building his own small, private world of activities because he never fitted in with the ordinary crowd of boys (Allport, 1967), and he admitted that the playground was misery for him, that his brothers were unkind and more conventionally masculine, and that he could not compete with them (Nicholson, 2003). He saw himself as good with words but poor at sports and games, and he worked hard to be the centre of attention among the few friends he had.

### Inferiority

Out of this isolation and rejection came **inferiority feelings**, which he tried to **compensate** for by striving to excel. (Notice how Adlerian this sounds; Chapter 4.) He wrote of an "identity quest" that grew from feeling inferior to his brothers and other children. As he grew up he came to identify with, and envy, his **oldest brother, Floyd**. He followed Floyd to **Harvard** and took a **Ph.D. in psychology**, just as Floyd had. Floyd became a noted social psychologist, and even when Gordon was making his own name the sense of living in his brother's shadow did not go away. At **31** he described himself, half joking, as the author of a few unimportant articles who should not be mixed up with his more distinguished brother (quoted in Nicholson, 2003).

Schultz draws the key inference here. Copying Floyd so closely may have threatened Gordon's sense of a separate identity. To assert his own individuality he may have needed to deny that his adult interests were simply a continuation of that childhood identification, and he did so in theory: adult motives, he would claim, are **independent of the childhood feelings** that first gave rise to them. He later formalised this as **functional autonomy** (7.5).

*Classic background:* Floyd H. Allport is often called a founder of experimental social psychology; his textbook *Social Psychology* (1924) was a landmark in that field.

> **Trap.** The brother who shaped Allport is **Floyd, the oldest**, not a younger sibling; Gordon was the youngest of four. Indian papers sometimes ask which theorist's concept of functional autonomy is traced to his relationship with a brother: Allport. Do not confuse this with Adler, whose rivalry was also with an older brother (Sigmund) but whose concept was the inferiority complex.

### College years

Allport finished **second in a high-school class of 100** but had no clear plan. At the end of the summer of **1915** he applied to Harvard and was accepted, and he later wrote that his world was remade almost overnight. College opened intellectual and cultural horizons for him. His first exam grades were poor, which shocked him; he doubled his effort and ended the year with **straight A's**.

His parents' concern with **social ethics and social service** was strengthened at Harvard. He volunteered at a boys' club, worked with a group of factory workers and with foreign students, and served as a **probation officer**. He enjoyed the work because he liked helping people, and he said it gave him a sense of competence that offset a general feeling of inferiority; he saw it as part of his search for identity (Allport, 1967). This is the life-source of two later ideas: **mastery and competence** as a principle of adult motivation (7.5), and his lifelong pairing of personality with social psychology and social reform (7.8).

He took some psychology courses but did not yet plan a career in the field. He graduated with a bachelor's degree in **1919**, on the **same day Floyd received his Ph.D.** He then spent a year teaching at **Robert College in Istanbul, Turkey**, after which he took up a Harvard fellowship for graduate study in psychology. His biographer suggests that becoming a psychologist, and so becoming more like his successful brother, was part of the appeal (Nicholson, 2003).

### Allport meets Freud

On the journey home from Istanbul, Allport stopped in **Vienna** to visit one of his brothers. He wrote to **Sigmund Freud** and was invited to call. In Freud's office the older man simply waited for the young American to explain why he had come. The silence stretched, and Allport, embarrassed and casting about for something to say, described a scene from his tram ride over: a small boy who seemed terrified of dirt, who thought everything around him was dirty and who changed seats and asked his mother not to let a dirty man sit next to him.

Freud looked at the neat, formal, carefully groomed visitor and asked, "Was that little boy you?" In psychoanalytic terms Freud was treating the anecdote as a disguised confession: the story Allport chose to tell revealed his own unconscious fears and conflicts. Pervin (1984) later observed that Allport struck Freud as having many traits Freud associated with the **compulsive personality** (neat, meticulous, orderly, punctual), and another psychologist remarked that Freud had hit the target squarely (quoted in Anderson, 1990).

Allport was badly shaken. For the rest of his life he denied that he was the fastidious little boy of the story, but the episode stayed with him, and decades later he called his single meeting with Freud **"traumatic"** (Allport, 1967, p. 22). He concluded that psychoanalysis digs too deep into the unconscious, as Freud had tried to do with him on a few minutes' acquaintance, and that psychology should attend more to **conscious, visible motives**. That became the direction of his whole approach to personality.

*Classic background:* this visit took place in **1920**, when Allport was 22; Elms (1994) devotes a chapter to the encounter.

A modern footnote: two American psychologists later used this very story in a study of teaching (**Kaufman & Bristol, 2001**). Lectures that included personal anecdotes of this kind were rated as more enjoyable, and students learned and remembered material better when it came wrapped in a **social and personal context**. The finding presumably applies to textbooks as well, which is one reason Schultz tells the story.

> **Trap.** Freud's question was about **fear of dirt** (a cleanliness or contamination theme), and the personality Freud read in Allport was **compulsive**, in Freudian terms anal. It was not about a phobia of animals, and it was not a therapy session. Also note the irony examiners like: the man who later insisted that adult motives are independent of childhood had just been "caught" by Freud tracing an adult's story back to his own childhood.

> **Trap.** Allport's reaction to Freud is the reason he emphasised the **conscious** and the **present**. If an item asks which experience pushed Allport toward studying conscious motivation, the answer is the meeting with Freud, not his relationship with Floyd (which is linked to functional autonomy).

### Becoming a success

Allport finished his **Ph.D. at Harvard in 1922** after only two years of graduate work. His dissertation, **"An Experimental Study of the Traits of Personality"**, anticipated his whole career and was the **first research on personality traits ever conducted in the United States**. A travelling fellowship then took him to **Germany and England for two years** to study with leading psychologists.

*Classic background:* in Germany he met the Gestalt psychologists and William Stern, whose person-centred, holistic outlook reinforced Allport's focus on the whole, unique individual.

Back at Harvard as an instructor, he taught a course on the psychological and social aspects of personality, which appears to have been the **first formal college course on personality in America**. He spent nearly **four decades at Harvard**, researching personality and social psychology and training generations of students. In later life he was treated as an elder statesman of the field. His honours included the **American Psychological Foundation's Gold Medal**, the **APA's Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award**, and the **presidency of both the American Psychological Association and the Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues (SPSSI)**.

*Classic background:* he was APA president in 1939.

**Life to theory, at a glance**

| Life event | Idea it fed |
|---|---|
| Strict moral, religious home | Conscious values and a unifying philosophy of life as the core of maturity; the religious value in the Study of Values |
| Isolation, inferiority, compensation by striving | Mastery and competence as adult motives; the identity quest |
| Lifelong identification with and envy of Floyd | Functional autonomy: adult motives independent of childhood feelings |
| Social service work at Harvard | Competence as an antidote to inferiority; personality joined to social psychology and reform |
| "Was that little boy you?" (Freud) | Distrust of depth analysis; emphasis on conscious, present, visible motives |
| 1922 dissertation on traits | A lifetime trait theory; first US trait research |

## 7.3 The Nature of Personality

### Allport's definition, phrase by phrase

Allport took definitions seriously. In **Pattern and Growth in Personality (1961)** he surveyed **about 50 definitions** of personality that other writers had offered and then gave his own, which has become the most quoted definition in the field:

> "Personality is the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine … characteristic behavior and thought" (Allport, 1961, p. 28).

Each phrase was chosen to rule something in or out, so it pays to take it apart.

- **Dynamic organisation.** Personality is always changing and growing, so it is *dynamic*; but the change is patterned, not chaotic, so it is an *organisation*. A student who becomes more confident during college has changed, yet the change makes sense as the development of the same person.
- **Within the individual.** Personality is located inside the person, not in the eye of the observer or in the role the person happens to be playing. (This prepares the ground for Allport's claim, in 7.4, that traits are real.)
- **Psychophysical systems.** Personality is neither purely mental nor purely biological; it is mind and body working as one unit. Temperament, which has a bodily basis, and a person's beliefs and plans, which are mental, belong to the same system.
- **Determine.** Personality is not a description sitting on top of behaviour; its parts actively start and steer specific actions and thoughts.
- **Characteristic behaviour and thought.** Whatever we do and think is typical of us, bears our stamp. Two people can both "help a friend", but the way each one helps, briskly and practically or warmly and at length, is characteristic of that person. This is where the definition ends up at **uniqueness**: if everything is characteristic of someone, each person is one of a kind.

*Classic background:* Pattern and Growth in Personality was Allport's revision of his 1937 book. The **1937 version of the definition** ended differently: personality was the dynamic organisation of psychophysical systems that determine the individual's **"unique adjustments to his environment"**. The usual explanation of the change is that "adjustment" sounded passive, like mere reaction to surroundings, whereas "characteristic behavior and thought" also covers the person's own plans, ideas and spontaneous expression.

> **Trap.** The 11th edition quotes the **1961** wording ("characteristic behavior and thought"). Older Indian keys and Hall and Lindzey often quote the **1937** wording ("unique adjustments to his environment"). Both are Allport's; if an option gives either ending attached to "dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems", it is Allport's definition. A definition built on "a pattern of characteristic thoughts, feelings and behaviours" without "psychophysical systems" is a generic modern definition, not his.

### The roles of heredity and environment

Allport's emphasis on uniqueness rests partly on how he divided the work between nature and nurture. **Heredity supplies the raw materials** of personality: **physique, intelligence and temperament**. **Environment then works on those materials**, shaping them, expanding them or holding them back. A child born with a high-energy temperament may become a sports captain in one family and a "problem child" in another; the raw material is the same, the shaping differs. By giving roles to both, Allport brings in both personal and situational variables, both genetics and learning.

But he gave heredity the larger share of what makes each of us **different**. The number of possible genetic combinations is effectively infinite, so, **except for identical twins**, the chance that anyone else carries exactly your genetic endowment is too small to consider. That endowment then interacts with a social environment that is never identical for two people either; even **siblings raised in the same house** do not share precisely the same environment (they differ in birth position, in how parents treat them, in friends and schools). Unique genes plus a unique environment can only yield a unique personality.

The methodological conclusion follows directly, and it is examined often. If every personality is one of a kind, then **psychology must study the individual case**, not average findings across groups. An average of a hundred people describes nobody in particular.

> **Trap.** Allport is not an environmentalist. He says both heredity and environment matter, but he assigns **genetic background the major portion of our uniqueness**, and Schultz lists "emphasising the role of genetic factors within a trait approach" as one of his most enduring contributions (7.11). This is also why his chapter sits in the part of the book called The Genetics Approach.

### Two distinct personalities for two stages of life

Allport saw personality as **discontinuous** in two senses. Each person is distinct from every other person, and each adult is also **cut off from his or her own past**. He found no continuum running from the child's personality to the adult's.

The infant is driven by **primitive biological urges and reflexes**; the adult functions at a far more **psychological** level, through intentions, values and plans. In effect there are **two personalities**, one for childhood and one for adulthood, and the adult one is not held captive by what happened in the childhood one. Consider a man who was bullied at school and grew up anxious about rejection. A Freudian would expect that history to keep operating underground in his adult choices. Allport would expect a healthy adult to have outgrown it: whatever he does now should be explained by what he currently wants and plans, unless the old anxiety is still active today as a present motive.

Schultz sums up what makes this view distinctive. Allport stresses:

1. the **conscious** rather than the unconscious;
2. the **present and future** rather than the past;
3. the **uniqueness** of personality rather than generalities about large groups;
4. the **normal** rather than the abnormal personality.

These four emphases recur in every later section, and in the chapter summary, and they are the cleanest answer to "How does Allport differ from Freud?"

> **Trap.** The discontinuity between child and adult holds for the **healthy** adult. Allport says that when a child's needs for affection and security are frustrated, the adult remains tied to childhood drives (7.6). So "for Allport, childhood never affects adult personality" is too strong; childhood matters by determining *whether* healthy, autonomous development happens, but in a healthy adult the motives no longer depend on it.

## 7.4 Personality Traits

### What a trait is, for Allport

Allport defined **traits** as **predispositions to respond in the same or a similar way to different kinds of stimuli**. Put more simply, traits are **consistent and enduring ways of reacting to our environment**. In the margin glossary Schultz adds two features: traits are **distinguishing characteristics that guide behaviour**, they are **measured on a continuum** (everyone has more or less of a trait), and they are **subject to social, environmental and cultural influences**.

The phrase "different kinds of stimuli" is the heart of it. A person high in friendliness does not wait for one specific trigger; a new neighbour, a lost tourist and a nervous junior colleague all become, for her, occasions for the same warm approach. The trait makes many different situations *equivalent* in her eyes and produces similar behaviour across them.

*Classic background:* Allport's more technical 1961 definition called a trait a **neuropsychic structure** that can render many stimuli **functionally equivalent** and can start and guide equivalent (meaningfully consistent) forms of behaviour. Older Indian keys sometimes quote this "neuropsychic structure" wording; it is the same idea as the 11th edition's "predisposition to respond in the same or a similar manner to different stimuli".

### Five characteristics of traits (Allport, 1937)

Allport summarised what traits are like in five points.

1. **Traits are real and exist inside each of us.** They are not theoretical constructs or convenient labels invented to account for behaviour. This is a strong philosophical claim; many later trait psychologists treat traits as summary descriptions, but Allport insisted they are actual structures within the person.
2. **Traits determine or cause behaviour.** They do not merely switch on when the right stimulus arrives. They **motivate us to seek out appropriate stimuli**, and they **interact with the environment** to produce behaviour. A sociable student does not just respond warmly when invited out; she goes looking for invitations, joins clubs and organises the class trip.
3. **Traits can be demonstrated empirically.** We cannot see a trait directly, but by observing behaviour **over time** we can infer it from the **consistency** of a person's responses to the same or similar stimuli.
4. **Traits are interrelated.** They can overlap even though they are different characteristics. Allport's example: **aggressiveness and hostility** are distinct traits, but they are related and are often seen together in the same person.
5. **Traits vary with the situation.** A person may show the trait of **neatness** in one setting and **disorderliness** in another. A surgeon may be meticulous in the operating theatre and chaotic at home.

> **Trap.** Students see "consistent and enduring" in the definition and then reject "traits vary with the situation" as un-Allportian. It is one of his five characteristics. Allport's position is that traits are consistent across *similar* situations and interact with circumstances, not that they show up identically everywhere. That flexibility is exactly what separates him from the fixed typologies of Hippocrates and Sheldon.

> **Trap.** Review Question 4 in Schultz asks for "four characteristics of traits", but the text itself lists **five** (Allport, 1937): real, causal, empirically demonstrable, interrelated, situation-dependent. If an MCQ asks how many characteristics Allport described in the 11th edition's list, the answer is five.

*Classic background:* the lexical work that later trait theorists built on started with Allport. **Allport and Odbert (1936)** went through an unabridged English dictionary and pulled out **17,953** words that could describe a person, of which about **4,500** referred to stable, consistent traits. Cattell (next chapter) started his factor analyses from this list, so the Big Five can trace its ancestry to it.

### Individual traits and common traits

Allport at first divided traits into two kinds.

- **Individual traits** are unique to one person and define that person's character.
- **Common traits** are shared by many people, for instance by the members of one culture.

Because common traits belong to cultures, **different cultures will have different common traits**. They also **change over time** as a society's standards and values change. Consider the trait of deference to authority: in one generation it may be widely shared and expected, while a generation later, as norms shift, the same society may prize independence instead. This is the sense in which common traits are **subject to social, environmental and cultural influences**. Common traits are what allow us to compare people at all ("she is more punctual than most people here"); individual traits are what make a particular person that person.

### Personal dispositions

Allport later saw that calling both of these things "traits" invited confusion, so he **changed his terms**:

- what he had called **common traits** he now called simply **traits**;
- what he had called **individual traits** he now called **personal dispositions**.

A personal disposition, in the glossary's words, is a trait **peculiar to one individual**, as opposed to one shared by many people. Personal dispositions are not all equally strong or important. Allport arranged them in three levels.

**Cardinal traits.** A **cardinal trait** is so pervasive and so powerful that it touches **almost every aspect of a person's life**. Allport called it a **ruling passion**, a force that dominates behaviour. His examples were **sadism** and **chauvinism**. Two qualifications matter for exams: **not everyone has a cardinal trait**, and even people who have one **do not show it in every situation**. Picture a person whose whole life is organised around winning: career choices, friendships, even family holidays become contests. That is a cardinal disposition.

*Classic background:* both of Allport's examples are **eponyms**, words made from people's names (sadism from the Marquis de Sade, chauvinism from Nicolas Chauvin). Allport pointed out that a cardinal trait is often so striking that a person becomes famous for it and gives the language a new word; "Machiavellian" and "quixotic" are similar cases.

**Central traits.** **Everyone** has a few central traits, roughly **5 to 10** themes that best describe a person's behaviour. Allport's examples were **aggressiveness, self-pity and cynicism**. These are the qualities you would list if a friend asked you to describe someone, or that you would put in a **letter of recommendation**: "reliable, warm, a little impatient, very funny, intensely curious".

**Secondary traits.** The weakest personal dispositions are **secondary traits**. They appear much **less consistently** than cardinal and central traits, and they can be so faint or inconspicuous that **only a close friend** would notice them. Examples are a mild preference for a particular kind of music or a particular food.

| Level of personal disposition | How pervasive | How many | Allport's examples | Who notices it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal** | Dominates nearly every aspect of life; a "ruling passion" | Not everyone has one | Sadism, chauvinism | Anyone; the person may become known for it |
| **Central** | The main themes of behaviour | About 5 to 10 in everyone | Aggressiveness, self-pity, cynicism | Anyone who knows the person; what goes in a recommendation letter |
| **Secondary** | Weak, inconsistent, situational | Many | A minor preference in music or food | Perhaps only a close friend |

> **Trap.** The 11th-edition renaming runs **common traits → "traits"** and **individual traits → "personal dispositions"**. Students reverse it. A quick check: a personal disposition is *personal*, that is, belonging to one individual. After the renaming, Allport's three levels are strictly cardinal, central and secondary **dispositions**; older books and keys still say cardinal, central and secondary **traits**. Treat the two sets of terms as the same thing.

> **Trap.** **Cardinal** (one ruling passion; not universal) vs **central** (5 to 10; universal) vs **secondary** (weak; noticed by close friends). An option claiming "every person has one cardinal trait" is false. An option giving "3 to 5 central traits" is false for Schultz; the figure is **5 to 10**. Also do not confuse **cardinal** with **common**: the cardinal trait is the most individual thing about a person, the opposite of common.

> **Trap.** Cattell (next chapter) also uses "common traits" vs "unique traits", and divides traits into surface and source traits. If the question mentions **ruling passion**, **personal dispositions** or **cardinal/central/secondary**, it is Allport; if it mentions **source traits**, **16 factors** or **factor analysis**, it is Cattell.

## 7.5 Motivation: What We Strive For

### Why motivation is the central problem

Allport thought that the real test of any personality theory is what it does with **motivation**. His answer follows from everything in 7.1 and 7.3: what counts is the person's **current** state. What happened in the past, during toilet training, at school or in some childhood crisis, is over. It is no longer active, and it explains nothing about adult behaviour **unless it survives as a motive operating now**.

He added that **cognitive processes**, meaning our **conscious plans and intentions**, are a vital part of personality. Here he aimed directly at Freud, whom he accused of attending to unconscious, irrational forces at the expense of the conscious and rational. For Allport, deliberate intentions are part of who we are, and **what we want and what we are striving for** are the keys to understanding what we do. That reverses the usual direction of explanation: Allport tried to explain the **present in terms of the future**, not in terms of the past. To understand why a young woman is studying late every night, ask what she is aiming at (a seat in a clinical psychology programme, say), not what her early relationship with her father was like.

> **Trap.** "Allport explained the present in terms of the future" is correct and directly from the 11th edition. Do not confuse it with Adler's **fictional finalism** (Chapter 4), which also looks forward but is about imagined ideals that guide a person. Allport's version is about conscious intentions and goals.

### Functional autonomy

Allport's best-known idea is the **functional autonomy of motives**. It holds that the motives of **mature, emotionally healthy adults** are **not functionally connected to the earlier experiences in which they first appeared**. Something that motivated us early in life can become **autonomous**, that is, independent of the circumstances that originally produced it. The word *functional* is the key: the history of the motive is not denied, but the motive no longer *depends* on that history to keep working.

Allport used two analogies.

- **The tree and the seed.** Every tree can be traced back to a seed. But a grown tree does not need the seed to feed it; it now sustains itself and is no longer functionally tied to its seed. Origin and present working are separate questions.
- **Children and parents.** As we grow up we become independent of our parents. We remain related to them, but we are no longer functionally dependent on them, and they no longer direct our lives (or at least, Allport adds, they should not).

His third example comes from working life. A new graduate starts a business career and works very hard because she wants financial success and security. Thirty years later she has more than enough money to retire, yet she keeps working just as hard. The **55-year-old** is not pursuing what the **25-year-old** was pursuing; the goal of security was reached long ago. What was once a **means to an end** (hard work in order to earn money) has become an **end in itself**. The motive has separated from its source and become autonomous.

The consequence for theory is direct. If adult motives are functionally autonomous, then you **cannot understand them by digging into the person's childhood**, as Freud believed. The only way to understand adult motives, Allport insisted, is to find out **why the person behaves as they do today**.

*Classic background:* Allport introduced functional autonomy in **1937**. His most famous illustration in the older literature is the **retired sailor** who still longs for the sea, although he first went to sea simply to earn a living; the craving for the sea has outlived the economic need that started it.

> **Trap.** Functional autonomy is a theory of **motives**, not of traits or of cognition. It must not be confused with **functional fixedness** (a problem-solving block in cognitive psychology) or with **autonomy vs shame and doubt** (Erikson's second stage). If an option says functional autonomy means "adult motives are independent of the childhood experiences in which they originated", it is right; if it says "adult motives are disguised continuations of childhood motives", that is the Freudian view Allport opposed.

Allport described **two levels** of functional autonomy.

### Perseverative functional autonomy

**Perseverative functional autonomy** is the **more elementary, lower level**. It covers behaviours such as **addictions** and **repetitive physical actions**, for instance the habitual way someone carries out a routine daily task. These behaviours keep going, **persevere**, on their own **without any external reward**. They once served a purpose; they no longer do; and they operate at too basic a level to count as an integral part of personality.

Allport offered animal and human evidence.

- **Animal:** a rat trained to run a maze for food may keep running the maze after it has been given **more than enough food**. It is obviously running for some reason other than food.
- **Human:** we prefer **routine, familiar behaviours** and keep doing them even when nothing external reinforces them. Someone who always takes the same route to work after the reason for choosing it (a friend who lived on the way) has disappeared, or who keeps the evening cup of tea long after the reason it began (a study break in college) has gone, is showing perseverative autonomy.

### Propriate functional autonomy

**Propriate functional autonomy** is the **more important** level and is **essential for understanding adult motivation**. The word *propriate* comes from **proprium**, Allport's term for the **ego or self** (7.6). Schultz's glossary links this level to our **values, self-image and lifestyle**; the chapter summary also lists interests, attitudes and intentions.

Propriate motives are **unique to each individual**. The **ego decides which motives are kept and which are dropped**, and it keeps the ones that **enhance self-esteem or self-image**. From this Allport derived a simple, testable-sounding claim: there is a **direct relationship between our interests and our abilities**; **we enjoy doing what we do well**.

His example is the **piano**. A child may start lessons under pressure from her parents and practise mainly to avoid their displeasure. Nothing about that original motive concerns music. As she becomes skilled, though, playing becomes part of how she sees herself. The original motive (fear of parental disapproval) has vanished, and playing has become **necessary to her self-image**. If you asked the adult pianist why she plays, "because my mother made me" would be a historical fact, not an explanation.

| | Perseverative functional autonomy | Propriate functional autonomy |
|---|---|---|
| Level | Elementary, low-level | Higher; central to adult motivation |
| Typical content | Addictions, repetitive physical actions, routines | Interests, values, attitudes, intentions, self-image, lifestyle |
| What keeps it going | Persists by itself without external reward | The ego (proprium) keeps it because it enhances self-esteem and self-image |
| Part of personality? | Too basic to be an integral part | The core of the mature personality |
| Allport's examples | Well-fed rat still running the maze; preference for familiar routines | Child forced into piano lessons who becomes a committed pianist |

> **Trap.** The rat-in-the-maze example illustrates **perseverative** autonomy; the piano example illustrates **propriate** autonomy. The working-hard-after-retirement example illustrates **functional autonomy in general** (a means becoming an end). Examiners swap these.

### The organising of our propriate functioning

Propriate functioning is an **organising process that maintains our sense of self**. It shapes **how we perceive** the world, **what we remember** from experience and **how our thinking is directed**. These processes are **selective**: out of the mass of stimulation around us, they pick out what is relevant to our interests and values. Two people walk through the same market; the cook notices the vegetables, the photographer notices the light. Allport said the organising process runs on **three principles**.

1. **Organising the energy level.** This principle explains how we **acquire new motives**. New motives arise **out of necessity**, to use up **excess energy** that might otherwise come out in **destructive or harmful ways**. Allport's illustration is **retirement**: people who stop working suddenly have spare time and energy, and ideally they should channel it into new interests and activities. A retired teacher who starts a reading programme for neighbourhood children has, in effect, organised her energy into a new motive.
2. **Mastery and competence.** This principle concerns the **level** at which we satisfy motives. Doing something adequately is not enough. Healthy, mature adults want to perform **better and more efficiently**, to **master new skills** and to **increase their competence**. A doctor who keeps learning new procedures long after she is competent at her job shows it.
3. **Propriate patterning.** This is the striving for **consistency and integration** of the personality. We organise our perceptual and cognitive processes **around the self**, keeping whatever enhances our self-image and rejecting the rest. So propriate motives depend on the **structure or pattern of the self**: someone who sees himself as an honest person will drop a profitable but shady scheme without much deliberation, because it does not fit the pattern.

> **Trap.** The three principles belong to **propriate** functional autonomy, not perseverative. Their names are exam items: **organising the energy level**, **mastery and competence**, **propriate patterning**. "Energy level" is about acquiring *new* motives to use spare energy, not about biological arousal or Freud's libido.

### What functional autonomy does not explain

Allport did not claim that every motive is functionally autonomous. Some behaviours lie **outside** the control of functionally autonomous motives:

- **reflexes**,
- **fixations**,
- **neuroses**,
- behaviours arising from **biological drives** (such as hunger).

The exclusion of fixations and neuroses is significant. These are precisely the behaviours in which the past *does* still run the person, which is how Allport could accept that Freud was describing something real in disturbed patients while denying that it described the healthy adult.

> **Trap.** "Which of the following is NOT governed by functionally autonomous motives?" Correct answers: reflexes, fixations, neuroses, biological drives. A well-paid professional who keeps working hard, or a hobby that became part of the self-image, **is** functionally autonomous.

## 7.6 Personality Development in Childhood: The Unique Self

### The proprium

Allport chose the term **proprium** for the self or ego. He **rejected the words "self" and "ego"** because other theorists had given them so many different meanings that they no longer communicated anything precise. The easiest way into the word, Schultz suggests, is through the adjective **appropriate** (in the sense of "one's own", as when we "appropriate" something). The proprium includes those aspects of personality that are **distinctive**, and so belong to, and are appropriate to, a person's **own emotional life**. These aspects are unique to each of us and **unite our attitudes, perceptions and intentions**.

> **Trap.** **Proprium** = Allport's term for the self or ego. **Propriate** = the adjective (propriate functional autonomy, propriate striving, propriate patterning). Do not confuse it with Jung's **persona** (the public mask), Rogers' **self-concept**, or Freud's **ego** (the reality-testing structure that mediates between id and superego). Allport invented a new term precisely to avoid those meanings.

### Before the proprium: the infant

Before the proprium starts to form, the infant has **no self-consciousness**, no awareness of a self, and no separation between "me" and everything else. Infants take in sensory impressions and react to them **automatically and reflexively**, with no ego standing between stimulus and response. Allport's description of infants is famously unsentimental: they are **pleasure-seeking, destructive, selfish, impatient and dependent**, and he called them **"unsocialized horrors"**. They have little that deserves the name personality; they are driven by reflexes to **reduce tension and maximise pleasure**.

Notice how neatly this fits the discontinuity of 7.3: the infant is a tension-reducing, biologically driven organism, while the healthy adult (7.8) seeks to *increase* tension. The two really are, in Allport's sense, two different personalities.

### Stages of development

Allport traced the development of the proprium through **seven stages** from infancy to adolescence.

**Stages 1 to 3 (from birth to about age 4).**

1. **Bodily self.** The infant becomes aware of a **"bodily me"**. A concrete sign is that the baby begins to tell its own fingers apart from the toy those fingers are holding.
2. **Self-identity.** The child develops a sense of **continuity**: she is the same person even though her body and abilities keep changing. Self-identity is strengthened when children **learn their names** and come to see themselves as **separate from others**.
3. **Self-esteem.** Children discover that they can **do things by themselves**. They want to build, explore and handle objects, and that can be destructive (the toddler who takes apart the remote control). If parents **frustrate the need to explore** at this stage, the emerging self-esteem can be crushed and replaced by **humiliation and anger**.

**Stages 4 and 5 (about ages 4 to 6).**

4. **Extension of self.** The child becomes aware of the people and objects around her and identifies them as **belonging to her**: "my house", "my parents", "my school". The self now extends beyond the body.
5. **Self-image.** The child forms images of how she **actually** sees herself and how she **would like** to see herself, an **actual** and an **ideal** self-image. These come from interaction with **parents**, who make clear what they expect and how far the child is meeting or failing those expectations. A child often praised for being "so responsible" and scolded for being "careless" builds both images around those words.

**Stage 6 (ages 6 to 12).**

6. **Self as a rational coper.** Children realise that **reason and logic** can be used to **solve everyday problems**: working out how to save pocket money for something, or how to settle a quarrel with a friend.

**Stage 7 (adolescence).**

7. **Propriate striving.** Adolescents begin to **form plans and long-range goals** for the future. Until they do, the proprium remains **incomplete**. A sixteen-year-old who decides she wants to become a clinical psychologist and starts organising her choices around that aim is in propriate striving.

**Table 7.1** The development of the proprium

| Stage | Age (Table 7.1) | What develops |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Bodily self** | First three years (stages 1 to 3) | Awareness of one's own existence; telling one's own body apart from objects around it |
| 2. **Self-identity** | First three years | Sense that one's identity stays intact through all the changes going on |
| 3. **Self-esteem** | First three years | Pride in one's own accomplishments |
| 4. **Extension of self** | Fourth to sixth year (stages 4 and 5) | Recognising the people and objects that are part of one's own world |
| 5. **Self-image** | Fourth to sixth year | Actual and idealised images of oneself; awareness of meeting or failing parental expectations |
| 6. **Self as a rational coper** | Ages 6 to 12 | Applying reason and logic to everyday problems |
| 7. **Propriate striving** | Adolescence | Forming long-range goals and plans |
| **Adulthood** | Adult | Normal, mature adults are functionally autonomous, independent of childhood motives; they act rationally in the present and consciously create their own lifestyles |

> **Trap.** The 11th edition gives two slightly different age labels for stages 1 to 3. The **text** says they span "birth to about age 4"; **Table 7.1** says they emerge "during the first three years". Stages 4 and 5 are "ages 4 to 6" in the text and "the fourth through sixth year" in the table. If an option says "first three years" or "birth to about 4" for the bodily self, self-identity and self-esteem, both trace to Schultz. The fixed points are **6 to 12 for the rational coper** and **adolescence for propriate striving**.

> **Trap.** Order matters: **bodily self → self-identity → self-esteem → extension of self → self-image → self as rational coper → propriate striving**. The commonest slip is to put self-image before extension of self, or self-esteem before self-identity. A mnemonic: **B**oys **I**n **E**ngland **E**at **I**ce-cream **R**ather **P**olitely (Bodily, Identity, Esteem, Extension, Image, Rational, Propriate).

> **Trap.** **Seven** stages of the proprium (Allport), **eight** psychosocial stages (Erikson, Chapter 6), **five** psychosexual stages (Freud). *Classic background:* in older formulations (Allport's 1955 *Becoming*, and Hall and Lindzey's account) the proprium is described by eight "functions" or aspects, adding **the self as knower**, the cognising function that ties the others together, and some older names differ (**ego-enhancement** for self-esteem, **ego-extension** for extension of self, **rational agent** for rational coper). The 11th edition uses seven stages.

### The importance of the infant-mother bond

Social interaction with parents matters at every stage of the proprium, but the single most important relationship is the **infant-mother bond**, as the source of **affection and security**.

**When the bond goes well.** If the mother or main caregiver gives enough affection and security, the proprium develops **gradually and steadily** and the child achieves positive psychological growth. Childhood motives are then free to be **transformed into the autonomous propriate strivings of adulthood**, a pattern of personal dispositions forms, and the result is a **mature, emotionally healthy adult**.

**When it fails.** If childhood needs for affection and security are **frustrated**, the self does not mature properly. The child becomes **insecure, aggressive, demanding, jealous and self-centred**, and psychological growth is stunted. The adult who results is **neurotic** and functions at the level of **childhood drives**. His motives **do not become functionally autonomous**; they stay tied to their original conditions. Traits and personal dispositions fail to develop, and the personality remains **undifferentiated**, as it was in infancy.

Here is the clinical picture this predicts. A 30-year-old who was emotionally neglected as a small child may still demand constant reassurance from his partner, flare up jealously at small slights and judge every situation by whether he is getting enough attention. In Allport's terms his adult motives are still the infant's motives; they have never become autonomous. Contrast this with a well-loved child who becomes an adult with clear, differentiated interests that stand on their own.

This section also answers Review Question 13 (how Allport's theory accounts for adult emotional disturbance): disturbance is the **failure of the proprium to develop** because early needs for affection and security were frustrated, so that functional autonomy never takes place.

> **Trap.** Neurosis is the one case where, for Allport, the **past still rules**, so his account of it sounds close to Horney (basic anxiety from lack of warmth) and Erikson (basic trust). The difference: no instincts or psychosexual stages, and healthy and neurotic adults are separate categories, not points on a continuum.

## 7.7 The Healthy Adult Personality

For Allport, healthy development is a journey from a **biologically dominated organism in infancy** to a **mature psychological organism in adulthood**. Along the way the person's motives separate from childhood and turn toward the **future**. Provided that childhood needs for affection and security were met (7.6), the proprium develops satisfactorily, and the adult personality grows **out of** childhood without being dominated or determined by childhood drives.

Allport described the normal, mature, emotionally healthy adult with **six criteria**. Each is easier to remember with a person in mind.

1. **Extension of the sense of self.** Mature adults extend their sense of self to **people and activities beyond themselves**. Their concerns are not limited to their own comfort; a teacher who genuinely cares whether her students flourish, or a volunteer absorbed in a cause, has an extended self. (Compare stage 4 of the proprium, where the child first says "my house"; the adult version extends to work, community and ideals.)
2. **Warm relating to others.** They relate warmly to other people, showing **intimacy, compassion and tolerance**: close and affectionate with family and friends, and respectful and patient with strangers and people unlike themselves.
3. **Emotional security through self-acceptance.** A high degree of **self-acceptance** helps them achieve **emotional security**. They can tolerate frustration and their own shortcomings without collapsing or lashing out.
4. **Realistic perception, skills and commitment to work.** They see life **realistically**, develop **personal skills**, and **commit themselves to some kind of work**. They deal with things as they are rather than as they wish or fear them to be.
5. **Humour and self-objectification.** They have a **sense of humour** and **self-objectification**, meaning an understanding of, or insight into, themselves. The person who can laugh at her own habits, and who knows her strengths and weaknesses accurately, shows both.
6. **A unifying philosophy of life.** They hold a **unifying philosophy of life** that directs the personality toward **future goals**. It might be religious, ethical or political, but it gives life a coherent direction. (This is the criterion the Study of Values was built to assess, 7.9.)

Adults who meet these six criteria are **emotionally healthy and functionally autonomous**, independent of childhood motives. They deal with the present and plan for the future without being **victimised by what happened to them in their early years**.

*Classic background:* Allport's own 1961 headings for the six criteria were extension of the sense of self; warm relating of self to others; emotional security (self-acceptance); realistic perception, skills and assignments; self-objectification (insight and humour); and a unifying philosophy of life. Older Indian keys may use these wordings.

> **Trap.** **Self-objectification** in Allport means **insight into oneself, paired with humour**. It is not "treating oneself as an object" in the feminist or social-psychological sense. In an MCQ listing "self-objectification" as one of Allport's criteria of maturity, it is correct.

> **Trap.** Allport's mature person (six criteria, 1961) is the forerunner of **Maslow's self-actualiser** (Chapter 9) and **Rogers' fully functioning person** (Chapter 10). If the question names "unifying philosophy of life", "extension of the sense of self" or "self-objectification", it is Allport. "Peak experiences" are Maslow; "unconditional positive regard" and "organismic valuing" are Rogers.

## 7.8 Questions about Human Nature

Because functional autonomy frees the healthy adult from childhood conflicts, Allport's theory gives an **optimistic** picture: adults in **conscious control** of their lives, dealing rationally with present situations, **planning for the future** and actively fashioning an identity. Always **in the process of becoming** (the title of his 1955 book), we creatively design and carry out a style of life suited to us, shaped more by the present and our plans than by our past. Here is his position on each of Schultz's six issues.

**Free will vs determinism: moderate.** Allport allowed **free choice** in how we deliberate about and plan our future. He also accepted that **some behaviour is determined** by our traits and personal dispositions, and that once these are formed they are **hard to change**. So he sits in the middle, leaning toward free will where conscious planning is involved.

**Nature vs nurture: both.** Heredity gives us our basic **physique, temperament and level of intelligence**; **learning and experience** then shape these raw materials. (Recall from 7.3 that he credits genetic background with the larger share of what makes us unique.)

**Importance of the past vs the present: the present (and future).** The healthy adult is guided by present circumstances and future plans, not by childhood. The past matters only when it is still active as a current motive, or in the neurotic whose development failed.

**Uniqueness vs universality: uniqueness.** Common traits do describe some universality in behaviour, but **individual traits, or personal dispositions, describe each person's nature more precisely**. Uniqueness is the headline of his whole system.

**Equilibrium vs growth: growth, through increasing tension.** For Allport the ultimate and necessary goal of life is **not to reduce tension**, as Freud proposed, but to **increase it**, pushing us continually toward new sensations and challenges. Once one challenge is met we look for another. The reward lies in the **process of achieving** rather than in the achievement, in striving toward the goal more than in arriving. He summed it up in the phrase that "getting there is more fun than being there". We constantly need new goals to keep us motivated and to maintain an **optimal level of tension** in the personality. A student who clears an entrance exam and then immediately sets her sights on a research degree is behaving exactly as Allport predicts.

**Optimism vs pessimism: optimistic.** Allport's hopeful view of human nature matched his personal **liberal politics** and his **interest in social reform**. The humanistic attitude of his writing was mirrored in his character; colleagues and students described him as someone who genuinely cared about people, and who was cared for in return.

| Issue | Allport's position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Free will vs determinism | Moderate; free will in planning the future | Conscious choice about goals, but formed traits and dispositions determine some behaviour and resist change |
| Nature vs nurture | Both | Heredity supplies physique, temperament, intelligence (the larger share of uniqueness); learning and experience shape them |
| Past vs present | Present and future | Functional autonomy frees the healthy adult's motives from childhood |
| Uniqueness vs universality | Uniqueness | Personal dispositions describe a person more precisely than common traits |
| Equilibrium vs growth | Growth | The goal of life is to increase tension and seek new challenges, not to reduce tension |
| Optimism vs pessimism | Optimistic | Adults are conscious, rational, future-directed and always "becoming"; reflected in his liberalism and social concern |

> **Trap.** **Freud: the goal of behaviour is tension reduction. Allport: the goal of life is tension increase.** Note the twist with the infant: Allport's newborn is still a tension-reducing, pleasure-maximising organism (7.6). It is the **mature adult** who seeks tension. An option saying "Allport, like Freud, saw tension reduction as the ultimate goal of life" is wrong.

> **Trap.** Allport's free-will position is **moderate**, not pure free will. Students who remember "optimistic, conscious, future" over-generalise to "free will"; the 11th edition explicitly says he took a moderate stance because traits, once formed, determine some behaviour.

## 7.9 Assessment in Allport's Theory

### Eleven methods, and no single best one

Allport wrote more about personality assessment than most theorists. In **Pattern and Growth in Personality (1961)** he argued that, although there were many approaches, there was **no single best technique**: personality is so complex that we must use **many methods** to evaluate it. He listed **11 major methods**.

| Method | What it involves, briefly |
|---|---|
| 1. Constitutional and physiological diagnosis | Bodily and physiological indicators (physique, health, physiological measures) |
| 2. Cultural setting, membership, role | The person's culture, group memberships and social roles |
| 3. Personal documents and case studies | Diaries, letters, autobiographies; detailed case histories |
| 4. Self-appraisal | The person's own description and evaluation of himself or herself |
| 5. Conduct analysis | Observing what the person actually does |
| 6. Ratings | Judgements of the person made by others |
| 7. Tests and scales | Standardised psychological tests |
| 8. Projective techniques | Responses to ambiguous material (TAT, Rorschach) |
| 9. Depth analysis | Probing for unconscious material, as in psychoanalysis |
| 10. Expressive behaviour | Spontaneous movements, gestures, voice, handwriting |
| 11. Synoptic procedures | Combining information from several sources into a **synopsis** |

Of these, Allport relied most on the **personal-document technique** and the **Study of Values**, and he also observed **expressive behaviour**, which Schultz treats under research (7.10).

> **Trap.** Projective techniques and depth analysis appear in Allport's list of 11, even though he argued that they can distort the picture of a **normal** personality (7.10). Listing a method as one of the available methods is not the same as recommending it for healthy adults. **Synoptic procedures** means pulling several sources together into one summary; it is not a test name.

### The personal-document technique

The **personal-document technique** means examining **diaries, autobiographies, letters, literary compositions** and other samples of a person's **written or spoken records** to work out **how many and which kinds of traits** the person has.

**The Jenny case.** Allport's most famous case study analysed a collection of **more than 300 letters** written over a **12-year period** by a **middle-aged woman** he called **Jenny** (Allport, 1965, 1966). It was later disclosed that Jenny was the **mother of Allport's college roommate** and had written the letters to Allport and his wife (Winter, 1993a).

*Classic background:* the letters were published, with Allport's commentary, as **Letters from Jenny** (1965).

**How the analysis works.** In the personal-document method, a group of **judges** reads the autobiographical or biographical material and records the traits they find. If the judges agree reasonably well, their many trait labels can be grouped into a relatively **small number of categories**. With Jenny's letters, **36 judges** listed **nearly 200 traits**; because many of the terms were synonyms, Allport reduced them to **eight categories**.

*Classic background:* the eight clusters for Jenny are commonly listed as quarrelsome-suspicious, self-centred, independent-autonomous, dramatic-intense, aesthetic-artistic, aggressive, cynical-morbid and sentimental.

**The computer check.** One of Allport's students, **Paige (1966)**, ran a **computer analysis** of the letters. The program counted **categories of words** that might signal a trait; for example, words expressing anger, rage, hostility and aggression were coded together as the trait of **aggression**. This approach is more **sophisticated and quantitative** than Allport's original reading because it involves **fewer subjective judgements**. The computer analysis produced **eight prominent traits** in Jenny's personality that were **similar to the categories Allport had identified**. Because the two methods agreed, Allport concluded that his **subjective approach** gave **valid** information about traits, comparable to the more objective computer analysis.

What this shows: the idiographic study of one person need not be mere impressionism. Agreement among 36 independent judges and with a word-count program is a form of **validation**, the same logic (convergence of methods) that a test constructor uses.

> **Trap.** Numbers for the Jenny case: **more than 300 letters**, **12 years**, **36 judges**, **nearly 200 traits**, reduced to **8 categories**; **Paige (1966)** computer analysis also found **8 traits**. Jenny was the **mother of his college roommate**, not his own mother, not a patient, and not a fictional composite.

### The Study of Values

With two colleagues, Allport developed an **objective self-report test** called the **Study of Values** (**Allport, Vernon, & Lindzey, 1960**). The rationale ties it to the healthy personality: our personal **values form the basis of our unifying philosophy of life**, which is one of the **six criteria** of maturity (7.7).

Allport treated **values as personality traits**: they represent strongly held interests and motivations. **Everyone has some degree of each type of value**, but **one or two** of them dominate in any given personality. The test measures **six** values.

| Value | What it is concerned with | A person in whom it might dominate |
|---|---|---|
| **Theoretical** | Discovering truth; an empirical, intellectual, rational approach to life | A research scientist |
| **Economic** | What is useful and practical | An entrepreneur |
| **Aesthetic** | Artistic experience; form, harmony and grace | A painter or classical dancer |
| **Social** | Human relationships, altruism and philanthropy | A social worker |
| **Political** | Personal power, influence and prestige in any field, not only politics | An ambitious executive |
| **Religious** | The mystical; understanding the universe as a whole | A monk or spiritual seeker |

A mnemonic for the six: **T**he **E**ager **A**rtist **S**ells **P**aintings **R**arely (Theoretical, Economic, Aesthetic, Social, Political, Religious).

*Classic background:* the six value types come from the German philosopher **Eduard Spranger's** *Types of Men*. The test first appeared as Allport and Vernon (1931), with Lindzey joining for later revisions (1951, 1960). Its items are forced choices between values, so the six scores are relative to one another (**ipsative**): a high score on one value must be balanced by lower scores elsewhere, which fits Allport's idea that one or two values dominate.

> **Trap.** The **political** value is about **power, influence and prestige in any activity**, not interest in party politics. The **social** value is about **altruism and love of people**, not sociability or extraversion. The Study of Values measures **six** values (not five, not seven).

> **Trap.** Although Allport championed the **idiographic** study of the individual, the Study of Values is a **nomothetic** instrument: a standardised test comparing people on common dimensions. Schultz names it as his example of Allport using nomothetic methods when he thought them appropriate (7.10).

## 7.10 Research on Allport's Theory

### Allport's stance on research method

Allport had firm views about how personality should be studied, and they explain both his own research and the criticism he later received.

**Against methodological narrowness.** He criticised psychologists who insisted that **experimental and correlational methods** were the **only legitimate** ways to study personality. Not every aspect of personality can be tested that way, he argued, and psychologists should be more **open and eclectic** in their methods.

**Against clinical methods for healthy people.** He also opposed carrying over methods designed for the emotionally disturbed into the study of healthy people.

- **Case studies**, in the clinical sense, focus on the person's **past**. Since the normal adult's personality is divorced from childhood influences, a method that reconstructs childhood is, for Allport, of **no value** for understanding normal adults.
- **Projective techniques** such as the **Thematic Apperception Test** and the **Rorschach inkblot test** may give a **distorted picture** of the normal personality, because they tap **unconscious forces**, which have little effect on healthy adults.
- His alternative: you get **more reliable** information by **simply asking people to describe themselves**, a method that reveals their **dominant traits**.

**Idiographic and nomothetic.** Allport favoured the **idiographic approach**, the intensive **study of the individual case**, as his use of personal documents shows. But he also used **nomothetic** methods, the **statistical analysis of differences among large samples**, when he judged them appropriate. Psychological tests such as the **Study of Values** are nomothetic.

| | Idiographic | Nomothetic |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of study | One individual, in depth | Large groups of people |
| Aim | Understand the unique pattern of one personality | Find general laws and differences across people |
| Typical tools | Personal documents, letters, diaries | Standardised tests, statistics |
| Allport's use | Preferred (the Jenny letters) | Used when appropriate (Study of Values) |

*Classic background:* Allport borrowed the terms idiographic and nomothetic from the German philosopher **Wilhelm Windelband**, and in later writing (1962) he proposed **morphogenic** as a better name for the science of the individual's unique pattern.

> **Trap.** Allport's list of 11 methods includes "personal documents and case studies", yet Schultz says he considered case studies valueless for normal adults. The two are reconciled by what is being studied: Allport rejected the **clinical case history that reconstructs childhood**, but valued **personal documents** that show the adult's present traits. And although he favoured the idiographic approach, he was **not exclusively** idiographic: the Study of Values is nomothetic.
### Expressive behaviour

#### The nature of expressive behaviour

Much of Allport's research concerned what he called **expressive behaviour**: behaviour that **expresses our personality traits**. He set it against **coping behaviour**, which is **directed toward a specific purpose** and is **consciously planned and carried out**. Coping behaviour is driven by **needs arising from the situation** and is usually aimed at **changing something in the environment**.

Expressive behaviour, by contrast, is **spontaneous** and reflects **basic aspects of personality**. It is **hard to change**, has **no specific purpose**, and is usually shown **without our awareness**.

Allport's illustration is a **public speaker**, who communicates with the audience at two levels at once.

- The **formal, planned level** is **coping behaviour**: the content of the lecture, prepared to inform the audience.
- The **informal, unplanned level** is **expressive behaviour**: the speaker's movements, gestures and tone of voice. She may be nervous, talk too fast, walk up and down, or keep fiddling with an earring. None of these serves the purpose of the lecture, yet they can reveal a good deal about her personality.

A clinical parallel: in an interview, what the client says about his week is coping behaviour (he chooses it); the way he taps his foot, avoids eye contact and speeds up when the topic turns to his father is expressive behaviour, and a trained observer reads it.

| | Coping behaviour | Expressive behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Planned? | Consciously planned and carried out | Spontaneous, usually outside awareness |
| Purpose | A specific purpose, usually to change the environment | No specific purpose; seemingly purposeless |
| Determined by | Needs aroused by the situation | Basic personality traits |
| Changeability | Can be altered deliberately | Difficult to change |
| Public-speaking example | The content of the lecture | Gestures, pacing, vocal inflection, fidgeting |

> **Trap.** "Coping" here is Allport's term for **purposeful, planned behaviour aimed at the environment**. It is not the stress-coping of Lazarus (problem-focused vs emotion-focused coping), and it is not Freud's defence mechanisms. Expressive behaviour is the one that **reveals traits**; coping behaviour reveals the **situation's demands**.

**The landmark study: Allport and Vernon (1933).** Allport gave participants a variety of tasks and then judged how **consistent their expressive movements** were across the different situations (**Allport & Vernon, 1933**). He found a **high level of consistency** in **voice, handwriting, posture and gestures**. From this consistency he inferred the existence of traits such as **introversion and extraversion**. The logic is that of trait characteristic 3 (7.4): a trait cannot be seen directly, but it can be inferred from consistent behaviour across varied situations.

*Classic background:* the work was published as the book *Studies in Expressive Movement* (1933).

**Later research.** A large body of theoretical and experimental work has since examined facial and vocal expressive behaviour (see **Russell, Bachorowski, & Fernandez-Dols, 2003**). It shows that personality can be assessed from **voice recordings, films and videotapes**. To a trained observer, **facial expressions, vocal inflections and idiosyncratic gestures and mannerisms** can reveal personality traits, and expressive behaviours linked to particular traits have been assessed even from **still photographs** (Schultz cites Allport & Cantril, 1934; Berry, 1990; DePaulo, 1993; Riggio & Friedman, 1986; Riggio, Lippa, & Salinas, 1990).

The evidence is now substantial that **some people can form reliable impressions of a stranger's personality** from facial appearance and expression alone (**Berry & Wero, 1993**). In one striking demonstration, observers accurately judged personality factors such as **anxiety** after watching a film of a person for **no more than 30 seconds** (**Ambody & Rosenthal, 1992**).

*Classic background:* the first author is usually spelled **Ambady** (Nalini Ambady); this 1992 work is the origin of the "thin slices" idea, that very brief samples of expressive behaviour carry real information about a person.

#### Effects of gender and age

- **Gender and age.** **Women and younger people** recognise emotions in facial expressions more accurately than **men and older people** (**Sasson, Pinkham, Richard, Hughett, Gur, & Gur, 2010**).
- **Children.** Children can read facial expressions accurately as early as **age 5**, and the skill improves rapidly after that (**Gao & Maurer, 2010**).
- **Yearbook smiles and later life.** **Harker and Keltner (2001)** analysed the **college yearbook photographs** of women graduates. Women who showed **positive emotional expressions at age 21** scored higher on self-report measures of **subjective well-being** when tested again at **ages 27, 43 and 52**. They also reported **better marriages** and scored higher in **affiliation, competence and achievement orientation** than women whose yearbook photos showed less positive emotion. A single expressive moment captured at 21 predicted outcomes three decades later, which is about as strong a demonstration of expressive behaviour reflecting enduring personality as one could ask for.

*Classic background:* the photographs came from Mills College yearbooks, and the positive expressions coded were genuine (**Duchenne**) smiles, which involve the muscles around the eyes as well as the mouth.

#### Interpreting facial expressions

Our own experiences and states can change how well we read other people's faces.

- **Abused children.** Physically abused children aged **8 to 10** identified **anger** in pictures of **adult women's faces** more readily than a control group of non-abused children (**Pollak & Sinha, 2002**). A child who has had to anticipate an adult's rage learns to spot its first signs.
- **Depression and happiness vs sadness.** Adults diagnosed with **major depression** needed to see **more intense** expressions before correctly identifying **happiness**, but **less intense** expressions to correctly identify **sadness** (**Joormann & Gotlib, 2006**). Their perception is tuned toward sadness.
- **Social anxiety and depression.** Children who scored high on **social anxiety** were **better** at correctly interpreting facial expressions, and **depressed adults** were better than non-depressed adults at recognising **sad** expressions (**Ale, Chorney, Brice, & Morris, 2010; Gollan, McCloskey, Hoxha, & Coccaro, 2010**).
- **Video games.** **Japanese children** who spent more time playing **video games** were better at recognising facial expressions than those who played less (**Tamamiya & Jiraki, 2013**).
- **Close friends.** **Close friends** are far more accurate than casual acquaintances in decoding emotions such as **sadness, anger and happiness** (**Zhang & Parmley, 2011**).

Schultz concludes that these and similar studies strongly support Allport's proposition that expressive behaviour reflects personality traits.

> **Trap.** Depressed people are **worse** at detecting happiness (they need a stronger smile) but **better** at detecting sadness (they need less). An option saying "depressed adults are worse at recognising all facial emotions" is wrong.

#### Coding facial expressions

Over many years of research, **Paul Ekman** showed that the faces of **seven emotions** can be told apart reliably and objectively. Those seven are **happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust and contempt** (**Ekman, Matsumoto, & Friesen, 1997**). Working from the **Human Interaction Laboratory** that he directed at the **University of California, San Francisco**, Ekman's team analysed the **43 facial muscles** and derived a coding scheme with **3,000 distinct configurations** for reading emotion from a face.

This **Facial Action Coding System (FACS)** is used in the United States by **police departments, the CIA and the FBI** to detect **lying** by criminal suspects and terrorists; according to FACS, tiny movements of facial muscles give the liar away (**Kaufman, 2002**). In **2009**, **Time** magazine named Ekman one of the world's **100 most influential people** (**Taylor, 2009**).

*Classic background:* FACS was first published by Ekman and Friesen in 1978; it scores faces in terms of numbered **action units** (individual muscle movements). Ekman's earlier cross-cultural work, including studies with the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, is the classic evidence for universal basic expressions; contempt was the later addition that turned six basic emotions into seven.

**Personality traits in the face.** Other research shows that broad personality dimensions show up in facial expression (Schultz cites **Keltner, 1997**):

| Trait | Facial expressions associated with it |
|---|---|
| **Neuroticism** | Looks of anger, contempt and fear |
| **Agreeableness** | Laughter and other expressions of friendly social interaction |
| **Extraversion** | Smiles, laughter and other signs of enjoyment and amusement |
| **Conscientiousness** | Expressions of embarrassment: a tightly controlled smile, averted gaze, head turned down and away from the observer |

**How we recognise smiles.** We recognise other people's smiles by **unconsciously mimicking** them. Using the same facial muscles as the smiling person sends signals that activate the same brain regions that are active in the person smiling at us (**Niedenthal, Mermillod, Maringer, & Hess, 2010; Zimmer, 2011**).

> **Trap.** Ekman's FACS: **43 muscles**, **3,000 configurations**, **seven** emotions in Schultz's list (anger, contempt, disgust, fear, sadness, surprise, happiness). Older textbooks give **six** basic emotions (without contempt). The computer program in the next section recognised **six** (happiness, sadness, fear, disgust, anger, surprise). Keep the three numbers apart.

#### Emotional states and facial expressions

- **Type A behaviour.** The **Type A** pattern, proposed as a risk factor for **heart disease**, has been distinguished from **Type B** by facial expressions of **disgust, glaring, grimacing and scowling** (**Chesney, Ekman, Friesen, Black, & Hecker, 1997**).
- **Suicide risk.** In a study of **depressed patients in Switzerland**, facial expressions distinguished those who **later attempted suicide** from those who did not (**Heller & Haynal, 1997**). The clinical implication is that a patient's face may carry risk information that his words do not.
- **Anxiety.** **Japanese college students** with high scores on an anxiety test showed different facial expressions from low scorers, especially around the **mouth** and on the **left side of the face** (**Nakamura, 2002**).

Schultz treats these results as confirming Allport's ideas: stable personality patterns and emotional states leak into expressive behaviour.

### Cultural differences in facial expressions

Are facial expressions the same everywhere, or do they vary by culture? The evidence points both ways.

**Evidence for universality.** Studies of **American and Chinese infants and adults** found that some **basic emotions** were shown by **identical facial expressions** in both cultures and in both age groups (**Albright et al., 1997; Camras, Oster, Campos, Miyake, & Bradshaw, 1998**).

**Evidence for cultural variation.**

- **Infants in three cultures.** Comparing **American, Chinese and Japanese infants**, **Camras (1998)** found that **Chinese infants** consistently showed **less variety** in facial expressive behaviour. American infants differed significantly from Chinese infants but **not so much from Japanese** infants.
- **A remote tribe.** Facial expressions of emotion in a **remote tribe in northwestern Namibia** did **not match** those of American participants, and the researchers concluded that **perceptions of emotion are not universal** (**Gendron, Roberson, van der Vyver, & Barrett, 2014**). *Classic background:* the group studied was the Himba.
- **Three-year-old girls.** **White** 3-year-old girls **smiled more** than **mainland Chinese or Chinese-American** girls. The **strictness of the mother** and the **number of other children and adults in the home** also affected how intensely the girls' faces showed emotion, so expressiveness depended on both **cultural and family** characteristics (**Camras, Bakeman, Chen, Norris, & Cain, 2006**).
- **Own-culture advantage.** Adults in the **United States and Japan** were each much **better at recognising the facial expressions of people from their own culture** (**Dailey et al., 2010**). The same East-West difference appeared when people judged emotional faces displayed by **robots** (**Trovato, Kishi, Endo, Zecca, Hashimoto, & Takanishi, 2014**).
- **Race and neutral faces.** When faces were digitally made **neutral**, **White faces** were more often judged to look **angry** than **Black faces**, and Black faces were more often judged to look **happy or surprised** (**Zebrowitz, Kikuchi, & Fellous, 2010**).
- **Eyes vs mouth.** People in **Eastern** cultures expressed emotion mainly with the **eyes**, whereas people in **Western** cultures relied more on the **eyebrows and mouth** (**Jack, Caldara, & Schyns, 2012**).
- **Facebook.** People in **Hong Kong, Singapore and Taiwan** showed **less intense** facial expressions in their **Facebook** pictures than Americans (**Huang & Park, 2013**).
- **Depression across cultures.** Among people high in **depression** watching a **humorous film**, **Caucasian Americans** smiled and reacted facially **less** than **Asian Americans** (**Chentsova-Dutton, Tsai, & Gotlib, 2010**).

**Making sense of it.** The pattern is that some basic expressions are shared, but how much emotion is shown, which parts of the face carry it, and how well outsiders read it are shaped by culture and even by family. *Classic background:* Ekman and Friesen explained such differences with **display rules**, the culturally learned norms about when and how strongly emotions may be shown.

> **Trap.** Schultz's summary says only that "some research suggests" a consistency of facial expressions across cultures. The 11th edition presents the question as **open**: evidence of shared basic expressions (Albright et al., 1997; Camras et al., 1998) sits alongside evidence of variation (Camras, 1998; Gendron et al., 2014; Jack et al., 2012). An option claiming that research has shown facial expressions to be **completely universal** is wrong. Also remember the direction of the Eastern finding: **eyes** (East) vs **eyebrows and mouth** (West).

### Computer recognition of facial expressions

If people can read faces, can computers? A program that monitors video images of faces at **30 frames per second** recognised **basic emotions**, namely **happiness, sadness, fear, disgust, anger and surprise**, with a high degree of accuracy (**Susskind, Littlewort, Bartlett, Movellan, & Anderson, 2007**).

And can computers carry emotion between people? **Derks, Bos, and von Grumbkow (2007)** studied **adolescents in the Netherlands (average age 16)** in **simulated chat rooms**. The teenagers used **more emoticons in social contexts** than in task- or job-oriented ones, and they used **positive emoticons** (smiles) in positive situations and **negative emoticons** (sad faces) in negative ones, just as people use facial expressions face to face. The researchers concluded that people express emotion in **computer-mediated communication** in much the same way as in face-to-face situations.

### Highlights: Research on expressive behaviour

Schultz closes the research section with a box of seven take-home findings, restated here:

- a person's face gives trained observers usable evidence about **personality traits**;
- facial reading skill is higher in **women** than in men, and higher in **children** (younger people) than in older adults;
- emotions are decoded far better by **close friends** than by strangers;
- up to **seven** distinct emotions can be separated in the face;
- recognising a smile involves **silently copying it** with our own facial muscles;
- people who are **depressed** pick up **sadness** more readily;
- **machines** can both identify basic emotions in faces and carry them in online messages.

> **Trap.** The Highlights box says "women and **children**" are better at reading faces, while the text it summarises (Sasson et al., 2010) says "women and **younger people**" (and the children's finding, Gao & Maurer, 2010, is only that the skill appears by age 5 and then improves). For MCQs, the safe statement is: **women** outperform **men**, and **younger** people outperform **older** people.

## 7.11 Reflections on Allport's Theory

Although expressive behaviour has generated a great deal of research, Allport's theory **as a whole has stimulated little research** testing its propositions. Schultz gives two historical reasons.

1. **Method.** Allport's **idiographic** approach ran against the main current of the psychology of his time, which favoured **nomothetic** research: the study of large groups of participants with sophisticated statistics. A theory whose natural unit is one person's letters does not fit a discipline built on group comparisons.
2. **Population.** His focus on **emotionally healthy adults** was out of step with **clinical psychology** of the period, which dealt mainly with **neurotic and psychotic** patients. The people most interested in personality theory were treating exactly the groups Allport set aside.

### Questions and criticisms

**1. The concepts are hard to test.** Allport's ideas are difficult to turn into specific, testable propositions for the experimental method. How would you observe **functional autonomy** or **propriate striving** in a laboratory? How could you manipulate them to see their effects? The criticism holds because these are concepts about long-term changes in the meaning of motives over a lifetime, which no short experiment can capture.

**2. Functional autonomy has no mechanism.** Allport never made clear **how an original motive is transformed into an autonomous one**. Take his own example: once someone is financially secure, through what process does "work hard to earn money" become "work hard for the sake of the work"? And if the process of transformation is not explained, there is **no way to predict which childhood motives will become autonomous** in adulthood. This is the most serious criticism, because it means functional autonomy describes an outcome after the fact rather than explaining it. Two children forced into piano lessons may end up as a committed pianist and as an adult who never touches a keyboard again, and the theory cannot say in advance which will be which.

**3. Uniqueness blocks generalisation.** Allport focused so exclusively on the **individual** that, critics argue, it becomes impossible to generalise from one person to another. Science needs at least some statements that hold across people; a theory of personal dispositions that are, by definition, peculiar to one person struggles to supply them.

**4. The discontinuities are doubtful.** Many psychologists reject Allport's proposed breaks between **child and adult**, **animal and human**, and **normal and abnormal**. Research on children, on animals and on emotionally disturbed people has in fact produced a great deal of knowledge about how normal, healthy adults function (learning theory from animal research, developmental psychology from children, much of what we know about emotion and memory from patients). On this point the history of psychology has simply gone against him.

> **Trap.** "Which of the following is NOT a criticism of Allport's theory?" The real criticisms are: concepts hard to test experimentally; no mechanism for how motives become functionally autonomous (so no prediction); emphasis on uniqueness prevents generalisation; questionable discontinuity of child/adult, animal/human, normal/abnormal; little research generated; idiographic method out of step with nomothetic psychology. **Not** criticisms of Allport: overemphasis on sex or instincts, pessimism, neglect of conscious processes, overreliance on case studies of neurotic patients, ignoring heredity. Those are criticisms of Freud, or the opposite of what Allport did.

### Recognition and influence

Despite the criticisms, Allport's theory has been well received, and some psychologists still maintain that his views give a **better basis for understanding personality** than those of most other theorists (**Piekkola, 2011**).

- **Humanistic psychology.** His approach to development, his emphasis on **uniqueness**, and his focus on the importance of **goals** are reflected in the work of **Abraham Maslow** and **Carl Rogers** (Chapters 9 and 10). He is often regarded as **one of the first psychologists to bring humanistic values and concerns** into the field (**Jeshmaridian, 2007**).
- **The trait revival.** Interest in his work has **revived** as part of the current focus on **personality traits**, and that trait research is providing **empirical support** for some of his ideas.
- **Emotion and neuroscience.** His work on the **expression of emotions** has been of vital importance to the development of **cognitive neuroscience**.
- **Personal documents.** There has been renewed interest in his **personal-document technique** (**Barenbaum, 2008; Zunshine, 2010**).
- **Style and appeal.** His books are readable and his concepts have **commonsense appeal**. His emphasis on **conscious, rational** determinants of behaviour offers an alternative to the psychoanalytic picture of people driven irrationally and unconsciously by forces they cannot control.
- **Hope.** His view that people are shaped more by **future expectations than by past events** fits a hopeful, humanistic philosophy.

Schultz names his **two most enduring contributions**: making the study of personality **academically respectable**, and emphasising the role of **genetic factors within a trait approach** to personality.

*Classic background:* outside personality theory proper, Allport is equally famous in social psychology for **The Nature of Prejudice (1954)**, the source of the **contact hypothesis** (prejudice falls when groups meet as equals, pursue shared goals and have institutional support) and of a five-step scale of acted-out prejudice (antilocution, avoidance, discrimination, physical attack, extermination). With Ross (1967) he also distinguished **intrinsic** religious orientation (religion lived as an end in itself) from **extrinsic** orientation (religion used for comfort or social standing). Interview panels sometimes move from Allport the personality theorist to these topics.

> **Trap.** Schultz's "most enduring contributions" are (1) **academic respectability** for personality and (2) **genetic factors within a trait approach**. Students often write "functional autonomy" as his most enduring contribution; it is his best-known concept, but it is also the one most heavily criticised.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the chapter. Tracing them turns a list of terms into a theory.

1. **The healthy, conscious adult as the proper subject.** Allport's three disagreements with Freud (the unconscious matters only in neurotics; no historical determinism for healthy adults; normal and abnormal are different in kind) set up everything else: the six criteria of maturity, the goal of tension increase, the preference for self-description over projective tests, and the criticism that he ignored what children, animals and patients can teach.
2. **Uniqueness, from definition to method.** "Characteristic behavior and thought" in the definition, unique genetic endowment plus unique environment, the renaming of individual traits as personal dispositions, the idiographic study of Jenny's letters, and the main criticism (no generalisation) are one idea seen five times.
3. **Discontinuity, and its price.** Two personalities (infant and adult), functional autonomy (motives cut free of their origins), the claim that case histories of childhood are useless for normal adults; and on the other side, the unexplained mechanism and the empirical case against the child/adult, animal/human, normal/abnormal breaks.
4. **The present explained by the future.** Conscious intentions, propriate striving in adolescence, a unifying philosophy of life, "getting there is more fun than being there", and the optimism rating all express the same forward-looking motivation. This links Allport to Adler's goal-directedness before him and to Maslow and Rogers after him.
5. **Traits as real, measurable and partly inherited.** Allport's five trait characteristics, the cardinal/central/secondary hierarchy, heredity's major share in uniqueness, and the lexical groundwork for later trait research make him the founder of the part of the book called The Genetics Approach. The next chapter keeps his idea of traits but replaces his single-case method with factor analysis of large samples (Cattell, Eysenck, the Five-Factor model), which is exactly the nomothetic turn he resisted.
6. **Life into theory.** The table at the end of 7.2 is the map: inferiority and compensation, the shadow of Floyd, the hour with Freud and a strict moral home each reappear as a concept.

**Relation to other theorists in the book.** Against **Freud** on nearly every axis. Close to **Adler** in compensation and forward-looking goals; close to **Horney** and **Erikson** in making early affection and security the condition for healthy growth (and seven proprium stages sit beside Erikson's eight). A forerunner of **Maslow** and **Rogers**; the ancestor of **Cattell, Eysenck and the Five-Factor model**, whose nomothetic method he resisted; and, through expressive behaviour, a direct line to **Ekman**.

## Worked example

*Ananya, 29, is interviewed for an M.Phil. Clinical Psychology seat. Asked to describe herself, she says she started Carnatic vocal lessons at 7 only because her grandmother insisted, but now practises daily and performs at small concerts. For three years she has volunteered every weekend at a crisis helpline; she first joined because a senior told her it would strengthen her application, but she has kept going long after her CV stopped needing it. She has kept a diary since she was 14. She always sits in the same seat on the office bus, though she cannot say why. Her friends, she says, would call her "organised, warm, stubborn, funny and a bit anxious". While she explains her research plans fluently, she twists her ring and speeds up whenever the panel asks about her failures. One panellist, psychoanalytically trained, suggests that the helpline work is really a reparation for guilt she felt when her father fell seriously ill when she was 10.*

Reason through it with Allport's concepts, as an interview panel would expect.

**What are her personal dispositions?** Her friends' list is a set of **central dispositions**: a handful of themes, within Allport's 5 to 10, of the kind that goes into a recommendation letter. Nothing rises to a **cardinal disposition** (a ruling passion), which is normal; not everyone has one. A liking for a particular snack would be a **secondary disposition**.

**Why does she still sing?** **Propriate functional autonomy**, structurally identical to Allport's piano example. The original motive (her grandmother's insistence) has gone, but singing is now part of her **self-image**, so the **proprium** keeps it. Her drive to keep improving and performing is **mastery and competence**.

**Why does she still volunteer?** A **means has become an end**, the general form of **functional autonomy** in Allport's career example. The helpline work began as an instrument (strengthening an application) and now continues for its own sake. It also shows the first criterion of maturity, **extension of the sense of self** to people and activities beyond herself.

**And the bus seat?** That is **perseverative functional autonomy**: a low-level routine that persists without any external reward and is too elementary to be part of her personality proper, like the well-fed rat that still runs the maze.

**How would Allport answer the psychoanalytic panellist?** He need not deny a historical link to her father's illness. But in a **healthy adult** the origin of a motive does not explain its present working, any more than a seed explains how a grown tree feeds itself. The question is why she volunteers **now**. The past matters only if it is still active as a **current motive** (guilt she still feels today), or if her development had failed and her motives stayed tied to childhood, Allport's picture of the **neurotic** adult. Nothing in her account suggests either.

**What does the panel see in her manner?** Her fluent answers about research plans are **coping behaviour**: planned and aimed at the situation (getting the seat). The ring-twisting and quickened speech are **expressive behaviour**: spontaneous, purposeless, outside awareness, and revealing of her **anxious** disposition, the kind of brief sample that Ambody and Rosenthal (1992) showed carries real information.

**How would Allport assess her more fully?** Not with projective tests, which he thought distort a healthy adult's picture. He would **ask her to describe herself**, apply the **personal-document technique** to her diary (several judges list traits, which are then grouped into a few categories, as with **Jenny's letters**), and perhaps give the **Study of Values**, where you would predict high **social** and **aesthetic** values, the core of her **unifying philosophy of life**.

**Does she look like Allport's mature adult?** Largely: extension of self (helpline), warm relating, commitment to work, and humour with **self-objectification** (she can name her own stubbornness and anxiety). Her anxiety about failure is the point to explore for **emotional security**. Her long-range career plan shows completed **propriate striving**.

**Where would a critic push back?** On **functional autonomy's missing mechanism**: the theory explains her singing only after the fact. Had her younger brother been sent to the same lessons and given up at 12, Allport's theory could not have predicted that difference in advance.

## MCQ traps

1. **Part opener names:** Hippocrates (**460 to 377 B.C.**), four types (happy, unhappy, temperamental, apathetic) caused by **humours**; Sheldon (**1899 to 1977**; Sheldon, **1942**) linked **endomorph** (sociable, relaxed), **mesomorph** (energetic, aggressive), **ectomorph** (inhibited, intellectual) to temperament; research has **not supported** Sheldon.
2. **The 1937 book** *Personality: A Psychological Interpretation* made personality an accepted academic subject; *Pattern and Growth in Personality* is **1961**; *Becoming* is **1955**.
3. **Definition:** "dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine ... characteristic behavior and thought" (**1961**); the **1937** version ended "unique adjustments to his environment". Allport reviewed about **50** definitions first.
4. **Unconscious:** Allport limited it to **neurotic or disturbed** people; he did not abolish it.
5. **Historical determinism** (the past determines the present) is Freud's; Allport rejected it for healthy adults.
6. **Family:** born **Montezuma, Indiana**; **youngest of four sons**; envied the **oldest** brother, **Floyd**, a noted social psychologist; Gordon graduated in **1919** on the day Floyd got his Ph.D.
7. **Freud meeting (1920, Vienna):** a little boy afraid of **dirt** on a tram; Freud asked "Was that little boy you?"; Allport called it **traumatic** and turned to **conscious** motives. Kaufman & Bristol (**2001**) used the anecdote to show personal stories improve learning.
8. **Ph.D. Harvard 1922** after two years; dissertation **"An Experimental Study of the Traits of Personality"** = **first US research on personality traits**; he then taught the **first formal US college course** on personality.
9. **Heredity** supplies **physique, intelligence, temperament**; genetic background gives the **major portion** of uniqueness; even siblings never share the same environment.
10. **Five trait characteristics (Allport, 1937):** real; cause behaviour (we **seek** stimuli); empirically demonstrable; interrelated (**aggressiveness and hostility**); **vary with the situation** (neat here, disorderly there).
11. **Renaming:** common traits → **traits**; individual traits → **personal dispositions**. Not the reverse.
12. **Cardinal** = ruling passion (**sadism, chauvinism**); **not everyone** has one, and it is not shown in every situation.
13. **Central** = **5 to 10** in everyone (**aggressiveness, self-pity, cynicism**); what goes in a **letter of recommendation**.
14. **Secondary** = weakest, least consistent; perhaps only a **close friend** notices (a food or music preference).
15. **Functional autonomy** = adult motives independent of the childhood experiences in which they first appeared. Analogy: **tree and seed**; also children becoming independent of parents.
16. **Means becomes end:** the **25-year-old** works for security; the retired **55-year-old** still works hard for its own sake.
17. **Perseverative** = low-level: **addictions, routines, repetitive actions**; **rat** keeps running the maze when **overfed**. **Propriate** = values, self-image, lifestyle; **piano** lessons forced, then self-chosen.
18. **Three principles of propriate functioning:** organising the **energy level** (new motives use excess energy; **retirement**), **mastery and competence**, **propriate patterning** (consistency around the self).
19. **Not functionally autonomous:** **reflexes, fixations, neuroses, biological drives**.
20. **Proprium** = Allport's term for **self/ego**, chosen because "self" and "ego" had too many meanings; understood via "appropriate".
21. Infants are **"unsocialized horrors"**: pleasure-seeking, destructive, selfish, impatient, dependent; no self-awareness.
22. **Seven proprium stages in order:** bodily self, self-identity, self-esteem (birth to about 4; table: first three years); extension of self, self-image (4 to 6); self as rational coper (**6 to 12**); propriate striving (**adolescence**).
23. **Infant-mother bond** (affection and security) is the most important relationship; if frustrated → insecure, aggressive, jealous child → **neurotic** adult whose motives never become autonomous.
24. **Six criteria of maturity:** extension of self, warm relating, emotional security (self-acceptance), realistic perception + skills + work, **humour and self-objectification**, **unifying philosophy of life**.
25. **Goal of life = increase tension** (Freud: reduce it); "getting there is more fun than being there".
26. **Free will = moderate** (formed traits are hard to change); **nature-nurture = both**; **uniqueness**; **growth**; **optimistic**; present and future over past.
27. **11 assessment methods**; the last, **synoptic procedures**, combines sources into a synopsis. Allport relied most on **personal documents** and the **Study of Values**.
28. **Jenny:** **300+ letters**, **12 years**, a **middle-aged** woman, mother of his **college roommate** (Winter, 1993a); **36 judges**, **nearly 200 traits** → **8** categories; **Paige (1966)** computer analysis also found **8**.
29. **Study of Values (Allport, Vernon, & Lindzey, 1960):** **six** values, theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political (= **power**, not party politics), religious; one or two dominate.
30. **Idiographic** (single case) preferred; **nomothetic** used when appropriate, e.g. the **Study of Values**.
31. Allport thought **case studies** (past-focused) and **projectives** (TAT, Rorschach; unconscious) unsuited to **normal** adults; better to ask people to **describe themselves**.
32. **Expressive** (spontaneous, purposeless, unaware, hard to change; reveals traits) vs **coping** (planned, purposeful, situational, aims to change the environment). Public speaker: lecture content = coping; fidgeting = expressive.
33. **Allport & Vernon (1933):** consistency in **voice, handwriting, posture, gestures**; inferred **introversion-extraversion**.
34. **Ambody & Rosenthal (1992):** anxiety judged accurately from **30 seconds** of film. **Berry & Wero (1993):** reliable impressions of strangers from faces.
35. **Harker & Keltner (2001):** positive expression in yearbook photos at **21** → more well-being at **27, 43, 52**, better marriages.
36. **Pollak & Sinha (2002):** abused children aged **8 to 10** spotted **anger** in **adult female** faces more readily. **Joormann & Gotlib (2006):** depressed adults need **more** intensity for happiness, **less** for sadness.
37. **Ekman's FACS:** **43** facial muscles, **3,000** configurations, **seven** emotions (including **contempt**); used by police, CIA, FBI to detect **lying**; *Time* 100 in **2009**.
38. **Keltner (1997):** neuroticism = anger, contempt, fear; extraversion = smiles, laughter; conscientiousness = **embarrassment** (controlled smile, averted gaze). **Type A** = disgust, glaring, grimacing, scowling (Chesney et al., 1997).
39. **Culture:** **Chinese** infants show **less** expressive variety (Camras, 1998); **Namibian** tribe's expressions did not match Americans (Gendron et al., **2014**); **East = eyes**, **West = eyebrows and mouth** (Jack et al., **2012**); own-culture advantage in US and Japan (Dailey et al., 2010).
40. **Computers:** **30 frames per second**, **six** basic emotions (Susskind et al., **2007**); Dutch teens, **average age 16**, more emoticons in **social** than task contexts (Derks et al., **2007**).
41. **Most enduring contributions (Schultz):** academic respectability for personality, and **genetic factors within a trait approach**; his ideas fed **Maslow and Rogers**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Trait (everyday sense)** | A distinguishing personal quality used to sum up what someone is like |
| **Humours** | Hippocrates' bodily fluids whose balance supposedly produced four temperament types (happy, unhappy, temperamental, apathetic) |
| **Endomorph / mesomorph / ectomorph** | Sheldon's (1942) soft-rounded / muscular / thin body types, linked respectively to relaxed sociability, bold energy, and inhibited intellectuality; not supported by research |
| **Historical determinism** | Freud's view that past events, especially childhood, determine present personality; rejected by Allport for healthy adults |
| **Personality (Allport's definition)** | The dynamic organisation within the individual of psychophysical systems that determine characteristic behaviour and thought (1961) |
| **Dynamic organisation** | Personality changes and grows, but in a patterned rather than random way |
| **Psychophysical** | Made of mind and body working together as one unit |
| **Discontinuity of personality** | Allport's claim that the adult's personality is separate from the child's, and normal from abnormal |
| **Traits (Allport)** | Real, enduring predispositions to respond similarly to many different stimuli; they guide behaviour, lie on a continuum, and are shaped by social, environmental and cultural influences |
| **Individual traits** | Allport's original term for traits unique to one person |
| **Common traits** | Traits shared by many people, such as members of a culture; differ across cultures and change over time; later called simply "traits" |
| **Personal dispositions** | Allport's later term for individual traits: traits peculiar to one person rather than shared |
| **Cardinal traits** | The most pervasive and powerful dispositions; a ruling passion that touches nearly every part of life; not everyone has one |
| **Central traits** | The 5 to 10 outstanding themes that best describe a person's behaviour |
| **Secondary traits** | The weakest dispositions, shown inconspicuously and inconsistently |
| **Functional autonomy of motives** | The principle that a mature adult's motives operate independently of the childhood experiences in which they began |
| **Perseverative functional autonomy** | The lower level of functional autonomy: addictions, routines and repetitive acts that persist without reward |
| **Propriate functional autonomy** | The higher level of functional autonomy, involving values, self-image, interests and lifestyle |
| **Proprium** | Allport's term for the self or ego, the distinctive core that unites a person's attitudes, perceptions and intentions |
| **Organising the energy level** | Propriate principle: new motives arise to use up surplus energy that could otherwise turn destructive |
| **Mastery and competence** | Propriate principle: healthy adults seek to perform better, learn new skills and grow more competent, not merely to get by |
| **Propriate patterning** | Propriate principle: striving for consistency and integration by organising perception and thought around the self |
| **Bodily self** | Proprium stage 1: awareness of one's own body as distinct from objects |
| **Self-identity** | Proprium stage 2: the sense of remaining the same person despite change |
| **Self-esteem** | Proprium stage 3: pride in doing things on one's own |
| **Extension of self** | Proprium stage 4: recognising people and objects as belonging to one's own world |
| **Self-image** | Proprium stage 5: actual and ideal pictures of oneself, shaped by parental expectations |
| **Self as a rational coper** | Proprium stage 6 (ages 6 to 12): using reason and logic on everyday problems |
| **Propriate striving** | Proprium stage 7 (adolescence): forming long-range goals and plans |
| **Extension of the sense of self** | Maturity criterion: concern for people and activities beyond oneself |
| **Self-objectification** | Maturity criterion: insight into oneself, together with a sense of humour |
| **Unifying philosophy of life** | Maturity criterion: an integrating set of values that directs the personality toward future goals |
| **Synoptic procedures** | Allport's eleventh assessment method: combining information from several sources into one summary |
| **Personal-document technique** | Assessing traits by studying a person's written or spoken records, such as diaries, letters and autobiographies |
| **Study of Values** | Objective self-report test (Allport, Vernon, & Lindzey, 1960) measuring six values: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, religious |
| **Idiographic approach** | Intensive study of the individual case; Allport's preferred approach |
| **Nomothetic approach** | Statistical study of differences across large samples to find general laws |
| **Expressive behaviour** | Spontaneous, apparently purposeless behaviour, usually outside awareness, that reveals personality traits |
| **Coping behaviour** | Consciously planned, purposeful behaviour driven by the situation's demands, usually aimed at changing the environment |
| **Facial Action Coding System (FACS)** | Ekman's system for reading emotion from 43 facial muscles in 3,000 configurations; used to detect lying |
| **Type A behaviour** | A behaviour pattern linked to heart-disease risk; distinguished from Type B by expressions of disgust, glaring, grimacing and scowling |
| **Display rules (classic background)** | Culturally learned norms about when and how intensely emotions may be shown |

## Likely exam points

1. **Allport's significance:** made personality academically respectable with *Personality: A Psychological Interpretation* (**1937**); founder of the modern trait approach.
2. **Three disagreements with Freud:** unconscious important only in neurotics; no historical determinism for healthy adults; sharp normal/abnormal distinction, so study healthy adults only.
3. **Life to theory:** youngest of four; isolation and inferiority compensated by striving; envy of **Floyd** → **functional autonomy**; **Freud meeting** (dirty-boy story, "Was that little boy you?") → emphasis on **conscious** motives.
4. **Firsts:** 1922 dissertation was the **first US study of personality traits**; he taught the **first US college course** on personality.
5. **Definition (1961)** and its parts: dynamic organisation, within the individual, psychophysical systems, determine, characteristic behaviour and thought; **1937** ending "unique adjustments to his environment".
6. **Heredity and environment:** heredity gives physique, intelligence, temperament and the **major share of uniqueness**; environment shapes it; so study the **individual case**.
7. **Discontinuity:** two personalities (infant and adult); conscious over unconscious, present-future over past, unique over general, normal over abnormal.
8. **Traits:** predispositions to respond similarly to different stimuli; **five characteristics** (real, causal, empirical, interrelated, situation-dependent).
9. **Common vs individual traits** and the renaming to **traits** vs **personal dispositions**.
10. **Cardinal (ruling passion; not universal), central (5 to 10), secondary (weak; close friends notice)** with Allport's examples.
11. **Motivation** is the central problem for any theory; explain the **present by the future**; conscious intentions matter.
12. **Functional autonomy** definition with the **tree/seed** analogy and the **25- vs 55-year-old** example.
13. **Perseverative** (addictions, routines; rat in maze) vs **propriate** (values, self-image; piano) functional autonomy.
14. **Three principles of propriate functioning:** organising the energy level, mastery and competence, propriate patterning.
15. **Exclusions** from functional autonomy: reflexes, fixations, neuroses, biological drives.
16. **Proprium:** why Allport coined it; link to "appropriate"; unites attitudes, perceptions, intentions.
17. **Seven stages** with ages (Table 7.1), and infants as "unsocialized horrors".
18. **Infant-mother bond:** security and affection → healthy proprium and functional autonomy; frustration → neurotic adult tied to childhood drives (Allport's account of disturbance).
19. **Six criteria of the mature personality**, especially **self-objectification** (insight + humour) and **unifying philosophy of life**.
20. **Human nature:** moderate free will; both nature and nurture; present/future; uniqueness; growth through **tension increase**; optimistic.
21. **Assessment:** no single best method; **11 methods**; reliance on personal documents and the Study of Values.
22. **Letters from Jenny:** 300+ letters, 12 years, 36 judges, about 200 traits → 8 categories; Paige (1966) computer analysis confirmed 8.
23. **Study of Values** (Allport, Vernon, & Lindzey, 1960): six values; basis of the unifying philosophy of life; a nomothetic test.
24. **Idiographic vs nomothetic**; Allport's objections to clinical case studies and projectives for normal adults; preference for self-description.
25. **Expressive vs coping behaviour**, with the public-speaker example; **Allport & Vernon (1933)** on consistency of voice, handwriting, posture, gesture.
26. **Thin-slice and yearbook evidence:** Ambody & Rosenthal (1992; 30 seconds); Harker & Keltner (2001; age 21 → 27, 43, 52).
27. **Clinical face-reading findings:** abused children and anger (Pollak & Sinha, 2002); depression and happiness vs sadness (Joormann & Gotlib, 2006); suicide prediction in Swiss depressed patients (Heller & Haynal, 1997).
28. **Ekman's FACS:** 43 muscles, 3,000 configurations, seven emotions; lie detection; Big Five-type traits visible in faces (Keltner, 1997).
29. **Universality vs culture:** shared basic expressions (Albright et al., 1997; Camras et al., 1998) vs cultural variation (Camras, 1998; Gendron et al., 2014; Jack et al., 2012; Dailey et al., 2010).
30. **Computers and emotion:** 30 fps recognition of six emotions (Susskind et al., 2007); emoticons mirror face-to-face expression (Derks et al., 2007).
31. **Criticisms:** concepts hard to test; no mechanism for functional autonomy, so no prediction; uniqueness blocks generalisation; doubtful discontinuities; little research; idiographic method out of step.
32. **Contributions:** academic respectability; genetic factors within a trait approach; influence on Maslow and Rogers; early humanistic voice; revival through trait research, cognitive neuroscience of emotion and personal documents.
