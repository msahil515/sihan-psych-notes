# Chapter 15: Personality in Perspective

**What this chapter is really about.** Fourteen chapters have given you close to two dozen theories, from Freud's psychoanalysis at the start of the twentieth century to the research programmes of the twenty-first. Read one after another they can look like a field in disarray: one theorist says personality is set by age 5, another says it is rebuilt at 40; one says we are driven by forces we cannot see, another that we are scientists testing hypotheses about our neighbours; one says genes do most of the work, another that reinforcement history does. Schultz and Schultz refuse to crown a winner. Their closing argument is that each theory grasped a real influence on personality and over-weighted it, so the useful question is not "which theory is right?" but "which influences does the evidence support, and which theorists saw each one?" They answer by sorting the book into **seven factors**: genetic, environmental, learning, parental, developmental, consciousness and unconscious.

That makes this the best chapter in the book for cross-theorist questions, which is where entrance papers set their traps: who thought personality was fixed by about 5, who put the great change at 35 to 40, who gave the unconscious no role, whose figures say intelligence is 80 percent inherited, who said the mother's care in the first year builds trust. The source chapter names many theorists in a sentence each. These notes add, in every module, a sentence or two tying each name back to its own chapter's concept (birth order, basic anxiety, the epigenetic principle, the proprium, unconditional positive regard, reinforcement, modelling), so the chapter works as a revision map of the whole book. Material taken from chapters 2 to 14 rather than from this chapter is marked **Classic background:**.

**How to read this chapter.** Modules 15.1 to 15.7 follow the book's order, and the first four form a deliberate chain: genes supply predispositions (15.1), the environment decides whether they are realised (15.2), it does so mainly through learning (15.3), and the most important early teachers are parents (15.4), whose influence is then challenged by peer and behaviour-genetics arguments. Module 15.5 asks whether all this is settled in childhood, and its closing model (McAdams's three levels) hands over to consciousness (15.6) and the unconscious (15.7). Module 15.8 is the book's closing comment. A theorist-by-factor comparison map then replaces the Questions about Human Nature tables of earlier chapters.

## 15.1 The Genetic Factor

### What it means to say a trait is inherited

Saying a trait is **inherited** does not mean a gene "for" extraversion switches extraversion on. It means that, across a population, part of the variation between people on the trait can be traced to variation in their genes. **Classic background:** the standard tool (Chapter 8) is the **twin study**. Identical (monozygotic) twins share essentially all their genes, fraternal (dizygotic) twins about half on average; if identical twins resemble each other more than fraternal twins do, even when reared apart, genes are doing part of the work. **Adoption studies** add a check: adopted children who resemble their biological more than their adoptive parents point to heredity. The resulting statistic, **heritability**, describes a group, not a person: a heritability of 50 percent does not mean half of *your* extraversion came from your genes. What genes give is a tilt, not a script, and that is where the module ends.

### The inherited dimensions the book lists

The book opens with dimensions for which the evidence of inheritance is now strong:

1. **Eysenck's three dimensions:** psychoticism, neuroticism and extraversion, the last derived from Jung's work.
2. **McCrae and Costa's five-factor model:** neuroticism, extraversion, openness to experience, agreeableness, conscientiousness.
3. **Buss and Plomin's three temperaments:** emotionality, activity, sociability.
4. **Zuckerman's sensation seeking**, described as influenced primarily by genetic factors.

**Classic background:** Eysenck (Chapter 8) defined **E** (extraversion versus introversion), **N** (neuroticism versus emotional stability) and **P** (psychoticism versus impulse control). He held all three to be determined primarily by heredity, with family influences limited, and supported this with twins (identical twins more alike even when reared apart) and adoptees (closer to biological parents). The genetic evidence was stronger for E and N than for P, and he estimated that some **80 percent of intelligence** is inherited (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1985). The Jung link: Jung's extraversion and introversion were **attitudes** of the psyche, outward or inward orientation; Eysenck turned the attitude into a measurable, heritable dimension.

**Classic background:** for the Big Five, Chapter 8 reports twin evidence that **neuroticism, extraversion, openness and conscientiousness** have the stronger hereditary component, while **agreeableness** has the stronger environmental one (Bergeman et al., 1993; Pedersen, Plomin, McClearn, & Friberg, 1988), and concludes that inheritance may account for **as much as 50 percent** of personality. For sensation seeking, Chapter 14 cites a study attributing **58 percent** of the trait to genes (Eysenck, 1983).

> **Trap.** Buss and Plomin's **temperaments** are not Cattell's **temperament traits**. The first are three inherited, early-appearing dispositions; the second are one of Cattell's three trait types (ability, temperament, dynamic), describing emotional style. Classic background: Buss and Plomin's 1975 model had a fourth temperament, **impulsivity** (EASI); the 1984 version dropped it, leaving **EAS**. A key listing four temperaments follows the earlier model.

### How many inherited traits are there?

The book treats the trait approach, with its emphasis on heredity, as a necessary line of research, but leaves open how many inherited factors there are: Cattell's **16**, Eysenck's **3**, McCrae and Costa's **5**, Buss and Plomin's **3**, or a number not yet found. Each count reflects choices about measurement and factor analysis rather than a settled fact.

**Classic background:** Cattell did most to estimate the genetic share, comparing twins and ordinary siblings, each reared together and apart. His data suggested **80 percent of intelligence (Factor B)** and **80 percent of timidity versus boldness (Factor H)** are genetic, yet his overall conclusion was that only **one-third** of personality is genetically based and **two-thirds** environmental. His terms encode the distinction: **constitutional traits** arise from physiological conditions; **environmental-mold traits** are learned from social interaction.

> **Trap.** The two Cattell numbers point opposite ways. **80 percent** applies to two specific factors, B and H. The **overall** split is **one-third heredity, two-thirds environment**. "Cattell concluded personality is mostly inherited" is wrong. And Eysenck also gave **80 percent** for intelligence, so the figure belongs to both researchers.

### Twin and cross-cultural evidence for the Big Five

- **Yamagata and colleagues (2006)** found support for a genetic basis of the five factors in twins from **Canada, Germany and Japan**; the principal author suggested the structure may "represent the common heritage of the human species" (Yamagata et al., 2006, p. 96). Three very different cultures yielded the same genetically based structure.
- **Shiner (2014)**: the Big Five became increasingly stable over **20 years**, childhood into adulthood.
- Twin and non-twin sibling studies in **Germany and Belgium** showed high trait stability from childhood through adolescence into young adulthood (DeFruyt, Bartels, Van Leeuwen, DeClercq, Decuyper, & Mervielde, 2006; Kandler, Bleidorn, Reimann, Spinath, Thiel, & Angleitner, 2010). Traits that hold steady while circumstances change are likely to be anchored biologically.
- A programme spanning **more than 50 cultures**, from **Israel** to **Korea** to **Turkey**, found the five factors in most nations. They appeared **less consistently** mainly in **developing countries**: **Ethiopia, Lebanon, Malaysia, Uganda** (Allik, Realo, & McCrae, 2013; Heine & Buchtel, 2009; Ispas, Illiescu, Ilie, & Johnson, 2014).

> **Trap.** Israel, Korea and Turkey are examples of the diverse cultures **where the factors held**, not exceptions. The exceptions were the developing countries.

### Psychopathy, anxiety and everyday behaviour

**Larsson, Andershed and Lichtenstein (2006)** studied fraternal and identical adolescent twins in **Sweden** and found a strong genetic component in the **psychopathic personality** prone to violent antisocial behaviour, one reason family history matters in forensic assessment. **Garcia and colleagues (2013)** found that the heritability of **anxiety increased between ages 14 and 18**, the opposite of what one might expect as experience accumulates. (Classic background: a common explanation is that older adolescents increasingly choose environments that suit their genetic tendencies, so the genes' influence shows more.)

Genetic influence also reaches ordinary behaviour. Studies in the **United States, Finland, French-speaking Canada and Sweden** found it in **sexual attitudes**, **eating in 2-year-olds**, **depression in bullied children** and **attitudes to practising a musical instrument**; and **identical twins were more likely than fraternal twins to own guns** (Barnes, Boutwell, & Beaver, 2014; Butkovic, Ullen, & Mosing, 2015; Dubois et al., 2013; Iyer, Dougall, & Jensen-Campbell, 2013; Westerlund, Santtila, Johansson, Jern, & Sandnabba, 2012). The bullying case is instructive: genes do not cause the bullying, but whether a bullied child becomes depressed depends partly on inherited vulnerability.

### Predispositions, not destinies

However many inherited traits there are, **not even the strongest advocate of the genetic approach claims heredity explains personality completely**. We inherit **predispositions**, tendencies rather than certainties, and whether they are realised depends on social and environmental conditions, above all in **childhood**. A child born with a tilt towards sociability can still become a withdrawn adult if every early approach to others is punished. That is the hinge into 15.2.

### Where every theorist stands on heredity

**Strong emphasis:** Eysenck; McCrae and Costa; Buss and Plomin; Zuckerman; Cattell for particular factors (B and H) though not overall.

**Innate potentials, shaped by experience.** **Classic background:** **Freud** took a middle ground: the **id** and the **psychosexual stages** are inherited, the rest is learned in early parent-child interaction. **Jung**'s **collective unconscious** is inherited by definition and the drive to individuation is innate. **Adler** made the potential for **social interest** innate, its expression a matter of experience. **Erikson**'s **epigenetic principle** makes the eight stages genetically given. **Allport** said heredity supplies physique, temperament and intelligence. **Maslow** called his needs **instinctoid** (with a hereditary component). **Rogers** made the **actualisation tendency** innate.

**Little role for heredity.** **Classic background:** **Horney** denied that biology condemns us to neurosis, locating its causes in childhood social relations. **Kelly** did not discuss heredity. **Skinner** saw people as products of learning. **Bandura** gave genetics only a minor role. **Rotter**'s locus of control and **Seligman**'s learned helplessness are learned facets.

> **Trap.** "Innate" is not "heritable trait". Maslow's needs, Rogers' actualisation tendency, Erikson's stages and Adler's social interest are **species-wide potentials** everyone has; they do not explain why people differ. Heritability claims concern **individual differences**, so "which theorist emphasised heritability of individual differences" wants Eysenck, Cattell or the Big Five, not Maslow or Rogers.

## 15.2 The Environmental Factor

### What counts as environment, and when it matters most

**Environment** here means every influence not carried in the genes: the family and the siblings already in it, the culture and its expectations of boys and girls, the historical period, the neighbourhood, the job, the marriage, the illness, the war. The first point is about timing. Research indicates that **genetic effects are most important in infancy and the earliest years**, while **environmental effects become increasingly important from childhood into adulthood** and go on acting throughout life (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; Kandler, 2012). The newborn shows mostly what was inherited; the adult shows, in addition, everything that has happened since.

**Classic background:** behaviour geneticists split the environment in two. The **shared environment** is what siblings reared together have in common (home, parents' income and education, general family atmosphere). The **non-shared environment** is unique to each child (being eldest or youngest, being favoured, different friends and teachers). Twin and adoption studies find the shared family environment has little effect on adult personality (Chapter 8: in many cases only "a minor influence"), so most environmental influence is non-shared. That is why two brothers raised under one roof can differ so much.

> **Trap.** **Shared** = the same for all children in the family; **non-shared** = different for each child. Adler's **birth order** is in effect a non-shared idea: Chapter 4 says siblings with the same parents in the same house still have different social environments. "Family environment contributes little" refers to the **shared** part; it does not mean experience is unimportant.

### What every theorist said about the environment

The book states that **every theorist it covers acknowledged the social environment**, and picks out examples.

**Adler: birth order.** Our position among siblings exposes us to different parental and social problems, depending on the age gaps or on having no siblings, and these different home environments produce different personalities. **Classic background:** the **first-born** has undivided attention until **dethroned**, and tends to be oriented to the past and concerned with power and order; the **second-born** competes with a pacesetter and tends to be ambitious; the **youngest** may race ahead or, if pampered, stay dependent; the **only child** never loses centre stage and may struggle when school does not grant it. Adler offered likelihoods, not rules.

**Horney: culture and gender.** Horney saw culture at work in the **different neuroses** of her **German** and **American** patients, a difference only social forces could explain. She stressed how differently boys and girls are raised, traced **female inferiority** to a **male-dominated culture**, and suggested women raised in a **matriarchal culture** might have higher self-esteem and different personalities. **Classic background:** this is the base of her **feminine psychology** (Chapter 5), including **womb envy** as her answer to penis envy.

**Allport and Cattell.** Even the founders of the trait approach agreed. **Allport**: genetics supplies the **raw material**, the social environment shapes the **finished product**. **Cattell**: heredity matters more for some of the **16 factors** than others, but environment eventually affects every factor to some degree (hence his **environmental-mold traits**).

**Erikson.** The **eight psychosocial stages are innate**, but the environment determines how those genetically based stages are realised, and **social and historical forces** shape **ego identity**. **Classic background:** Erikson's own adolescent uncertainty about his father's identity, and his rejection by both Jewish and gentile classmates, fed his concept of the identity crisis.

**Maslow and Rogers.** Both made **self-actualisation innate** and both said environmental factors can **inhibit or promote** it. **Classic background:** for Maslow, unmet lower needs keep a person working at the base of the hierarchy; for Rogers, the actualising process depends more on social than biological forces, above all on whether regard from others is conditional.

**Classic background, the rest:** **Skinner** and **Bandura** are environmentalists by definition (reinforcement contingencies; models and **reciprocal determinism**). **Jung** let experience aid or thwart individuation. **Kelly** is the exception: we are not wholly determined by the environment, because our **interpretation** of events, not the events themselves, shapes personality.

### Societal events and the birth cohort

**Wars and recessions** can restrict life choices and shape identity; ordinary changes such as becoming a parent, divorcing or changing jobs can also alter personality. Even one's generation matters, because standards, tastes and external threats differ for each. **Twenge (2000)** compared personality data from **more than 50,000** college students and young people in a **1950s** and a **1980s** birth cohort. The **1980s** cohort scored **substantially higher on anxiety and neuroticism**, which Twenge attributed to **declining social connectedness**: more divorce, fewer births, later first marriage, more people living alone.

> **Trap.** The **later** cohort was **more** anxious and neurotic, and the proposed cause is reduced **social connectedness**, not economic hardship or war.

### Parenthood, work and stressful events

**Parenthood.** In **Finland**, becoming a parent was linked to more **stress and anxiety** and lower **marital and life satisfaction**, and **emotionality** rose after the transition, rising more with **each additional child** (Jokela, Kivimaki, Elovainio, & Keltikangas-Jarvinen, 2009). Studies in the **United States, England and Turkey** found likewise that parenthood lowers marital satisfaction, more so with more children (Wendorf, Lucas, Imamoglu, Weisfeld, & Weisfeld, 2011). Here the child is the environmental event and the parent is the one who changes.

**Work.** **Roberts, Caspi and Moffitt (2003)** measured young **New Zealanders** at **18** and again at **26**. Those holding **satisfying, high-status jobs** at 26 had grown in **positive emotionality** (well-being, social closeness, achievement) and fallen in **negative emotionality** (aggressiveness, alienation, stress). The researchers concluded that the workplace can modify even innate traits; because personality was measured before the job, the change is not simply happy people choosing good jobs.

**Stressful events.** **Sutin, Costa, Wethington and Eaton (2010)** studied American adults in their **mid-40s** and **10 years later**. Those high in **neuroticism** came to see a stressful event (such as a spouse's death or job loss) as a **turning point**; those high in **extraversion** expected to **learn a lesson** from it. People who read the event as a **negative turning point became more neurotic**; those who saw a chance to learn **became more extraverted**. Personality shapes how an event is read, and the reading then reshapes personality.

### Ethnicity, culture, region and neighbourhood

**Ethnicity.** Earlier chapters showed ethnic differences in **sensation seeking, locus of control and need for achievement**, and showed that minority members build an **ethnic identity** alongside **ego identity** and must adapt to two cultures, with psychological health depending on how well they manage it. Among self-identified minority members in **Canada**, those with the **strongest ethnic identities** had the **highest self-esteem and subjective well-being** (Usborne & Taylor, 2010).

**Culture.** **Western** cultures tend to be more **individualistic** than **Eastern** ones, and Westerners tend to score **higher on extraversion, sensation seeking and subjective well-being**. **Classic background:** individualistic cultures prize independence and self-expression, collectivist cultures group harmony and duty, so a culture that rewards standing out produces more stimulation seekers.

**Region.** Chapter 14 showed regional differences in well-being, and other traits vary too. In the **United States**, **neuroticism** is higher in the **northeastern and southeastern** states, **agreeableness** higher in the **south**, and **extraversion** higher in the **northeast** and lower in the **west** (Rentfrow, 2010).

**Neighbourhood.** Children from **economically disadvantaged neighbourhoods** are more likely to show **low resiliency** and **problem behaviours** that lead to later social and emotional difficulties (Hart, Atkins, & Matsuba, 2008). In **London**, **life satisfaction, agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness** differed between neighbourhoods (Jokela, Bleidorn, Lamb, Gosling, & Rentfrow, 2015).

The impact of environmental and social forces is therefore undeniable, and the book adds the key step: their most important route to personality is **learning**.

## 15.3 The Learning Factor

### Learning as the channel

**Learning** here means any relatively lasting change in behaviour or disposition produced by experience. The evidence that it shapes virtually all behaviour is described as overwhelming, extending even to our **major life goals** (Bleidorn, Kandler, Hulsheger, Reimann, Angleitner, & Spinath, 2010). Every environmental force in 15.2 works **by way of learning**, and learning acts even on inherited facets, which can be **modified, disrupted, prevented or allowed to flourish**. A child with an inherited tilt towards sensation seeking may learn to channel it into mountaineering or into crime.

### Skinner and Bandura

**Skinner** showed the importance of **positive reinforcement**, **successive approximation** and **superstitious behaviour** in shaping what others call personality and he called simply an **accumulation of learned responses**. **Classic background:** **reinforcement** strengthens a response by following it with a reward; **operant conditioning** changes a response's rate by changing its consequences; **successive approximation** (shaping) builds complex behaviour such as speech by reinforcing steps ever closer to the target; **superstitious behaviour** persists because it happened to be followed by reinforcement, like the student's lucky exam shirt. Skinner rejected internal explanations as unobservable.

**Bandura** added learning by watching models (**observational learning**) and from the consequences others receive (**vicarious reinforcement**), and agreed with Skinner that most behaviour is learned and genetics plays a limited role. **Classic background:** in the Bobo doll studies, preschoolers who watched an adult attack an inflatable doll imitated the aggression, less so if the model was punished. Unlike Skinner, Bandura kept **cognition**: observation runs through attention, retention, production and motivation, and **reciprocal determinism** makes person and environment determine each other.

> **Trap.** Skinner and Bandura agree that most behaviour is learned and genes play a minor role; they disagree on whether cognition counts. "Skinner's theory is cognitive" and "Bandura rejected internal processes" are both wrong. **Vicarious reinforcement** is Bandura's term.

### Control: the learned dimension that keeps reappearing

The book gathers learned facets from earlier chapters, **self-efficacy** (Bandura), **locus of control** (Rotter), and **learned helplessness** and **optimism versus pessimism** (Seligman), and argues they share one core idea: the person's **level of control**. Someone who believes he or she controls life will be **high in self-efficacy**, **internal in locus of control**, **free of learned helplessness** (which is by definition a lack of control) and, in Seligman's terms, **optimistic**. High control has been linked to **better coping**, **fewer stress effects**, **better mental and physical health**, **perseverance**, **higher aspirations and self-esteem**, **lower anxiety**, **higher grades**, and **greater social skills and popularity**.

Under any of its names, this sense of control comes from the social and environmental world rather than the genes. Children acquire it in infancy and the early years, adults can still revise it, and specific parental behaviours foster it, so it is one of several learned dimensions in which **what parents do is paramount**. That is the bridge into 15.4.

**Classic background:** the earlier chapters say which parental behaviours. **Rotter**: parents of internally oriented children were supportive, praised achievement, were consistent in discipline and not authoritarian, and later encouraged independence. **Seligman**: a consistent **explanatory style** forms by about **age 8**, strongly shaped by the parents' own ("pessimistic parents also have pessimistic children"), with some genetic contribution. **Bandura**: early efficacy experiences centre on parents who respond to the infant's attempts to act and allow exploration; peers, siblings and teachers matter later.

> **Trap.** **Locus of control** (Rotter) is a belief about **where reinforcement comes from**. **Self-efficacy** (Bandura) is a belief in **one's own competence**. **Learned helplessness** (Seligman) is the **condition** following perceived lack of control, and **explanatory style** is how one **explains** that lack; an optimistic style prevents helplessness spreading.

### Learning in the other theories

**Classic background:** Adler's **style of life** is learned socially and set by **age 4 or 5**. Allport's learning shapes the genetic raw material. Maslow's needs are innate but the behaviours that satisfy them are **learned**. Erikson thought learning and experience outweigh heredity. Kelly's constructs are revised by experience like a scientist's hypotheses. Zuckerman allowed a learned component in sensation seeking (small but significant differences between young people in Turkey and Wales).

## 15.4 The Parental Factor

### Why parents get a factor of their own

Parents are part of the environment, but for most theorists in this book they are its first and most powerful part: the people who control nearly all of a young child's experiences, rewards and models. **Freud was the first theorist to stress parental influence**, and virtually every later theorist echoed him to some degree, even those who rejected the rest of psychoanalysis.

### What each theorist said about parents

**Freud.** **Classic background:** parents run the psychosexual drama. Their handling of feeding and toilet training can leave **fixation** (from too much frustration or gratification); the **Oedipus complex** (ages 4 to 5 in Schultz) resolves through identification with the same-sex parent; and the **superego** internalises parental values, its **conscience** holding what was punished and its **ego-ideal** what was praised.

**Adler.** Children who feel **unwanted or rejected** by parents can become **insecure**, **angry** and **deficient in self-esteem**. **Classic background:** **neglect** is one of three childhood sources of an **inferiority complex**, with **organic inferiority** and **pampering**.

**Horney.** Writing from her own childhood, Horney showed how a **lack of parental warmth and affection** undermines a child's **security** and leaves feelings of **helplessness**. **Classic background:** childhood is ruled by the **safety need**, and parents undermine it through favouritism, unfair punishment, erratic behaviour, broken promises, ridicule and isolation. The repressed hostility this provokes becomes **basic anxiety**, "an all-pervading feeling of being lonely and helpless in a hostile world" (Horney, 1937, p. 89), the root of neurosis.

**Allport.** The **infant's relationship with the mother** is the primary source of **affection and security**, which later development depends on. **Classic background:** with enough security the **proprium** (Allport's self) develops steadily and childhood motives can become **functionally autonomous**; without it the adult stays tied to childhood drives.

**Cattell.** **Infancy** is the major formative period, with **parents and siblings** shaping character. **Classic background:** his infancy stage runs from **birth to 6** and covers weaning, toilet training, and ego and superego formation, ideas borrowed from Freud.

**Erikson.** The relationship with the mother in the **first year** is vital for a **trusting attitude**. **Classic background:** stage 1, **trust versus mistrust (birth to 1)**, basic strength **hope**.

**Maslow.** Parents must satisfy the child's **physiological and safety needs in the first two years**, a prerequisite for the higher needs. **Classic background:** too much permissiveness also threatens safety needs, so the ideal is a **balance of permissiveness and regulation**.

**Rogers.** Parents are responsible for **unconditional positive regard**. **Classic background:** regard given only for approved behaviour (**conditional positive regard**) creates **conditions of worth**, which Rogers likened to the superego, and these produce **incongruence** between self and experience.

**Classic background, the rest:** **Jung** saw a young child's personality as largely a reflection of the parents'. **Bandura, Rotter and Seligman** tied self-efficacy, locus of control and explanatory style to specific parental behaviours (15.3). **Zuckerman** noted that low-sensation-seeking parents may forbid adventure while high-sensation-seeking parents encourage it.

### Parents shape even inherited traits

Parental behaviour can build or undermine **self-efficacy, locus of control, learned helplessness or optimism, and subjective well-being**, and can influence **primarily inherited traits** such as **sensation seeking**. Uncaring, punitive parents could easily stifle inherited **extraversion, sociability, agreeableness and openness**. This is 15.1 from the other side: genes supply the predisposition, parents control much of whether it can show.

### Parenting styles

Much evidence shows that children of **authoritative** parents, **warm but firm**, are **more competent and mature** than children of **permissive, harsh or indifferent** parents. **Steinberg and Morris (2001)** carried the finding into adolescence: the combination of "parental responsiveness and demandingness" is consistently tied to adolescents' adjustment, school performance and psychosocial maturity (p. 88), as in earlier childhood. **Ang (2006)** found that **Singapore** adolescents with **authoritative** parents had **more confidence in their abilities** and **better social adjustment** than those with **authoritarian** parents (strict, harsh, demanding obedience).

**Classic background:** the typology is **Diana Baumrind**'s (1960s and early 1970s), built on **responsiveness (warmth)** and **demandingness (control)**: authoritative = high on both, with explanation; authoritarian = low warmth, high control; permissive = high warmth, low control. Maccoby and Martin (1983) added **neglectful (uninvolved)**, low on both, the book's "indifferent".

> **Trap.** **Authoritative** and **authoritarian** are both high in control; only **authoritative** is also **warm**. The good outcomes in this chapter belong to authoritative parenting.

### Parents' personalities and culture

A large analysis found that parents **high in extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness and openness** behaved more **warmly and consistently** towards their children, and those **higher in agreeableness and lower in neuroticism** gave more **support for independence** (Prinzie, Stams, Dekovic, Reijntjes, & Belsky, 2009). Parents also teach children to be **kind, caring and helpful** (Fortuna & Knafo, 2014).

Style varies with culture. Parents in **Arab cultures** tend to be more **authoritarian**. Among mothers who had immigrated to **Canada**, those from **collectivist** cultures (**Egypt, Iran, India, Pakistan**) were more authoritarian than those from **individualistic Western Europe** (Rudy & Grusec, 2006). **Classic background:** cross-cultural researchers (for example Ruth Chao's 1994 work on the Chinese idea of "training") argue that strict parenting in collectivist settings is often experienced as care rather than coldness, so one style can predict different outcomes in different cultures, a point worth raising in an Indian interview.

### Praise, and the costs of negative parenting

**Praise** from parents promotes **autonomy**, **realistic standards**, **competence**, **self-efficacy** and **intrinsic motivation** to achieve. Negative parenting does lasting harm:

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Brewin, Andrews & Gotlib (1993)** | Review linking early childhood to adult psychopathology | Depressed and anxious adults had more **rejecting, abusive**, less **caring** parents |
| **Kochanska, Clark & Goldman (1997)** | Mothers and children | Mothers with **negative emotions, disagreeableness** had children higher in **defiance, anger, disobedience** |
| **Raikkonen, Katainen, Keskivaara & Keltikangas-Jarvinen (2000)** | **12-year** study, **Finland**; mothers measured at child's ages **3 and 6** | **Hostile** child-rearing attitudes predicted **hostile** children by **15** |
| **Kasser, Koestner & Lekes (2002)** | US longitudinal, ages **5 and 31** | **Restrictive, cold, strict** parenting at 5 → adults **high in conformity, low in self-direction** |

The last two measured parenting years before the outcome, so they cannot be explained by troubled adults recalling their parents harshly; the review is more open to that recall bias.

### What determines how parents behave

It was long assumed that parents simply reproduce the parenting they received. **Klahr and Burt (2014)** studied **more than 20,000 families** in countries from **Australia to Japan to the United States** and found that **genetic factors accounted for up to 40 percent of parental behaviour**, and that some parental behaviour responds to **the child's own behaviour**. Parents both **influence and reflect** their children.

> **Trap.** The **40 percent** is the genetic share of **parents' behaviour**, not of children's personality; don't confuse it with Chapter 8's "up to 50 percent" for personality. And the finding is **bidirectional**: a correlation between harsh parents and difficult children does not prove the parents caused it.

### When parents are not the primary caregivers

**Harvey (1999)** analysed a **national longitudinal survey** of children aged **3 to 12**: mothers' employment outside the home produced **no significant problems** with behaviour or self-esteem, so care by others had no negative impact on these variables.

Israel's **kibbutzim** (communal settlements) are a natural laboratory. Mothers care for infants only for the **first few months**; professional caregivers then take primary charge, and children spend more time with them than with parents. **Aviezer and colleagues (1994)** found:

- kibbutz children **functioned and adapted well**, provided they had a **secure bond with their parents in infancy**;
- the **strength of that bond** best predicted children who became **dominant, independent and achievement oriented**;
- **barracks-style communal sleeping** in early childhood could produce a more **anxious, restrained, emotionally flat** personality;
- adults who **failed to bond** with parents or caregivers showed **introversion**, a **reduced capacity for friendship** and **less emotional intensity** in relationships.

Shared care was not the problem; failing to form a secure early bond was, which fits Erikson's and Allport's emphasis.

### The peer challenge

In the **late 1990s** a controversy erupted over the claim that parental behaviour has **no long-term effect on personality outside the home**. **Peers**, on this view, matter more: children adopt classmates' and friends' behaviour, attitudes and values to win acceptance. Parents' influence is not denied, but held to be **confined to the home**. Harris (quoted in Sleek, 1998) said children leave home-learned behaviour behind when they go out, as they would discard an embarrassing sweater their mother made them wear. **Classic background:** this is **Judith Rich Harris**'s **group socialisation theory**, from her 1998 book **The Nurture Assumption**.

**Loehlin (1997)** gave modest support: among twins in **late adolescence**, those with **more friends in common** were **more alike in personality**, suggesting friends mattered more than home. Researchers who give genes primacy also minimise the parental effect, saying **family environment contributes little**. However this is settled, it raises the next question: is personality **fixed early**, or can it change later?

> **Trap.** The peer view does **not** say parents have no influence, only that it is **context-specific**. Loehlin's support is **modest**, from **late-adolescent twins**, with **shared friends** predicting similarity.

## 15.5 The Developmental Factor

### Settled in childhood, or open for life?

The earlier factors ask **what** shapes personality; this one asks **when**. At one pole is Freud, who held that personality is **shaped and fixed by about age 5** and hard to change afterwards. The research view the book reports is more moderate: childhood is crucial, but personality **keeps developing well beyond it**, perhaps across the **whole lifespan**. **Classic background:** the glossary name for Freud's position is **historical determinism**, the view that personality is basically fixed in the early years; in his theory id, ego and superego are formed by about 5, and latency (about 5 to puberty) adds nothing new to the structure.

### Who allowed later change, and who put the big change in middle age

1. **Childhood important, but personality modifiable later:** **Cattell, Allport, Erikson**.
2. **Middle age a time of major change:** **Jung, Maslow, Erikson, Cattell**. (Other theorists stressed continuing development in adolescence.)

Erikson and Cattell are in both lists, which is exactly what examiners exploit.

**Classic background, theorist by theorist:**

- **Jung** placed major change **between 35 and 40**. With the external tasks of the first half of life done, energy turns inward, the attitude shifts towards introversion, the unconscious must be confronted, and the goal becomes **individuation**. His own midlife crisis after the break with Freud is the source.
- **Erikson** spread development over **eight stages**. In Schultz, **adulthood (35 to 55)** is **generativity versus stagnation** (strength **care**) and **maturity (55 and over)** is **ego integrity versus despair** (strength **wisdom**); a failure at one stage can be repaired later.
- **Cattell** gave **six stages** across life: infancy (birth to 6), childhood (6 to 14), adolescence (14 to 23), maturity (23 to 50), **late maturity (50 to 65)**, old age (65 on). In late maturity people re-examine values and seek a new self, which Chapter 8 itself compares with Jung's midlife.
- **Maslow** found only 1 of 3,000 college students self-actualising, turned to the **middle-aged and older**, and still found fewer than **1 percent** who qualified.
- **Allport**: the proprium develops through **seven stages** from infancy to adolescence, but the healthy adult is **functionally autonomous**, guided by the **present and future** more than the past.

The rest (all **Classic background**): **Adler** said the style of life is crystallised by **age 4 or 5** but that we use childhood creatively rather than being its victims, and are pulled by **fictional goals** for the future. **Horney** held that human nature is flexible, so **adult experience may matter as much as childhood**. **Rogers** said later experience outweighs childhood and **present feelings** matter most. **Kelly** denied any path fixed in childhood; constructs look to the **future**. **Skinner** implied childhood matters most because basic behaviours form then, but anything learned can be modified at any age. **Bandura** allowed childhood learning may count for more but said adults are not its victims, and traced self-efficacy from childhood to old age.

> **Trap.** "Past versus present" is not "when is personality fixed". Only **Freud** says personality is essentially **fixed by about 5**. **Adler** gives a similar age for the **style of life** but rejects being a victim of the past and stresses future goals. **Jung** looks to the **future** and puts the great change at **35 to 40**; **Allport** and **Rogers** stress the **present**; **Kelly** and **Adler** the **future**.

> **Trap.** **Allport** is in the "modifiable later" list but **not** the middle-age list; **Jung** and **Maslow** are in the middle-age list but not the other; Freud is in neither.

### Does personality change? Yes. Is it stable? Also yes.

The book admits the evidence supports diverse views. Does personality change? Yes. Is it stable? Probably yes. Refine the question to "do some characteristics stay stable over a lifetime while others change?" and the answer is an unqualified yes.

**Classic background:** a developmental distinction resolves much of the conflict. **Rank-order stability** asks whether people keep their position relative to one another; **mean-level change** asks whether a whole age group moves up or down on a trait. Both can hold at once: everyone can grow more agreeable while keeping their relative order. Roberts and DelVecchio's meta-analysis (below) is about rank-order consistency; "agreeableness and conscientiousness rise" is a mean-level finding.

### Stability from about 30, and the direction of drift

The basic foundation of **enduring dispositions**, including the **Big Five**, stays stable over many years, and the evidence suggests these traits and capacities are enduring **from age 30 on** (Nave, Sherman, Funder, Hampson, & Goldberg, 2010; Terracciano, McCrae, & Costa, 2010).

Within that stability there is a predictable drift. From college age to middle age, **neuroticism, extraversion and openness decline**, while **agreeableness and conscientiousness increase**. Cross-cultural work has found the same pattern in South Korea and Australia, in Croatia and Portugal, in Italy, Germany and the Netherlands, and in the United States (McCrae & Costa, 1997; McCrae et al., 1999; Allemand, Zimprich, & Hendriks, 2008; Lucas & Donnellan, 2009). An age trend that replicates from Seoul to Lisbon is unlikely to be one culture's expectations about growing up.

> **Trap.** "**N, E, O down; A, C up**", college age to middle age. Distractors reverse extraversion or put openness among the rising traits.

### Findings that complicate the picture

- **Helson, Jones and Kwan (2002)** followed **several hundred people for 40 years**: **dominance and independence peaked in middle age**, and personality **kept changing after 20**.
- **Roberts and DelVecchio (2000)**: a **meta-analysis of 152 longitudinal studies** with **more than 55,000 people** found high **consistency at all ages**, highest in **adulthood**, reaching its peak **after age 50**.

> **Trap.** Two stability ages appear. **About 30** is when basic traits become enduring (Nave et al.; Terracciano et al.; Ferguson). **After 50** is when Roberts and DelVecchio found consistency **highest**. Consistency rises from 30 and keeps climbing. Pair Roberts and DelVecchio with **152 studies, 55,000+ people**.

### Adolescence, college and later life

- **Pullmann, Raudsepp and Allik (2006)**: **Estonian** teenagers aged **12 to 18** had **stable** Big Five scores over the **two-year** study.
- **Vaidya, Gray, Haig and Watson (2002)**: American college students over **30 months** became **more open, agreeable and conscientious**.
- **Ferguson (2010)**: **normal and abnormal** personality factors may reach peak stability **around 30** and stay fairly stable thereafter.
- **Allemand, Zimprich and Martin (2008)**, **Switzerland**: people in their **60s** become **less neurotic, less extraverted, less open** with age.
- **Twins aged 64 to 98**: **extraversion, conscientiousness and perceived control** decreased, with some sign of **rising neuroticism**, but **no significant change in well-being** (Berg & Johansson, 2014; Kandler, Kornadt, Hagemeyer, & Neyer, 2014). In the oldest group the adult trends partly reverse, plausibly as health and independence decline, yet well-being holds.

### The age curves for conscientiousness and agreeableness

**Srivastava, John, Gosling and Potter (2003)** surveyed **more than 32,000 people aged 21 to 60** online: **conscientiousness and agreeableness rose** through early and middle adulthood, **conscientiousness most in the 20s**, **agreeableness most in the 30s**. **Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006)**: from young adulthood to middle age people become **more socially dominant, more conscientious and more emotionally stable**. Large studies also support general stability **after 30 and into late adulthood** (Johnson, McGue, & Krueger, 2005; Terracciano, Costa, & McCrae, 2006).

> **Trap.** **C in the 20s, A in the 30s** (Srivastava et al., 2003; **32,000+**, ages **21 to 60**, online). Do not confuse the sample with Roberts and DelVecchio's 55,000 or Twenge's 50,000.

### Maturation in early adulthood

**Roberts, Caspi and Moffitt (2001)** followed **New Zealanders from 18 to 26**: they showed growing **psychological maturity**, becoming **more self-controlled** and **socially confident** and **less angry and alienated**, with **women more mature overall than men**. **Shiner, Masten and Tellegen (2002)** surveyed children aged **8 to 12** and again **10 years later**: changes between **18 and 22** were significantly predictable from **childhood personality**. Personality does change into early adulthood, and the change grows out of what the child already was.

> **Trap.** The same team and the same New Zealand 18-to-26 cohort appear twice. **2001** (this module): rising **maturity**, women more mature. **2003** (15.2): **satisfying high-status jobs** raised positive emotionality.

### What drives change in adulthood

Many psychologists trace adult change to **social and environmental influences and our adjustments to them**: shifts in finances, leaving college, marriage and parenthood, divorce, job loss or promotion, midlife crises, ageing parents (McAdams & Olson, 2010).

- **Van Aken and colleagues (2006)**: a **3-year** study in the **Netherlands** of people in their **40s**; those who had adapted to expected roles (career and family success) scored higher on the five factors, so change accompanied successful adaptation to midlife.
- **Job loss** brought significant **rises in neuroticism** and **falls in conscientiousness and extraversion**; adults **actively dating** and keeping up relationships were **lower in neuroticism** and **higher in extraversion, conscientiousness and self-esteem** (Costa, Herbst, McCrae, & Siegler, 2000; Neyer & Asendorpf, 2001).

### McAdams's three levels of personality

To reconcile stability and change the book presents **McAdams (1994)**: personality develops over time on **three levels**.

| Level | Content | Change |
|---|---|---|
| **Dispositional traits** | Inherited traits of the McCrae and Costa kind (basic neuroticism, extraversion) | Stable and relatively unchanging **from about 30** |
| **Personal concerns** | **Conscious** feelings, plans and goals: what we want, how we pursue it, how we feel about people in our lives | Can change **often** as situations change |
| **Life narrative** | The life story: shaping a self, gaining an **identity**, finding a **unified purpose** | Revised in response to circumstances and each life stage |

A woman high in neuroticism at 30 is probably still high at 50, but her goals may have moved from building a career to caring for ageing parents, and her story from "the anxious one" to "the one who holds the family together". **Traits stay largely constant; conscious judgements about who we are and want to be stay open to change.** "Conscious judgements" is the bridge to the consciousness factor.

> **Trap.** Order: **traits, personal concerns, life narrative**. Only traits are **inherited** and **stable from 30**; personal concerns are explicitly **conscious**. Classic background: the narrative level echoes **Erikson's ego identity**.

## 15.6 The Consciousness Factor

### What consciousness means here

**Consciousness** in this chapter means the **cognitive** side of personality: perceiving, thinking, judging, remembering, planning and anticipating, with awareness. The book's claim is that almost every theory deals with conscious processes, explicitly or implicitly. This is the least disputed factor; the interest lies in how differently each theorist handled it.

### The psychoanalysts: an ego that perceives and remembers

Even **Freud and Jung**, famous for the unconscious, described an **ego** or conscious mind that perceives, thinks, feels and remembers, letting us deal with the real world; through it we perceive stimuli and later recall images of them. **Classic background:** Freud's ego is the **rational** part of personality, working by the **reality principle** and using mature **secondary-process thought**, and it operates partly below awareness. Jung's ego is simply the **conscious aspect of personality**, the centre of consciousness.

**Jung** also wrote of **rational functioning**, consciously judging and evaluating experience. **Classic background:** his **rational functions** are **thinking** and **feeling**, because both evaluate (what does this mean; is it pleasant or acceptable?). **Sensing** and **intuiting** are **irrational** because they receive experience without judging it.

> **Trap.** In Jung's system **feeling is a rational function**. Rational = thinking and feeling (judging); irrational = sensing and intuiting (perceiving).

### Adler, Allport, Rogers and Maslow

**Adler** saw humans as **conscious, rational beings** who plan and direct their lives: we form hopes, plans and dreams, delay gratification and anticipate the future. **Classic background:** for Adler the conscious was the core of personality, the basis of **fictional finalism** and the **creative power of the self**.

**Allport** held that people who are **not neurotic** function consciously and rationally, aware of and in control of their motives. **Classic background:** the unconscious, for him, matters only in neurotics, which is why he distrusted projective tests for normal adults and preferred self-description.

**Rogers** saw people as primarily **rational**, governed by a **conscious perception** of themselves and their world of experience. **Classic background:** his **experiential world** is reality as each person consciously perceives it, so therapy works with what the client reports, and he was wary of inferring experiences outside awareness.

**Maslow** recognised consciousness in the **cognitive needs**, the innate needs **to know and to understand**. **Classic background:** these are a separate set of needs, not a level of the five-level hierarchy.

> **Trap.** In Schultz the hierarchy has **five** levels and the cognitive needs are **separate**. Classic background: many other texts and older keys show a seven or eight-level pyramid (adding cognitive, aesthetic and sometimes transcendence needs). Answer from the version the question assumes.

### Kelly: the most complete cognitive theory

**Kelly** receives the strongest billing: the **most complete theory based on cognitive factors**. We form **constructs** about the environment and other people and use them to make **predictions (anticipations)**, framing hypotheses about our social world and testing them against experience. Everyday life makes it hard to deny that people construe and anticipate how others will act and adjust accordingly. **Classic background:** a **construct** is a **bipolar** hypothesis (honest versus dishonest) used to interpret events; Kelly's model person is a **scientist**; the theory has a **fundamental postulate** (our psychological processes are channelled by how we anticipate events) and **11 corollaries**; **constructive alternativism** says we can revise or replace constructs. His tools were the **Role Construct Repertory (REP) Test** and **fixed role therapy**.

### Bandura: anticipating consequences we have only watched

**Bandura** credited us with learning by **example** and **vicarious reinforcement**, which requires us to **anticipate and appreciate the consequences** of others' actions. We **picture** the outcome we would get by copying the model, though we may never have experienced it. A child who sees an older sibling scolded for lying does not need to be scolded too.

### The others

**Classic background:** **Horney** believed we can **consciously shape and change** personality, hence her emphasis on **self-analysis**. **Erikson** said we can **consciously direct** growth, with more choice in the last four stages than the first four. **Rotter**'s expectancies and **Seligman**'s explanatory style are cognitive beliefs. The dissenter is **Skinner**, who refused to explain behaviour by any internal state, conscious or unconscious.

The conclusion: **wide agreement** that consciousness exists and influences personality, and **much less agreement** about the role, or even the existence, of the unconscious.

## 15.7 The Unconscious Factor

### Freud's unconscious

**Freud** opened up the **unconscious**, a murky store of our darkest fears, conflicts and forces that still steer conscious thought and behaviour. Psychologists have found **some evidence** that thoughts and memories are **repressed** into it and that **repression** and other **defence mechanisms** can operate unconsciously.

**Classic background:** Freud's three levels are the **conscious** (current awareness), the **preconscious** (material not in awareness but easily recalled) and the **unconscious** (the largest level: instincts, wishes and memories kept out of awareness because they would provoke anxiety). **Repression** is the unconscious denial of something that causes anxiety; **defence mechanisms** are ego strategies against anxiety that deny or distort reality. Chapter 2 also covers the controversy over **repressed memories** of childhood abuse, with evidence that such memories can exist and that **false memories** can be implanted.

> **Trap.** **Repression** is **unconscious**. Classic background: **suppression** is **conscious** and deliberate, and **denial** in Schultz's glossary concerns an **external** threat or traumatic event. The **preconscious** is not the unconscious; its contents are accessible on demand.

### The modern revival: the non-conscious

The **cognitive movement**, which restored conscious processes to psychology, also revived interest in the unconscious. Research now shows it is powerful, **perhaps more pervasive** than Freud suggested, but the modern version is not Freud's: researchers study **unconscious cognitive processes** and describe them as **more rational than emotional**. This rational unconscious is often called the **non-conscious**, to separate it from Freud's "dark cauldron" of repressed wishes. **Classic background:** Kihlstrom's (1987) term **cognitive unconscious** names the same idea: perception, memory and information processing outside awareness with no motive to hide them.

### Studying it: subliminal activation

In **subliminal activation**, stimuli are presented **below the threshold of conscious awareness**. Participants cannot report them, yet their conscious processes and behaviour are **activated** by them: people can be influenced by stimuli they can neither see nor hear. **Classic background:** the glossary term is **subliminal perception** (*sub*, below; *limen*, threshold). Chapter 2's examples include Shevrin (1977), where unseen words and pictures surfaced in later free associations (a picture of a bee produced "sting" and "honey").

### "Mommy and I are one"

The book recalls **Silverman and Weinberger (1985)**, where subliminal stimuli influenced **cognitive and emotional** responses and had **therapeutic value**, although participants were unaware of the messages. Its conclusion is that the unconscious may have **both a rational and an emotional component**. **Classic background:** in Chapter 2's account, "Mommy and I are one" was flashed for **4 milliseconds** with a picture of a man and woman joined at the shoulders; **male schizophrenic patients** improved more than controls, while **female** patients improved only with "Daddy and I are one". Other studies used the message to help people stop smoking and drinking, become more assertive, eat better and reduce fears (Weinberger & Silverman, 1990).

> **Trap.** **Non-conscious** = modern, **rational, cognitive** unconscious; Freud's = **emotional**, repressed wishes. "Mommy and I are one" is the book's evidence that the modern unconscious has an **emotional** side too, hence "both rational and emotional". It is a **subliminal** study, not a dream or free-association study.

### Who kept the unconscious and who dropped it

The book's verdict: the unconscious is an active research topic today, yet **many theorists after Freud ignored it**. The line-up (all **Classic background**):

- **Freud**: the largest, most powerful level of mind.
- **Jung**: two layers. The **personal unconscious** holds once-conscious, forgotten or suppressed material organised into **complexes**; the **collective unconscious** holds the inherited experience of the species as **archetypes**.
- **Adler**: minimised it; consciousness is the core.
- **Horney**: kept unconscious motivation; children **repress** hostility towards parents, which feeds basic anxiety.
- **Erikson**: kept the psychoanalytic framework but gave the ego more independence; the unconscious is present but not central.
- **Allport**: relevant **only for neurotics**.
- **Cattell, Eysenck, McCrae and Costa**: measured traits by questionnaires, ratings and tests and did not build the unconscious in.
- **Maslow**: acknowledged Freud's unconscious (*Toward a Psychology of Being*) but built on conscious growth.
- **Rogers**: criticised for **ignoring** unconscious forces.
- **Kelly**: denied we are driven by unconscious forces or conflicts (though he allowed constructs at a low level of awareness, such as **preverbal** constructs and the **submerged** pole of a construct, which are not a Freudian unconscious).
- **Skinner**: rejected unconscious forces, defences and traits as unobservable, no more scientific than the soul.
- **Bandura**: did not deal with supposed unconscious conflicts; the symptom itself is the target.

The module ends humbly. Freud's **emotional unconscious**, the startling idea that marked the formal start of personality study, remains the **least understood factor**, still **mysterious and inaccessible**, much as in Freud's day.

> **Trap.** Jung's **collective unconscious is inherited**, so it belongs to the genetic factor as well. Classic background: older texts and keys call it the **racial unconscious**. Chapter 3 compares Jung's **personal unconscious** with Freud's **preconscious** (easy two-way traffic with the ego); the collective unconscious was never conscious in the individual's life.

## 15.8 Final Comment

The book closes by admitting that much of personality is still mysterious and not fully accessible. It has covered many ways of defining and describing personality, and each theory added part of the answer to what personality is. The journey ran from **Freud**'s emphasis on **anxiety, the unconscious, and a life of fear and repression** to **positive psychology** and the **happy personality** (Chapter 14), with many ideas between, and the authors expect new findings and theories not yet imagined. Enormous progress has been made in charting personality and the factors that shape it, but the field remains active, and "What is personality?" may be psychology's most important question because asking it is the attempt to understand ourselves.

**Classic background:** keep Chapter 1's definition ready for the interview: personality is the unique, relatively enduring **internal and external** aspects of a person's character that influence behaviour in different situations. The seven factors map onto it. "Unique" is a particular mix of genes, environments, parents and learning. "Relatively enduring" is the finding that traits settle from about 30 while concerns and stories keep changing. "Internal" covers conscious and unconscious processes, "external" what others observe. The book's own shape tells the field's story too: from **grand theories** built on clinical observation to the **limited-domain approach** of Chapter 14, which studies one facet (locus of control, sensation seeking, learned helplessness, happiness) with rigorous measurement.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Predispositions realised through experience.** The first four factors are one chain: genes give tendencies (15.1); the environment decides whether they are realised, more so with age (15.2); it acts through learning (15.3); and parents are the chief early teachers (15.4).
2. **Every theorist was partly right.** Each was right about the factor he or she stressed and wrong mainly in ignoring the others. Freud was right that parents and the unconscious matter, wrong that personality closes at 5; Skinner right about learning, wrong to drop cognition; the trait researchers right about heritability and stability, yet even they concede the environment.
3. **Control is the recurring learned dimension.** Self-efficacy, internal locus of control and optimism are three names for a learned sense of control, fostered by particular parenting and tied to health, coping and achievement.
4. **Influence runs both ways.** Children shape parenting as parents shape children (Klahr & Burt, 2014); personality shapes how events are read, and the reading reshapes personality (Sutin et al., 2010).
5. **Stable core, changing surface.** Basic traits settle from about 30, with consistency still rising after 50, while personal concerns and life narratives keep changing (McAdams, 1994).
6. **Consciousness agreed, unconscious disputed.** Nearly every theorist built in conscious cognition; the unconscious split the field, and the modern non-conscious is more rational than Freud's, though "Mommy and I are one" suggests an emotional side remains.
7. **From grand theory to research.** The book moves from comprehensive clinical theories to limited-domain research and behaviour genetics, but the grand theorists' questions (how much is inborn, how much parents matter, when personality settles, how much we know of ourselves) are exactly the seven factors.

## Worked example

*Meera, a 26-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru, is referred to an outpatient clinic six months after losing her job in a restructuring. She reports constant worry, poor sleep and the belief that "nothing I do changes anything", and says she has "always been the anxious one". Her mother, a schoolteacher, is tense and critical; her father worked in the Gulf through most of her childhood. Her brother, two years younger, is relaxed and outgoing. From 10 to 15 she attended a residential school, where she found a close group of hard-working friends and did well. She has cancelled two of her last four sessions "by mistake", both in the week of her mother's birthday. She has started a journal and says she wants to "stop being the girl things happen to". In a viva you are asked to formulate her difficulties using Schultz's seven factors.*

**Genetic.** Lifelong anxiety in her and her mother suggests a predisposition to high **neuroticism**, a Big Five factor with a strong hereditary component and one of Eysenck's dimensions he considered primarily inherited; anxiety's heritability also rises from 14 to 18 (Garcia et al., 2013). But the mother is also her model and main environment, the very confound twin and adoption designs exist to break. A predisposition, not a destiny.

**Environmental.** Job loss is linked to rising neuroticism and falling conscientiousness and extraversion (Costa et al., 2000), and Sutin and colleagues (2010) supply the mechanism: high-neuroticism people tend to read stress as a negative turning point and then grow more neurotic. Her difference from her brother is a **non-shared environment** question. Adler would add birth order: dethroned at 2, more expected of her, and first-borns described as often oriented to the past and pessimistic about the future. That is a hypothesis, not a rule.

**Learning.** "Nothing I do changes anything" is close to **learned helplessness**, with an **external locus of control** (Rotter), low **self-efficacy** (Bandura) and a pessimistic **explanatory style** (Seligman): three faces of low perceived **control**, all learned, all linked to poorer coping and higher anxiety. Bandura would add **modelling** of her mother's anxiety; Skinner would note that avoidance, such as cancelling sessions, is reinforced by the relief it brings.

**Parental.** A critical mother fits Horney's undermining of security leading to **basic anxiety**, and Rogers' **conditional positive regard** creating **conditions of worth** ("I am acceptable only if I make no mistakes"). Kasser et al. (2002) and Brewin et al. (1993) support the concern. Two cautions: Klahr and Burt (2014) show parenting is partly genetic and partly a response to the child, so a sensitive child may have drawn more criticism; and the peer argument (Harris; Loehlin, 1997) suggests her school friends may have shaped her conscientiousness as much as home did. The kibbutz findings concern communal sleeping in early childhood and should not be applied directly to a residential school at 10.

**Developmental.** At 26 she is below the age (about 30) at which basic traits settle, in a period of increasing maturity (Roberts, Caspi, & Moffitt, 2001). Freud would trace the anxiety to before 5 and expect change to be slow; Erikson would see a young adult in **intimacy versus isolation** (18 to 35) reworking earlier mistrust; Allport would suspect her worry is now **functionally autonomous**, sustained by the present. McAdams gives the practical frame: **dispositional** neuroticism may shift little, but **personal concerns** and the **life narrative** ("the girl things happen to") can change, and her journal is already rewriting the narrative.

**Consciousness.** Kelly would map her **constructs** (perhaps "in control versus at others' mercy", "perfect versus failure") and use **constructive alternativism** to replace them. Bandura would build self-efficacy through graded mastery; Rogers would offer the unconditional positive regard she missed.

**Unconscious.** Sessions missed "by mistake" in the week of her mother's birthday is what a Freudian reads as **resistance**, perhaps reflecting **repressed** hostility towards the mother, which Horney would link to basic anxiety. A contemporary view adds a **non-conscious** bias: Chapter 2 reports that people high in anxiety sensitivity detect subliminal anxiety words more readily. Since the emotional unconscious is the least understood factor, this is the most tentative part of the formulation.

**Synthesis.** The defensible answer is an interaction: an inherited tilt towards neuroticism, strengthened by critical parenting that taught low control, triggered by a job loss read as a negative turning point, maintained by reinforced avoidance and possibly by unacknowledged conflict with her mother. Because she is under 30 and her concerns and narrative are changeable, the prognosis is reasonable. Asked which factor matters most, the chapter's own answer is that each contributes a piece.

## MCQ traps

1. **Seven factors, in order:** genetic, environmental, learning, parental, developmental, consciousness, unconscious. Culture is part of the environmental factor, not a separate one.
2. **Eysenck's E came from Jung**, for whom extraversion was an **attitude**; Eysenck made it a heritable dimension with N and P.
3. **Cattell: 80 percent** for **intelligence (B)** and **timidity vs boldness (H)**, but **one-third heredity, two-thirds environment** overall. **Eysenck** also gave **80 percent** for intelligence (1985).
4. **Big Five twins:** N, E, O, C more hereditary; **agreeableness** more **environmental**. Inheritance up to **50 percent** of personality.
5. **Buss and Plomin = EAS** (emotionality, activity, sociability); classic background: 1975 version added **impulsivity** (EASI). Not Cattell's temperament traits.
6. **Inherited-trait counts:** Cattell **16**, Eysenck **3**, Big Five **5**, Buss and Plomin **3**; the book leaves the number open.
7. **Yamagata et al. (2006):** twins in **Canada, Germany, Japan**; Big Five as "the common heritage of the human species".
8. **50+ cultures:** Big Five consistent in most; **less consistent in developing countries** (Ethiopia, Lebanon, Malaysia, Uganda). Israel, Korea, Turkey are where it **held**.
9. **Larsson et al. (2006):** Swedish adolescent twins, genetic **psychopathy**. **Garcia et al. (2013):** anxiety heritability **rose from 14 to 18**.
10. **Gun ownership:** identical twins more likely than fraternal; listed with sexual attitudes, eating in **2-year-olds**, depression in bullied children, attitudes to practising music.
11. **Genetic effects strongest in infancy**; environmental effects grow into adulthood (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014).
12. **Shared environment** has little effect on adult personality; **non-shared** carries most. Adler's birth order is non-shared.
13. **Twenge (2000):** **50,000+**, **1950s vs 1980s**; the **1980s** cohort **higher** in anxiety and neuroticism, from **declining social connectedness**.
14. **Parenthood:** emotionality **up** with each child (Jokela et al., 2009, **Finland**); marital satisfaction **down** (Wendorf et al., 2011, **US, England, Turkey**).
15. **Roberts, Caspi & Moffitt, NZ, 18 to 26:** **2003** = good **jobs** raised positive emotionality; **2001** = rising **maturity**, women more mature.
16. **Sutin et al. (2010):** mid-40s plus 10 years; negative turning point → **more neurotic**; lesson learned → **more extraverted**.
17. **Usborne & Taylor (2010):** **strongest ethnic identity** → highest self-esteem and well-being (Canadian minorities).
18. **Rentfrow (2010):** neuroticism high **northeast and southeast**; agreeableness high **south**; extraversion high **northeast**, low **west**.
19. **Control cluster:** self-efficacy (**Bandura**) + internal locus of control (**Rotter**) + no learned helplessness (**Seligman**) = optimism.
20. **Skinner and Bandura** agree behaviour is mostly learned and genes minor; they split on **cognition**. **Vicarious reinforcement** is Bandura's.
21. **Freud was first** to stress parents. Adler: rejection → insecurity, anger, low self-esteem. Horney: no warmth → helplessness. Allport: infant-mother bond. Cattell: infancy formative. Erikson: mother, **first year**, trust. Maslow: needs met in **first two years**. Rogers: **unconditional positive regard**.
22. **Authoritative** (warm but firm) gives the best outcomes (Steinberg & Morris, 2001; **Ang, 2006, Singapore**); **authoritarian** = strict, harsh, obedience.
23. **Rudy & Grusec (2006):** immigrant mothers in **Canada** from **Egypt, Iran, India, Pakistan** more **authoritarian** than Western Europeans.
24. **Raikkonen et al. (2000):** **12-year Finnish** study, measured at **3 and 6**, hostile children by **15**. **Kasser et al. (2002):** **5 and 31**, cold parenting → **conformity, low self-direction**.
25. **Klahr & Burt (2014):** **20,000+ families**; genes up to **40 percent of parental behaviour**; children shape parents too.
26. **Harvey (1999):** ages **3 to 12**; maternal employment did **no** harm to behaviour or self-esteem.
27. **Kibbutz (Aviezer et al., 1994):** secure **infant bond** best predicted dominant, independent, achieving children; **barracks sleeping** → anxious, restrained, flat; no bond → introversion, poor friendships.
28. **Peer view (Harris, late 1990s):** parental influence **confined to the home**, not absent. **Loehlin (1997):** twins with **more shared friends** more alike.
29. **Freud: fixed by about 5.** **Later change possible:** Cattell, Allport, Erikson. **Middle-age change:** Jung, Maslow, Erikson, Cattell.
30. **Jung 35 to 40; Cattell's late maturity 50 to 65; Erikson's generativity 35 to 55** in Schultz (classic background: other texts give roughly 40 to 65).
31. **College to middle age: N, E, O down; A, C up**, replicated in eight countries.
32. **Stable from about 30** (Nave et al.; Terracciano et al.; Ferguson). **Roberts & DelVecchio (2000):** **152** studies, **55,000+**, consistency highest **after 50**.
33. **Srivastava et al. (2003):** **32,000+**, **21 to 60**, online; **C up most in the 20s, A in the 30s**.
34. **Helson et al. (2002):** **40 years**, dominance and independence **peak in middle age**. **Allemand et al. (2008):** Swiss 60s less N, E, O. **Twins 64 to 98:** E, C, perceived control down; well-being unchanged.
35. **Shiner et al. (2002):** ages 8 to 12, retested 10 years on; change from 18 to 22 predicted by **childhood**. **Pullmann et al. (2006):** Estonian 12 to 18, **stable** over 2 years.
36. **Job loss:** N up, C and E down. **Dating:** N down; E, C, self-esteem up.
37. **McAdams (1994):** **dispositional traits** (stable from 30), **personal concerns** (conscious, change often), **life narrative** (identity, purpose).
38. **Kelly = most complete cognitive theory.** **Jung's rational functions = thinking and feeling.** **Maslow's cognitive needs** sit outside the five-level hierarchy.
39. **Non-conscious** = rational, cognitive; Freud's unconscious = emotional "dark cauldron". Studied by **subliminal activation**.
40. **Silverman & Weinberger (1985):** "Mommy and I are one" had **therapeutic** value; the unconscious is **both rational and emotional**. Classic background: **4 ms**; men improved, women with "Daddy and I are one".
41. **Least understood factor:** Freud's **emotional unconscious**. Allport: unconscious only in **neurotics**; Adler: consciousness the core; Skinner: rejected; Rogers: criticised for ignoring it.
42. **Older-key differences (classic background):** collective unconscious once called the **racial unconscious**; Eysenck's early model had only **E and N** (EPI), **P** added later (EPQ); Freud's phallic stage is **4 to 5** in Schultz but **3 to 5 or 6** elsewhere; Schultz's stage 5 is "identity **cohesion** vs role confusion"; Schultz gives **seven** proprium stages, while Allport's 1961 account (and Hall and Lindzey) adds an eighth aspect, the **self as knower**.

## Key terms

Chapter 15 has no margin glossary of its own. These are the book-glossary terms and named constructs it uses, in these notes' own words.

|---|---|
| **Personality** | The unique, relatively enduring internal and external aspects of character that influence behaviour across situations |
| **Behavioural genetics** | Study of how hereditary factors relate to personality traits |
| **Predisposition** | Inherited tendency, realised or not depending on environment |
| **Heritability** (classic) | Share of trait variation in a population attributable to genetic differences |
| **Twin study** (classic) | Comparing identical and fraternal twins to estimate genetic influence |
| **Shared / non-shared environment** (classic) | Experience common to siblings / unique to each sibling |
| **Extraversion / introversion** | Jung's outward / inward attitudes; later Eysenck's E dimension |
| **Neuroticism** | Tendency to anxiety and negative emotion (Eysenck's N; a Big Five factor) |

| **Temperament traits (Cattell)** | Traits describing general emotional style of response |
| **Source traits** | Cattell's stable basic factors from factor analysis (16) |

| **Sensation seeking** | Zuckerman's need for varied, novel, complex experience |

| **Basic anxiety** | Horney's pervasive loneliness and helplessness; the root of neurosis |

| **Psychosocial stages** | Erikson's eight lifespan stages, each with a crisis |
| **Ego identity** | Adolescent self-image integrating who we are and want to be |
| **Ethnic identity** | A minority member's sense of ethnic belonging, alongside ego identity |
| **Proprium** | Allport's term for the self or ego |
| **Functional autonomy** | Allport: adult motives independent of their childhood origins |
| **Hierarchy of five innate needs** | Maslow's physiological, safety, belongingness and love, esteem, self-actualisation |
| **Instinctoid needs** | Maslow's innate needs with a hereditary component |
| **Cognitive needs** | Maslow's innate needs to know and understand |
| **Self-actualisation** | Fullest development of the self |
| **Unconditional positive regard** | Rogers: acceptance regardless of behaviour |
| **Conditions of worth** | Rogers: feeling worthy only when behaving in approved ways |
| **Reinforcement** | Strengthening a response by following it with a reward |
| **Operant conditioning** | Skinner: changing a response's consequences changes how often it occurs |
| **Successive approximation** | Skinner: shaping by reinforcing steps towards a target behaviour |
| **Superstitious behaviour** | Behaviour coincidentally, not functionally, tied to reinforcement |
| **Observational learning** | Learning new responses by watching others |
| **Vicarious reinforcement** | Learning from the consequences others receive |
| **Reciprocal determinism** | Bandura: behaviour determined by both person (cognition) and environment |
| **Self-efficacy** | Bandura: feeling of adequacy and competence in coping |
| **Internal / external locus of control** | Rotter: reinforcement depends on oneself / on others, fate or luck |
| **Learned helplessness** | Seligman: condition from perceiving no control |
| **Explanatory style** | Seligman: how one explains lack of control; optimistic styles prevent helplessness |
| **Subjective well-being** | A person's own judgement of happiness and life satisfaction |
| **Authoritative / authoritarian parenting** | Warm but firm / strict, harsh, obedience-demanding |
| **Kibbutz** | Israeli communal settlement with professional child care after early infancy |
| **Historical determinism** | View that personality is fixed in the early years |
| **Dispositional traits / personal concerns / life narrative** | McAdams's three levels: stable traits / conscious goals / evolving life story |
| **Ego** | Freud: rational part, reality principle; Jung: conscious aspect of personality |
| **Rational functions** (classic) | Jung's thinking and feeling, which judge experience |
| **Construct** | Kelly's bipolar hypothesis for interpreting and predicting events |
| **Personal construct theory** | Kelly's account of personality as interpreting and predicting events |
| **Constructive alternativism** | Kelly: we can revise or replace our constructs |
| **Unconscious** | Freud's store of repressed wishes and conflicts acting outside awareness |
| **Personal / collective unconscious** | Jung's forgotten once-conscious material / inherited species experience (archetypes) |
| **Repression** | Unconscious denial of something that causes anxiety |

| **Non-conscious** | Modern rational, cognitive unconscious, distinct from Freud's |
| **Subliminal perception / activation** | Perceiving stimuli below awareness / using them to activate thought and behaviour |
| **Positive psychology** | Seligman's study of strengths, virtues and happiness |

## Likely exam points

1. The seven factors in order, and the book's claim that each theory supplied part of the puzzle.
2. Inherited dimensions: Eysenck's P, E, N (E from Jung); the Big Five; Buss and Plomin's EAS; Zuckerman's sensation seeking.
3. The open count (16, 3, 5, 3) and Cattell's estimates: 80 percent for B and H, one-third genetic overall.
4. Big Five genetic evidence: Yamagata et al. (2006); Shiner (2014); 50+ cultures, weaker in developing countries.
5. Larsson et al. (2006) on psychopathy; Garcia et al. (2013) on rising anxiety heritability.
6. Predispositions, not destinies: realisation depends on environment, especially in childhood.
7. Genetic effects strongest in infancy, environmental effects increasing with age.
8. Environmental views of Adler, Horney, Allport, Cattell, Erikson, Maslow and Rogers.
9. Twenge (2000) cohort study and the social-connectedness explanation.
10. Parenthood (Jokela et al., 2009; Wendorf et al., 2011), work (Roberts et al., 2003) and life events (Sutin et al., 2010).
11. Ethnic identity (Usborne & Taylor, 2010), individualism, US regions (Rentfrow, 2010), neighbourhoods (Hart et al., 2008; Jokela et al., 2015).
12. Learning as the channel; Skinner's learned responses; Bandura's observational learning and vicarious reinforcement.
13. The control cluster, its benefits and its parental origins.
14. Freud first to stress parents; the parental views of Adler, Horney, Allport, Cattell, Erikson, Maslow, Rogers.
15. Authoritative parenting (Steinberg & Morris, 2001; Ang, 2006) and cultural differences (Rudy & Grusec, 2006).
16. Parents' Big Five and warmth (Prinzie et al., 2009).
17. Negative parenting: Brewin et al. (1993); Kochanska et al. (1997); Raikkonen et al. (2000); Kasser et al. (2002).
18. Klahr & Burt (2014): 40 percent genetic parental behaviour, bidirectional influence.
19. Harvey (1999) and the kibbutz findings (Aviezer et al., 1994).
20. The peer controversy (Harris) and Loehlin (1997).
21. Freud's fixed-by-5 view against the later-change and middle-age theorists.
22. Stability from about 30; Roberts & DelVecchio (2000), consistency highest after 50.
23. N, E, O down and A, C up; Srivastava et al. (2003), C in the 20s, A in the 30s.
24. Later life: Allemand et al. (2008); twins 64 to 98; stable well-being.
25. Roberts, Caspi & Moffitt (2001) maturation; Shiner et al. (2002) childhood prediction.
26. Causes of adult change (McAdams & Olson, 2010; Van Aken et al., 2006; job loss and dating).
27. McAdams's three levels reconciling stability with change.
28. The consciousness factor across theorists; Kelly as the most complete cognitive theory.
29. Freud's repression and defences; the rational non-conscious; subliminal activation.
30. Silverman & Weinberger (1985): the unconscious both rational and emotional.
31. The emotional unconscious as least understood; many post-Freudians ignored it.
32. The arc from Freud's anxiety and repression to positive psychology and the happy personality.
