# Chapter 14: Facets of Personality: Taking Control, Taking Chances, and Finding Happiness

**What this chapter is really about.** Every chapter so far has handed you a whole system: what drives us, how we develop, what goes wrong and what can be done about it. This chapter offers instead four small, sharp ideas, each explaining one corner of personality well and claiming nothing about the rest. Julian Rotter asks whether a person believes that what happens to them is produced by their own actions or by luck, fate and powerful others; his answer, **locus of control**, predicts health, school performance, job satisfaction and coping under missile attack. Marvin Zuckerman asks how much novelty, intensity and risk a person needs; **sensation seeking** is largely inherited and predicts everything from skydiving and drunk driving to tattoos, politics and career choice. Martin Seligman starts with dogs that stop trying to escape shock, builds a theory of **learned helplessness**, and then shows that the way we *explain* bad events (our **explanatory style**) decides whether a setback stays a setback or becomes depression. Finally Seligman asks what makes people **happy**, and launches **positive psychology**.

The facets are not a random collection. Locus of control and learned helplessness are *learned* beliefs about control, descended from the behavioural and social-learning tradition of Chapters 12 and 13; sensation seeking is a largely *inherited* trait descended from Eysenck; the happy personality ties the others together, because internal control, optimism and engagement all predict well-being. The thread through the whole chapter is **perceived control**: believing you can act on your world, and explaining failures in a way that keeps you acting.

**How to read this chapter.** The opening module folds in Schultz's Part opener, *The Limited-Domain Approach*; the contrast with global theories is a stock question. Module 14.1 (Rotter) runs from the life and the I-E Scale through research sorted by age, gender, culture, behaviour, mental and physical health and childhood, ending with the locus-of-control versus self-efficacy debate. Module 14.2 (Zuckerman) centres on the Sensation Seeking Scale and its four components, good versus bad sensation seeking, and a long list of correlates. Module 14.3 (Seligman) covers the dog experiments, the human replications, the rat tumour percentages, explanatory style and its three dimensions, the depression link (Table 14.4) and childhood origins. Module 14.4 covers positive psychology: Seligman's life, the factors linked to subjective well-being, and the pleasant, engaged and meaningful lives and five pillars of flourishing. The 11th edition does not rate these theorists on the Questions about Human Nature; the note at the end of the opening module explains what to do about that.

## Opening: The Limited-Domain Approach

### Global theories and why they ruled the field

Look back at the theorists you have met. Almost all of them tried to cover **everything**: motivation, development, structure, adjustment and pathology. Schultz calls this the **global** approach. It dominated personality psychology for a simple historical reason. Freud, Jung and Adler were clinicians first. Their raw material was the patient on the couch, a whole suffering human being who needed to function again in the real world. A therapist cannot treat one trait and ignore the rest of the person, so the theories these men built had to account for the total personality. The breadth of the global theories is a direct consequence of where they were born.

### From the clinic to the laboratory

The picture changed when personality research moved out of the consulting room and into the **research laboratory**. Experimental psychologists work in a different way from clinicians. They isolate **one variable at a time** and hold everything else constant, which forces them to study a narrow slice of behaviour. They then gather large amounts of data on how that one variable relates to what comes before it (its **antecedents**) and what follows from it (its **behavioural consequences**).

This produces a different *kind* of evidence. A global theory is supported mainly by case material, clinical observation and the theorist's own interpretation. A limited-domain theory is supported by questionnaire scores, experiments, correlations and replications across samples and cultures. This is why the four modules in this chapter read so differently from the Freud or Jung chapters: they are long lists of studies with sample sizes, ages, countries and percentages, not long arguments about the structure of the psyche.

A growing number of contemporary personality psychologists have concluded that **no single theory can explain all of personality and behaviour**. The **limited-domain approach** is their response: deliberately restrict the scope to a few well-defined personality factors that can be tested experimentally far more easily than a theory of the whole person.

### Researchers, not clinicians

A second difference follows from the first. The people who build limited-domain theories are typically **researchers, not clinicians**, and they are more interested in *investigating* personality than in *changing* it. Schultz is careful about what this does and does not mean. It does **not** mean the approach has no treatment applications; you will see locus of control used in addiction therapy, stress-inoculation training used to raise perceived control in dental patients, and Seligman's exercises used to teach optimism. It means only that these theories were **not developed specifically for use with patients**, as many of the earlier theories were.

> **Trap.** "Limited-domain theories have no therapeutic applications" is false. The correct statement is that they place **less emphasis** on therapy and were **not designed for patients**, but applications exist. If an option says limited-domain theories are built on clinical case material, it is also wrong: their supporting data come from laboratory and questionnaire research.

### The five facets and the approaches they come from

The chapter examines five personality variables then under active investigation: **locus of control**, **sensation seeking**, **learned helplessness**, **optimism versus pessimism**, and **the happy personality**. Schultz links each to an approach you have already studied.

| Facet | Theorist | Origin in the book's scheme | Learned or inherited? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Locus of control | Julian Rotter | Social-learning (social-cognitive) approach; behaviourist and cognitive influence | Learned, strong cognitive component |
| Sensation seeking | Marvin Zuckerman | Trait approach; one of the traits within **Eysenck's E** (extraversion versus introversion) dimension; behavioural genetics | Primarily **inherited** |
| Learned helplessness | Martin Seligman | Behaviourist, social-learning and cognitive movements | Learned, strong cognitive component |
| Optimism versus pessimism (explanatory style) | Martin Seligman | Grows out of the learned helplessness research | Learned (with some genetic influence, as you will see) |
| The happy personality | Martin Seligman and the positive psychology movement | Outgrowth of **positive psychology** | Mixed; strong genetic component through traits |

Schultz adds two cautions. These facets are **not comprehensive systems**, and they are **not the only** limited-domain theories in the field. They are offered as samples, to give the flavour of studying personality one facet at a time.

> **Trap.** The Part opener places sensation seeking inside **Eysenck's E (extraversion)** dimension. Later in the chapter the "bad", impulsive form of sensation seeking is tied to high scores on Eysenck's **psychoticism (P)** scale, and the disinhibition component correlates with both E and the asocial side of P. Both statements are in the book: the trait as a whole is filed under E; the unsocialised variety goes with P. Also note the pairing: locus of control and learned helplessness are the **learned** facets; sensation seeking is the **inherited** one.

### A note on Questions about Human Nature

From Freud to Bandura, Schultz rates each theorist on six issues: free will versus determinism, nature versus nurture, past versus present, uniqueness versus universality, equilibrium versus growth, and optimism versus pessimism. **Chapter 14 gives no such ratings for Rotter, Zuckerman or Seligman**, which fits the limited-domain idea: a theory of one facet takes no position on the whole of human nature. These notes do not invent ratings. Where the text clearly speaks to an issue (above all nature versus nurture), that is noted in the module and collected in one table in "How the chapter fits together", labelled as what the text implies.

> **Trap.** Asked how Schultz rates Zuckerman on free will versus determinism, the answer is that the 11th edition gives no rating. The only position it states outright is that his emphasis on **heritability** separates him from the behavioural and social-learning approaches.

## 14.1 Julian Rotter: Locus of Control

### The life of Rotter (1916 to 2014)

Julian Rotter was born in **Brooklyn, New York**, in **1916**, the **youngest of three brothers**. Looking back as an adult, he remarked that the three of them matched Adler's birth-order portraits rather neatly: an oldest, a middle and a "fighting" youngest child (Rotter, 1993). Hold that detail, because Adler turns up again two paragraphs later and the link is not accidental.

The family was comfortable until the **economic depression of 1929**, when Rotter's father **lost his business**. Rotter later said this turn of fortune started his **lifelong concern with social injustice** and taught him how strongly **situational conditions** shape personality and behaviour (1993). The seed of the theory is visible: a family's fortunes overturned by forces none of them controlled, and a boy watching how people behave when the situation changes. Locus of control is a theory about how people interpret such events, as something they caused or something done to them.

In high school Rotter read **Freud and Adler** on psychoanalysis and, as a game, began **interpreting his friends' dreams**. Learning how few jobs psychologists could get, he majored in **chemistry** at **Brooklyn College**, where he happened to meet **Alfred Adler** and switched to psychology even though he knew it was impractical. Adler's emphasis on the social context of the person and on goals and expectations is part of the soil from which Rotter's **social-learning** theory grew.

He wanted an academic career but met the widespread **prejudice against hiring Jewish faculty** in American universities; at Brooklyn College and in graduate school he was warned that Jews could not get academic posts whatever their credentials, and he judged the warnings justified (Rotter, 1982). A man told that merit would not decide his outcomes had personal reason to think about rewards controlled by others.

After his **Ph.D. from Indiana University** (the 11th edition prints the year as **1951**), Rotter worked at a **state mental hospital in Connecticut**, served as a **psychologist with the U.S. Army during World War II**, and then took a teaching post at **Ohio State University**, where **George Kelly** (Chapter 11) directed the clinical psychology programme. At Ohio State he developed his social-learning approach to personality and attracted a stream of strong graduate students. One of them later called those years the "glory days", with Rotter and Kelly both refining their theories and writing their major works at the same time (Sechrest, 1984).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition dates Rotter's Ph.D. to **1951** and *then* places his state hospital work and **World War II** Army service, which cannot be right since the war ended in 1945. Classic background: standard biographies give **1941**, which fits the sequence. A paper keyed to the 11th edition will say 1951; know both.

In **1963** Rotter moved from Ohio State to the **University of Connecticut at Storrs**. In **1988** he received the **American Psychological Association's Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award**. Into his 80s he still played vigorous tennis and squash and kept a weekly poker game (Strickland, 2014). He died in **2014**, by then regarded as one of the most eminent psychologists of the twentieth century.

**From the life to the theory.** The Depression showed him situations overriding effort; the hiring warnings showed him that some people have good reason to believe others control their rewards; Adler gave him a model of personality as goal-directed and built on expectations. Together they yield a theory of **beliefs about who or what controls reinforcement**.

### Internal versus external control of reinforcement

Rotter wanted to explain behaviour by looking **both outside and inside** the organism: at the external reinforcements a person receives, as a behaviourist would, and at the cognitive processes through which the person interprets them. Over a long research programme he found that some people believe their reinforcers **depend on their own actions**, while others believe them **controlled by other people and outside forces**. He named this dimension **locus of control** (locus is Latin for "place": where is the control located?).

- People with an **internal locus of control** believe the reinforcement they receive is under the control of their **own behaviour and abilities**. A student with an internal locus who scores well on an examination says, "I prepared properly." When she scores badly she says, "I did not prepare enough."
- People with an **external locus of control** believe that **other people, fate, or luck** control the rewards they receive. They are convinced they are **powerless** against outside forces. The external student explains a good mark as "an easy paper" and a bad one as "the examiner was unfair" or "my luck was bad".

Notice that the distinction is about **belief**, not about reality. Two people in the same objective situation can hold opposite beliefs about it, and Rotter's point is that the belief, not the situation, shapes what they do next.

Why does it matter? If externals believe nothing they do changes their reinforcers, they see **little value in effort**: why try when you expect no control over present or future events? Internals believe they have a **firm grip on their lives** and act accordingly. Compared with externals, internals:

- **perform better at work**;
- are **less susceptible to attempts to influence them**;
- **value their own skills** more highly;
- are **more alert to environmental cues** that they then use to guide behaviour (if outcomes depend on you, information is worth gathering);
- report **lower anxiety, higher self-esteem and greater happiness**;
- enjoy **better mental and physical health** (Saric & Pahic, 2013).

Classic background: in Rotter's wider social-learning theory (*Social Learning and Clinical Psychology*, 1954), behaviour depends on the **expectancy** that it will be reinforced and on the **reinforcement value** of the outcome. Locus of control is a **generalised expectancy**, a cross-situational belief about whether reinforcement follows one's own behaviour; the title of the 1966 monograph cited for the I-E Scale says exactly that. "Generalised" is what later separates it from Bandura's self-efficacy.

> **Trap.** **Internal locus** (Rotter): a generalised belief that *my behaviour* produces my reinforcements. **External locus** (Rotter): a generalised belief that *luck, fate or powerful others* produce them; a belief on a continuum, held by ordinary healthy people. **Learned helplessness** (Seligman): a *condition*, produced by experience of uncontrollable events, in which the organism stops trying even when control is possible, seen as passivity (dogs lying down in the shuttle box). An external person need not be helpless, and helplessness was first shown in dogs, which have no I-E score.

### Assessment of locus of control

**The I-E Scale.** Rotter measured locus of control with **self-report inventories**. The best known is the **Internal-External (I-E) Scale** (Rotter, **1966**), made up of **23 forced-choice** item pairs. In each pair the respondent picks the statement that best matches their own beliefs. One statement in each pair expresses an internal view and the other an external view, and, as Schultz observes, it is not hard to tell which is which.

**Table 14.1** What the I-E Scale items look like

| Feature | What the book's sample shows |
|---|---|
| Format | Pairs of statements; the respondent must choose one of the two (forced choice), never both or neither |
| Number of sample pairs shown | Six (the full scale has 23 scored pairs) |
| Life domains covered by the samples | Personal misfortune, war and political involvement, whether people get the respect they deserve, fairness of teachers and grading, what makes someone a leader, and why some people are disliked |
| How the options differ | One option attributes the outcome to the person's own actions, effort or skill; the other attributes it to luck, accident, the system, or forces no individual can change |
| Paraphrased example | On misfortune: one option says much of the unhappiness in people's lives comes from bad luck (external); the other says people's misfortunes follow from their own mistakes (internal) |
| Source | Rotter (1966), *Psychological Monographs*, 80 |

Classic background: the published I-E Scale has **29 pairs**, of which **6 are filler items** meant to disguise the scale's purpose, leaving the **23** scored pairs the book mentions. The score is the number of **external** choices, so it runs from 0 to 23 and a **higher score means a more external** orientation. Students who assume a high score means "more internal" lose marks on this.

**The Nowicki-Strickland scales.** For children there is the **Children's Nowicki-Strickland Internal-External Scale**, a widely used **40-item** test translated into **two dozen languages** (Nowicki & Strickland, **1973**; Strickland, 1989). There is also an **adult form**, and a **cartoon version for preschool children** (Nowicki & Duke, **1983**), which solves the obvious problem that a four-year-old cannot read forced-choice statements about politics.

**Domain-specific variants** of the I-E Scale measure locus of control for particular behaviours, such as beliefs about **successful dieting and weight loss** and performance in various situations. One variant, **health locus of control**, appears in the health research below.

> **Trap.** Numbers that get swapped: the **I-E Scale** has **23** forced-choice (scored) pairs and dates from **1966**; the **Children's Nowicki-Strickland** scale has **40** items, dates from **1973**, and exists in about **24** languages; the **cartoon preschool** version is **Nowicki & Duke (1983)**. The Sensation Seeking Scale in 14.2 also has **40** items, so "40 items" alone does not identify a test.

### Age and gender differences

**Age.** Attempts to control the environment begin in **infancy** and become more pronounced **between ages 8 and 14**. **College students** generally show an **internal** orientation. People become **more internal as they grow older**, **peaking in middle age** (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995), and the belief stays modifiable: among Americans aged **60 to 75**, **cognitive training** significantly increased internal control (Wolinsky et al., 2009). A belief that training can shift is a learned belief, not a fixed trait.

**Gender.** Here the findings are inconsistent, and the inconsistency is itself examinable.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Manger & Ekeland (2000) | 14- and 15-year-olds in **Norway** | **Girls** scored significantly **more internal** than boys |
| DeBrabander & Boone (1990) | Adults in the **United States** | **No significant difference** between men and women on overall I-E scores |
| Holland, Geraghty & Shah (2010) | Men and women aged **18 to 29** in **England** | **Women** were **more external** than men |
| Tong & Wang (2006) | Adults in **China** | **Men** scored **more internal** than women |

So there is **no consistent gender difference**; the direction depends on country and age group.

### Cultural differences

#### Ethnic group comparisons

Studies in **Africa** found that native Africans, like American-born Blacks in general, scored **more external** than American-born Whites (Okeke, Draguns, Sheku, & Allen, 1999). In **Botswana**, Black adolescents were more external than White U.S. adolescents, but in **both** countries teenagers of **higher socioeconomic status** were **more internal** (Maqsud & Rouhani, 1991). That second finding matters most: much of an apparent ethnic difference may be a difference in **economic circumstances**, as you would expect if the belief is learned from whether one's actions pay off.

A comparison of **Caucasian, Hispanic, Asian and African-American** teenagers found that having an internal locus of control was regarded as **more important by the Caucasian** teens than by the other groups (Kang, Chang, Chen, & Greenberger, 2015). In the **Netherlands**, immigrants from **Morocco and Turkey** scored much **more external** than native-born Dutch people (van Dijk et al., 2013). Among **Hispanic immigrants to the United States**, those with a **more internal** locus adapted more effectively to their new culture and coped better with **discrimination and marginalisation** (Llamas & Consoli, 2014).

#### Collectivist versus individualistic cultural differences

In general, **Asians** have been found to be **more external** than Americans. Schultz explains this through cultural values. American culture traditionally prizes **self-reliance and individualism**; Asian cultures emphasise **collectivism, community reliance and interdependence**. In a collectivist frame, success is naturally seen as produced by the family, the group and circumstances as much as by the individual, which an I-E Scale scores as "external".

The orientation shifts with **contact**. Chinese residents of **Hong Kong** were more external than **Americans of Chinese heritage**, who in turn were more external than **Americans of European heritage** (Uba, 1994). The ordering, from most external to most internal, is worth remembering: Hong Kong Chinese, then Chinese Americans, then European Americans.

A large comparison of **18 cultures** confirmed that people in **collectivist** cultures such as China scored more external than people in Westernised countries, who placed greater value on internal control (Cheng, Cheung, Chio, & Chan, 2013). Among college students, those in **South Africa** scored significantly **more internal** than those in **Lebanon**, which the researchers read as another instance of the contrast between a more individualistic culture (South Africa) and a more collectivist, structured one (Lebanon) (Nasser & Abouchedid, 2006).

> **Trap.** The book explains Asian externality by **cultural values** (collectivism, interdependence), not deficiency, and the Botswana study shows **socioeconomic status** cutting across ethnicity. In the South Africa versus Lebanon study the **African** sample was **more internal**, because South Africa counts as the more individualistic culture.

### Behavioural differences

**Work and sport.** Workers in **China** and athletes in **Sweden** who were high in internal control were better able to **adapt and commit to change**, and scored higher on tests of **mental skills** (Chen & Wang, 2007; Fallby et al., 2006). At work, internal locus of control is positively related to **job satisfaction, job commitment and general life satisfaction** (Wang, Bowling, & Eschleman, 2010).

**Study.** In **Korea**, students high in **internal academic locus of control** (believing that their own efforts would bring success at school) were more likely to **persist in an online college programme** (Joo, Joung, & Sim, 2011). In **Turkey**, college students high in internal academic locus of control were far **less likely to become addicted to Internet use** (Iskender & Akin, 2010).

**Groups.** Just as Bandura proposed a **collective efficacy** (Chapter 13), there may be a **collective locus of control** characterising groups that work or study together. Research on **entrepreneurial work teams in Austria** found that the teams highest in internal control performed **far more efficiently and effectively** than teams lower in internal control (Khan, Breitenecker, & Schwarz, 2014).

### Mental health differences

The mental health findings point in one direction: internal control goes with better adjustment.

- Internals are **less likely to have emotional problems** or to become **alcoholics**, and they **cope better with stress**. In a study of **nurses in Germany**, those reporting more work-related **stress and burnout** were **more external** than colleagues less troubled by stress (Owen, 2006; Schmitz, Neumann, & Oppermann, 2000).
- Across very diverse groups, including **pregnant women, female prisoners and police officers**, externals were more prone to **fantasies, anxiety, depression, fear of death on the job (in the police sample), psychotic episodes and hopelessness**, and were more likely to become **victims of cyberbullying** (Ariso & Reyero, 2014; Asberg & Renk, 2014; and others).
- College students in **Greece**, a family-oriented and highly protective culture, were followed as they left home, many for the first time. Internals **adjusted more readily** than externals to the social and emotional challenges (Leontopoulou, 2006).
- First-year college students in **Turkey** who were high in external control were far **more indecisive in new situations** (Bacanli, 2006).
- High external control has been linked with **compulsive hoarding** (Benson-Townsend & Silver, 2014).
- People with various **addictions** benefit from therapy when they are able to **develop a more internal locus** of control (Amram & Benbenishty, 2014). This is the clearest therapeutic application in the module: if a person believes the drug, the stress or bad luck runs their life, part of recovery is learning that their own behaviour can change outcomes.
- Internals experience **less anxiety and depression**, are **less likely to be lonely or to attempt suicide**, and find **greater meaning in life** (Castro, Echavarria, & Velasquez, 2010; Spann et al., 2006; and others).

> **Zeidner (1993): teenagers under missile attack.** During the **Persian Gulf War** (dated **1990** in the book), Israeli cities were hit by frequent **Scud missile** explosions that caused widespread injury and destruction. Israeli **adolescents** who scored higher on **perceived control** experienced significantly **less anxiety** and **fewer stress-related symptoms** during the fighting than adolescents lower in perceived control. The study is valuable because the danger was real and identical for everyone; the differences in distress therefore came from the belief, not from the situation.

Similarly, people high in internal control show **better mental health**, lead **more meaningful lives** and score higher on **subjective well-being** (Shojaee & French, 2014; Singh & Choudhri, 2014), a link you will meet again in 14.4.

### Physical health differences

**Heart disease.** Internals tend to be **physically healthier**, with **lower blood pressure and fewer heart attacks**. When they do develop cardiac problems, they **cooperate better with hospital staff** and are **released sooner** than externals. A study in **Norway** found that they also **returned to work sooner** (Bergvik, Sorlie, & Wynn, 2012). Among patients recovering from **coronary artery bypass surgery**, those high in internal control had reached a **higher level of physical functioning at six weeks and at six months** after surgery (Barry et al., 2006).

**Cancer.** Among **older cancer patients in the Netherlands**, internals experienced **less depression** than externals (Aarts et al., 2015). Another Dutch study found that cancer patients high in **external** control had **more trust in their oncologists** (Hillen et al., 2014), which is what you would predict: if outcomes are controlled by powerful others, you place your faith in the most powerful other in the room. In **Nigeria**, internals were more likely to undergo **screening for cervical and breast cancer** (Adebimpe & Oladimeji, 2014).

**Health behaviours.** Internals tend to look after themselves more carefully: in several studies they were the ones who buckled their seat belts, ate sensibly, exercised regularly and gave up smoking (Phares, 1993; Segall & Wynd, 1990; and others).

**The exception.** Not every result fits. Research in **France, Germany and South Africa** reported that people scoring high on **internal health locus of control** were, overall, **less careful** about their health, although they showed **fewer eating disorders** and **fewer HIV-risk behaviours** (Grotz et al., 2011; Gwandure & Mayekiso, 2010; Scoffier et al., 2010). Belief in personal control does not guarantee prudence; a confident internal may also feel invulnerable.

**Who scores low.** Research in **Sweden** found that **up to one-third** of older people, people with **little formal education**, and **immigrant groups** all scored **low in internal health locus of control** (Lindstrom, 2011).

> **Trap.** "Internals always take better care of their health" is too strong: in **France, Germany and South Africa** internals took **less** general care of their health (with fewer eating disorders and HIV-risk behaviours). And it is **externals** who **trust their oncologists** more.

### Developing locus of control in childhood

The evidence suggests that locus of control is **learned in childhood** and is directly related to **parental behaviour** (Ahlin & Lobo Antunes, 2015). This is where Rotter's theory is most clearly a social-learning theory: children learn, from the contingencies their family provides, whether their actions make a difference.

Conditions associated with an **external** orientation in children:

- homes **without an adult male role model**;
- **more siblings** (external beliefs rise with the number of siblings);
- **large single-parent families headed by women** (Schneewind, 1995);
- mothers who are **depressed** and have **little formal education or income** (Freed & Tompson, 2011);
- growing up in **low-income families**, which lowers children's sense of control in **all** areas of life (Mittal & Griskevicius, 2014).

Parents of children with an **internal** locus of control were found to be:

- **highly supportive**;
- generous with **praise**, that is, positive reinforcement, for achievements;
- **consistent in discipline**;
- **not authoritarian**;
- and, as the children grew older, **encouraging of independence**.

Why do these features work? **Consistency**. When praise reliably follows achievement and discipline reliably follows misbehaviour, the child learns that outcomes depend on his own actions. When responses are erratic, or a stressed, depressed or poor household cannot respond reliably, the child learns that outcomes arrive from outside whatever he does.

### Reflections on locus of control

**Locus of control and self-efficacy.** A large research programme with **college students and sales representatives** found a **strong relationship** between Rotter's locus of control and Bandura's **self-efficacy** (Judge, Erez, Bono, & Thoresen, **2002**). Both concern perceived control over life events and the ability to cope with them. The **major difference** is scope: **locus of control generalises across situations**, while **self-efficacy is specific to a situation**. A person can be internal in general yet low in self-efficacy for public speaking.

**Bandura** rejected the overlap firmly (1997): believing that *I can perform* an action (self-efficacy) is not the same as believing that *actions affect outcomes* (locus of control). A student may be sure that hard study determines results (internal locus) yet doubt that *she* can keep studying hard for six weeks (low self-efficacy). Conversely, a skilled worker may trust his abilities (high self-efficacy) yet believe promotions go to the boss's relatives whatever anyone does (external locus).

> **Trap.** Three constructs, three questions. **Rotter's locus of control:** do outcomes depend on my actions or on luck, fate and powerful others? (a **generalised** expectancy, measured by the I-E Scale). **Bandura's self-efficacy:** can I carry out the action this situation requires? (**situation-specific**; Bandura denied it equals locus of control). **Seligman's explanatory style:** when something bad has happened, how do I explain why? (habitual **attribution** along internal/external, stable/unstable and global/specific dimensions). Do not equate Rotter's "internal" with Seligman's: an internal locus is healthy, whereas attributing *failures* to internal causes is part of the *pessimistic* style.

**Evaluation.** Rotter's research has been **highly rigorous and well controlled**, using **objective measures** wherever possible, with considerable empirical support. The I-E Scale has generated a **wealth of research** and been applied in **clinical and educational settings**; Rotter called locus of control **"one of the most studied variables in psychology"** (1990), and research on it continues well into the twenty-first century.

A critic would add that the evidence is mostly **correlational** (does internal control produce health and prosperity, or do they produce a feeling of control? the socioeconomic findings make this pressing), that gender findings are inconsistent, and that the health findings include a reversal.

> **Highlights: research on Rotter's ideas.** According to the book's research summary, people high in **internal** locus of control tend to live in **wealthy, individualistic cultures**; do **well at school**; **avoid Internet addiction**; report **high job and life satisfaction**; have **low anxiety and depression**; **cope better with stress**; enjoy **good physical health**; and have **parents who are themselves high in internal control**.

## 14.2 Marvin Zuckerman: Sensation Seeking

### Zuckerman and the idea of "taking chances"

**Marvin Zuckerman** (born **1928**; the 11th edition prints him as living) worked at the **University of Delaware**, and from the **1970s** ran a research programme on a limited-domain aspect of personality he called **sensation seeking**. Schultz stresses at once that the trait has a **large hereditary component**, already noted by **Eysenck**, which makes Zuckerman the odd one out in this chapter: Rotter's and Seligman's facets are learned beliefs, Zuckerman's is closer to an inborn temperament.

Zuckerman's definition has two halves. Sensation seeking is (1) a **desire for varied, novel, complex and intense sensations and experiences**, plus (2) a **willingness to take physical, social, legal and financial risks** to have them (Zuckerman, 1994a). Schultz's shorthand is **"taking chances"**. The second half keeps the concept from being trivial: everyone enjoys some novelty, but the sensation seeker will risk injury, reputation, the law or money for the experience.

Classic background: the idea grew out of 1960s **sensory deprivation** research, in which some volunteers tolerated dark, silent isolation far worse than others; the first Sensation Seeking Scale appeared in **1964** (Zuckerman, Kolin, Price, & Zoob). The early explanation was an **optimal level of stimulation or arousal**, higher in sensation seekers, so that ordinary life leaves them under-stimulated (the 11th edition uses the phrase "optimal level of stimulation" in the birth-order research). Zuckerman died in **2018**.

### Assessing sensation seeking

**Building the Sensation Seeking Scale.** Zuckerman constructed the **Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS)**, a **40-item** paper-and-pencil questionnaire. The way he validated it is worth understanding, because it is a textbook example of the **contrasted-groups** method. He gave the test to people whose *behaviour* already matched his definition:

- people who **volunteered for psychological experiments** involving novel experiences;
- people whose **jobs involved physical danger**, such as **police officers and race-car drivers**;
- people who admitted **experimenting with drugs or varied sexual experiences**.

He then compared their scores with those of people who **deliberately avoided** novel or risky activities. The adventurous groups scored high and the cautious groups scored low. In other words, the scale was shown to separate people who demonstrably differ on the trait in real life. The test has since been revised and is now in its **fifth version (Form V)**, and there is also a **scale for children**.

**Table 14.2** What the Sensation Seeking Scale (Form V) items look like

| Feature | What the book's sample shows |
|---|---|
| Format | Forced choice: the respondent picks whichever of two statements they prefer |
| Number of sample pairs shown | Eight (the full Form V has 40 items) |
| Topics covered | Kinds of party, familiar versus new faces, dangerous or frightening activities, planned versus unplanned travel, parachute jumping, sexual content in films, novel experience for its own sake (even if unconventional or illegal), and conventional versus individual dress |
| How the options differ | One option expresses a preference for intensity, novelty, risk or lack of restraint; the other expresses a preference for calm, familiarity, planning, safety or convention |
| Paraphrased example | Given a choice between a quiet gathering with good conversation and a wild, uninhibited party, the high scorer picks the wild party |
| Second paraphrased example | Given a choice between carefully planning a trip's route and timetable and setting off with no plan at all, the high scorer picks no plan |
| Source | Zuckerman (1994), Cambridge University Press |

The sample pairs touch all four components described next (parachuting for thrill and adventure, wild parties for disinhibition, unplanned travel and unconventional dress for experience seeking, tiring of familiar faces for boredom susceptibility); the book does not label the items, so this mapping is only illustrative.

Classic background: Form V scores **four subscales of 10 items each**, one per component, and their sum gives a **total sensation-seeking score**.

#### Components of sensation seeking

Using **factor analysis**, Zuckerman (1983; Zuckerman & Aluja, 2015) identified **four components** of sensation seeking. This list is the single most-tested fact in the module.

| Component | What it is | Typical expressions |
|---|---|---|
| **Thrill and adventure seeking** | Desire for physical activities involving **speed, danger, novelty and defiance of gravity** | Parachuting, scuba diving, bungee jumping |
| **Experience seeking** | Search for **novel experiences** through the mind and senses and through an unconventional way of living | Travel, music, art, a **nonconformist lifestyle** among like-minded people |
| **Disinhibition** | Need to seek **release in uninhibited social activities** | Risky sex, impulsiveness, aggressiveness, antisocial behaviour |
| **Boredom susceptibility** | **Aversion to repetition**, routine work and predictable people, with **restless discontent** when stuck with them | The person who cannot bear the same commute, the same job or the same friends |

Notice the logic of the four. Two are about *what is sought* (physical thrill; novel inner and cultural experience), one is about *throwing off restraint* (disinhibition), and one is about *intolerance of its absence* (boredom susceptibility).

> **Trap.** The four components are **thrill and adventure seeking, experience seeking, disinhibition and boredom susceptibility**. Distractors: "novelty seeking" (Cloninger's term), "impulsivity" (part of the bad type), "risk taking" (a consequence), "excitement seeking" (a NEO extraversion facet). Do not confuse **disinhibition** here with Bandura's **disinhibition** effect in Chapter 13 (a model weakening an observer's inhibitions).

#### Good and bad sensation seeking

Zuckerman later drew a distinction between **good** and **bad** sensation seeking:

- The **good** type, which he called **non-impulsive socialised sensation seeking**, involves the **thrill and adventure seeking** component. Think of the mountaineer who trains carefully, uses safety equipment and channels the need for intensity into a legal, socially accepted activity.
- The **bad** type, **impulsive unsocialised sensation seeking**, consists of high scores on **disinhibition, experience seeking and boredom susceptibility**, together with high scores on **Eysenck's psychoticism scale** (Roberti, 2004; Zuckerman, 1994b). Think of the young man who drinks heavily, drives drunk at speed for the excitement, gets into fights and cannot hold a job because routine bores him.

> **Trap.** Good = **one** component (thrill and adventure seeking), non-impulsive, socialised. Bad = the **other three** components **plus high psychoticism**. Students often file experience seeking as "good" because travel and art sound harmless; the book puts it with the bad type. Classic background: Zuckerman's later "alternative five" model has a factor called **impulsive sensation seeking (ImpSS)**; related, but not the same label as the 11th edition's "impulsive unsocialised sensation seeking".

#### Other scales

Other countries have produced their own measures.

- German psychologists published the **Need Inventory of Sensation Seeking**, in German and English (Roth & Hammelstein, 2012). Later research suggested it might be **even more reliable and valid** than Zuckerman's original scale (Marker & Schneider, 2015).
- For **China**, the **Brief Sensation Seeking Scale** has proved reliable and valid in predicting **reckless motorcycle riding**, **excessive alcohol and cigarette use**, and **risky sexual behaviour** (Chen et al., 2013; Fan et al., 2014).

### Characteristics of sensation seekers

#### Age differences

Differences in sensation seeking show up **very early**. In a study of American **second-graders**, the high scorers chose to watch a video about **scary sharks**, while the low scorers chose one about a **funny bunny** (Trice, 2010).

**Younger people** in general are more drawn to adventure, risk and novelty. Among **high school and college students in the United States and Canada**, high sensation seekers were more likely to engage in **reckless behaviour** and **uncontrolled gambling** (Collado et al., 2014; Gupta, Derevensky, & Ellenbogen, 2006; and others). Across adolescence to age **60**, sensation seeking **rises in the middle school years and declines in the twenties** (Lynne-Landsman et al., 2011).

Two non-findings are also examinable. There were **no significant differences by educational level**, and **college students did not score higher or lower** than people who had not attended college.

#### Zuckerman's sensation-seeking experiences

Zuckerman's own life fits the age data. As a college student, he said, he reached his **full sensation-seeking potential** through **drinking, sex and hitch-hiking around the country**. At **74** he recalled having imagined a retirement of **hang gliding, parachute jumping and flying lessons**, and explained in his own terms why it never happened: **thrill and adventure seeking and disinhibition fall rapidly with age, but experience seeking does not** (Zuckerman, 2004). He still seeks new experiences, only less physical ones.

> **Trap.** Age decline is **not uniform**: **thrill and adventure seeking and disinhibition** fall steeply, **experience seeking** stays stable. A 70-year-old learning a new language and travelling to unfamiliar countries is still a sensation seeker on that component.

#### Gender differences

In countries as different as the **United States and Iran**, **men** consistently score **higher in sensation seeking** and **lower in impulse control** than women (Khodarahimi, 2014; Shulman et al., 2015). The differences also appear at the level of the four components:

- **Men** score higher on **thrill and adventure seeking, disinhibition and boredom susceptibility**.
- **Women** score higher on **experience seeking**.

The same pattern has been found in samples from the **United States, England, Scotland, Japan and Thailand**.

> **Trap.** "Men score higher on all four components" is wrong. **Women are higher on experience seeking.** This is a favourite MCQ.

#### Racial and cultural differences

Researchers have found significant racial and cultural differences in SSS scores. **Asians score lower** than people in **Western** countries, and **White** participants score **higher** than non-Whites.

### Behavioural differences between high and low sensation seekers

#### Physical risk taking

Physical risk taking is the most obvious correlate. **Skydivers, firefighters, riot-control police officers, bungee jumpers and race-car drivers** all score higher on the SSS than groups not engaged in such activities. Among **American motocross** riders, the **most experienced** (those who had competed in the most races) had the **highest** sensation-seeking scores (Smith, Bissett, & Russo, 2014). Among **male college undergraduates in Israel**, high sensation seekers were more likely to take part in **risky sports** and to **volunteer for military combat units** (Hobfoll, Rom, & Segal, 1989).

#### Different types of risk taking

Not all risk takers are alike. Levenson (1990) identified **three types**:

| Type | Examples | SSS scores |
|---|---|---|
| **Antisocial** risk takers | Drug addicts, criminals | Significantly **higher** |
| **Adventurous** risk takers | Mountain climbers, skydivers | Significantly **higher** |
| **Pro-social** risk takers | Police officers, firefighters | **Lower** than the other two groups |

The pro-social group's motives for taking risks are **not mainly thrill and adventure seeking**; a firefighter entering a burning building is usually driven by duty, training and the wish to save lives.

High sensation seekers are also more willing to **relocate** from familiar to unfamiliar places and to **travel to exotic destinations**, even when the journey is physically hazardous.

> **Trap.** Firefighters appear twice in the book. In the general list of physical risk takers they score **higher than groups not engaged** in dangerous work. In **Levenson's (1990)** three-way comparison they are classed as **pro-social** risk takers, who score **lower** than antisocial and adventurous risk takers. Both statements are true; they compare firefighters with different groups.

#### Drugs, drinking, crime, fast cars, and online poker

High sensation seekers are more likely to **use and sell illicit drugs**, and to start **at an earlier age**; to **drink alcohol**; to **shoplift**; and to commit **delinquent acts**. The samples behind this conclusion were deliberately varied:

- **Black youths in the United States** aged **9 to 15**;
- **university students in South Africa** aged **16 to 49**;
- **high school students and adults in the United States**;
- **teenagers in Norway** aged **12 to 16** (Bacon, Burak, & Rann, 2014; Hansen & Breivik, 2001; and others).

Among **American high school and college students**, high sensation seekers were more likely to **smoke, drink, use drugs, drive fast, have car accidents**, be **convicted of reckless or drunk driving**, and **have sex frequently** (McAdams & Donnellan, 2009; Ravert et al., 2009; and others). Studies in **Spain and France** confirmed the link with **speeding and drunk driving**, and French **online poker** players scored higher than non-players and reported **strong arousal** while playing (Barrault & Varesconi, 2013).

#### Risky sexual behaviours

- Among **young Black women in the United States** aged **15 to 21**, high scorers on a measure of **sexual sensation seeking** reported more sexual risk taking: **more frequent intercourse, more partners and less condom use** (Spitalnick et al., 2007).
- In Zuckerman's own research, **16 percent** of high sensation seekers reported **risky homosexual encounters**, compared with **7 percent** of low sensation seekers.
- Among **college men**, sensation-seeking scores correlated positively with sexual behaviour that the men **knew could expose them to AIDS** (Zuckerman, 1994b), and studies of older participants confirmed this.
- Among **gay men, both Black and White**, the correlation between sensation seeking and risky sex was so strong that researchers concluded that **high-sensation-seeking males constitute a high-risk group for AIDS** (Fisher & Misovich, 1990; Kalichman et al., 1994).

#### Cheating, colour choices, and tattoos

- Studies in **Israel** found that high sensation seekers are more likely to **cross the street on foot against a red light**, and that they prefer **"hot" colours such as red and orange** to **pastels such as light blue** (Rosenbloom, 2006a, 2006b).
- Among young people in **Germany** aged **14 to 24**, significantly more high sensation seekers had **tattoos and body piercings** (Stirn, Hinz, & Braehler, 2006).
- **American college students** high in sensation seeking were more likely to **cheat on academic tests** (DeAndrea et al., 2009).

#### Computer use

In **China**, high-sensation-seeking students were more likely to become **obsessed with computer games and the Internet** (Qing-Xin, Rong-Gang, & Yan, 2005; Zheng et al., 2006), and American **workers** high in sensation seeking often used **workplace computers for personal purposes** such as email, games and sexual websites (Everton, Mastrangelo, & Jolton, 2005). But a study of **American college students** found **no** such relationship (Velezmoro, Lacefield, & Roberti, 2010): a mixed finding.

#### Job performance

Among **233 low-level employees** in the United States, high sensation seekers had **lower job performance**, formed **fewer social relationships at work** and **sought less information** from co-workers and supervisors. The researchers suggested such jobs are **not stimulating enough** for them (Reio & Sanders-Reio, 2006): the problem lies in the fit between person and job.

### Personality differences

Zuckerman and others correlated SSS scores with a range of personality measures.

**Eysenck's dimensions.** SSS scores, **especially disinhibition**, were related to Eysenck's **extraversion** and to the **asocial tendencies** associated with **psychoticism**. From this Zuckerman concluded that high sensation seekers are **egocentrically extraverted**: interested in other people **only as an audience or a source of stimulation**, not in a dependent or nurturing way. The high sensation seeker at a party wants listeners and excitement, not intimacy.

**Jung's extraversion.** High SSS scores also correlated positively with extraversion as Jung described it and as the **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator** measures it (Morehouse, Farley, & Youngquist, 1990).

**Neuroticism.** Studies of sensation seeking and neuroticism found **no correlation**. Zuckerman's reading was that high SSS scores do **not** point to abnormal or neurotic behaviour. If anything, neuroses such as **phobias and obsessive-compulsive behaviour** might be related to **low** sensation seeking, which makes intuitive sense: the phobic and the obsessional person are organised around avoiding the unpredictable.

**The five-factor and HEXACO models.** Among college students and adults in the **Netherlands**, high sensation seekers also scored high on **openness to experience, extraversion and conscientiousness**, three of the five factors, and on two factors of the **HEXACO** model, **emotionality** and **honesty-humility** (de Vries, de Vries, & Feij, 2009). Roberti (2004) found positive correlations with **openness to experience** and **agreeableness**.

**Other characteristics.** High scorers show a high degree of **autonomy**. They **express their emotions openly**, are **assertive**, **nonconforming** and confirmed **risk takers**. They act **independently of social conventions** and of other people's needs and attitudes, and, governed mainly by their own needs, they arrange their lives to **maximise opportunities for self-fulfilment**.

> **Trap.** Sensation seeking goes with **extraversion** (Eysenck's and Jung's), **psychoticism** (especially disinhibition) and **openness**, but **not neuroticism**; phobias and OCD may go with **low** sensation seeking. "High sensation seekers are more neurotic" is a trap.

### Cognitive processes

**Intelligence.** Correlations between sensation seeking and intelligence test scores are **generally positive but not high**. The strongest single study comes from the **Republic of Mauritius**, an island nation in the Indian Ocean off the African coast.

> **Raine, Reynolds, Venables, & Mednick (2002).** Children assessed for sensation seeking at **age 3** were tested for intelligence at **age 11**. Those who had been high in sensation seeking at 3 scored **12 points higher** on intelligence tests at 11 than those who had been low. The result held for **both boys and girls** and was **not affected by parents' occupation or education**. One plausible reading is that a young child who actively seeks out stimulation gives their own developing brain a richer environment.

**School grades.** Despite this, high sensation seekers **did not earn better grades**. Zuckerman's explanation was practical: they spend so much time on active recreation that they **study less**.

**Creativity and fantasy.** High sensation seekers show a **greater capacity for original thinking** on creativity tests, though not always in schoolwork, and are drawn to **speculative, bizarre, even pseudoscientific** ideas. They also show more of what Freud called **primary-process thought**: images, dreams and daydreams so vivid that inner experience and outer reality blur. Zuckerman's reading ties this to the core need: when the outside world cannot supply enough novelty, they **turn inward and build a fantasy world**.

**What the chapter summary adds.** The 11th edition's chapter summary states two further cognitive findings that the body text does not develop: high sensation seekers **recognise symbols and figures more quickly**, and they **prefer complexity in visual stimulation**. It also states that they show **stronger physiological responses to novel stimuli**. Learn these as stated; they are carried over from earlier editions.

### Occupational preferences

Because high sensation seekers need more varied and stimulating experience, they choose different kinds of work. On vocational interest tests such as the **Kuder Occupational Interest Survey**, high SSS scores correlated **positively with scientific interests** and **negatively with clerical interests**.

- **Men** with high SSS scores scored high on the **Strong Interest Inventory** scales for the **helping professions**: **psychologist, physician, psychiatrist, social worker and minister**. Their scores correlated **negatively** with **business** occupations such as **accountant, purchasing agent and banker**.
- **Women** with high SSS scores showed high interest in being a **lawyer** and low interest in being an **elementary school teacher**.
- High sensation seekers of **both sexes** who were drawn to the helping professions preferred the **risky, cutting-edge** end of them: **crisis intervention** work, or **paramedic** duty on emergency response teams.

> **Trap.** High-sensation-seeking men lean to **science and helping professions**, not business; for women the named pair is **lawyer (high)** versus **elementary school teacher (low)**. "Thrill seekers become bankers" is wrong.

### Attitudes

High sensation seekers are **more liberal in politics and religion**, more often **atheistic**, and **more permissive about sex**, their own and others'. **Low** sensation seekers are more often **frequent churchgoers**, score high on **authoritarianism** (rigid opinions and prejudiced attitudes) and have **low tolerance for ambiguity**, treating ambiguous ideas and situations as **threats rather than challenges** (Zuckerman, 1994a). The chapter summary states the mirror image: high sensation seekers are **more tolerant of ambiguity** and **lower in authoritarianism**.

### Heredity versus environment

**The genetic evidence.** A large and consistent body of research shows a **strong hereditary basis** for sensation seeking (Zuckerman, 2013). A study by **Eysenck** suggested that **58 percent** of the trait could be accounted for by genetic factors (Eysenck, **1983**). A **twin study** run jointly by **Zuckerman and Sybil Eysenck** found an **even greater genetic component** (Zuckerman, 1993).

**The environmental evidence.** Heredity is not the whole story.

- Comparing **14- to 20-year-olds in Turkey and Wales** on their motivation for risky behaviour, researchers found **small but significant cultural differences**, which point to the importance of **learning** (Kloep et al., 2009).
- Zuckerman himself recognised **parental sensation seeking** as an environmental influence. **Low-sensation-seeking parents** may be overly **fearful, protective and inhibiting**, forbidding adventurous behaviour. **High-sensation-seeking parents** may **encourage and reinforce** unusual activities, creating an environment that promotes still more sensation seeking. (Notice that this is also a gene-environment correlation: the same parents who pass on the genes provide the matching environment.)
- In **Sweden**, teenagers high in **impulsivity and sensation seeking** who attended **more privileged schools** committed **fewer crimes** than equally high-scoring teens at less privileged schools (Eklund & Fritzell, 2014).
- Surprisingly, children raised in **orphanages or other institutional care** who were high in sensation seeking were **much less likely to get into trouble** than equally high scorers raised in **private homes** (Loman et al., 2014).

**Birth order.** Early SSS studies supported the idea that **first-borns and only-borns** (boys and girls alike) are **higher** in sensation seeking than later-borns. The proposed mechanism: first-borns and only children receive **more stimulation and attention** from parents early in life, which exposes them to a **higher optimal level of stimulation** and predisposes them to seek it as adults (Zuckerman, 1979). However, later research in **England** found **no correlation** between birth order and sensation seeking (Crozier & Birdsea, 2003).

> **Trap.** **Eysenck (1983): 58 percent** genetic; the **Zuckerman and Sybil Eysenck** twin study: **even higher** (Sybil Eysenck was Hans Eysenck's wife and collaborator, not a misprint). On birth order, **early** studies found **first-borns and only-borns** higher, but **Crozier & Birdsea (2003)** in England found **no relationship**. "Early studies linked birth order to sensation seeking" is safe; "birth order reliably predicts it" is not.

Classic background on biology: high sensation seekers tend to have **low platelet monoamine oxidase (MAO-B)**, an enzyme that breaks down monoamine transmitters such as dopamine; they show a stronger **orienting response** to novel stimuli (matching the summary's "stronger physiological responses to novel stimuli") and tend to be cortical **augmenters**, whose evoked responses grow with stimulus intensity. None of this is in the 11th edition's body text, but it is the standard account of why the trait is so heritable.

> **Highlights: research on Zuckerman's ideas.** The book's research box says high sensation seekers tend to score high on **adventure seeking, disinhibition and boredom susceptibility**; to live in **Westernised** cultures; to **take risks, use drugs and alcohol, drive fast and have frequent sex**; to spend a lot of time on **computer games**; to be **extraverted, open to experience, conscientious and emotional**; to be interested in the **helping professions** (such as psychologist or social worker) rather than business; to hold **liberal political and religious views**; to be **only or first-born** children; and to have **more tattoos and body piercings**.

### Reflections on sensation seeking

Zuckerman's single-trait focus keeps stimulating research linking sensation seeking to **behavioural, cognitive, personality and physiological** variables. His emphasis on **heritability** sets his work apart from the **behavioural and social-learning** approaches with their stress on situations and learning; this is the one place in the chapter where Schultz explicitly puts a theorist on the nature side.

The theory also has **commonsense appeal**. We readily accept that people differ in their need for excitement, risk and change; we know our own level and can judge friends' and relatives' levels from what they enjoy or avoid. Zuckerman tested this self-knowledge by having high and low sensation seekers choose the adjectives that best described them (Zuckerman, 1978).

**Table 14.3** How high and low sensation seekers describe themselves

| Theme | High sensation seekers chose | Low sensation seekers chose |
|---|---|---|
| Energy and delight versus fear | Enthusiastic, elated | Frightened, fearful |
| Boldness versus alarm | Adventurous, daring | Panicky, upset |
| Lightness versus tension | Playful, zany | Tense, shaky |
| Imagination and mischief versus anxious arousal | Imaginative, mischievous | Nervous, worried |

The book prints two plain lists of eight adjectives; the grouping into themes is only a memory aid. The high scorers' words are all **positive, active and approach-oriented**; the low scorers' are all **states of fear and anxiety**. The same intense or unfamiliar situation that exhilarates one person threatens the other, which is why the trait reaches so far into behaviour: it changes the emotional meaning of novelty itself.

Evaluation: on the credit side, a clearly defined construct, a validated scale with factor-analysed components, many replicated correlates across countries, and genetic evidence anchoring the trait. On the debit side, the evidence is largely **correlational** (does sensation seeking cause drug use, or do drug-using peers raise scores?), several findings are **mixed** (computer use, birth order), and the theory says little about helping a high sensation seeker beyond the implied advice of the good/bad distinction: find a socialised, stimulating outlet rather than trying to extinguish the need.

## 14.3 Martin E. P. Seligman: Learned Helplessness and the Optimistic/Pessimistic Explanatory Style

### A graduate student's first day

In the **mid-1960s**, **Martin Seligman** (born **1942**) began research at the **University of Pennsylvania** on a limited-domain facet he would call **learned helplessness**. The same man later led the positive psychology movement (14.4): the theorist of helplessness and the theorist of flourishing are one person, and the second project grew out of the first.

Seligman first saw the phenomenon in an experiment on **dogs**, on his **first day as a graduate student**. Classic background: the laboratory was that of **Richard Solomon**, a learning theorist, where fellow graduate students **J. Bruce Overmier** and **Steven Maier** were working on the interaction between classical and instrumental conditioning.

The experiment Seligman walked into had two parts, and you need to see both to understand why the result was a surprise.

1. **Pavlovian (classical) conditioning.** The dogs were taught to associate a **high-pitched tone** with an **electric shock**. This is **respondent** behaviour in Skinner's sense: the dog does nothing; tone and shock are simply paired.
2. **Operant (instrumental) learning in a shuttle box.** Each dog was then placed in a large box with **two compartments separated by a low wall**. Shock came through the floor of the compartment the dog was in. To escape, the dog had only to perform an easy **operant** response: **jump the low barrier** into the other compartment, where there was no shock. Dogs normally learn this quickly. Once they had, the plan was to test whether the **tone alone**, without shock, would make them jump, showing transfer from the Pavlovian to the operant phase.

### A shocking finding

The study did not go as planned. When shock came through the floor, the dogs **did not jump** the barrier. They **lay down, whimpered, and made no effort to escape**.

The other experimenters were baffled. Seligman thought he understood. In the first phase, he reasoned, the dogs had learned that they were **helpless**: when the tone sounded, nothing they did could prevent the shock that followed. Having learned that responding was pointless, they **generalised** that lesson to the shuttle box, even though escape was now easy and available. The animals had not failed to learn; they had learned something, namely that their actions did not matter.

Seligman saw the implications at once. If a dog could learn something as abstract as the **futility of its own actions**, here was a laboratory analogue of the **human helplessness** he saw in the urban poor, the newborn infant and the despondent patient, one that could be used to study how helplessness arises, how to cure and prevent it, which drugs act on it and who is vulnerable (Seligman, 1990). He defined **learned helplessness** as a condition resulting from the **perception that we have no control over our environment**, that **nothing we do can change our situation**, and later widened the programme to **optimism versus pessimism** and then **happiness**.

> **Trap.** The definition is about **perception** of no control, and its result is **passivity**. It is not "a lack of ability", not "an external locus of control" and not "depression". Learned helplessness is a laboratory phenomenon that Seligman later proposed as a **model** of depression.

### Early research

#### Studies with dogs

In Seligman's initial experiments, dogs were **harnessed** and given **painful but not physically harmful** electric shocks from which **no action** could free them. After a series of these shocks they were placed in the two-compartment **shuttle box**, and shock was again delivered through the floor. Their behaviour was compared with that of a **control group** of dogs that had **not** received the earlier inescapable shocks.

- **Control dogs**, when shocked in the shuttle box, **raced around** the compartment until they happened to **leap the barrier** into the safe side. On each later trial they jumped **faster**, having learned that jumping ended the shock. This is ordinary escape learning.
- **Experimental dogs**, which had received the inescapable shocks first, ran around for **about 30 seconds**, then **gave up**, dropped to the floor and whimpered. They **never learned to escape**, not even when the experimenters tried to **lure them over the barrier with food**. They had become **passive and helpless** (Overmier & Seligman, **1967**; Seligman & Maier, **1967**).

Classic background: the decisive version used a **triadic design**. One group could end its shocks by pressing a panel; a **yoked** group received identical shocks at the same moments but had no control; a third group had no shock. In the shuttle box the first and third groups learned to escape and most yoked dogs did not. Because the first two groups had identical shocks, the design shows that **uncontrollability**, not shock as such, did the damage. The rat tumour study below uses the same three conditions.

#### Studies with humans

The effect has been shown in many studies with **human** participants. The classic one is Hiroto's.

> **Hiroto (1974).** An **experimental group** heard a **loud, irritating noise** and were told they could stop it by pressing buttons in the right sequence, but the task was rigged so that **no sequence worked**. A **control group** could stop the noise with a sequence that was easy to learn. Next, everyone faced a new task in which stopping the noise needed only a **hand movement from one side of a box to the other** when a light came on. **Controls learned it quickly; experimental participants sat passively** and did nothing about the noise. As with the dogs, uncontrollability in one setting produced passivity in a new setting where control was easy.

Later research confirmed and extended these findings.

- Learned helplessness can be acquired simply by **observing helpless models**, especially when the observers see the models as **similar to themselves** (Chamber & Hammonds, 2014). The observers seemed to reason that if the model could do nothing about the situation, neither could they. This is Bandura's vicarious learning (Chapter 13) applied to helplessness.
- A **meta-analysis of 132 studies** involving several thousand people found that the effects of inescapable shock were **even stronger in humans than in animals**. Learned helplessness has been documented in adult men and women, college students, adolescents, children, elderly people, unemployed women and psychiatric inpatients (Li, Mardhekar, & Wadkar, 2012; Villanova & Peterson, 1991).
- It occurs in **everyday life** whenever we are subjected to continuous intrusive stimuli we cannot control: **noise** from a neighbour or from outdoors, or being **cyberbullied** on social media (Evans & Stecker, 2004; Rabinowitz, 2005; Zucchi, Bacheller, & Muscarella, 2012).

> **Trap.** Dates and animals. **Dogs**, **mid-1960s**, Penn, Seligman's **first day as a graduate student**; **Overmier & Seligman (1967)** and **Seligman & Maier (1967)**; a **two-compartment shuttle box** with a **low barrier**. Humans: **Hiroto (1974)**, **inescapable noise**, **hand-shuttle**. **Rats**, not dogs, in the **tumour** studies (**1982**, **1985**), the **immune** study (Maier, Laudenslager, & Ryan, **1985**) and the **sex-difference** study (Dalla et al., **2008**). Meta-analysis: **132 studies**, effects **stronger in humans**. Not **Harlow's monkeys**, not **Pavlov's** salivating dogs.

Classic background: in 2016 Maier and Seligman revised the theory using neuroscience. **Passivity under prolonged aversive events is the default, unlearned response**; what animals **learn** in the escapable condition is **control**, detected by the medial prefrontal cortex, which then inhibits the default. On this view animals do not so much learn helplessness as fail to learn control. The 11th edition does not include this; use it in interviews, not MCQs.

### Learned helplessness as torture

The same techniques used in the laboratory have been applied by the **Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)** and other organisations engaged in counter-terrorism. Prisoners were placed in situations where no escape from painful stimulation was possible. The book gives examples: one prisoner was confined **inside a coffin for days**; another was **waterboarded 83 times over 17 days**; others were **stripped and left in solitary confinement** for days while deafening rock music was played continuously.

Afterwards the prisoners showed the **same symptoms** as laboratory participants: they became **compliant** and stopped trying to change their conditions. When Seligman learned years later how his findings had been used, he was **"grieved and horrified"** (McCoy, 2014), a standard example of basic research turned to harmful use.

### Learned helplessness and emotional health

If the lack of control harms people, the possession of control should help them, and the evidence on that point is wide.

> **Thompson et al. (1993): cancer patients.** Among cancer patients aged **29 to 80**, those with the **highest perception of control** were **better adjusted** than those who believed they had little control. This held **even for patients severely debilitated** by their illness. Patients who believed they could exert some influence over their illness, their recovery and their emotions showed **better psychological adjustment than people in better physical condition who perceived little control**. Perceived control mattered more than actual physical state.

People can also **learn to increase** their sense of control.

> **Law, Logan, & Baron (1994): dental patients.** Men and women with an **average age of 55**, scheduled for extensive dental treatment, were first assessed for **anxiety** and for their **desire for control** in a dental setting. **Half** watched a **stress inoculation training** video; the **other half** (the control group) watched a video about **local sights**. The patients who benefited most were those who had **little control** in the dental chair but a **strong desire for more**. After the training video they believed they had **more control** and felt **less pain** during the actual treatment than the untrained controls.

The dental study is the applied side of the theory: if perceived lack of control causes distress, teaching people a sense of control should reduce it, most of all for those who want control and lack it.

### Animal research on learned helplessness and physical health

Could helplessness affect the body as well as behaviour and mood? Seligman and his colleagues tested this with **rats** and **tumours**.

> **Visintainer, Volpicelli, & Seligman (1982).** Rats were injected with **malignant tumour cells**, in a dose at which **half** of them would ordinarily be expected to **reject** the cells and survive. They were then exposed to one of **three conditions**: **escapable** shock, **inescapable** shock, or **no shock**.
>
> | Condition | Percentage rejecting the tumour |
> |---|---|
> | No shock (control) | **50 percent** (as expected) |
> | **Escapable** shock (some control) | **70 percent** |
> | **Inescapable** shock (learned helplessness) | **27 percent** |
>
> The rats that could control their shock did **better** than rats given no shock at all, and the helpless rats did far **worse**. Since the escapable and inescapable groups both received shock, the difference reflects **control**, not stress as such.

The result was extended to development with **young rats** (Seligman & Visintainer, **1985**), exposed to the three conditions when young and given malignant cells **as adults**. **Most rats that had learned helplessness when young failed to reject the tumours as adults**; **most that had learned control rejected them**. Seligman concluded that early **mastery** acted like an **immunisation** and early **helplessness** put adult rats at risk for cancer (Seligman, 1990), an idea that returns in the childhood section and in his optimism programmes.

**The immune mechanism.** Helplessness was found to **weaken rats' immune systems** (Maier, Laudenslager, & Ryan, **1985**). Two kinds of defending cell were affected: **T-cells**, which normally multiply quickly when a specific invader appears, stopped doing so in rats given **inescapable** shock, and **NK (natural killer) cells**, which destroy viruses, bacteria and tumour cells, lost their killing power. That offers a plausible **physiological route** from helplessness to the rats' failure to reject tumours.

**A sex difference.** Later research found that **most male rats** exposed to uncontrollable stress **never learn to escape**, whereas **most female rats do** learn (Dalla, Edgecomb, Whetstone, & Shors, **2008**). Learned helplessness was therefore **easier to establish in males**, which suggests that females may be less subject to the dangerous physiological changes found in the earlier studies.

> **Trap.** Tumour rejection: **50 (no shock), 70 (escapable), 27 (inescapable)**. The common error is to assume no shock does best; **escapable** shock, which gives **control**, produced the **highest** survival. And in the sex-difference study it was **males** who more readily became helpless, the opposite of what students expect from the higher rate of depression in human women.

### Explanatory style: optimism and pessimism

#### From helplessness to explanation

The human studies exposed a problem. Not everyone exposed to uncontrollable events becomes helpless, and of those who do, some recover in hours and others sink for months. Something must stand between the event and the response. Seligman's answer was that what matters is not only the **lack of control** but **how we explain it to ourselves**: our **explanatory style**.

- An **optimistic explanatory style** **prevents** helplessness. The optimist explains a failure in a way that limits its reach.
- A **pessimistic explanatory style** **spreads** helplessness to all facets of life. The pessimist explains a failure in a way that makes it personal, lasting and everywhere.

Put simply, in Carver and Scheier's words, **optimists expect good things to happen to them and pessimists expect bad things to happen to them** (Carver & Scheier, 2002). Which way a person leans may shape much of their life. Optimists, for instance, build **better social networks and connections** and **cope better with physical pain** (Carver & Scheier, 2014; Ramirez-Maestre, Esteve, & Lopez, 2012).

#### The three dimensions of explanatory style

The body of the 11th edition describes pessimistic explanations through examples, and the chapter summary states the formal model, which it calls the **attribution model of learned helplessness**: when we fail, we **attribute** the failure to some cause, and the cause can be described on three dimensions.

| Dimension | The question it answers | Pessimist's attribution for a failure | Optimist's attribution for a failure | Seligman's everyday label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Internal versus external** | Is it about me or about the situation? | **Internal**: "It's all my fault." | **External**: "The paper was unusually hard." | Personal (personalisation) |
| **Stable versus unstable** | Will the cause last? | **Stable**: "It's always going to be this way." | **Unstable**: "I was unwell that week." | Permanent (permanence) |
| **Global versus specific** | Does it affect everything or just this? | **Global**: "It's going to affect every aspect of my life." | **Specific**: "I'm weak at statistics, not at everything." | Pervasive (pervasiveness) |

The three pessimistic sentences in the table are close to the book's own examples of how pessimists talk about negative events, in "personal and pervasive terms". The chapter summary puts it as **personal, permanent and pervasive** explanations, and states the rule cleanly: **pessimists attribute failures to internal, stable and global causes; optimists attribute failures to external, unstable and specific causes**. That is how helplessness changes, in the pessimist, **from brief and localised to long-lasting and generalised**.

Classic background: this is the **reformulated learned helplessness theory** of **Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale (1978)**. **Internal/external** explains **loss of self-esteem** (my fault versus anyone would have failed), **stable/unstable** explains **how long** helplessness lasts, and **global/specific** explains **how widely** it spreads. For **good** events the pattern reverses: optimists explain successes as internal, stable and global, pessimists as external, unstable and specific.

> **Trap.** The dimensions are **internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific**; the summary's everyday words are **personal, permanent, pervasive**, in the same order. Pessimists explain **bad** events as **internal, stable, global**; optimists as **external, unstable, specific**. Weiner's **controllability** is not one of Seligman's three. And Seligman's "internal" is not Rotter's: internal locus is healthy, while an internal attribution for *failure* is one third of the pessimistic style.

#### Measuring explanatory style

The 11th edition refers to people's "attributional style" and reports optimism and pessimism scores, but it does **not name** the instruments. Classic background: two methods dominate Seligman's research.

- **The Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ)** (Peterson et al., 1982). The respondent imagines **hypothetical events**, half good and half bad (for example, a long unsuccessful job search), writes down the **one major cause** of each, and rates it on **seven-point scales** for **internality, stability and globality**. A children's version, the **Children's Attributional Style Questionnaire (CASQ)**, was used in the schoolchildren studies.
- **The CAVE technique (Content Analysis of Verbatim Explanations)**. Coders extract the **causal explanations people have already given** in letters, diaries, interviews, therapy transcripts, speeches or press quotations, and rate them on the same three dimensions. This allows **retrospective** assessment of people who cannot be tested; for example, pessimistic explanations in Harvard graduates' accounts of their wartime experiences predicted **poorer health in middle age** (Peterson, Seligman, & Vaillant, 1988). The nuns study below rests on the same logic.

#### Physical health

According to Seligman, people with an optimistic style tend to be **healthier**. The mechanism he proposes is behavioural: pessimists believe their actions make little difference, so they are **unlikely to change** their smoking, diet, exercise or timing of medical care to prevent illness.

- **Young adults**: optimists were **less likely to get sick**, and when they did, they were far more likely to **take responsibility** for their own care, by resting, seeing a doctor or drinking appropriate fluids (Peterson, Maier, & Seligman, 1993).
- **College undergraduates**: pessimists had **twice as many infectious illnesses** over **one year** as optimists.
- **Finland**: after the **severe illness or death of a family member**, optimists **regained a positive attitude more quickly** (Kivimaki et al., 2005).
- Optimists cope better with health problems, **recover faster** from certain medical procedures and **manage stress** better (Aspinwall & Tedeschi, 2010).
- **Breast cancer**: among patients with a **recurrence**, optimists **lived longer over a five-year** period, **independent of the severity** of their illness. Among women in **Norway** diagnosed with breast cancer, high scorers on pessimism were far more likely to be **anxious and depressed one year after treatment** (Schou et al., 2004). In the same women, levels of optimism and pessimism stayed **stable for the year** after treatment **whatever their prognosis** (Schou et al., 2005), which suggests that explanatory style is a trait-like disposition, not simply a reaction to good or bad news. Optimistic breast cancer patients also **function better socially and mentally** and report a **higher quality of life** (Colby & Shifren, 2013).
- **Immunity and the heart**: optimists seem to develop **stronger immune systems**, are more likely to **recover from heart attacks**, and experience **less pain and fewer symptoms after heart surgery** (Peterson & Seligman, 1987; Peterson, Maier, & Seligman, 1993; Ronaldson et al., 2014).
- **First-semester law students**: those with an optimistic style had significantly **more T-cells and NK cells** (Segerstrom & Taylor, 1998). Compare this with the immune findings in helpless rats: the same two cell types appear on both sides.
- **Adults aged 30 to 45**: high pessimism went with **more negative beliefs about life** and **higher blood pressure** (Räikkönen et al., 1999).

> **Taylor et al. (1992): optimism and AIDS.** Participants were **homosexual and bisexual men**, some **HIV-negative** and some **HIV-positive** (likely to develop AIDS). High scorers on optimism had **less distress**, **fewer illness worries**, believed their **risk** of AIDS was **lower** and expected **more control** over the disease. Strikingly, the **HIV-positive men were more optimistic about not developing AIDS than the HIV-negative men**. The beliefs may have been **illusory**, but they helped the men cope and limited the depression that often comes with serious illness, and the most optimistic were **no more likely** to take sexual risks; in general they **took better care of their health**.

#### Longevity

Optimists may **live longer**.

- **The nuns study** (Danner, Snowdon, & Friesen, 2001). American nuns wrote **life stories in their early 20s**. Those whose stories showed **optimism** had a significantly **lower death rate** when surveyed **60 years later** than those whose writing showed pessimism. Because nuns share diet, housing, work and access to medical care, many of the usual confounds are controlled.
- **Medical patients** (Maruta, Colligan, Malinchoc, & Offord, 2002). Among patients treated for various conditions, optimists had **19 percent greater longevity** when surveyed **30 years later**.
- **Elderly people in England**: high scorers on optimism were in **far better physical health** (Steptoe et al., 2006).

#### Age

People **over 65** tend to have a **more optimistic** explanatory style and higher **subjective well-being** than younger people. Studies of the elderly in **China and the United States** found that the more optimistic reported **greater well-being and less depression** (Isaacowitz, 2005a; Leung, Moneta, & McBride-Chang, 2005; and others). Elderly optimists even **under-reported their weight**, while pessimists claimed to weigh **more** than they did (Sutin, 2013).

#### Culture

- Among **Black and Latina grandmothers** who were primary caregivers for their grandchildren, and among **Muslim college students in Kuwait and Oman**, optimists were **happier**, had **less depression, anxiety and hostility**, and reported **fewer sleep disorders** (Abdel-Khalek & Lester, 2010; Alansari & Kazem, 2008; Conway et al., 2008).
- **United States versus China** (Lee & Seligman, 1997): **American** students were **more optimistic** than **Chinese-American** students, who were **more optimistic** than **mainland Chinese** students. The ordering parallels Uba's locus-of-control ordering in 14.1: closer contact with American individualism goes with more internal control and more optimism.
- College students in **Kuwait** scored **lower** on optimism than U.S. students (Abdel-Khalek & Lester, 2006).
- Among college students in **Italy**, **men** were **more optimistic** than women (Colombo, Balbo, & Baruffi, 2006).
- **United States versus Japan** (Chang, Asakawa, & Sanna, 2001): **Japanese** students were **more pessimistic**. American students thought positive events far more likely to happen to **themselves** than to others; Japanese students thought positive events far more likely to happen to **other people**.

#### Stress

Stressful life experience can wear optimism down.

- **Alzheimer's caregivers** (Robinson-Whelen et al., 1997): adults caring for a relative with Alzheimer's disease grew **increasingly pessimistic over four years** and had more **anxiety, stress and physical health complaints** than non-caregivers.
- College students who were more optimistic at the **start of their first semester** had significantly **less stress and depression** during it (Brissette, Scheier, & Carver, 2002).
- **Middle-aged** optimists reported **fewer depressive symptoms** than pessimists (Chang & Sanna, 2001).
- Children from **third to sixth grade** who were most optimistic had **fewer symptoms of depression** and **fewer behaviour problems** (Ey et al., 2005).

#### Performance: school, sport and work

**School.** American college students who were optimists typically **earned better grades**, and so did optimists in **Kuwait** (El-Anzi, 2005). When pessimists got **lower grades than their peers**, they became **more depressed** about it than optimists with similarly low grades (Gibbons et al., 2000).

**Sport.** In **France**, optimism affected how well adolescents could **dribble a basketball** (Martin-Krumm, Sarrazin, Peterson, & Famose, 2003). Boys and girls aged **14 to 16** were led to believe they had **failed** a dribbling test. On a second test, those high in optimism were **less anxious, more confident and performed better** than those high in pessimism. The design is a small version of the learned helplessness paradigm: an induced failure, then a measure of how people respond to the next opportunity.

**Work.** The book says Seligman applied explanatory style to **job performance** but gives no study. Classic background: with **Metropolitan Life insurance agents**, whose job involves constant rejection, optimists on the ASQ **sold more and quit less** than pessimists (Seligman & Schulman, 1986).

#### Cognitive functioning

Optimism and pessimism also shape **attention and thinking**. Pessimistic college students attended more to **negative stimuli**, whereas optimists attended to **both positive and negative** ones (Segerstrom, 2001): optimists are not blind to bad news, pessimists are tuned to it. In **Germany**, optimists were **flexible and adaptable** in thinking, pessimists **rigid** and more likely to **give up** their goals (Weber, Vollmann, & Renner, 2007).

#### The downside of optimism

An optimistic style is **not always** an advantage (Schneider, 2001).

- Some optimists hold **unrealistic views of their own vulnerability**. They may **drink or use drugs to excess**, telling themselves that such behaviour cannot harm them because their attitude is so positive, despite evidence to the contrary.
- **Highly optimistic** college students who expected to do well on an examination felt **greater disappointment** on seeing their scores than less optimistic students (Sweeny & Shepperd, 2010).
- At a **casino** near their university, optimistic students were far **more likely to keep gambling** through consistent losses, while pessimists were more likely to **stop**; the optimists kept expecting to win even during a losing streak (Gibson & Sanbonmatsu, 2004).
- This kind of **unrealistic optimism**, which assumes good outcomes will come to oneself far more readily than to other people, turns up **more often in individualistic** societies (the United States) than in **collectivist** ones (China), and **more often in men** (Lin & Raghubir, 2005).

The opposite error is also harmful. **Unrealistic pessimism** in the face of adversity such as serious illness, telling yourself that you will never cope or overcome it (which also reflects **low self-efficacy**), can lead to **lack of effort** and therefore **lack of success** (Blanton et al., 2001). The lesson of the two lists together is that what helps is **flexible, reality-based** optimism, not optimism at any price.

> **Highlights: research on Seligman's concept of optimism.** According to the book's research box, high scorers on optimism tend to **get sick less often** and experience **less stress and depression**; **live longer**; **recover faster from a family member's death**; live in **individualistic** cultures such as the United States rather than collectivist ones; **earn better grades**; are **more flexible and adaptable** in their thinking; and even **dribble a basketball better**.

#### Pessimism and depression

Seligman found a **strong association between learned helplessness and depression**. A major symptom of depression is the **feeling of being unable to control life events**, and Seligman called depression the **"ultimate pessimism"**. Severely depressed people believe they are **helpless**; they see little point in doing anything because they expect nothing to turn out well. Seligman noticed several **similarities** between the symptoms of depression and the features of learned helplessness (Seligman, 1990).

Everyone feels helpless at times, and most of us **recover**. Some people do not: they **generalise** a failure in one area (a poor grade, a missed promotion) to other areas and to their **self-worth**, and lose the impetus to strive. That is the three dimensions at work: the failure has become *internal* (about self-worth), *global* (all situations) and, if it persists, *stable*.

**Table 14.4** Learned helplessness and depression share the same symptom picture

| Feature | Learned helplessness (laboratory) | Depression (clinical) |
|---|---|---|
| Activity level | Passivity | Passivity |
| Learning about control | Trouble learning that responding brings **relief** | Trouble learning that responding brings **outcomes** |
| Hostility | **Lack of aggression** | Hostility turned against the self (**introjected hostility**) |
| Appetite and drive | **Weight loss and anorexia** (loss of appetite) | **Loss of libido** |
| Neurochemistry | **Norepinephrine depletion** | **Norepinephrine depletion** |
| Bodily stress | **Ulcers and stress** | **Ulcers and stress**, plus **feelings of helplessness** |

Source: adapted by Schultz from Seligman's *Learned Helplessness and Depression in Animals and Men*. The book's footnote explains that **norepinephrine** is a neurotransmitter and that **severe depression is associated with norepinephrine deficiency**. "Anorexia" in the left column means loss of appetite and food intake, not the eating disorder anorexia nervosa.

As the table shows, depression goes with signs of poor physical health such as **ulcers, stress and norepinephrine deficiency**, and the deficiency can cause **low energy** and **deepen** the depression. Depression also puts people at risk for physical illness by **weakening the immune system**: it **suppresses NK cell activity** and **alters the white blood cell count**, findings confirmed by **more than 40 studies over a 10-year period** (Herbert & Cohen, 1993; Weisse, 1992).

**What decides who becomes depressed?** According to Seligman, the crucial difference between people who recover from a temporary low and those who do not is their **explanatory style**. His argument (Seligman, 1990) runs like this: a pessimistic style turns learned helplessness from something **brief and local** into something **long-lasting and general**; when the person who fails is a **pessimist**, learned helplessness becomes **full-blown depression**, whereas in an **optimist** a failure produces only **brief demoralisation**.

> **Seligman's undergraduate study.** At the **start of a semester**, students were assessed for **explanatory style** and asked which course grade they would count as a **personal failure**. After the **midterm**, they were tested for **depression**. Of **optimists** who got a grade they had defined as failure, **30 percent** showed depressive symptoms; of **pessimists**, **70 percent**. Because each student defined "failure" in advance, the same subjective setback is compared across styles. Similar results came from other college samples and **third-graders**: explanatory style **predicted** depression (Nolen-Hoeksema, Girgus, & Seligman, 1987; Zullow & Seligman, 1985).

Research in the **United States and Finland** found that people high in pessimism were more likely to **take antidepressant medication** and to be **at risk for suicide** (Chang et al., 2013; Kronstrom et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** Learned helplessness is **not** depression, and the theory does not say helplessness always produces it. The claim is diathesis-stress: helplessness becomes depression when it meets a **pessimistic explanatory style**. Order the figures correctly: **optimists 30, pessimists 70**.

### The development of learned helplessness in childhood

Learned helplessness can occur at any age, but Seligman argued that we are **especially vulnerable in infancy and early childhood**, and that helplessness experienced in these formative years can **predispose** a person to the **pessimistic explanatory style** (Seligman, **1975**).

**Infancy.** Infants begin life **totally helpless**, with no control over their environment. As they mature they gain control step by step. They can **cry**, which brings a parent or caregiver. They learn to **crawl, walk and speak**, and each new skill opens new possibilities for control, and also for failure. Through these early interactions with the physical and social world, the child's sense of **helplessness or of mastery** is laid down.

**Response-outcome learning.** An infant's response may change the environment (food, a reachable toy, a hug) or change nothing. Infants form primitive associations between **responses and outcomes**. If responses **do not** produce outcomes, the infant learns that they do not work and may **generalise** this to all responses, with a pervasive **sense of no control over life**. When responses and outcomes are **highly correlated**, the feedback builds **mastery and control**. Compare an infant whose cries are answered reliably with one in an overstretched institution whose cries are answered at random.

**The role of parents.** A **consistent explanatory style develops by about age 8**, and it is **strongly influenced by the parents' explanatory style**: in the words of Seligman and his co-authors, **"pessimistic parents also have pessimistic children"** (Peterson, Maier, & Seligman, 1993). Studies in **India** found that children's optimism or pessimism can be strongly influenced by **parents' level of education**: the more formal education the parents had, the **more optimistic** their children (Daraei & Ghaderi, 2012).

**Genes as well as learning.** Research with adolescents in the **Netherlands** and other countries found that attributional style is influenced by **learning and by genetic factors** (Lau, Rijsdijk, & Eley, 2006; Mavioglu, Boomsma, & Bartels, 2015). So although Seligman treats explanatory style as learned, the evidence the book cites shows a genetic contribution too.

**Later childhood.** Helplessness can also develop later, in response to **bullying by peers**, a **harsh school environment** or other negative experiences. **Race and poverty** are related to its development. Students who have frequent **conflicts with peers** and who are treated by classmates and teachers **as though they are less intelligent or less skilled** may develop learned helplessness (Orejudo et al., 2012). **Low social support** from parents and other family members can lead to **high pessimism** in children (Ciarrochi & Heaven, 2008).

**What the chapter summary adds.** The summary names the **major causes** of learned helplessness in childhood as **maternal deprivation** and an environment offering **low levels of stimulation and feedback**. That is exactly the response-outcome account above: a child without a responsive caregiver and without an environment that reacts to their actions has little chance to learn that acting changes things.

> **Trap.** Seligman's *Helplessness: On Depression, Development, and Death* is **1975**; a consistent explanatory style appears by **about age 8**; the depression study's schoolchildren were **third-graders**; the Indian finding concerns **parents' education** (Daraei & Ghaderi, 2012). Do not confuse Seligman's **age 8** with Rotter's **8 to 14**, when control attempts become more pronounced.

### Reflections on learned helplessness

Learned helplessness and optimism versus pessimism have generated **hundreds of studies**, and Seligman and his associates have applied the concepts to **sports, politics, religion, child rearing and job performance**. Schultz's verdict is that **a large and impressive body of data** supports the concept. Seligman also took the findings out of the laboratory, proposing a **programme of exercises to teach optimism** to adults and children for use at home and at work, and he then extended his ideas to positive psychology and to the question of what makes people happy.

Classic background: the method in *Learned Optimism* (1990) adapts Ellis's ABC model into **ABCDE**: note the **Adversity**, the **Beliefs** about it and their **Consequences**, **Dispute** the pessimistic beliefs, and notice the **Energisation** that follows. The school version is the **Penn Resiliency Program**.

> **Trap.** **Learned helplessness** is the **problem**: passivity produced by experience of non-control. **Learned optimism** is the **remedy**: a **taught** shift in explanatory style toward external, unstable, specific explanations of bad events. Both are learned, which is Seligman's hopeful point: what is learned can be unlearned. If asked which is a **therapeutic programme**, the answer is learned optimism.

A critic would note that the animal work is experimental, but most human evidence on explanatory style, health and longevity is **correlational** (social class or early illness could produce both pessimism and poor outcomes); that the book's own "downside of optimism" findings complicate the story; and that genetic findings complicate the picture of a purely learned style. These limit the causal claims without undermining replicated core findings.

## 14.4 Martin Seligman: Positive Psychology

Seligman launched **positive psychology** in the **late 1990s**, during his term as **president of the American Psychological Association**. Psychology, he argued, had become a science of damage and repair; it should also study **the best of human functioning**: **happiness, excellence and optimal human growth**. After thirty years on helplessness and depression, he turned from "what goes wrong?" to "what goes right?".

Classic background: the presidential year was **1998**, consistent with the book's remark that the first journal appeared in 2000, "only two years" later. In 2000 Seligman and **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi** framed the field around **three pillars**: positive subjective experiences, positive individual traits and positive institutions.

### The life of Seligman (1942 to present)

Schultz opens with a deliberately ironic question: does Seligman's own life meet his criteria for happiness?

**A not particularly happy man.** By his own account Seligman is **rarely happy** in the sense of feeling joyful, despite great success. In **2010** he placed himself in the **lowest 30 percent** of people for **positive feelings and emotions**, and told a reporter that a life can be good and satisfying without positive feeling, his own now running on **meaning and purpose** (Burling, 2010). Of an earlier study of students in the **top 10 percent of happiness**, he remarked that he would not have qualified (Diener & Seligman, 2002). But he holds that there are **different kinds of happiness**, and that his deepest satisfaction comes from being **fully engaged in work**, which his wife confirmed. Here already is the later distinction between the **pleasant life** (which he lacks) and the **engaged** and **meaningful** lives (which he has).

Classic background: Diener and Seligman's (2002) happiest 10 percent were notably **social**, with **strong romantic and other relationships**, a necessary though not sufficient condition for very high happiness.

**A father's stroke.** When Seligman was **13**, his father had a **severe stroke** that left him disabled and almost **helpless**. The boy's emotions were, in his own phrase, "frozen over", and he later called the ordeal a lasting shadow over his life (Burling, 2010). The book does not draw the link, but it is hard to miss: the scientist of **helplessness** watched his father made helpless when he was a teenager.

**An outsider at school.** Seligman attended a **private preparatory school in Albany, New York**, where most pupils came from **wealthy upper-class families**. He felt like an **outsider**, keenly aware of the differences between himself and the other boys, isolated and alone. A close friend said that he never fully lost that feeling and may still struggle inwardly with being accepted (Burling, 2010).

**Career.** He was able and hardworking enough to be admitted to **Princeton** and to earn his **Ph.D. at the University of Pennsylvania in 1967**. He is now **director of Penn's Positive Psychology Center**. He is also a **champion bridge player** who has won a number of tournaments.

> **Trap.** Seligman: born **1942**; Penn graduate student in the **mid-1960s**; **Ph.D. 1967** (the year of Overmier & Seligman and Seligman & Maier); *Helplessness* **1975**; APA president in the **late 1990s** (1998); *Authentic Happiness* **2002**; *Flourish* **2011**. Father's stroke at Seligman's age **13**; self-rated positive emotion in **2010**: **lowest 30 percent**; the student group he would not have joined: **top 10 percent**.

### Characteristics and causes of happiness

What does positive psychology say about the **happy personality**? Psychologists have labelled it in various ways, most often **subjective well-being** or **life satisfaction**, and define it as including two things:

1. a **cognitive evaluation** of the quality of one's life experience (a judgement: "my life is going well"), and
2. the presence of **positive moods and emotions** (a feeling).

Happiness therefore has both a **rational** and an **emotional** side. This matters for interpreting the research, because some factors (income, for example) affect the judgement more than the feeling.

Classic background: Ed Diener divides subjective well-being into **life satisfaction**, **frequent positive affect** and **infrequent negative affect**, measured typically with his five-item **Satisfaction With Life Scale** (Diener, Emmons, Larsen, & Griffin, 1985) and the **PANAS** (Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988). The 11th edition names no specific happiness scale.

The research has uncovered many factors linked to happiness. They are taken here in the book's order.

### Money

The old saying holds: **money does not buy happiness**. But the **absence** of money can produce **unhappiness**. An income adequate to meet **basic needs** is a **necessary but not sufficient** condition for happiness.

- In **Sweden, Turkey and other European countries**, the **lowest** happiness was found in **low-income households**, and a major source of satisfaction was having a **cash reserve for minor emergencies** (Berlin & Kaunitz, 2014; Drakopoulos & Grimani, 2013).
- A survey of **123 nations** confirmed that **life satisfaction rises with income**: people who earn more report being **more satisfied, though not necessarily happier** (Diener, Ng, Harter, & Arora, 2010). This is the judgement-versus-feeling distinction at work: income improves the evaluation of one's life more than the day-to-day emotional experience of it.
- Money may be mentioned as important for happiness **more often by Americans than by Europeans** (Mogilner, 2010).
- People who lack money for essentials are unhappy, but money **well beyond** what is needed has **little measurable effect**. Even a **huge lottery win** usually brings only a **temporary** rise in well-being (DeNeve & Cooper, 1998; Diener, Suh, Lucas, & Smith, 1999; King & Napa, 1998).
- **Materialism** works against happiness: one researcher concluded that the more people endorse materialistic goals, the less happy and satisfied they are (Van Boven, 2005).
- **High-income** people tend to experience **more stress** and spend **less time on relaxation and leisure** (Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwartz, & Stone, 2006).

Classic background: the lottery result is usually explained by **hedonic adaptation**, getting used to improved circumstances so that happiness drifts back toward its usual level (Brickman, Coates, & Janoff-Bulman, 1978, comparing lottery winners and paralysed accident victims).

### Physical attractiveness

Are attractive people happier? Research in the **United States, Canada, England and Germany** says **yes**: attractiveness does seem to **increase happiness**. But the effect may be **indirect**, because attractiveness generally improves **marriage prospects, job opportunities and income potential** (Anderson, Adams, & Plaut, 2008; Bennett, 2011; Hamermesh & Abrevaya, 2011). The link was **stronger in cities** than in rural areas. In **Finland**, people who **appear obese** were found to be **less happy** than those who do not (Bockerman et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** The 11th edition's answer on attractiveness is **yes, attractive people are happier**, probably **through** better marriage, job and income prospects, and the link is **stronger in cities**. Some older textbooks and summaries state that attractiveness has little or no relationship to happiness; for an 11th-edition-keyed paper, that is wrong.

### Health

Poor health can **diminish** happiness, but good health does **not guarantee** it: another **necessary but not sufficient** condition. The arrow also runs the other way: a review of **more than 160 studies** found that high subjective well-being contributes to **longevity and better health** (Diener & Chan, 2011). Across **25 countries**, people who **exercise** report **higher life satisfaction** (Dolan, Kavetsos, & Vlaev, 2014).

### Age

A survey of **more than 300,000 Americans** aged **18 to 85** found that well-being and life satisfaction were **high at 18**, **declined until about age 50**, and then **rose again**, so much that **85-year-olds** reported **greater life satisfaction than 18-year-olds** (Stone, Schwartz, Broderick, & Deaton, 2010). Plotted against age, that is a **U-shaped** curve.

These findings have **not been duplicated elsewhere**. A worldwide **Gallup** survey found that in **English-speaking developed nations and Western Europe** well-being **dipped between 45 and 54**, while in **Eastern Europe, the former Soviet Union and Latin America** happiness **declined steadily with age** (Steptoe, Deaton, & Stone, 2015). The U-shape may belong to wealthy countries rather than to ageing itself.

> **Trap.** "Happiness follows a U-shaped curve across the life span" is true of the **300,000-American** sample (low point **around 50**; **85-year-olds above 18-year-olds**), but the book stresses that it was **not replicated everywhere**: in Eastern Europe, the former Soviet Union and Latin America happiness simply **declined** with age. Give both halves.

#### Adolescence

- American adolescents high in well-being are far more likely to have **parents who value them, take an interest in them and care about their future**; the link is **stronger for girls** than for boys (Rayle, 2005).
- Happier American adolescents also had **more positive peer relationships**, **lower anxiety and depression**, **greater hope for the future**, and a stronger sense of **personal control** over their lives (Gilman & Huebner, 2006). Here locus of control (14.1) reappears as a correlate of happiness.
- Happier teenagers in **China** had **better-educated parents** who **rarely quarrelled** with each other (Guo-Xing & Hai, 2006).
- In **Israel**, adolescents who felt strong **social support from the community and from friends** were happier (Ronen et al., 2014).

#### Older people

Happiness does **not necessarily decline** in old age, but the findings are inconsistent: some studies show happiness **rising** with age, others suggest it **peaks around 65** and falls thereafter (Lacey, Smith, & Ubel, 2006; Mroczek & Spiro, 2005).

- Among older Germans aged **70 to 103**, those with **health problems and physical limitations** showed a **decline** in well-being (Kunzmann, Little, & Smith, 2000).
- Among older Germans **hospitalised with disabilities**, however, it was **not the impairment itself** so much as the person's **attitude** toward it that lowered well-being; those with **more positive attitudes** were happier (Schneider et al., 2006).
- Among elderly people in **Slovakia**, those high in **belief in a just world** were happier (Dzuka & Dalbert, 2006).
- **Exercise**, both **aerobic** and **resistance (strength) training**, correlates strongly with happiness in the elderly (Netz et al., 2005).
- Older people with **stronger social networks and supportive friends** are happier than isolated people (Pinquart & Soerensen, 2000).
- Among people aged **70 to 100** in the **United States, England and Germany**, **impending death** brings a decline: the **last three to five years** of life are often marked by a **rapid fall** in reported happiness (Gerstorf et al., 2008, 2010).

### Marriage and social support

If social support matters for older people, are married people happier? **Yes.** Research in **more than 40 countries** with about **60,000** people found that married people report **more happiness** than people who are **divorced, separated, widowed or never married** (Carr, Freedman, Cornman, & Schwarz, 2014; Diener, Gohm, Suh, & Oishi, 2000).

- **Married women** of all ages are happier than unmarried women, and **married men** happier than unmarried men, in many countries including the **United States and Poland** (Batanowska-Ratij et al., 2014; Mastekaasa, 1995).
- **Children** are another matter. Happiness **declines for both husbands and wives after the first child** is born. In general, people **without children** report **more happiness** than parents, and parents of **very young children** report the **least** (Munsey, 2010). The **lowest** happiness of all is found among **unmarried mothers** as a group (Ifcher & Zarghamee, 2014).
- A **35-year longitudinal** study of married couples found that each partner's happiness **tracks the other's**: one partner's unhappiness tends to make the other unhappy, and the same holds for happiness (Hoppmann, Gerstorf, Willis, & Schaie, 2011).
- In **Germany**, people who had been through **more than one divorce** were **more satisfied after the second divorce than after the first** (Luhmann & Eid, 2009).

**Social support** in general correlates strongly with well-being in most countries. In **Israel**, life satisfaction was higher among people with strong support networks, especially **new immigrants** (Litwin, 2005); in **Finland**, active social support was critical to the psychological well-being of **recent immigrants** (Jasinskaja-Lahti et al., 2006).

> **Trap.** Married people are happier, but **people without children are happier than parents**, parents of **very young** children are **least** happy, and **unmarried mothers** are the **lowest** group. The Highlights box sums this up as "married and without children". An option saying "having children increases marital happiness" contradicts the book.

### Culture

- In all major international surveys, **Denmark** consistently ranks **highest** in happiness (Hussain, 2014).
- A nation's **relative wealth** is of major importance. Countries so poor that basic needs are hard to meet have **much lower** happiness than economically advanced countries (Diener, Diener, & Diener, 1995; Veenhoven, 2005), and people in **wealthier** countries rate themselves **happier** (Delhey & Dragolov, 2015; and others).
- In **collectivist Eastern** cultures such as **China and Turkey**, there was **little relationship between social support and well-being** (Dan, Jun, & Ji-Liang, 2006; Turkum, 2005). Among college students in **Turkey**, the chance to **give** support to others was significantly related to well-being, but **receiving** support had **no effect** (Gencoz & Ozlale, 2004).
- Subjective well-being is markedly **higher in individualistic** cultures such as the United States than in **collectivist** cultures such as China (Diener, Suh, Smith, & Shao, 1995; Park & Huebner, 2005; Wirtz et al., 2009).
- **Descendants of immigrants** tend to show the well-being level of their ancestral culture even without continued contact: **Danes and Swedes** are high, and so are **Americans of Danish and Swedish descent**; Americans descended from lower-well-being cultures such as **Hungary and Lithuania** are lower (Rice & Steele, 2004).

> **Trap.** Social support predicts well-being **in most countries**, but not in **China and Turkey**, where (in Turkey) **giving** support mattered and **receiving** it did not.

### Geography: where you live is important

A nationwide survey of Americans aged **25 to 75** found that people in different regions use **different criteria** for happiness (Plaut, Markus, & Lachman, 2002):

| Region | What people there treated as necessary for well-being |
|---|---|
| **New England** | **Physical well-being**, **autonomy**, and **not feeling constrained** |
| **West south-central** states (Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana) | **Personal growth** and feelings of **cheerfulness and happiness** |
| **East south-central** states (Kentucky, Tennessee, Mississippi, Alabama) | **Social responsibility** and **contributing to the welfare of others** |

Other research found well-being **highest in the West Coast and mountain states**, **moderately high on the East Coast**, and **lowest in the Midwest and South**. Overall, happiness was significantly related to **higher income, more education and greater tolerance** for other people's views (Rentfrow, Mellander, & Florida, 2009). In **Germany**, over a **24-year** period, the life satisfaction of people in **affluent regions** stayed **higher** as they aged than that of people in less affluent regions (Gerstorf et al., 2010).

### Race and ethnicity

- In multicultural societies, people with a **stronger sense of ethnic identity** report **higher** well-being (Le, Lai, & Wallen, 2009).
- **African-American college students** who felt more **identification with and acceptance by the Black community** on campus reported **higher** psychological well-being (Postmes & Branscombe, 2002).
- African-American adults who had experienced **discrimination** reported **lower** life satisfaction. Those who attended **predominantly White schools** reported **higher** life satisfaction than those from predominantly Black or mixed-race schools, though the book notes this may have changed in the decades since the research (Broman, 1997).
- Among older African Americans aged **55 to 93**, those reporting the stresses of **racial discrimination** had **lower** life satisfaction (Utsey et al., 2002), and Black college students reporting more perceived discrimination had **lower** life satisfaction and **more depressive symptoms** (Prelow, Mosher, & Bowman, 2006; Seaton & Yip, 2009).

The chapter summary condenses this into a single clause: high well-being goes with **not being a member of a minority group that experiences discrimination**.

### Personality

A great deal of research links happiness to the **five-factor model** (Chapter 8). People **low in neuroticism** and **high in extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness** report **high** well-being (DeNeve & Cooper, 1998; Hayes & Joseph, 2003; and others). In **China**, **extraversion** was one of the most important predictors (Zhang & He, 2010).

- A study of **Eysenck's three factors in 39 nations**, and of the **Big Five in 26 nations**, found that **low neuroticism and high extraversion** correlated significantly with **national** levels of well-being (Steel & Ones, 2002).
- Comparing adults in the **United States and Germany**, **low neuroticism** was the **strongest** predictor of well-being (Staudinger, Fleeson, & Baltes, 1999).
- Research in **more than 30 countries** confirmed **low neuroticism and high extraversion** as major correlates (Gomez et al., 2009; Lynn & Steel, 2006; and others).
- The same pattern appears in **apes**. The happiest **orangutans** in zoos in the **United States, Canada and Australia** were those rated by keepers as **high in extraversion, low in neuroticism and high in agreeableness** (Weiss, King, & Perkins, 2006).

Because neuroticism, extraversion and agreeableness are **primarily inherited**, their importance across so many cultures (and species) suggests that **happiness has a strong genetic component**.

Other personality variables add to the picture.

- College students in the **United States and South Korea** identified four contributors to happiness: **autonomy, competence, relatedness and self-esteem** (Sheldon, Elliot, Kim, & Kasser, 2001).
- In **England**, **gratitude** was strongly related to well-being among university students: the more grateful people were for their circumstances, the happier they were (Wood, Joseph, & Maltby, 2009).
- A **German** study of nearly **10,000 adults** found a **downside of conscientiousness**: among people who **lost their jobs**, the **most conscientious** suffered a **far larger drop** in life satisfaction (Boyce, Wood, & Brown, 2010). The trait that helps you keep a job hurts more when you lose one.

> **Trap.** **Low neuroticism** and **high extraversion** are the most consistent correlates (low neuroticism **strongest** in the U.S.-Germany comparison). Conscientiousness is generally positive **but** deepens the fall in satisfaction after **job loss**. Contrast sensation seeking, which is **unrelated** to neuroticism. Some older summaries call personality "the strongest predictor of happiness"; the 11th edition makes no such overall claim.

Classic background: *Authentic Happiness* (2002) summarised this evidence as **H = S + C + V**: enduring happiness reflects a genetically influenced **set range**, plus **circumstances**, plus **voluntary** activities.

### Self-efficacy and internal locus of control

Both **self-efficacy** (Chapter 13) and **internal locus of control** (14.1) are **positively related** to life satisfaction: we are happiest when we feel **competent** to cope and **in control** of the reinforcers that matter. In **Germany and Australia**, **self-mastery** and feeling **in control** of one's life were strongly related to well-being (Lang & Heckhausen, 2001; Windsor & Anstey, 2010). **Autonomy**, a strong sense of **self-determination**, contributes significantly (Schmutte & Ryff, 1997; Sheldon et al., 2005), and **trust, emotional stability, self-esteem** and **dealing positively with stress** all correlate with high well-being (DeNeve & Cooper, 1998). Here the chapter's facets meet: Rotter's internal locus and Bandura's self-efficacy are ingredients of Seligman's happy personality.

### Thinking positively

**Positive emotions** such as **joy, interest, love and enthusiasm** are linked to well-being (Fredrickson, 2001; the 11th edition spells the name "Frederickson"), and **negative emotions** detract from it.

- Among **eighth- and ninth-graders in Finland**, having opportunities for **self-fulfilment** and **social relationships** in and out of school correlated highly with well-being (Konu, Lintonen, & Rimpelae, 2002).
- Among college students, **vengefulness** (the desire to seek revenge or hurt others) led to **lower** life satisfaction (McCullough, Bellah, Kilpatrick, & Johnson, 2001).
- Among **Holocaust survivors** living in **Israel**, the trauma's effects persisted **60 years later** as negative emotions and low well-being (Ben-Zur & Zimmerman, 2005).

Classic background: Fredrickson's **broaden-and-build theory** explains why positive emotions matter beyond feeling good. Negative emotions narrow attention to an immediate threat; positive emotions **broaden** the range of thoughts and actions a person considers, and over time this **builds** lasting personal resources (skills, relationships, resilience).

### Goals

Happy and unhappy people differ in their **motives and goals**.

- A research review concluded that life satisfaction is enhanced when people's goals concern **personal growth and contributions to the community**, are **realistic**, and are **valued by their culture**. People high in life satisfaction were **intensely committed** to their goals and believed they were **making progress** toward them (Klug & Maier, 2015; Lyubomirsky, 2001).
- Among more than **3,000 managers** in the United States aged **25 to 74**, life satisfaction was strongly related to an **orientation toward the future**, including **active planning**; the relationship was **stronger for older** managers (Prenda & Lachman, 2001).
- A **German** study ranked which life goals matter most for happiness: choosing a partner with the right **characteristics**, keeping a **healthy lifestyle**, getting the **work-leisure balance** right, and being **socially involved** (Headey, Muffels, & Wagner, 2010).

**How happy and unhappy people see their lives.** Diener, Lucas, Oishi and Suh (2002) drew on two very large international student samples, more than **13,000** students in **31** countries and more than **7,000** in **41** countries, and showed that happy and unhappy people **construe** the same kinds of events differently. In judging their lives, **unhappy** people give more weight to **what might be wrong**; **happy** people look through rose-tinted glasses and weigh the **positive** aspects more heavily. This is the cognitive half of well-being at work: the evaluation is part of the happiness.

### Happiness and success

Which comes first? Are people happy because they are successful, or successful because they are happy? The research tends to show that **happiness leads to the behaviours that produce success** (Boehm & Lyubomirsky, 2008). People high in subjective well-being are more likely to **get job interviews**, to be **rated more positively by supervisors** once hired, and to show **superior performance and productivity** (Lyubomirsky, King, & Diener, 2005).

> **Highlights: research on Seligman's concept of happiness.** The book's research box says happy people tend to **have more money and live in wealthy countries**; be **more attractive**; **exercise more as they age**; be **married and without children**; have a **strong ethnic identity**; be **extraverted, conscientious and high in self-efficacy and internal control**; be **low in neuroticism**; be **enthusiastic, optimistic and grateful**; have **goals, a healthy lifestyle and strong social involvement**; keep a **good balance between personal life and work**; and spend time on the Internet, **or perhaps not** (the box's own hedge, explained next).

### Internet use

Does online life affect happiness? The evidence is **mixed**, which is why the Highlights box hedges.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Biao-Bin et al. (2006) | Adolescents in **China** | **No significant effect** of online social interaction on happiness |
| Contarello & Sarrica (2007) | College students in **Italy** | **Increased** well-being and closeness to their group and society after forming online relationships |
| Huang (2010a) | **Taiwan** | **Slightly negative** effect |
| Stepanikova, Nie, & He (2010) | Large **American** sample | **Stronger negative** effect: the more time online (browsing, texting, news, chat rooms), the **lonelier and less happy** |
| Brooks (2015) | American college students doing a classroom task | Personal **social media use** during the task went with **poorer performance, more stress and less happiness** |
| Penard, Poussing, & Suire (2013) | Large sample from rich and poor nations | Overall, Internet communication made people **happier**, more so for some than others; **lower-income** people and those in **developing nations** gained the most life satisfaction |
| Kelly (2010) | Internet users | **More women than men** reported feeling **empowered** by Internet use |

The book's conclusion is simply that the Internet helps some people's well-being more than others'.

### Different kinds of happiness: meaning and flourishing

#### The pleasant, engaged and meaningful lives

As positive psychology developed, Seligman distinguished **three kinds of happiness**, or three routes to finding satisfaction in life:

| Kind of happiness | Seligman's label | What it consists of | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive emotion** | **The pleasant life** | A great deal of positive emotion: satisfaction, contentment at work, serenity, optimism | The person who savours good meals, music and holidays and generally feels good |
| **Engagement** | **The engaged life** | Engagement, involvement, purpose, commitment and **absorption in work**; time passes quickly, attention is wholly on the activity, and the **sense of self is lost** (Seligman, Rashid, & Parks, 2006) | The surgeon in a long operation or the musician in performance who looks up to find hours have passed |
| **Meaning** | **The meaningful life** | Using one's **talents, abilities and strengths** to belong to, serve or commit to something **larger than the self**: a religion, organisation, political party, ideal, or anything that transcends the self; it brings a sense of satisfaction and of having **lived well** | The doctor who spends a career in rural public health, or the volunteer who builds a charity |

Two research findings attach to this scheme. Having a **purpose in life** is related not only to meaning but to **healthy ageing and longevity** (Hill & Turiano, 2014). And Seligman's own research has shown that the pursuit of **meaning and engagement** is **much more strongly correlated with happiness** than the pursuit of **pleasure** (Schueller & Seligman, 2010). That second finding is exactly what Seligman's self-description predicted: a man low in positive emotion can still be a satisfied man.

Classic background: the engaged life is Mihaly **Csikszentmihalyi's flow**, complete absorption when a **challenge matches one's skills**, with loss of self-consciousness and distorted time. Seligman links it to using one's **signature strengths**, drawn from **Peterson and Seligman's (2004)** *Character Strengths and Virtues*: **24 strengths** under **six virtues** (wisdom and knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence), measured by the **VIA Inventory of Strengths**. None of this is in the 11th edition.

> **Trap.** Which of the three corresponds most closely to **Maslow's self-actualisation**? (This is one of the book's own review questions.) The best answer is the **meaningful life**, which, like self-actualisation, means fully using one's talents, abilities and strengths, and, like Maslow's self-actualisers who were devoted to a mission outside themselves, commits them to something larger than the self. The **pleasant life** is closest to ordinary hedonic happiness; the **engaged life** is closest to **flow** (and to Maslow's peak experiences in its absorption, though not identical).

#### Flourishing and the five pillars

In *Flourish: A Visionary New Understanding of Happiness and Well-Being*, Seligman argued that **flourishing** is a better term than happiness or well-being for the people at the **top** of the happiness scale. This group, roughly **10 to 18 percent of the world's population**, includes those who not only **feel happy** but also **excel in their relationships and accomplishments**.

He concluded that **happiness alone is not enough** to give life full and deep meaning. To flourish we must **cultivate our talents and abilities**, form **deep and meaningful relationships**, and **contribute positively** to the world around us. Happiness, which he now calls **positive emotion**, is only **one of five pillars** of the flourishing personality (Seligman, 2011):

1. **Positive emotion** (which includes happiness and life satisfaction)
2. **Engagement**
3. **Relationships**
4. **Meaning**
5. **Achievement**

Classic background: the five pillars are universally known by the acronym **PERMA** (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment). The 11th edition does not use the acronym, and it calls the fifth pillar **achievement**; Seligman's own book uses **accomplishment** (sometimes "accomplishment or achievement"). They are the same pillar.

> **Trap.** Do not merge the two schemes. The **three kinds of happiness** are the **pleasant, engaged and meaningful** lives. **Flourishing** adds **relationships** and **achievement/accomplishment** to make **five** pillars (PERMA), demoting "happiness" to the **P**. The 11th edition's text dates *Flourish* to **2012**, but its own citation and suggested readings give **2011**, the correct year. Flourishers are **10 to 18 percent** of the world; the **top 10 percent** belongs to the Diener and Seligman (2002) student study.

### Comment

Seligman's call for a positive psychology met an **enthusiastic** response, and Schultz lists the milestones.

- **2000**: the *Journal of Happiness Studies*, the **first** journal in the field, began publication, **only two years** after Seligman introduced the topic.
- **2002**: Seligman published the popular book *Authentic Happiness: Using the New Positive Psychology to Realize Your Potential for Lasting Fulfillment*. Reviews were highly favourable; **Newsweek** called positive psychology a new age for the discipline; **Time** put Seligman on its **cover** with a **40-page** special issue.
- At **Harvard**, the most popular undergraduate course, with **more than 800** students, was on positive psychology.
- **2006**: the *Journal of Positive Psychology* began publication.
- **2012**: the **Girl Scouts** introduced a **Science of Happiness Badge**, earned through a **month-long** course in strategies for achieving happiness (Wojcik, 2012).
- **By 2014**: **more than 10,000** journal articles a year were being published.
- **By 2015**: a Google search for "happiness" returned almost **350 million** hits.

Schultz's own judgement is that positive psychology has become a **phenomenally successful and widespread** branch of psychology.

#### Evaluating positive psychology

The 11th edition's "Comment" is a story of success, and it offers **no formal criticisms**. But the book's own evidence contains several cautions, and these are fair game in a "which is NOT supported" question.

- **Inconsistent findings:** the U-shaped age curve did not replicate in several regions, social support mattered little in China and Turkey, Internet effects ran both ways, and studies of old age disagree.
- **Correlation and direction:** most findings are correlational, and the book itself raises the direction problem for success and for health.
- **Culture:** well-being is consistently higher in **wealthy, individualistic** societies, raising the question of whether the measures carry Western ideals.
- **The downside of optimism** (14.3): unrealistic optimism keeps gamblers gambling and heavy drinkers drinking.
- **The founder's own case:** Seligman's low positive emotion alongside evident satisfaction suggests that happiness as feeling is too narrow a target, the argument of *Flourish*.

Classic background: critics outside the book question the movement's novelty, since **humanistic psychology** (Maslow and Rogers, Chapters 9 and 10) already argued for studying health and growth, and **Maslow** used the phrase **"positive psychology"** in 1954. The real difference, and the best answer to the book's review question comparing the two, is method: the humanists relied on case study, phenomenology and clinical experience, while Seligman insists on **quantitative empirical research**. Critics also cite heavy reliance on **self-report**, popular claims that outran the evidence (a mathematical "positivity ratio" for flourishing, published in 2005, was later shown to be invalid), and the risk of implying that the unhappy, ill or poor are to blame, which sits uneasily with the book's own evidence on poverty and discrimination.

## How the chapter fits together

Four small theories, but the same few threads run through all of them.

1. **Perceived control is the hidden centre.** Rotter's internal locus is a belief that one's actions control reinforcement; learned helplessness is what happens when experience destroys that belief; explanatory style decides whether the damage stays local or spreads into depression; and internal control, self-efficacy and autonomy all predict happiness. Even the tumour rats with **control** over their shock did **better** than rats given no shock. Zuckerman is the partial exception: sensation seeking concerns the need for stimulation, though its "bad" form is defined by impulsiveness.
2. **Learned versus inherited.** Locus of control and helplessness are **learned** (parental consistency, response-outcome contingencies) and **trainable** (cognitive training at 60 to 75, stress inoculation, optimism exercises). Sensation seeking is **primarily inherited** (58 percent, or more in the Zuckerman-Sybil Eysenck twin study), with environment shaping its expression. Happiness lies between, with a strong genetic component through neuroticism, extraversion and agreeableness; even attributional style shows genetic influence.
3. **Childhood lays the groundwork.** Locus of control comes from supportive, consistent, praising, non-authoritarian parenting (or its absence); helplessness has its window of vulnerability in infancy, with a consistent style by about age 8; sensation-seeking differences show at age 3 and in second grade.
4. **Culture runs the same way across every facet.** Collectivist and Asian samples are **more external**, **lower** in sensation seeking, **less optimistic** and **lower** in well-being; social support predicts happiness except in China and Turkey; unrealistic optimism is commoner in individualistic cultures. The uniformity is itself a reason to ask whether the measures carry Western assumptions.
5. **Mind and body.** Internals recover faster from heart surgery; optimists have more T-cells and NK cells and live longer; helpless rats lose immune function and fail to reject tumours; depression suppresses NK activity; happiness predicts longevity.
6. **What limited-domain research looks like.** Questionnaires (I-E, Nowicki-Strickland, SSS Form V), laboratory paradigms (shuttle box, Hiroto's noise, tumour rats) and large surveys, replicated across many countries; the cost is that most human findings are correlational.
7. **Links to the rest of the book.** Rotter met **Adler** and worked beside **Kelly**; locus of control is set against **Bandura's** self-efficacy, and helplessness can be learned vicariously. Sensation seeking sits inside **Eysenck's** E, its bad form goes with **P**, it correlates with **Jung's** extraversion (MBTI) and the **five-factor** model, and high scorers show **Freud's** primary-process thought. The dog experiment joins **Pavlovian** and **Skinnerian** conditioning. Positive psychology inherits the questions of **Maslow and Rogers** and answers them with experimental and survey methods.

### What the text implies on the human-nature issues

Schultz gives **no** ratings for these theorists. The table records only what the chapter's text says or shows; issues it does not touch (free will, uniqueness versus universality) are left out.

| Issue | Rotter | Zuckerman | Seligman |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature versus nurture** | Nurture: learned in childhood from parents, family size and income; trainable | Nature: "primarily inherited"; the book says heritability sets him apart from behavioural and social-learning approaches | Mainly nurture for helplessness and explanatory style, with some genetic evidence; happiness has a strong genetic component |

## Worked example

*A 20-year-old second-year engineering student is referred to the college counsellor after failing two of six semester papers. He says the papers "are set to fail people" and that marks depend on "which examiner you get" and on contacts. In the next breath: "I'm just not built for engineering. It'll be the same next semester. My whole future is finished." He has stopped attending classes for three weeks. Over the past year he has started racing motorbikes on the highway at night, drinks heavily at weekends, recently got a large tattoo, and calls lectures "unbearably boring". His father, a shopkeeper with little schooling, reacts unpredictably to his marks, sometimes ignoring them, sometimes shouting for hours. His elder sister is a keen trekker who volunteers with an ambulance service.*

**Locus of control.** "Set to fail people", "which examiner" and "contacts" place control of his reinforcements in **powerful others and luck**: an **external locus**. The theory predicts his response: if effort does not affect outcomes, effort is pointless. The developmental account fits: internals' parents are **supportive and consistent**, while his father's responses were **inconsistent**, the kind of environment in which a child learns that outcomes do not follow from his behaviour.

**Explanatory style.** "Not built for engineering" is **internal**, "the same next semester" **stable**, "my whole future" **global**: the full **pessimistic** style for a bad event.

**A contradiction?** He blames the examiners (external) and himself (internal) at once. There is no contradiction once the constructs are kept apart. **Locus of control** is a generalised belief about whether **actions affect outcomes**; **explanatory style** is how he explains **why a bad event happened**; and in Bandura's terms he also has **low self-efficacy** for study. All three push towards giving up.

**Learned helplessness and depression risk.** He shows the left column of **Table 14.4**: **passivity**, and failure to act on the fact that his responses could still change outcomes (he passed four papers). In a **pessimist**, Seligman predicts, helplessness becomes long-lasting and general; in the undergraduate study **70 percent** of pessimists with a self-defined failing grade became depressed, against **30 percent** of optimists. He should be assessed for **depression**, and, since high pessimism goes with **suicide risk**, asked directly about safety.

**Sensation seeking.** Highway racing is **thrill and adventure seeking**; doing it drunk and illegally adds **disinhibition**; finding lectures unbearable is **boredom susceptibility**. The mix looks like **impulsive unsocialised** sensation seeking, with the predicted risks: **fast and drunk driving, accidents, convictions**. Being **largely inherited**, the trait probably runs in the family; his sister expresses it in the **socialised** way, through trekking and emergency work (the risky end of the **helping professions** and Levenson's **pro-social** risk taking). Sensation seeking is **unrelated to neuroticism**, so it does not explain his distress, and high scorers do **not** get better grades, so poor results need not mean low ability. At 20 he is near the age at which sensation seeking starts to **decline**.

**What the concepts recommend.**

- **Re-attribution (learned optimism):** two failures out of six are **specific**; preparation can change, so the cause is **unstable**; four passes contradict "not built for this". Aim for external, unstable, specific explanations of this failure and internal, stable, global explanations of his successes.
- **Rebuild contingency:** small, controllable goals with quick, visible feedback, so that responses repeatedly produce outcomes; raising **perceived control** helps most those who want it and lack it (Law, Logan, & Baron, 1994), and becoming more internal helps recovery from addiction (Amram & Benbenishty, 2014).
- **Channel, do not extinguish, the sensation seeking:** socialised thrill and adventure outlets with real safety rules (his sister's trekking group), direct work on drinking and driving, and varied, hands-on project work, since high sensation seekers do poorly in **under-stimulating** jobs.
- **Well-being:** absorbing **engagement**, **relationships** and social support, and **realistic** goals tied to growth.

**What not to say.** Not "you are just lazy", which hands him another internal, stable, global explanation; and not "just think positive", because the **downside of optimism** findings show that **unrealistic** optimism has its own costs.

## MCQ traps

1. **Limited-domain theories** come from **laboratory** research by **researchers**, cover a few factors, and place **less** (not zero) emphasis on therapy; global theories came from **clinical practice**.
2. **The inherited facet** is **sensation seeking** (within **Eysenck's E**); **locus of control** and **learned helplessness** are **learned** with a strong cognitive component.
3. **Rotter's Ph.D.:** the 11th edition prints **1951** yet places WWII service after it; the correct year is **1941** (Classic background).
4. **Rotter's stations:** Brooklyn College (met **Adler**), Indiana, Connecticut state hospital, Army, **Ohio State** (with **Kelly**), **Connecticut at Storrs (1963)**; APA award **1988**.
5. **I-E Scale (1966) = 23 forced-choice pairs** (Classic background: 29 including 6 fillers; scored for **external** choices). **Nowicki-Strickland = 40 items (1973)**, about 24 languages; cartoon preschool form **1983**.
6. **LOC and age:** control attempts pronounced at **8 to 14**; internality **peaks in middle age**; trainable at **60 to 75**.
7. **LOC and gender is inconsistent:** Norwegian **girls** more internal; **no** U.S. adult difference; English women and Chinese women more external.
8. **Uba (1994), most to least external:** Hong Kong Chinese > Chinese Americans > European Americans. **South Africans** more internal than **Lebanese**.
9. **Health LOC reversal:** in France, Germany and South Africa internals took **less** general care of health; **externals** trust oncologists more.
10. **LOC vs self-efficacy:** **generalised** vs **situation-specific**; Judge et al. (2002) strong link; **Bandura (1997)** denied overlap.
11. **Zuckerman's definition** needs both halves: **varied, novel, complex, intense** experience **and** willingness to take **physical, social, legal, financial** risks.
12. **SSS = 40 items, Form V**, contrasted-groups validation; "40 items" also fits the Nowicki-Strickland, so check the test name.
13. **Four components:** **thrill and adventure seeking, experience seeking, disinhibition, boredom susceptibility**; "novelty seeking" is Cloninger's, not Zuckerman's.
14. **Good** (non-impulsive socialised) = **thrill and adventure seeking**; **bad** (impulsive unsocialised) = **Dis + ES + BS + high psychoticism**.
15. **Women** score higher on **experience seeking**; men on the other three.
16. **Age:** SS rises in **middle school**, falls in the **twenties**; **ES does not decline** (Zuckerman at 74); **no** education or college effect.
17. **Firefighters** score above non-risk groups, yet in **Levenson (1990)** they are **pro-social** risk takers, **lower** than antisocial and adventurous ones.
18. **16 vs 7 percent:** risky homosexual encounters, high vs low sensation seekers.
19. **SS and neuroticism: no correlation**; phobias and OCD may go with **low** SS. SS goes with E, P (especially disinhibition), openness and, in Dutch data, conscientiousness.
20. **Mauritius (Raine et al., 2002):** high SS at **3** = **12** IQ points higher at **11**; yet **no** better grades.
21. **Occupations:** high-SS men favour **science and helping professions** (psychologist, psychiatrist), not business; high-SS women favour **lawyer** over **elementary school teacher**.
22. **Heritability:** **Eysenck (1983) 58 percent**; **Zuckerman and Sybil Eysenck** twin study **higher**. Birth order: early studies yes (first- and only-borns), **Crozier & Birdsea (2003)** no.
23. **Original experiment:** **dogs**, **mid-1960s**, Seligman's **first day** as a Penn graduate student; tone-shock then **shuttle box**; **Overmier & Seligman (1967)**, **Seligman & Maier (1967)**.
24. **Helpless dogs** ran **about 30 seconds**, then lay down and never escaped, **even when lured with food**.
25. **Hiroto (1974):** humans, **inescapable noise**, then a **hand-shuttle** box.
26. **132-study meta-analysis:** helplessness effects **stronger in humans** than animals.
27. **Rat tumours (1982):** **no shock 50, escapable 70, inescapable 27** percent rejection; control beats no shock.
28. **Dalla et al. (2008):** helplessness easier to establish in **male** rats.
29. **Pessimistic style for bad events = internal, stable, global** (personal, permanent, pervasive); optimistic = external, unstable, specific; controllability is **not** a dimension.
30. **Undergraduate study:** self-defined failing grade led to depression in **30 percent of optimists, 70 percent of pessimists**.
31. **Table 14.4:** lack of aggression pairs with **introjected hostility**; weight loss and anorexia with **loss of libido**; **norepinephrine depletion** in both.
32. **Longevity:** nuns' writing in their **early 20s**, death rate **60 years** later; **19 percent** greater longevity over **30 years**; pessimists **twice** the infections in a year.
33. **Explanatory style** is consistent by **about age 8**; parents' **education** predicted optimism in **India**.
34. **Happiness:** attractive people **are** happier (11th edition); the **U-curve** (low near **50**) holds in the U.S. sample of **300,000+** but not everywhere; the **childless** are happier and **unmarried mothers** lowest; **Denmark** ranks highest.
35. **Three kinds of happiness** (pleasant, engaged, meaningful) vs **five pillars** of flourishing (positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, **achievement** = accomplishment); flourishers are **10 to 18 percent**; *Flourish* is **2011** (text says 2012).
36. **Milestones:** *Journal of Happiness Studies* **2000**; *Authentic Happiness* **2002**; *Journal of Positive Psychology* **2006**; Girl Scouts badge **2012**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Limited-domain approach** | Studying a few narrowly defined personality factors, mainly by laboratory and questionnaire research |
| **Global theory** | A theory that tries to explain every aspect of personality, typical of the early clinicians |
| **Locus of control** | Rotter's term for where a person believes control over their reinforcements lies |
| **Internal locus of control** | Belief that one's rewards follow from one's own behaviour and abilities |
| **External locus of control** | Belief that one's rewards are decided by other people, fate or luck |
| **Generalised expectancy** | A broad, cross-situational expectation about how reinforcement works; locus of control is one |
| **I-E Scale** | Rotter's 1966 forced-choice measure with 23 scored internal-versus-external pairs |
| **Children's Nowicki-Strickland Internal-External Scale** | 40-item children's locus-of-control test (1973), with adult and cartoon preschool forms |
| **Health locus of control** | Belief that one's health depends on one's own behaviour rather than on doctors, chance or fate |
| **Collective locus of control** | A shared belief in control held by a working or studying group |
| **Self-efficacy** | Bandura's belief in one's capability to perform what a specific situation requires |
| **Sensation seeking** | Need for varied, novel, complex and intense experience, with willingness to take risks for it |
| **Sensation Seeking Scale (SSS)** | Zuckerman's 40-item forced-choice questionnaire, now Form V, with a children's version |
| **Thrill and adventure seeking** | Seeking physical activities involving speed, danger, novelty and defiance of gravity |
| **Experience seeking** | Seeking novelty through travel, music, art and a nonconformist lifestyle |
| **Disinhibition** | Seeking release in uninhibited social behaviour: risky sex, impulsiveness, aggression |
| **Boredom susceptibility** | Intolerance of routine, repetition and predictable people, with restless discontent |
| **Non-impulsive socialised sensation seeking** | The "good" type, centred on thrill and adventure seeking |
| **Impulsive unsocialised sensation seeking** | The "bad" type: high disinhibition, experience seeking, boredom susceptibility and psychoticism |
| **Egocentric extraversion** | Interest in others only as an audience or source of stimulation |
| **Pro-social, adventurous and antisocial risk takers** | Levenson's three risk-taker types; pro-social ones score lowest on sensation seeking |
| **Primary-process thought** | Freud's term for vivid imagery and fantasy blurring with reality; more common in high sensation seekers |
| **Authoritarianism** | A rigid, prejudiced personality style; higher in low sensation seekers |
| **Tolerance for ambiguity** | Treating unclear situations as challenges rather than threats; low in low sensation seekers |
| **Optimal level of stimulation** | The stimulation level at which a person functions best; invoked to explain first-borns' sensation seeking |
| **Learned helplessness** | A condition produced by perceiving no control over one's environment, shown as giving up |
| **Shuttle box** | Two-compartment box with a low barrier, used to test escape learning |
| **Yoked control** | Classic background: an animal given the same shocks as another but with no control over them |
| **Stress inoculation training** | Coping training that raised dental patients' perceived control and reduced pain |
| **T-cells and NK cells** | Immune cells weakened in helpless rats and in depression, more plentiful in optimists |
| **Explanatory style** | Habitual way of explaining one's lack of control; optimistic styles prevent helplessness, pessimistic ones spread it |
| **Optimistic explanatory style** | Explaining failures as external, unstable and specific |
| **Pessimistic explanatory style** | Explaining failures as internal, stable and global (personal, permanent, pervasive) |
| **Attribution model of learned helplessness** | Helplessness depends on the cause to which a failure is attributed, on the three dimensions |
| **ASQ and CAVE** | Classic background: the Attributional Style Questionnaire, and content analysis of people's own verbatim explanations |
| **Unrealistic optimism** | Belief that good things are far likelier to happen to oneself than to others |
| **Introjected hostility** | Hostility turned against the self; depression's counterpart of the helpless animal's lack of aggression |
| **Norepinephrine depletion** | Shortage of a neurotransmitter found in both learned helplessness and severe depression |
| **Learned optimism** | A taught shift from a pessimistic to an optimistic explanatory style |
| **Positive psychology** | Seligman's movement to study the best of human functioning: happiness, excellence, optimal growth |
| **Subjective well-being** | A cognitive judgement of one's life plus positive moods and emotions |
| **Life satisfaction** | The cognitive, evaluative part of subjective well-being |
| **Hedonic adaptation** | Classic background: getting used to improved circumstances, so happiness returns toward its usual level |
| **Pleasant, engaged and meaningful lives** | Seligman's three kinds of happiness: positive emotion, absorption in activity, service to something larger |
| **Flow** | Classic background: Csikszentmihalyi's absorbed state when challenge matches skill; the engaged life |
| **Flourishing** | Being both happy and excellent in relationships and accomplishments; 10 to 18 percent of people |
| **Five pillars (PERMA)** | Positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, achievement (accomplishment) |
| **Character strengths and virtues** | Classic background: Peterson and Seligman's 24 strengths under six virtues (VIA Inventory) |
