# Chapter 16: Consciousness

**What this chapter is really about.** For most of the twentieth century consciousness was close to a forbidden word in psychology. The behaviourists, John Watson above all, wanted it removed from the science altogether, and they distrusted **introspection** (examining and describing your own thoughts) as unscientific. Eysenck and Keane open Part V of the book, "Broadening horizons", by noting how far the field has swung back. Endel Tulving once told the first author that he judged a cognitive psychology textbook partly by how much space it gave to consciousness; the fourth edition of this very book gave it two pages out of 525, and the sixth edition had grown that to 22. The reason for the change is that the rest of cognitive psychology kept discovering unconscious processes (subliminal perception, automatic processing, implicit memory, possibly unconscious decision making). Once you accept that some processing is conscious and some is not, the obvious next question is what the difference is, and that question is the subject of this chapter.

The chapter asks four questions, one per main section after the introduction. **What is consciousness for?** (Section 16.2: perception, social communication, controlling action, and the long argument about whether "free will" is an illusion, with Libet's readiness potential at its centre.) **How can we measure it?** (Section 16.3: verbal report and its limits, the vegetative-state patients who answered questions with their brain activity, and Lamme's claim that **recurrent processing** is the neural signature of seeing.) **What is the best general theory?** (Section 16.4: the **global workspace** approach of Baars and of Dehaene, tested assumption by assumption.) **Is there one consciousness per person?** (Section 16.5: split-brain patients, Sperry's two minds against Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere **interpreter** and Bayne's switch model.)

For a clinical psychology student this is not an abstract philosophy chapter. Disorders of consciousness (coma, vegetative state, minimally conscious state) are bedside problems; confabulation, the interpreter and anarchic hand are neuropsychology; and the free-will research is regularly dragged into legal and forensic arguments about responsibility. The 7th edition's general stance is cautious throughout: striking findings, then a careful list of why they prove less than the headlines claim. Examiners like exactly that pattern.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 16.1 gives you the vocabulary (content versus level, access versus phenomenal, hard versus easy problems); every later section uses it, so learn the distinctions cold. Section 16.2 is the longest and most quotable, and the free-will material in it is the commonest source of MCQs. Section 16.3 has three separate strands (under-reporting, brain-damaged patients, neural correlates); keep them apart. Section 16.4 is organised around four numbered assumptions of global workspace theory, and the findings are grouped under the same four headings, so learn the four first and hang the studies on them. Section 16.5 sets three theories against two kinds of evidence ("two streams" and "one stream"). There is one "In the real world" box in 16.2 (brain reading and free will) and one in 16.3 (vegetative-state patients); both are high-yield.

## 16.1 Introduction

### Two senses of the word "consciousness": content and level

The first job is to notice that "consciousness" is used in two different senses, a distinction Eysenck and Keane take from **Bor and Seth (2012)**.

**Conscious content** is *what* you are aware of at a given moment: the coffee cup in front of you, the ache in your shoulder, the thought that the exam is next week. The book borrows a dictionary definition from **Colman (2001)** that lists the ingredients: experienced perceptions, thoughts and feelings, awareness of the outside world and, in humans, often awareness of oneself. The authors immediately point out the weakness of definitions like this one: they are built out of words such as "experience" and "self-awareness" that are themselves just as hard to pin down as "consciousness", so the definition shifts the puzzle rather than solving it.

**Conscious level** is *how* conscious you are: the overall state, on a scale running from the complete unconsciousness of **coma** to full alert wakefulness. Anaesthesia, deep sleep and the vegetative state all sit low on this scale.

The two are related in one direction. You need some non-zero conscious level before you can have any conscious content at all; nobody in deep coma is aware of a coffee cup. But a high level does not fix the content, which changes from moment to moment while the level stays constant.

This chapter is mostly about **content**, that is, conscious awareness of particular things. Conscious level returns in Section 16.3, where brain-damaged patients are graded by level (coma, vegetative state, minimally conscious state).

> **Trap.** *Content* is what you are aware of; *level* is how awake and aware you are overall. A question describing a patient who moves from coma to the vegetative state is about a change in **level**. A question describing someone who is fully awake but fails to notice a change in a scene (change blindness) is about **content**. Do not let the word "awareness" in a stem push you to the wrong one: both senses use it.

### Two schemes that split conscious content further

Within conscious content, theorists draw a further line, and the chapter gives two versions of it. They overlap in vocabulary, which is where students go wrong.

**Block's scheme (e.g., Block, 2012): access versus phenomenal consciousness.**

- **Access consciousness** is the part of consciousness you can *report*, and whose contents are *available* to other cognitive processes such as attention, memory, reasoning and the control of speech. If you can tell someone about it, act on it, or remember it, it was access-conscious.
- **Phenomenal consciousness** is raw, private experience: what it is *like* to see red or to feel pain, independent of whether you can report it.

The importance of the distinction becomes clear in Section 16.3: Block argues that phenomenal consciousness is *richer* than access consciousness, so we experience more than we can ever report.

**Baumeister and Masicampo's scheme (2010): basic phenomenal consciousness versus higher-level consciousness.**

- Their **phenomenal consciousness** is the *basic* form: feelings, sensations and being oriented to the present moment. Many other species probably share it.
- Their **higher form of consciousness** involves reasoning, reflecting on one's own experiences, and having a sense of self that extends beyond the present moment into the past and future. They suggest it is probably unavailable to other species, though the book notes that definitive evidence is lacking.

**Pinker (1997)** gives the classic illustration of the higher form: it is one thing to feel pain and see red, and another to think, in effect, "here I am, Steve Pinker, feeling pain and seeing red". The second is consciousness that has turned on itself.

| Scheme | First type | Second type | What divides them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Block (2012)** | Access consciousness: reportable, usable by other processes | Phenomenal consciousness: raw private experience | Reportability and availability |
| **Baumeister & Masicampo (2010)** | Phenomenal (basic): sensations, feelings, the present moment | Higher-level: reasoning, reflection, extended self | Self-reflection and time beyond the here-and-now |

> **Trap.** The word **phenomenal** appears in both schemes but is contrasted with different things. In **Block's** scheme phenomenal consciousness is set against *access* (reportable) consciousness, and the argument is about whether experience outruns report. In **Baumeister and Masicampo's** scheme phenomenal consciousness is the *basic* level set against a *higher*, self-reflective level, and the argument is about levels of sophistication. If an option pairs "phenomenal versus access" with Baumeister, or "basic versus higher" with Block, it is the distractor.

### The hard problem and the easy problems

The last distinction in the introduction comes from **Chalmers (2007)**.

The **hard problem** is the problem of *subjective experience* itself: why does physical processing in the brain give rise to an inner life at all, and how? We know that experience arises from a physical basis, but nobody has a good explanation of why it should. The book is frank that cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience have shed little light on this. In terms of the previous section, the hard problem is the problem of phenomenal consciousness.

The **easy problems** are the problems of explaining the *functions* associated with consciousness: how we discriminate and categorise stimuli, how information is integrated, how we access our own internal states, and how behaviour is deliberately controlled. Chalmers called these "easy" only in comparison. Eysenck and Keane stress that they are in fact extremely difficult; the point is that they are the kind of problem the ordinary methods of cognitive science can make progress on, and much progress has been made.

> **Trap.** "Easy problems" does not mean simple or solved. It means *tractable by standard methods* (mechanisms of discrimination, integration, access, control), in contrast to the hard problem of why any of that is accompanied by experience. Almost everything in the rest of this chapter (recurrent processing, global workspace, the interpreter) is work on the easy problems. A stem claiming that global workspace theory "solves the hard problem" is wrong by the book's own account.

### Where these distinctions return

The access/phenomenal split drives the under-reporting debate in 16.3; conscious *level* organises the brain-damage material in the same section; global workspace theory (16.4) is essentially a theory of how content becomes *globally available*, close to Block's access; and 16.5 asks whether a split-brain patient has one stream of conscious content or two.

## 16.2 Functions of Consciousness

### The functions most researchers agree on

What is consciousness *for*? The book admits this is controversial but identifies five functions on which there is reasonable agreement. Learn them as a list, because "which of the following is NOT a function claimed for consciousness" is an easy question to set.

1. **Perceiving the environment.** Conscious awareness accompanies our perception of the world around us.
2. **Social communication and understanding others.** Consciousness is crucial for working out what other people are thinking and for communicating with them (developed below).
3. **Controlling action.** It seems to play a part in what we decide to do (developed below, and heavily contested).
4. **Thinking beyond the here-and-now.** Consciousness lets us think about events and issues far removed from the present situation. This is an everyday occurrence: **Kane et al. (2007)** found that people's conscious thoughts wander away from what they are currently doing about **30% of the time** on average.
5. **Integrating information.** **Tononi and Koch (2008)** argued that the most important property of consciousness is that it is extraordinarily informative. Their reasoning is worth spelling out. Whenever you are in one particular conscious state (seeing *this* red apple on *this* table in *this* light), you are thereby *not* in an astronomically large number of other possible states. Each experience rules out a vast range of alternatives, and that is what makes it informative. Being informative in that way requires many types of information (colour, shape, location, meaning) to be combined into one state, so on this view consciousness is fundamentally about **integration**.

Keep function 5 in mind; it returns twice. Lamme's account of recurrent processing (Section 16.3) and the global workspace theory (Section 16.4) both make integration the hallmark of conscious processing.

> **Trap.** The **30%** mind-wandering figure belongs to **Kane et al. (2007)**, and it supports the *thinking beyond the here-and-now* function, not the integration function. The "extraordinarily informative" argument belongs to **Tononi and Koch (2008)**. These two are easy to cross-wire because they sit next to each other in the list.

### How much can unconscious processes do?

The question of what consciousness adds only makes sense against the background of what can be done *without* it, and here opinion has swung widely.

One long-standing view is that unconscious processes are of **limited value** and **inflexible**: they can run well-practised routines but cannot cope with novelty (the automatic processing material of Chapter 5 is in this tradition). At the other extreme stands **Sigmund Freud**, who famously credited the unconscious with enormous power over behaviour. The book's own position, built up across many earlier chapters, is that unconscious processes clearly do a great deal: perceptual processes (Chapter 2), learning (Chapter 6) and memory (Chapter 7) can all operate without awareness, and so possibly, though this is contested, can decision making (Chapter 13).

**Hassin (2013)** pushed the argument as far as it can go with what he called the **"Yes It Can" principle**: unconscious processes can carry out the same *high-level* cognitive functions as conscious ones, including goal pursuit, cognitive control and reasoning.

> **Key study, Marien et al. (2012).** Participants did a **proofreading task**, searching a text for errors. In one condition the researchers planted an unconscious goal of **socialisation** by flashing socialisation-related words (socialisation, partying, dancing, celebrating) below the threshold of awareness. The unconscious goal **impaired proofreading**, but only in participants for whom socialising was an **important goal**. In the figure, error-detection scores closer to zero mean better performance, and the high-importance group in the unconscious-goal condition was the one that suffered.

The study fits Hassin's principle in a specific way: a goal the participants never consciously adopted competed with the task they *had* consciously adopted, which is just what a conscious goal would do. The interaction with goal importance matters too. It shows the effect was not a blanket disruption from flashing words but depended on how strongly the person valued the goal.

**The book's caution.** Eysenck and Keane do not endorse the strong form of "Yes It Can". There is good evidence that many cognitive functions can occur unconsciously, but in each case the unconscious version is **much more limited in scope** than the conscious one. "Can occur unconsciously" is not the same as "can occur unconsciously to the same extent".

### The sceptics' case: consciousness as after-the-fact storytelling

A popular view among those who doubt that consciousness does much is that it often supplies a **misleading interpretation or rationalisation** of events *after* they have happened. On this view consciousness is less a pilot than a press office.

> **Key study, Nisbett and Wilson (1977).** Participants judged which of **five essentially identical pairs of stockings** was the best quality. Most chose the **rightmost** pair, so their choice was being driven by **spatial position**. Asked why they had chosen it, they cited small differences in colour, texture and so on, and when asked directly whether position had influenced them they **vehemently denied it**.

The lesson is not that people lie. They genuinely did not know the real cause of their choice, and their conscious explanation was a plausible story constructed afterwards. Hold on to this study: it is the everyday-life version of what Gazzaniga's split-brain interpreter does in Section 16.5, where the left hemisphere confidently explains actions it did not initiate.

> **Trap.** Nisbett and Wilson's participants chose the **rightmost** pair (a position effect), and they *denied* position mattered. A stem saying participants "correctly identified position as the reason" or "chose the leftmost pair" is wrong on both counts. Classic background: the 1977 paper is widely known by its title, "Telling more than we can know", which captures the point that people report reasons they have no real access to.

The book now turns to two of the major proposed functions of consciousness in detail: social communication first, then the control of action.

### Social communication

#### Humphrey: consciousness evolved for social life

**Humphrey (1983, 2002)** argued that the *main* function of consciousness is **social**. The argument is evolutionary. Humans have lived in social groups for tens of thousands of years, and survival in a group depends on predicting, understanding and manipulating what others will do. That task becomes far easier if you can imagine yourself in the other person's position. So, on Humphrey's account, humans developed conscious awareness of *themselves*, and that self-awareness was what let them model *others*.

#### Graziano and Kastner: awareness as a social model of attention

**Graziano and Kastner (2011)** made the social link even tighter. Their central claim is that the brain machinery that computes *other people's* awareness is the same machinery that computes *our own*. On their view, awareness is your social intelligence perceiving where your own attention is focused.

Their figure (Figure 16.2) makes this concrete with two people, **Abel** and **Bill**. Abel watches Bill and, using mechanisms specialised for social perception, builds a model of Bill's mental state that includes the attribution "Bill is aware of the cup". The theory's twist is that the same social-perception mechanisms can be turned inward, so that Abel's own awareness is his brain's model of where Abel's attention is pointed.

Laid out as a sequence:

1. Social-perception machinery evolved to track what *other* people are attending to.
2. That machinery represents another person's focus of attention as their "awareness" of something (Bill is aware of the cup).
3. The same machinery, applied to oneself, represents one's own focus of attention: that representation *is* one's own awareness.
4. An evolutionary implication follows: awareness may have developed first for understanding others, with self-awareness coming later.

**Where in the brain.** Graziano and Kastner located this social machinery in the **temporo-parietal junction (TPJ)**, the region where the temporal and parietal lobes meet. The book gives two lines of support.

- **Sowden and Catmur (2014)** applied **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** to the TPJ, temporarily disrupting it. People became worse at **switching between representations of the self and representations of another person**. If one region handles both self and other, interfering with it should blur the boundary between them, and it did.
- **Out-of-body experiences.** If the same mechanism perceives awareness in others and in ourselves, it should occasionally get confused about whose awareness, and from where, it is representing. That is roughly what an out-of-body experience is: a vivid sense that one's consciousness has detached from the body and is viewing it from a remote position (Blanke et al., 2002, defined it in these terms). When **Blanke et al. (2002)** electrically stimulated the TPJ of a **43-year-old woman**, she reported out-of-body experiences, such as seeing herself lying in bed from above.

> **Trap.** The region linked to self/other representation, Graziano and Kastner's social machinery, and out-of-body experiences in this chapter is the **temporo-parietal junction**, not the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate or the supplementary motor area (those belong to the global workspace and Libet material). The **43-year-old woman** was stimulated *electrically* (Blanke et al., 2002); the self/other switching study used *TMS* (Sowden & Catmur, 2014).

#### Conscious thought is for talking

There is a second, more down-to-earth route from consciousness to social communication. **Baumeister et al. (2011a)** pointed out that many researchers *operationally define* a conscious thought as a thought the person can report to someone else. They turned the definition into a functional claim: perhaps the very purpose of conscious thought is to let people tell their thoughts to one another. The book's summary phrase is that much of conscious thinking is **for talking**. Note how neatly this connects with Block's "access consciousness" in 16.1: what is reportable is what is shareable.

### Controlling our actions?

The question mark in the book's heading is deliberate. Here the chapter takes on **free will**.

#### The everyday intuition and the sceptical tradition

Many times a day we think of doing something and then find ourselves doing it: you think "I'll get a coffee", and a few minutes later you are drinking one. Experience like this strongly suggests that we have **free will**, which **Aarts and van den Bos (2011)** defined as the ability to make choices and determine one's own outcomes free from constraints. The book's key-term box puts it more simply: the notion that we freely or voluntarily choose what to do from among various options.

The sceptical tradition is old.

- **Thomas Huxley (1874)** proposed the **"steam whistle hypothesis"**. Conscious thought, he said, is like the whistle on a steam locomotive. The whistle tells you something about what is happening inside the engine, but it does nothing to move the train. Consciousness, likewise, would be a by-product that reports on the brain's activity without causing any of it. Classic background: this position is usually called **epiphenomenalism** (consciousness as an epiphenomenon, a side effect with no causal power).
- **Wegner (2003)** made the modern version of the argument: we have only the **illusion of conscious will**. Our actions are really caused by unconscious processes, but we wrongly infer that our conscious intentions caused them.

#### Wegner's three principles of apparent mental causation

Wegner's theory specifies *when* we will experience an action as willed. Three conditions jointly produce the feeling of authorship:

| Principle | What it requires | Example (thinking "coffee", then getting coffee) |
|---|---|---|
| **Priority** | The thought occurs in consciousness *just before* the action | The coffee thought came first, the walk to the café second |
| **Consistency** | The thought *matches* the action | The thought was about coffee, and the action was getting coffee |
| **Exclusivity** | There is *no conspicuous alternative cause* of the action | Nobody pushed you, nobody else ordered for you |

When all three are met, we experience conscious will and claim authorship of the action. The theory's radical implication is that these are *inference rules*, not a read-out of causation, so they can be fooled: arrange for the right thought to occur just before an action you did not cause, with no obvious rival cause, and you should feel you caused it.

> **Trap.** Wegner's three principles are **priority, consistency and exclusivity**. Do not mix them up with the social psychology triad from Kelley's attribution theory (consensus, **consistency**, distinctiveness); the shared word "consistency" is what makes the swap tempting. And remember what Wegner's principles explain: the *experience* of will, which he claims can arise even when the conscious thought was not the cause.

The research on whether consciousness controls action falls into two groups: behavioural studies and cognitive neuroscience.

---
### Findings: behavioural research

#### Evidence that conscious intentions do shape behaviour

**Baumeister et al. (2011b)** took the opposite side to Wegner. They accepted that conscious thoughts are often *preceded* by unconscious processes, but argued that behaviour nonetheless generally depends crucially on those conscious thoughts. They reviewed a large body of evidence; the book selects implementation intentions as its example.

**Gollwitzer (1999)** studied **implementation intentions**: plans that spell out *when*, *where* and *how* you will make the response that leads to a goal. The contrast is with a plain **goal intention**. "I will pass my exam" is a goal intention. "When I get home from college at 5 pm, I will sit at my desk and do one past paper before switching on my phone" is an implementation intention. Gollwitzer's idea was that a conscious implementation intention creates an **"instant habit"**: the specified situation becomes a cue that triggers the planned behaviour almost automatically, which makes the goal easier to reach and protects you from distraction by irrelevant activities.

> **Key study, Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006).** A **meta-analysis** (a statistical combination of many studies) of implementation-intention research. Goal achievement was **much more likely** with implementation intentions than with less specific goal intentions such as "I will achieve my goal". Classic background: the meta-analysis pooled 94 independent tests and found an effect of medium-to-large size (d of about 0.65).

**What it shows, and what it leaves open.** The result suggests that conscious intentions can influence behaviour. The book's reservation is about causal precision: in these studies there is usually a **long delay** between the conscious manipulation of the intention and the later behaviour, so many other factors could intervene, and it is hard to pin down exactly what caused the improvement.

**Habits as the enemy of goals.** Our goals are often undermined by habits. The book's example is a student who habitually turns on the television at exactly the time he or she should be studying for an important examination. **Adriaanse et al. (2011)** found that implementation intentions made the **alternatives to the habitual response** more consciously accessible, and so reduced habitual responding. That is a direct, clinically useful mechanism: the plan works by bringing the competing response into awareness at the moment the old habit would otherwise run.

#### Evidence that the feeling of will can be manufactured

> **Key study, Wegner and Wheatley (1999).** Two people sat together with their fingers resting on a small board. Moving the board moved a **cursor** around a screen showing many pictures of small objects. Roughly **every 30 seconds** they were told to stop the cursor and to rate whether they had **consciously intended** it to stop where it did. Both wore headphones. One of the pair was a genuine participant; the other was a **confederate** of the experimenter. The genuine participant believed both were hearing different words through their headphones, but in fact the confederate was receiving instructions to make particular movements. On critical trials the confederate was told to stop on a particular object (for example, a cat), and the genuine participant heard the word "cat" either **30 seconds before, 5 seconds before, 1 second before, or 1 second after** the stop.

**The result.** When the genuine participants heard the object's name **one or five seconds beforehand**, they wrongly believed they had caused the cursor to stop there.

**Why it supports Wegner.** Map it onto the three principles. Hearing "cat" just before the stop gave **priority**. The word matched the object the cursor stopped on, giving **consistency**. And because both people's fingers were on the board, there was no conspicuous alternative cause, giving **exclusivity**. With all three satisfied, participants experienced a will they had not exercised. Notice how the timing conditions act as controls. At 30 seconds before, the thought is too early to count as "just before"; at 1 second after, it comes too late. Those conditions did not produce the illusion, which is what the priority principle predicts.

> **Key study, van der Weiden et al. (2013).** Participants watched a rapid sequence of word strings and pressed a key to stop it in response to a stop cue. Unknown to them, the computer, not their keypress, determined the word on which the sequence stopped (for example, "glass"). Their sense of **self-agency** (the belief that they had caused the outcome) was high when the stopped word matched their **goal**, that is, the word they had intended to stop on. It was also high when the stopped word matched a **prime presented below conscious awareness**.

The second finding is the striking one. A thought the participant never consciously had still inflated the sense of authorship. So people can be misled about agency in more than one way: by a matching conscious goal and by a matching unconscious prime.

#### Limitations of the behavioural research

The book raises two problems.

1. **The effects are weak.** In Wegner and Wheatley (1999), only **60%** of participants believed their conscious intention had moved the cursor *even when all three principles applied*. The illusion is real but far from overwhelming.
2. **The set-ups are artificial.** The experiments were deliberately designed to make it hard for participants to realise that their intentions had not caused the outcome. The book's analogy is that nobody would conclude that visual perception is hopelessly unreliable just because we misidentify objects in thick fog. Showing that a system can be fooled in contrived conditions does not show that it is usually wrong.

> **Trap.** The **60%** figure is the proportion of Wegner and Wheatley's participants who *did* feel they had caused the stop under the most favourable conditions. It is offered by Eysenck and Keane as a **limitation** (the effect is weak), not as strong evidence for the illusion of will. And the illusion appeared with the word heard **1 or 5 seconds before**, not 30 seconds before or 1 second after.

### Findings: cognitive neuroscience

#### Libet et al. (1983): the readiness potential

This is the single most examined study in the chapter.

> **Key study, Libet et al. (1983).** Participants flexed their **wrist and fingers** whenever they chose to, and indicated *when* they first became consciously aware of the intention to move. Meanwhile the researchers recorded **event-related potentials (ERPs)** to measure the **readiness potential**, a slow build-up of electrical activity thought to reflect the brain's pre-planning of a bodily movement.

**The three time points**, which you must be able to place in order:

| Event | Timing |
|---|---|
| Readiness potential begins | About **350 ms before** the reported moment of conscious intention |
| Conscious awareness of the intention | About **200 ms before** the movement |
| Actual movement | Time zero |

Adding the two intervals, the readiness potential starts roughly 550 ms before the movement itself. The superficial reading is dramatic: much of the processing involved in deciding *when* to move seems to happen before the person is aware of having decided, so the conscious intention looks more like a late report than a cause.

> **Trap.** Direction and numbers. The readiness potential comes **first**, about **350 ms before awareness**; awareness comes about **200 ms before movement**. A stem that says awareness preceded the readiness potential, or gives 200 ms as the gap between readiness potential and awareness, has swapped the two intervals. Classic background: Libet himself argued that consciousness might retain a "veto" over a movement already being prepared (sometimes called "free won't"); the 7th edition does not discuss the veto, so treat it as background only.

#### Why the timing of a conscious intention is hard to measure

The weakness in Libet's method is the self-report of *when* the intention was felt. **Haggard (2005)** observed that the conscious experience of intending is thin and elusive (his words were "quite thin and evasive"). If the experience itself is faint and fleeting, the timing people give for it will be imprecise and open to influence by other cues.

> **Key study, Banks and Isham (2009).** Some participants saw their own hand movement on a **video delayed by 120 ms**. In that condition, the decision time they reported was **44 ms later** than when their hand was in full view.

**What it shows.** If people were reading off an internal timestamp of their decision, seeing the movement later should not change it. It did, which means the reported time of the "decision" is partly *reconstructed* from the perceived time of the movement. That undermines the precision on which Libet's 350 ms argument rests.

### In the real world: brain reading and free will

Libet's study had two limitations that later researchers tried to fix.

1. The readiness potential it measured comes from the **supplementary motor area** and reflects the **final stages of getting a movement ready**. The higher-level decision processes that might come earlier were never examined.
2. It studied only **"when" decisions** (when shall I move my hand?) and ignored **"what" decisions** (which movement shall I make?).

> **Key study, Soon et al. (2008).** Participants decided freely whether to press a button with their **left or right index finger**, and brain activity was recorded in **prefrontal and parietal cortex**, areas associated with decision making. Pattern analysis of activity in **frontopolar cortex (BA10)** and part of the **parietal cortex** predicted which finger would be chosen as much as **seven seconds** ahead of the moment participants said they had made up their minds. In addition, activity in the **supplementary and pre-supplementary motor areas** about **five seconds** before awareness predicted the **timing** of the response.

**What it shows.** Two things. First, decision-related information appears in the brain long before awareness, much earlier than Libet's few hundred milliseconds. Second, the "what" decision (left or right) could be read out *earlier* (about 7 s) than the "when" decision (about 5 s), suggesting that processing of what to do comes before processing of when to do it.

> **Key study, Bode et al. (2011).** A near-replication of Soon et al. with a **more powerful scanner** giving better temporal and spatial resolution. Participants again chose between the left and right index finger. Guided by Soon et al.'s findings, the analysis focused on **anterior frontopolar cortex**. Decisions could be predicted from activity in **left frontopolar cortex** up to **7.5 seconds** before awareness. However, prediction accuracy was **rarely above 55%** except very shortly before the conscious decision, where **chance is 50%** (Figure 16.3 plots decoding accuracy against time, with times before awareness shown as negative numbers).

**The problem of unreported thoughts.** Participants signalled only the moment they reached a *conscious decision*. Weaker decision-relevant thoughts on the way there, such as a slight leaning towards the left hand, were probably never recorded. **Fahle et al. (2011)** argued that a convincing demonstration that brain activity predicts later conscious decisions would need to take such thoughts into account. Otherwise the "unconscious" brain activity at minus seven seconds may simply be the neural trace of a faint conscious preference that nobody asked about.

> **Trap.** Do not attribute the seconds-long lead to **Libet**: his gap was a few hundred milliseconds and concerned only *when* to move. The **7 s** (which finger, frontopolar BA10) and **5 s** (when, SMA/pre-SMA) figures are **Soon et al. (2008)**; the **7.5 s** figure with near-chance accuracy is **Bode et al. (2011)**. In Soon et al. the *what* signal appears **earlier** than the *when* signal, not later.

### Overall evaluation of the free-will research

Several strands of research appear to converge on a counter-intuitive conclusion: at least some of our decisions are prepared **preconsciously**, before we are aware of making them. Eysenck and Keane urge real caution before accepting it, for two reasons.

**First, the research is narrow in scope.**

- The behavioural studies of **Wegner and Wheatley (1999)** and **van der Weiden et al. (2013)** used highly artificial situations.
- In the tasks of **Soon et al. (2008)** and **Bode et al. (2011)** there was **no reason at all** to prefer the chosen response to the other one, a point made by **Mele (2013)**. Being told to respond unpredictably and at random probably reduces conscious deliberation before the decision to a minimum, because participants fall back on what Mele called "automatic tie-breaking mechanisms".
- Real decisions that matter are quite different. People typically think long and consciously before deciding whether to marry someone or whether to commit a crime. Findings from arbitrary left-or-right finger choices may simply not generalise to those.

**Second, the neuroscience is less informative about free will than is often claimed.**

- In **Bode et al. (2011)**, prediction from earlier brain activity was **only slightly above chance**. That leaves plenty of room for conscious processing to have played a very important part in determining what participants did.
- More fundamentally, early brain activity and conscious processing are **not mutually exclusive** causes. An action could depend on *both*: early preparation that biases the outcome and conscious processing that settles it. Showing that the first exists does not show that the second is idle.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of the brain-reading research on free will?" Genuine limitations in the book: artificial tasks; arbitrary choices with no reason to prefer either option (Mele, 2013); "automatic tie-breaking"; prediction accuracy barely above chance (Bode et al., 2011); unrecorded decision-relevant thoughts (Fahle et al., 2011); early brain activity and conscious processing not mutually exclusive; Libet's focus on late motor preparation and on *when* only. A distractor such as "the samples were all patients with frontal damage" or "the studies used no brain imaging" is not on the list.

## 16.3 Assessing Consciousness and Conscious Experience

### Behavioural measures and their two problems

How do you find out whether someone is conscious of something? By far the commonest answer has been to use **behavioural measures**. You can ask people to describe what they saw (a **verbal report**), or ask them to make a **yes/no decision** about whether a target object was present. Both are behavioural, and both have problems.

**Problem 1: report may be poorer than experience.** **Lamme (2010)** argued that our actual conscious experience is often much *richer* than what we report about it. His reasoning (Lamme, 2006) was that you cannot know whether you have had a conscious experience without using other cognitive functions, such as **attention**, **memory** or **inner speech**, to get at it. Any limit on those intervening processes will therefore show up as a limit on report, even if the experience itself was complete. If Lamme is right, verbal reports are a bottleneck and may often be a very inadequate measure of what someone experienced.

**Problem 2: different behavioural measures disagree.** The book's example comes from subliminal perception (Chapter 2). Observers sometimes show "awareness" of a stimulus when forced to make a choice about it (for example, guessing which of two locations it appeared in), yet say they saw nothing when asked to report their experience.

| Threshold | How it is measured | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective threshold** | Forced-choice decisions about the stimulus | Whether information about the stimulus is available to guide performance |
| **Subjective threshold** | The person's own report of their experience | Whether the person thinks they saw it |

Between the two lies a zone where performance is above chance but the person reports no awareness. Which measure is the "real" index of consciousness? Behavioural methods alone cannot settle that.

> **Trap.** The **objective** threshold is defined by **forced-choice performance**; the **subjective** threshold by **self-report**. Stimuli can be above the objective threshold (performance better than chance) while below the subjective threshold (reported as unseen). Reversing the two labels is the standard error.

### The neural-correlates alternative

A growing number of researchers (e.g., Lamme, 2010; van Gaal & Lamme, 2012) want to lean more heavily on the **neural correlates of consciousness**. The working method is to take a behavioural index of awareness and ask which patterns of brain activity go along with it.

The book identifies two advantages claimed for this approach.

1. **Comparison is informative in itself.** Setting behavioural and neuroimaging measures side by side, and seeing where they agree and disagree, is theoretically important.
2. **A purer measure.** Neuroimaging might give a more **direct** assessment of consciousness, not contaminated by the extra processes (attention, memory) that stand between experience and report.

Keep the second claim in view, because the chapter's final evaluation of this approach (at the end of this section) questions it.

### Under-reporting of conscious experience?

#### Sperling (1960): seeing more than you can say

The classic evidence that reports underestimate experience comes from **Sperling (1960)**, already met in Chapter 6.

> **Key study, Sperling (1960).** A visual array of **three rows of four letters** (12 letters in all) was flashed for **50 ms**. Asked to report the whole array, participants could usually name only **four to five letters**, yet they insisted they had seen many more. Sperling's explanation was that the visual information faded before most of it could be reported. To test this he asked for **partial report**: a cue after the display told participants which part of the array to recall. As predicted, partial recall was **high**, provided the cue came **very soon after the display ended**.

**What it shows.** Because participants did not know in advance which row would be cued, high partial report means that most of the array must have been briefly available. The bottleneck lay in getting information *out* before it decayed, not in what had been taken in. Classic background: Sperling's cue was a tone (high, medium or low pitch for top, middle or bottom row); performance of about three letters per cued row implies that roughly 9 of the 12 letters were briefly available, and the advantage fades within about a second as the brief visual store (iconic memory) decays.

#### Lamme's change-detection version

**Lamme (2010)** described several experiments with the same logic. An array of objects is shown briefly, followed by a **blank interval of several seconds**, and then the array reappears with a cue pointing at one object; the observer judges whether that object has **changed**. With the cue arriving only with the second array, performance is **poor**. But if the cue appears within about **two seconds** after the first array disappears (that is, during the blank interval, before the second array arrives), performance is **very good**. So observers retain access, possibly conscious access, to a substantial amount of information for a while after the first array has gone.

#### Block: phenomenal consciousness overflows access

**Block (2012)** used evidence of this kind to support his distinction from Section 16.1. Only **access consciousness** can be reported. The reason we feel our experience is richer than our report is that **phenomenal consciousness is more extensive than access consciousness**: we experience the whole Sperling array phenomenally, but only four or five letters make it into access consciousness before the rest fade.

#### Kouider: the richness is an illusion

**Kouider et al. (e.g., 2010, 2012)** took the opposite view. On their account there is **no phenomenal consciousness separate from access consciousness**, and our impression that experience is very rich is largely **illusory**.

Two lines of evidence support them.

- **Change blindness**, the inability to detect a changed aspect of the visual scene (Chapter 4). People are poor at spotting such changes, and most people **substantially overestimate** how well they would avoid change blindness (**Levin et al., 2002**). If experience really were as detailed as it feels, changes should be easy to see.
- **Expectations fill in the gaps.** What observers claim to experience is shaped by what they *expect* as well as by the visual input itself.

> **Key study, de Gardelle et al. (2009).** A modified Sperling task. Participants expected letters, but on some trials **pseudo-letters** (real letters **rotated and flipped**) were shown. Participants rarely noticed the pseudo-letters and believed that only real letters had been presented.

**What it shows.** The "rich" experience of the unreported part of the array was not a faithful record of what was there; it was partly filled in by the expectation of letters. Conscious experience was less accurate and less detailed than participants believed.

#### The book's conclusion on under-reporting

Most people believe their conscious visual experience is much richer than they can report. Eysenck and Keane judge it **likely, but not certain,** that this richness is **illusory** and depends on **top-down processes** such as expectation. Their conclusion is therefore the reverse of the "under-reporting" claim: people's self-reports of visual experience are more likely to involve **misreporting** than **under-reporting**.

> **Trap.** Sperling's data are used on *both* sides. **Block** reads them as phenomenal consciousness overflowing access (we really see more than we report). **Kouider** and the 7th edition's conclusion read the impression of richness as largely an **illusion** produced by expectation (de Gardelle et al., 2009) and over-confidence (Levin et al., 2002). If a question asks for the book's conclusion, it is "misreporting more than under-reporting", not "experience is richer than report".

### Consciousness in brain-damaged patients

#### Three degrees of degraded consciousness

Here the chapter switches from conscious content to conscious **level**. Three stages of degraded consciousness are recognised in brain-damaged patients, from most to least severe:

| State | Wakefulness | Awareness | Key feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coma** | Absent | Absent | No awareness and no wakefulness |
| **Vegetative state** | Present | Apparently absent | "Wakefulness without conscious awareness" |
| **Minimally conscious state** | Present | Some evidence | Some, often inconsistent, signs of consciousness |

The **vegetative state** has attracted most research. In the definition the book takes from **Owen and Coleman (2008)**, the patient shows no awareness of self or environment, no response to stimuli that suggests purpose or volition, and no evidence of understanding or producing language. The key-term box condenses this: brain damage has left the patient partly awake, yet there seems to be no awareness and no purposeful behaviour. The crucial word is *seems*. By definition, **behavioural** measures give no evidence of awareness in these patients. But neuroimaging studies have now shown that at least some vegetative-state patients do have some conscious awareness while performing cognitive tasks (reviewed by **Owen, 2013**).

> **Trap.** The vegetative state is **wakefulness without awareness**; coma is **neither**; the minimally conscious state shows **some** awareness. A patient whose eyes open and who has sleep-wake cycles but who never follows a command is described by the vegetative state, not coma. Classic background: do not confuse any of these with **locked-in syndrome**, in which the patient is fully conscious and aware but almost entirely paralysed (often able to move only the eyes); and note that older papers speak of the "persistent vegetative state", while some newer clinical writing uses the label "unresponsive wakefulness syndrome" for the same condition.

### In the real world: vegetative-state patients

#### Why active tasks are needed

Which tasks could show that a vegetative-state patient is conscious? **Owen (2013)** emphasised **active tasks**, in which the patient cannot respond automatically but must *generate* the response. The logic is worth stating. A brain can respond automatically to its own name or to speech without any awareness, so passive responses prove little. A pattern of activity that appears only because the patient *chose* to follow an instruction, and holds it for many seconds, is much harder to explain without consciousness.

#### The studies

> **Key study, Owen et al. (2006).** A **23-year-old woman** in a vegetative state after a very serious **road accident** was asked to imagine either **playing tennis** or **visiting the rooms of her house**. The two imagery tasks produced **different patterns of brain activity**; imagining tennis, for example, activated the **supplementary motor area**. Her patterns were **very similar to those of healthy participants** doing the same tasks.

Classic background: Owen et al. used fMRI, and in healthy people imagined spatial navigation through a house characteristically engages regions such as the parahippocampal gyrus and posterior parietal cortex, which is why the two tasks give separable patterns.

> **Key study, Monti et al. (2010).** A vegetative-state patient was asked **yes/no questions** such as "Is your father's name Thomas?". He had been instructed to imagine **playing tennis** for "**Yes**" and to imagine **navigating his house** for "**No**". His brain-activity patterns **matched the correct answers**.

This is a step beyond Owen et al. (2006): the imagery is now being used as a **communication code**, and the answers depend on autobiographical knowledge the experimenters could check. Classic background: this patient was one of a larger sample of 54 patients with disorders of consciousness, only a handful of whom could wilfully modulate their brain activity.

> **Key study, Cruse et al. (2011).** **16 vegetative-state patients** were tested on two commands, "squeeze your right hand" and "squeeze your toes". In healthy people these produce activity over the **hand** and **toe** motor areas respectively. **Three** of the 16 patients showed very similar patterns of activation (Figure 16.4 shows focal effects over the hand and toe motor areas in one healthy control and the three patients). The measure here was the **EEG**, specifically changes in particular frequency bands associated with motor imagery.

**Why these findings are more impressive than they first look.** Succeeding on any of these tasks requires a whole set of cognitive processes, not just a flicker of awareness:

1. **Sustained attention**, to keep imagining for the required period;
2. **Language comprehension**, to understand the instructions;
3. **Working memory**, to remember which task goes with which instruction;
4. **Response selection**, to choose between the two imagery tasks.

> **Key study, Monti et al. (2013).** A patient with a severe disorder of consciousness was shown **superimposed pictures of a face and a house**. In healthy people, attending to the face and attending to the house activate different brain regions. When told to switch attention from face to house or back, the patient showed **very similar** patterns of activation to healthy controls.

**What it adds.** The display never changed. So the change in brain activity was driven by the **patient's intention** to shift attention, not by the stimulus. That is close to a pure demonstration of voluntary, internally generated control.

> **Key study, Coleman et al. (2009).** If neuroimaging validly measures awareness in these patients, it should **predict recovery**. In **41 patients**, neuroimaging signs of consciousness at the start of the study **did not correlate** with the behavioural assessment made at the same time. But, as predicted, they correlated **highly** with **behavioural recovery six months later**.

The pattern is what matters. If the imaging measure were just a noisier version of the bedside behavioural measure, it should have agreed with it at the start. Instead, it disagreed with the bedside measure but predicted the future, which suggests it was picking up awareness that behaviour could not yet show.

#### What the vegetative-state studies show

Taken together, vegetative-state patients have displayed several processes normally thought to need consciousness: **language comprehension, sustained attention, switching attention, use of imagery and question answering**. And the patients with the most neuroimaging evidence of consciousness generally make the greatest behavioural recovery afterwards.

#### Limitations

1. **Most patients show nothing.** In the Cruse et al. (2011) study, **81%** of vegetative-state patients (13 of 16) showed no sign of consciousness on the neuroimaging tasks. Nobody knows whether this means they are truly unaware or whether the measures are simply too insensitive. A negative result cannot be read as proof of absent awareness.
2. **We know little about their inner state.** Even for patients who do show signs of consciousness, much remains unknown. **Owen (2013)** put it as a list of unanswered questions: whether such patients are depressed, whether they are in pain, whether they want to live or die. We cannot presume to know.

> **Trap.** Imaging method is a classic distractor: the tennis/house imagery studies (**Owen et al., 2006**; **Monti et al., 2010**) used **fMRI**, while the hand/toe command study (**Cruse et al., 2011**) used **EEG**. And keep the two Monti studies apart: **2010** is yes/no **communication** by imagery, **2013** is **attention switching** between superimposed faces and houses.

### Neural correlates of consciousness

#### The basic method

Research on the **neural correlates of consciousness** has grown rapidly. The core design is a contrast: find conditions in which participants are and are not consciously aware of a stimulus (while keeping the stimulus as similar as possible), and compare the brain activity in the two. Whatever differs is a candidate correlate of awareness.

#### Lamme's theory: feedforward sweep versus recurrent processing

**Lamme (e.g., 2010)**, as noted earlier, argues that it may be better to *define* consciousness in neural rather than behavioural terms. He concentrates on **visual consciousness**, the conscious experience of visual objects, and his theory distinguishes two phases of processing.

**Phase 1: the feedforward sweep.** When a visual stimulus appears, it is processed extremely rapidly, and essentially automatically, at successive levels of the visual cortex, starting in early visual cortex and moving up to higher areas (Chapter 2). This fast **feedforward sweep** is complete within about **100 to 150 ms**.

**Phase 2: recurrent processing.** The sweep is typically followed by **recurrent processing**, a form of top-down processing in which **higher areas feed back to lower areas**, setting up extensive interactions among many regions.

**The claim.** Lamme's link to consciousness is direct: **recurrent processing is accompanied by conscious experience; the feedforward sweep is not.**

| | Feedforward sweep | Recurrent processing |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Lower to higher visual areas | Higher back to lower areas, with widespread interaction |
| Timing | Within about 100 to 150 ms | After the sweep |
| Character | Fast, essentially automatic, fragmented by feature | Integrated, shaped by top-down influences |
| Consciousness (Lamme) | Not accompanied by conscious experience | Accompanied by conscious experience |

**Why recurrent processing, not the sweep?** The book gives two reasons.

1. **Integration.** The feedforward sweep handles different kinds of information (shape, colour, motion) in a somewhat **fragmented** way, each in its own stream. Recurrent processing **integrates** them. Since integration is what makes conscious experience so informative (Tononi & Koch, 2008, in Section 16.2), the integrating phase is the natural candidate.
2. **Coherence.** Conscious visual experience is usually **coherent and unambiguous**, even when the scene itself is ambiguous and objects partly hide one another. The top-down processes involved in recurrent processing, such as expectations, are important in producing that coherence (**O'Reilly et al., 2013**).

#### How to switch recurrent processing off

To test the theory, you need a way to remove or reduce recurrent processing while leaving the feedforward sweep intact. Two methods are used.

- **Masking.** A stimulus is followed very quickly by a second stimulus, the **mask**, which blocks processing and conscious perception of the first. In key-term form: a second stimulus arriving almost immediately after the first suppresses both its processing and the awareness of it. The idea is that the mask arrives in time to let the feedforward sweep through but to disrupt the later feedback.
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS).** A magnetic pulse is applied to **early visual cortex** at a moment chosen to be late enough after the stimulus that the feedforward sweep has passed, so the pulse disrupts the recurrent feedback arriving back at that area.

> **Trap.** In Lamme's account the **feedforward sweep** is the *fast, unconscious* phase (about **100 to 150 ms**) and **recurrent processing** is the phase *accompanied by awareness*. Do not reverse them. And note that "recurrent" here means feedback from higher to lower areas, a kind of **top-down** processing; it is not the same thing as "rehearsal" or "repetition".

#### Findings

> **Key study, Fahrenfort et al. (2007).** Observers decided whether a **texture-defined square** had been presented, under **masked** and **non-masked** conditions, while the **EEG** was recorded. Under masking, the EEG showed that **feedforward processing was intact even when target detection was at chance**. But there was **practically no evidence of recurrent processing** in the masked condition.

**What it shows, and its limit.** The result suggests conscious visual perception depends more on recurrent than on feedforward processing: the sweep happened whether or not the target was seen, the recurrence did not. But the evidence is **correlational**. It shows that recurrent processing and awareness go together, not that recurrent processing is *essential* for awareness.

> **Key study, Koivisto et al. (2011).** To address causality, observers judged whether **natural scenes contained animals**. In this kind of task, feedforward processing runs from early visual cortex (**V1/V2**) up to **lateral occipital cortex**, and recurrent processing then runs back from lateral occipital cortex to V1/V2. The researchers applied **TMS** after processing had already reached lateral occipital cortex, so as to disrupt the returning, recurrent signal.

**Result and meaning.** As predicted, TMS that disrupted recurrent processing **reduced conscious visual perception**. Because TMS is an intervention, not just a recording, this is causal evidence that conscious vision depends at least in part on recurrent processing.

> **Key study, Boly et al. (2011).** Brain activity was compared in **minimally conscious patients**, **vegetative-state patients** and **healthy controls** while they heard **expected and unexpected tones**. **Feedforward** processes were comparable in all three groups. But **only the vegetative-state patients** had impaired **top-down connectivity from frontal to temporal areas**, indicating reduced recurrent processing.

**What it adds.** The study links Lamme's theory to conscious *level* and extends it beyond vision to hearing. The group without awareness (vegetative state) differed from both groups with some awareness (minimally conscious and healthy) on recurrence, but not on the feedforward sweep. That is the pattern Lamme's view predicts.

#### Evidence that recurrent processing is not always necessary

> **Key study, Koivisto et al. (2014).** Photographs were flashed briefly, some followed by a **mask** to prevent recurrent processing, and participants responded to those containing an **animal**. Animal detection was about the same with and without masking (**86%** masked, **88%** unmasked). But participants reported **less awareness of what each photograph showed** under masking than without it (Figure 16.5 plots the percentage of trials with reported awareness of content for animal and non-animal photographs under the two conditions).

**What it shows.** With an easy task, people could perform well, and had at least some conscious perception, even when recurrent processing was blocked. So recurrent processing is **not always required** for conscious visual perception, but it **enhances** perceptual experience: the masked observers knew an animal was there but saw less of the picture.

#### Evidence that recurrent processing is not always sufficient

> **Key study, Scholte et al. (2006).** Observers searched for **white vowels** in a stream of black and white letters. Occasionally, **unexpected square figures** were also shown. These figures produced **recurrent processing**, yet **50%** of participants said afterwards that they had **not seen** them. Only when recurrent processing was **widespread** were the figures consistently seen.

**What it shows.** Some recurrent processing occurred without awareness. Because attention was on the letters, the squares needed *fairly extensive* recurrent processing before they reached consciousness. Inattention raises the bar.

**Thakral (2011)** reported a similar result: unexpected visual stimuli that were **not** consciously perceived were nonetheless associated with extensive neural activity in brain areas involved in recurrent processing, such as the **prefrontal cortex**.

#### Evaluation of the recurrent-processing approach

**Strength.** There is much support for the idea that conscious visual perception is *generally* associated with recurrent processing, and that feedforward processing on its own typically does **not** lead to conscious perception. In principle, then, recurrent processing could serve as a **neural index or marker** of consciousness.

**Limitations.** The book lists four.

1. **Narrow focus on vision.** Almost all the evidence concerns seeing. Awareness of things that are not in front of you, such as remembering **past events** or imagining **future** ones, has barely been studied in these terms.
2. **Not always necessary.** How necessary recurrent processing is probably depends on the **complexity** of the scene and the **difficulty** of the task. With a sufficiently easy task it may not be needed at all (Koivisto et al., 2014).
3. **Not always sufficient.** Fairly extensive recurrent processing can occur **without** conscious awareness, for example under **inattention** (Scholte et al., 2006; Thakral, 2011).
4. **Do not overrate the neural measure.** Recurrent processing looks useful as an index mainly *because* it usually correlates highly with **self-report and other behavioural measures** of consciousness. Its credentials are borrowed from the very measures it was supposed to replace. The book therefore prefers an approach that **combines** brain-activation and behavioural measures over one that gives excessive weight to brain activation.

> **Trap.** The two awkward findings cut in opposite directions. **Koivisto et al. (2014)** shows recurrent processing is **not necessary** (easy task, masking barely hurt detection); **Scholte et al. (2006)** and **Thakral (2011)** show it is **not sufficient** (recurrence without awareness under inattention). Also, Koivisto's **2011** study (TMS, causal support) and **2014** study (masking, a challenge) are on opposite sides of the argument.

## 16.4 Global Workspace Theoretical Approach

### Two versions of one idea

Of the many theories of consciousness, the book concentrates on the very influential **global workspace** approach, which comes in two closely related versions.

| Theory | Proposed by | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Global workspace theory** | **Baars (1988)**; **Baars and Franklin (2007)** | Behavioural data; the cognitive architecture |
| **Global neuronal workspace theory** | **Dehaene and Naccache (2001)**, developed by **Dehaene and Changeux (2011)** | Identifying the main **brain areas** associated with conscious awareness |

The two differ mostly in that second column. Baars built the theory from the functional side; Dehaene and colleagues set out to locate the workspace in the brain.

> **Trap.** **Baars** = global workspace theory (behavioural emphasis, theatre metaphor). **Dehaene** (with Naccache, then Changeux) = global **neuronal** workspace theory (brain areas, "ignition"). A question asking which version emphasises brain areas wants Dehaene; one asking for the originator of the global workspace idea (1988) wants Baars.

### The four main assumptions

Everything in this section is organised around four assumptions shared by both versions. The findings that follow are grouped under the same four headings, so it pays to learn these first.

**Assumption 1: many unconscious special-purpose processors work in parallel.** Much human information processing is carried out by **numerous special-purpose unconscious processors** running **in parallel**. They are distributed across many brain areas, and each does a specialised job (colour processing, motion processing and so on). *Prediction:* **early processing** of a stimulus should be **very similar whether or not it is consciously perceived**, because the early stages are the work of these unconscious processors.

**Assumption 2: consciousness is late, integrated and widespread.** Consciousness is associated with **integrating information from several special-purpose processors**, relatively **late** in processing. So, in Baars and Franklin's (2007) phrase, conscious contents evoke widespread brain activation. The trigger is what the theory calls **"ignition"**: when **bottom-up** input is joined by **top-down attentional control**, activity suddenly becomes **synchronised over large stretches of the brain**. That synchronised activity makes the information **globally available** to the whole system, and global availability *is* conscious experience on this theory.

**Assumption 3: some brain areas matter especially.** Which areas are involved varies with the **content** of awareness (seeing a face engages different areas from hearing a tune). But some areas are much more likely than others to be active during conscious awareness. **Dehaene and Naccache (2001)** stressed the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)** and the **anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** (Figure 16.6, adapted from MacDonald et al., 2000, shows these two regions). **Dehaene and Changeux (2011)** similarly emphasised **prefrontal, cingulate and parietal regions**. Involvement of these areas comes with widespread synchronised activity, and that activity is often **relatively long-lasting**.

**Assumption 4: attention and consciousness are tightly linked.** Think of the pairs "we look in order to see" and "we listen in order to hear". **Baars (1997)** drew the distinction this way: the first word of each pair (look, listen) is about *selecting* an experience, which is **attention**; the second (see, hear) is about *being conscious of* what was selected, which is **consciousness**. His analogy: attention is like **choosing a television channel**, and consciousness is like the **picture on the screen**.

**The theatre metaphor.** **Baars and Franklin (2007)** described the attention-consciousness relationship with a theatre. The **unconscious processors** sit in the **audience**. On the **stage** is a **bright spot**, and whatever is in it is **conscious**; its contents are **broadcast** to the whole audience. **Control of where the bright spot falls** is **selective attention**.

| Element of the theatre | What it stands for |
|---|---|
| Audience | The many specialised unconscious processors |
| Bright spot on the stage | The current content of consciousness |
| Broadcast from the bright spot to the audience | Global availability of conscious information |
| Control of the bright spot | Selective attention |

> **Trap.** In the theatre metaphor, the **audience** is *unconscious* processing, the **bright spot** is *consciousness*, and **steering the bright spot** is *attention*. In Baars's channel analogy, **choosing the channel** is *attention* and **the picture** is *consciousness*. Options that label the audience "the conscious mind" or the picture "attention" have reversed the mapping.

### Findings: early processing (Assumption 1)

The first assumption predicts that early processing is unaffected by whether a stimulus will later be consciously perceived. There is much support.

> **Key study, Lamy et al. (2009).** Participants located a target and reported whether they were **subjectively aware** of it, while **ERPs** were recorded. Trials were sorted into **aware-correct**, **unaware-correct** and **unaware-incorrect**. The amplitude of the **early ERP components was unaffected** by awareness. But awareness was associated with a **late wave of activity, the P3, between 400 and 600 ms** after stimulus onset, spread widely across the brain. Brain positivity was far more widespread when there was awareness than when there was none (Figure 16.7).

**Why the unaware-correct condition matters.** It shows that the stimulus was processed well enough to guide a correct response without reaching awareness, and yet that condition lacked the widespread late P3. So the late wave tracks awareness itself, not merely successful processing.

> **Key study, Melloni et al. (2011).** If a stimulus is **expected**, top-down processes might let awareness arise sooner. They did: ERP differences between **seen and unseen** stimuli began at about **200 ms** for expected stimuli but at about **300 ms** for stimuli about which no expectation could be formed. Crucially, however, the **early stages** of processing for seen and unseen stimuli were **very similar** whether or not the stimuli were expected.

> **Trap.** **Lamy et al. (2009)**: the awareness signature is the **P3**, at **400 to 600 ms**, widespread. **Melloni et al. (2011)**: seen/unseen differences start at **200 ms (expected)** versus **300 ms (unexpected)**. Expectation brings awareness *forward* but does not change early processing.

### Findings: integrated brain functioning (Assumption 2)

The idea that integration is crucial is appealing on its face, because what we are aware of is almost always integrated. It is nearly impossible, for instance, to perceive an object while ignoring its colour.

> **Key study, Melloni et al. (2007).** Hard-to-perceive **words** were presented, and brain activity was compared for those consciously perceived and those not. Words that were not consciously perceived still produced enough EEG activation to suggest they had been **thoroughly processed**. Even so, **only consciously perceived words** produced **synchronised neural activity** linking **frontal, parietal and occipital** areas, especially between **40 and 182 ms** (Figure 16.8 shows the synchronisation as lines connecting cortical sites in the visible and invisible conditions).

The contrast is exactly what Assumption 2 needs: thorough processing alone was not enough for awareness; long-range synchrony was the difference.

> **Key study, Gaillard et al. (2009).** **Epileptic patients** with **electrodes implanted** in their brains (for clinical reasons) were shown **unmasked** stimuli, which they consciously perceived, and **masked** stimuli, which they did not. Recording directly from the brain allowed more precise measurement than scalp recording. There was **far more synchronised brain activity** in the unmasked (conscious) condition between **300 and 500 ms** after onset, the period during which conscious perception typically occurs.

> **Key study, King et al. (2013).** **EEG** was used to measure **integrated brain functioning** in response to **auditory** stimuli, with an index called **weighted symbolic mutual information (wSMI)**, a measure of how much information is shared between brain regions. Four groups with differing levels of awareness were compared:
>
> 1. patients in a **vegetative state** (no conscious awareness);
> 2. **minimally conscious** patients;
> 3. **conscious patients with brain damage**, often recovering from a vegetative or minimally conscious state;
> 4. **healthy** participants.
>
> There were **dramatic differences** in integrated activity across the groups: much more integration in the groups with high levels of awareness than in those with little or none (Figure 16.9 shows overall information sharing and sharing over short, medium and long distances for the four groups).

**The causality issue.** All these findings are as global workspace theory predicts. But there is a tricky question of **causality** (**de Graaf et al., 2012**). Synchronised neural activity might *directly reflect* conscious awareness. It might instead **precede and influence** awareness (a prerequisite), or occur **merely as a consequence** of it. Correlational data cannot tell these apart, and the book leaves the question to future research.

> **Trap.** The earliest awareness-related synchrony in the chapter is **Melloni et al. (2007)** at **40 to 182 ms**, far earlier than **Gaillard et al.'s (2009)** 300 to 500 ms; do not assume all awareness signatures are late. And showing that synchrony accompanies awareness does not show that synchrony *is* awareness (de Graaf et al., 2012).

### Findings: prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate (Assumption 3)

#### Correlational support

> **Key study, Eriksson et al. (2006).** **Auditory** stimuli (sounds of objects) and **visual** stimuli (pictures of objects) were presented under conditions that made them hard to identify. Activation in the **lateral prefrontal cortex** and the **anterior cingulate** was associated with conscious awareness in **both** modalities.

The cross-modal result matters: an area involved in awareness of both sounds and pictures looks like part of a general workspace rather than a sensory-specific processor.

**Gaillard et al. (2009)** again. Looking at the proportion of electrodes activated in different regions over the first 800 ms (Figure 16.10), they found that **conscious awareness, from about 350 ms**, had much greater effects on activation in the **frontal cortex** than in any other region (parietal, temporal, occipital). And brain activity lasted **considerably longer** for seen than for unseen stimuli, matching the "relatively long-lasting" part of Assumption 3.

#### The problem: attention as a confound

These findings are basically **correlational**, so prefrontal activation might not be *causally* related to awareness. The specific worry is **attention**. There is much evidence (below) that attention is a necessary prerequisite for conscious experience, and attentional processes themselves typically activate the prefrontal cortex (Chapter 5). So it is hard to separate brain activity associated with **attention** from activity associated with **awareness** (de Graaf et al., 2012).

> **Key study, Wyart and Tallon-Baudry (2008).** Participants directed attention **towards or away from** faint stimuli that were consciously perceived only **50% of the time**, which allowed attention and awareness to be varied independently. Both attention and awareness were associated with activity in **parietal and occipital** regions. The key finding was that the brain activities associated with attention and with awareness were **independent of each other**.

This partly answers the confound: attention and awareness can be dissociated in the brain, at least in this design.

#### Getting closer to causality

Two approaches move beyond correlation.

> **Key study, Del Cul et al. (2009).** Patients with **damage to prefrontal cortex** and healthy controls saw a **digit** followed by a **mask**, and indicated whether they had seen the digit. For the digit to be consciously perceived, the mask had to be **delayed longer** for the patients than for controls. So prefrontal damage makes conscious perception harder to achieve, and the effect was **larger in the patients with the most prefrontal damage**.

The dose-response pattern (more damage, bigger effect) strengthens the causal reading considerably.

> **Key study, Rounis et al. (2010).** **TMS** was used to disrupt **prefrontal** processing while participants made visual discriminations and rated how visible the stimuli were. TMS had **no effect on discrimination performance**, but it **lowered visibility ratings** for correctly identified stimuli.

**What it shows.** Disrupting the prefrontal cortex left the processing intact but made participants **less aware of the quality** of their own visual processing. In other words, the prefrontal cortex seems to contribute to knowing that (and how well) you see, a metacognitive component of awareness, rather than to the seeing itself.

#### Prefrontal activity without awareness

There is also growing evidence that the prefrontal cortex can be active during **unconscious** processing (van Gaal & Lamme, 2012).

> **Key study, van Gaal et al. (2010).** Participants were to respond to a visible stimulus **unless** it was preceded by a **small white square** (a stop signal). When the square was **visible**, participants showed good **inhibitory control**, with extensive prefrontal activation. The theoretically important condition was when the square was presented **below awareness**. It still **slowed responding** and produced some activation in the **inferior frontal cortex** and the **pre-supplementary motor area**.

**What it shows.** Unconscious attempts at inhibitory control occurred, and they recruited some prefrontal machinery. So prefrontal activation is **not a unique marker** of consciousness, and the same study is evidence for Hassin's "Yes It Can" idea that cognitive control can operate unconsciously.

> **Trap.** Sort the prefrontal studies by method. **Correlational**: Eriksson et al. (2006), Gaillard et al. (2009). **Dissociating attention from awareness**: Wyart and Tallon-Baudry (2008). **Causal**: Del Cul et al. (2009, lesions) and Rounis et al. (2010, TMS). **Against PFC as a unique marker**: van Gaal et al. (2010). In Rounis et al. it is **visibility**, not accuracy, that falls.

### Findings: attention and consciousness (Assumption 4)

The fourth assumption is that consciousness **depends on prior selective attention**. It sounds reasonable, but several theorists reject it. **Koch and Tsuchiya (2012)** argued that the two can come apart in **both** directions: attention without consciousness, and consciousness without attention.

#### Attention without consciousness

> **Key study, Jiang et al. (2006).** Pictures of **male and female nudes** were made **invisible** by presenting them to the **non-dominant eye** while participants performed a task on **noise patches** shown to the **dominant eye** (the strong input to one eye suppresses awareness of the other eye's image). Where attention had gone was measured by performance on a stimulus presented after the displays: good performance at a location meant attention had been drawn there. The invisible nudes **did attract attention**: **heterosexual males** attended to invisible **female** nudes, and **heterosexual females** to invisible **male** nudes.

**Troiani et al. (2014)** offered a mechanism for how unseen emotional stimuli can steer attention: unseen **fearful faces** increased **amygdala** activation, and this was associated with activation of brain areas belonging to an **attentional network**.

> **Key study, Naccache et al. (2002).** Participants decided as fast as possible whether a **target digit** was greater or smaller than **5**. Just before it, another digit was presented **invisibly**. The two digits were **congruent** (both on the same side of 5) or **incongruent** (on opposite sides). Attention to the display was manipulated by having a **cue present or absent**. On **uncued** trials the invisible digit made **no difference** to response times, whereas on **cued** trials its congruence had a **highly significant** effect (Figure 16.11 shows mean reaction times for congruent and incongruent trials, cued and uncued).

**What it shows.** Attention **amplified** the information extracted from the invisible digit, yet did so **without making the digit conscious**. Attention without awareness, in a clean experimental form.

#### Consciousness without attention?

Whether awareness is always *preceded* by attention is the most controversial part of the theory. **Koch and Tsuchiya (2007, 2012)** say consciousness does not depend on prior attention; **Bor and Seth (2012)** argue the opposite. **Change blindness** and **inattentional blindness** (Chapter 4) are relevant: both suggest that new objects, or changes to objects, in a scene are not consciously detected when attention is elsewhere.

Koch and Tsuchiya (2007) put forward several phenomena as examples of consciousness without attention. The book examines two.

**1. Natural-scene gist.** We seem to grasp the gist of natural scenes without attending to them. That may be an illusion.

> **Key study, Cohen et al. (2011).** Participants watched rapidly presented **digits and letters** against **changing chessboard masks** and counted the digits. Unexpectedly, a **natural scene** containing an **animal or a vehicle** replaced one of the masks. Only **23%** could immediately identify the object in the scene, and **50%** reported **no conscious perception** of the scene at all. When participants were then told to **attend to the background** and ignore the digits and letters, they perceived and classified the scenes accurately **93%** of the time.

These figures strongly suggest that attention is required for conscious perception of scene gist.

**2. Visual pop-out.** A target **pops out** when it differs obviously from its surroundings (in colour, say, or orientation) and can be detected very fast. It has often been assumed that pop-out needs no attention. **M.A. Cohen et al. (2012)** reviewed the evidence and found that pop-out typically **fails** when observers' attention is engaged by another task, so pop-out too appears to **require attention**.

> **Trap.** **Cohen et al. (2011)**: **23%** identified the scene object, **50%** saw nothing, **93%** when attending. These numbers argue *against* Koch and Tsuchiya's consciousness-without-attention. Do not confuse **Cohen et al. (2011)** (the natural-scene experiment) with **M.A. Cohen et al. (2012)** (the review on pop-out and the A/B/C sketch-map). And remember who is on which side: **Koch and Tsuchiya** say attention and consciousness are separable; **Bor and Seth (2012)** say consciousness needs attention.

### Attention and unconscious processes

There is still **no consensus** on how attention and consciousness relate. But most of the evidence fits the view that attention is **necessary but not sufficient** for conscious awareness. **M.A. Cohen et al. (2012)** offered a sketch-map with three routes (Figure 16.12):

| Route | What happens | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Attention operates on the information | It can reach **conscious awareness**; this is the *only* way in |
| **B** | Attention operates, but the item does not reach consciousness | It can still influence behaviour (for example, **priming**) and produce neural changes |
| **C** | The information is **not attended** | Only **modest priming** effects and some neural activation |

Route A expresses "necessary"; route B expresses "not sufficient".

A growing body of research shows that **unconscious processes can be strongly influenced by attention**: Naccache et al. (2002) and Jiang et al. (2006) in this section, and, from earlier in the chapter, Marien et al. (2012), whose unconscious goals affected task performance, and van Gaal et al. (2010), who showed inhibitory control at the unconscious level.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition's settled position is **attention is necessary but not sufficient** for consciousness. "Sufficient but not necessary" is the reversed distractor. Route **B** is attention *without* consciousness; route **C** is neither, yet still gives modest priming.

### Overall evaluation of the global workspace approach

**Strengths.** All the major assumptions have received support.

- **Early processing** of seen and unseen stimuli typically does not differ (Lamy et al., 2009; Melloni et al., 2011).
- Conscious awareness is generally accompanied by **widespread integrated or synchronised activity** (Melloni et al., 2007; Gaillard et al., 2009; King et al., 2013), including in the **prefrontal cortex** and **anterior cingulate** (Eriksson et al., 2006; Gaillard et al., 2009; Del Cul et al., 2009).
- There are **close links between attention and awareness**, and it is plausible, though controversial, that awareness is mostly or always preceded by attention (Cohen et al., 2011; M.A. Cohen et al., 2012).

**Limitations.** The book gives three.

1. **Narrow focus.** The approach concentrates on the processes, such as information integration, behind **conscious visual perception**. Richer kinds of consciousness, such as knowing about oneself (**self-knowledge**), are left almost untouched (**Timmermans et al., 2012**). In 16.1 terms, it addresses basic perceptual awareness rather than Baumeister and Masicampo's higher, self-reflective consciousness.
2. **Correlate is not substrate.** The integrated brain activity that accompanies awareness is not necessarily its **neural substrate**; it could be a **prerequisite** or a **consequence** of conscious experience (de Graaf et al., 2012).
3. **Neglect of psychology.** Identifying the brain areas and patterns associated with awareness is valuable, but the focus on cognitive neuroscience has led to relative **neglect of the psychological processes** involved.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of global workspace theory?" Real limitations: narrow focus on visual perception and neglect of self-knowledge (Timmermans et al., 2012); integrated activity may be a prerequisite or consequence rather than the substrate (de Graaf et al., 2012); relative neglect of psychological processes. A distractor such as "its prediction of similar early processing for seen and unseen stimuli has been disconfirmed" is false: that prediction is one of the best supported. Prefrontal activation during unconscious processing (van Gaal et al., 2010) is a complication for Assumption 3, but the book does not list it among the three formal limitations.

## 16.5 Is Consciousness Unitary?

### Why the question arises

Almost everyone assumes they have one single, unified consciousness (the book allows itself the joke that a few people are "in two minds" about it). The assumption became controversial because of **split-brain patients**: patients in whom most of the direct links between the two cerebral hemispheres have been cut (key-term definition). In most cases the **corpus callosum**, the bridge between the hemispheres, was cut surgically to **confine epileptic seizures to one hemisphere**. The corpus callosum is a collection of about **250 million axons** linking sites in one hemisphere with sites in the other. Figure 16.13 contrasts a normal brain, with an intact corpus callosum, and the brain of split-brain patient **JW**, in which it is entirely absent (Gazzaniga, 2013).

> **Trap.** Eysenck and Keane give the corpus callosum as about **250 million** axons. Pinel's biopsychology text (in this same library) gives about **200 million**. Both figures circulate in exam keys; if the question is framed on the Eysenck and Keane chapter, use 250 million, and if an option offers "about 200 million" alone, recognise it as the Pinel figure rather than a wild error.

### Some communication survives

Cutting the corpus callosum does not leave the two hemispheres in total isolation.

- In the patient studied by **Uddin et al. (2008)**, surgery had severed the **whole corpus callosum**. Functional neuroimaging nonetheless showed **coordination of networks in both hemispheres**, achieved through **subcortical mechanisms**.
- **Tyszka et al. (2011)** studied **eight** people who **lacked a corpus callosum from birth**, because of developmental abnormalities rather than surgery. Surprisingly, they had **largely normal functional networks** in both hemispheres. A likely reason is that their brains underwent extensive functional **reorganisation early in life**. The implication is a warning: people born without a corpus callosum may differ importantly in cognitive functioning from patients who lose it through surgery in adulthood, so the two groups should not be pooled.

> **Trap.** **Uddin et al. (2008)**: *surgical* split-brain patient, interhemispheric coordination via **subcortical** routes. **Tyszka et al. (2011)**: **eight** patients with **developmental** absence of the corpus callosum (classic background: callosal agenesis), largely **normal** networks because of early reorganisation. A stem that uses Tyszka et al. to describe surgical patients is wrong.

### Everyday life and the strategies patients use

It is sometimes believed that split-brain patients cope badly in daily life. Typically they do not, although they may find it hard to carry out **complex sequences of actions**, such as **cooking** (**Ferguson et al., 1985**). In fact, the early surgeons did not realise at first that cutting the corpus callosum caused any problems. The reason is that the patients **move their eyes around**, so information from the environment reaches **both** hemispheres. Deficits only show up in the laboratory, when a visual stimulus is flashed **briefly** to **one hemisphere** so that the information is not available to the other.

Patients also find indirect ways of getting information across. The book's example is **VP**. The word **"break"** was presented to her **right** hemisphere and **"fast"** to her **left** hemisphere. She began to say "bre-", pronounced as in *brake*, then instantly corrected herself to "breck", as in *breakfast*. The left hemisphere, which controls speech and had seen "fast", used the **sound** of what had just been said, the auditory input arising from the right hemisphere's half of the word, to work out the compound and produce the correct pronunciation. This is called **self-cueing**: information crosses between the hemispheres by an external route (here, overt speech heard by both) rather than through the callosum.

> **Trap.** Eysenck and Keane call the VP phenomenon **self-cueing**. Pinel's chapter on lateralisation calls the same general strategy **cross-cuing** (for example, the left hemisphere hearing its own wrong answer and the right hemisphere signalling with a frown). They describe the same idea: interhemispheric communication through the outside world. Know both labels, and do not confuse self-cueing with the brainstem or subcortical routes reported by Uddin et al. (2008).

### Three theories

#### Sperry: two consciousnesses

**Roger Sperry** argued that split-brain patients have **two consciousnesses**. In his 1968 account, the **minor (right) hemisphere** is a second conscious entity that runs alongside the more dominant stream of consciousness in the **major (left) hemisphere**. He called the left hemisphere dominant because **language processing** is typically centred there. Classic background: Sperry shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this split-brain work.

#### Gazzaniga: one consciousness, the left-hemisphere interpreter

**Gazzaniga (e.g., 2013)** argued instead that split-brain patients have only **one conscious system**, based in the **left hemisphere**, which he called the **interpreter**. Its job, in his description, is to try to make sense of the many independent functions running in the brain at any moment. Importantly, the interpreter keeps working **even when it has very little information**, which is the situation of many brain-damaged patients. **Cooney and Gazzaniga (2003)** described what it produces: a causal understanding of events that feels subjectively complete and obvious, even when that understanding is actually incomplete. In other words, it generates confident explanations whether or not it has the facts. This is the same phenomenon as Nisbett and Wilson's stocking choosers (Section 16.2), seen in an extreme form.

#### Bayne: one stream that switches

**Bayne's (2008, 2010) switch model** differs from both. There is a **single stream of consciousness**, but it **switches between the two hemispheres**. Which hemisphere hosts consciousness at a given moment depends on how **attentional resources** are allocated. This ties the model to Section 16.4, where much evidence supported the general view that conscious experience depends on prior selective attention.

**A doubt about "single".** It is not obvious that split-brain patients really have one stream. Most experiments are designed precisely to give each hemisphere **different information**. A more likely description, suggested by **Schechter (2012)**, is that these patients have **two distinct streams of activity**, even if only **one** is accessible to consciousness at any moment.

**Advantage and disadvantage of the switch model.**

- *Advantage:* it explains why there are **relatively few disagreements** between the hemispheres. If only one hemisphere is conscious at a time, there is rarely a second conscious party to disagree.
- *Disadvantage:* it seems to predict that patients would report **rapid changes in the contents of consciousness** as the stream flips from one side to the other. In fact such reports are **very rare**.

| Theory | Number of consciousnesses | Where | Key claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sperry (1968)** | Two | Both hemispheres, left dominant | Right hemisphere is a second conscious entity running in parallel |
| **Gazzaniga (2013)** | One | Left hemisphere | The **interpreter** builds coherent, often confabulated, explanations |
| **Bayne (2008, 2010)** | One stream, location varies | Switches between hemispheres | Attention determines which hemisphere is conscious at a time |

> **Trap.** **Sperry** = two minds. **Gazzaniga** = one mind, left-hemisphere **interpreter**. **Bayne** = one stream that **switches** with attention; its weakness is that it predicts frequent reported shifts in content, which are rare. **Schechter (2012)** is not a fourth theory so much as a refinement: two streams of *activity*, one conscious at a time. Older textbooks often present Sperry's two-minds view as the established conclusion of split-brain research; the 7th edition presents the left-hemisphere-dominant interpreter view as the commonest position.

### Findings

#### Two orienting facts

Before the evidence, two anatomical facts (Chapter 2). Information from the **left visual field** goes to the **right hemisphere**, and information from the **right visual field** goes to the **left hemisphere**. More generally, the **right hemisphere controls the left side of the body** and the **left hemisphere controls the right side**. So in the studies below, "left hand" means "right hemisphere" and vice versa.

The second fact is a methodological obstacle. The right hemisphere of most split-brain patients has **limited language ability**, which makes it hard to tell whether it has a consciousness of its own: it cannot simply tell you.

#### Evidence for two streams

If Sperry is right, there should be occasional **disagreements** between the hemispheres, and some have been reported.

- **Mark (1996)** described a patient who had **speech in both hemispheres**. She remarked that she had no feeling in her left hand. When Mark repeated her remark back to her, she said she was not numb, and there followed a rapid string of alternating "Yes!" and "No!" answers, ending with a despairing "I don't know!" It reads like two speakers with opposite views competing for one mouth.
- **Baynes and Gazzaniga (2000)** studied **VJ**, whose **writing** is controlled by her **right** hemisphere and her **speech** by her **left**. They described her as the first split-brain patient to be frequently dismayed by the independent behaviour of her two hands. She was unsettled that her left hand wrote fluently about stimuli she had not consciously seen, and distressed that her right hand could not write words she could read aloud and spell. The book's cautious reading is that VJ *speculatively* suggests **limited dual consciousness**.
- **Schiffer et al. (1998)** tested whether the hemispheres can operate **simultaneously and independently**. **Two patients** answered **emotionally sensitive questions**, responding **with each hand separately at the same time**, with their hands hidden behind a screen. The **right hemisphere** gave **more emotional** answers, consistent with the idea that the right hemisphere plays a key part in emotional experience.
- **Trevarthen (2004)** reviewed studies of what each hemisphere can do in split-brain patients. The **right hemisphere outperformed the left** on tasks involving visual or tactile perception of **complex shapes**, manipulation of **geometric patterns**, and judgements based on **exploring shapes by hand**.
- **Self-recognition.** Recognising one's own face is often taken as a sign of reasonable self-awareness. **Uddin et al. (2005)** found that **NG**, a **70-year-old** split-brain patient, recognised her own face **equally well** whether it was shown to her left or right hemisphere, and her performance was close to that of healthy people. This suggests some **basic self-awareness in both hemispheres**.
- A review of neuroimaging studies by **Keenan and Gorman (2007)** found that self-awareness is generally associated with **greater right-hemisphere than left-hemisphere activation**. So some aspects of conscious experience may depend on the right hemisphere **more than Gazzaniga (2013) assumes**.

> **Trap.** Keep the patients apart. **VJ** (Baynes & Gazzaniga, 2000): right-hemisphere *writing*, left-hemisphere *speech*, dismayed by her hands, suggests **limited dual consciousness**. **VP**: "break" plus "fast" gives "breck", an example of **self-cueing**. **NG** (Uddin et al., 2005): **70-year-old**, own-face recognition **equal in both hemispheres**. **JW**: the patient whose scan shows total absence of the corpus callosum (Figure 16.13). **Paul S**, **GH** and **AC** are below.

#### Evidence for one stream

**Gazzaniga (e.g., 2013)** emphasised a stream of consciousness centred in the **dominant left hemisphere**, while accepting that much processing also goes on in the right. Several lines of evidence support him.

**Subjective reports.** **Colvin and Gazzaniga (2007)** pointed out that no split-brain patient has ever woken from callosotomy (cutting of the corpus callosum) feeling that their sense of self had fundamentally changed, or that two selves now shared one body. If the operation created a second mind, you would expect at least some patients to notice.

**Patients with right-hemisphere language.** Because the usual right hemisphere cannot speak, the important cases are patients whose right hemisphere has reasonable language.

> **Key case, Paul S (Gazzaniga & Ledoux, 1978).** A split-brain patient with **unusually well-developed right-hemisphere language**. He showed **limited** evidence of right-hemisphere consciousness by answering questions with **Scrabble letters** arranged by his **left hand** (right hemisphere): he could spell his own name, his girlfriend's name, his hobbies, his current mood and so on. The hemispheres sometimes differed: asked about his ambitions, his **right** hemisphere spelled out that he wanted to be a **racing driver**, while his **left** hemisphere said a **draughtsman**.

On its own, that might look like two minds. But other studies of Paul S, described by **Gazzaniga (1992, 2013)**, point the other way.

> **Key case, the chicken claw and the snow scene.** A **chicken claw** was shown to Paul S's **left** hemisphere and a **snow scene** to his **right** hemisphere. Asked to pick related pictures from an array, his **right hand** (left hemisphere) chose a **chicken**, and his **left hand** (right hemisphere) chose a **shovel**. Asked why, he answered: "The chicken claw goes with the chicken and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed" (Gazzaniga, 1992, p. 124).

**Why this supports Gazzaniga.** The speaking left hemisphere had seen only the claw. It had no access to the snow scene that actually drove the left hand's choice. Instead of saying "I don't know", it **invented a plausible reason** that tied the shovel to the only thing it knew about, the chicken. In effect, the right hemisphere had acted and the left hemisphere **supplied the story**, with the right hemisphere contributing almost nothing to the explanation. A similar thing happened with commands: when his **right** hemisphere was told to **walk**, Paul S got up and walked, and his **left** hemisphere explained the action with something like wanting to get a **Coke**.

> **Trap.** In the chicken-claw study, the **claw** went to the **left** hemisphere (so the **right hand** chose the **chicken**) and the **snow scene** went to the **right** hemisphere (so the **left hand** chose the **shovel**). The **explanation** came from the **left** hemisphere, which had never seen the snow. Reversing the hemispheres is the standard distractor. Also note the spelling: the book cites "Gazzaniga and Ledoux (1978)"; the co-author is Joseph LeDoux, so either spelling may appear on a paper.

**Anarchic hand: GH.**

> **Key case, GH (Verleger et al., 2011).** A **69-year-old man** whose corpus callosum was damaged, producing **anarchic-hand syndrome**: complex, goal-directed hand movements that the patient does not voluntarily initiate and cannot interpret (key-term definition). In GH it was his **left** hand that did not act as intended and sometimes counteracted the right. In a shop, for example, he would put money on the counter with his **right** hand and his **left** hand would take it back.

GH was asked to respond to stimuli presented to his left or right hemisphere. His responses were **slower and more error-prone** when he responded with his **left hand**, that is, after stimuli to his **right hemisphere**. His **ERPs** showed a much **smaller P3 component** (at about **300 to 400 ms**) to stimuli presented to the **right** hemisphere than to the left. Since the P3 is linked to attention and control of processing (and, from Section 16.4, to awareness), this suggests his ability to attend and control processing was much weaker in the right hemisphere. Consistent with this, his everyday experience strongly suggested that his **consciousness resided in his left hemisphere**: the left hand behaved like an outsider.

> **Trap.** Classic background: some neuropsychologists distinguish **anarchic hand** (the patient accepts that the hand is their own but it acts against their will) from **alien hand** (the patient feels the hand does not belong to them). The 7th edition uses "anarchic-hand syndrome" and defines it by movements that are goal-directed, not voluntarily initiated and not interpretable by the patient. GH's anarchic hand was the **left**, controlled by the right hemisphere.

**AC: the P3 asymmetry.**

> **Key case, AC (Hesselmann et al., 2013).** A man with **severe damage to the corpus callosum**. Two stimuli were presented **100, 300 or 800 ms** apart, one to each hemisphere, and AC responded as fast as possible to both. Looking at the ERPs to the **first** stimulus, AC had a **pronounced P3** to stimuli presented to the **left** hemisphere but **no P3** to stimuli presented to the **right** hemisphere (Figure 16.14 plots the ERPs from each hemisphere for the three intervals, the stimulus onset asynchronies).

**What it shows.** AC appeared to have conscious access to information about stimuli in his **left** hemisphere, but **much delayed and/or reduced** conscious access to stimuli in his **right** hemisphere. Along with GH, AC supplies the ERP evidence the book relies on when it argues that the right hemisphere has much less conscious awareness than the left.

### Summary and evaluation

Research has **not fully resolved** whether one person can have two separate consciousnesses. The book's verdict has four parts.

1. **The commonest view** is that in split-brain patients the **left hemisphere plays the dominant role** in conscious awareness, because it houses an **interpreter** or **self-supervisory system** that produces coherent interpretations of events.
2. **Support for that view** comes from findings in which the left hemisphere **overrules** the right (Paul S's explanations, GH's experience) and from the persistent **failure to observe genuine dialogues** between the hemispheres. Gazzaniga (2013) summed up the point by arguing that the impressively unified speech behaviour we observe is produced by separate, relatively independent specialised modules that cue one another so heavily that the output appears coherent.
3. **The right hemisphere is not idle.** It carries out various processing activities: **basic self-awareness** (NG; Keenan & Gorman, 2007), **emotional processing** (Schiffer et al., 1998), and aspects of **visual and tactile perception** (Trevarthen, 2004). Whether it is *conscious* is hard to assess because its language is so limited. Even so, the recent **ERP** findings from GH and AC suggest that the right hemisphere is **far less conscious** than the left.
4. **The way forward.** Earlier sections of the chapter identified neural correlates of consciousness (recurrent processing, the late P3, long-range synchrony, prefrontal involvement). The book suggests it would be of great theoretical value to find out whether these correlates are present or absent **within the right hemisphere** of split-brain patients. That would let researchers ask the unity question without relying on speech.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition's conclusion is **not** "two equal minds". It is: the left hemisphere is **dominant** for conscious awareness and acts as an **interpreter**; the right hemisphere does real processing (self-recognition, emotion, shapes) but, on ERP evidence, has **only limited consciousness**. The **rarity of reported disagreements** between the hemispheres supports the left-dominant view (and is the switch model's advantage), while the **rarity of reported rapid shifts** in conscious content is the switch model's main problem.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the five modules.

1. **Two meanings, two methods.** Conscious *content* and conscious *level* (16.1) map onto the chapter's two kinds of evidence: content is studied by contrasting seen with unseen stimuli in healthy people (masking, Sperling, global workspace studies); level is studied by comparing coma, vegetative, minimally conscious and healthy groups (Owen, Cruse, Boly, King). Several studies (Boly et al., 2011; King et al., 2013) deliberately join the two.
2. **Integration is the recurring signature.** Tononi and Koch's "informative" argument (16.2), Lamme's recurrent processing (16.3), global workspace ignition and long-range synchrony (16.4), and King et al.'s information sharing across brain regions (16.4) are four versions of one idea: a conscious state is one in which many specialised processes are bound together and made widely available.
3. **Consciousness narrates more than it knows.** Nisbett and Wilson's stocking choosers, Wegner's illusion of will, Banks and Isham's reconstructed decision times, Kouider's illusory richness, and Gazzaniga's interpreter all show the conscious system producing a confident account that runs ahead of the evidence it has.
4. **Report versus experience.** Block says experience overflows report; Kouider and the book say the impression of richness is largely expectation. The same tension decides how to read a silent vegetative-state patient: no behavioural report is not the same as no experience.
5. **Correlation is not constitution.** Every neural marker in the chapter (recurrent processing, P3, synchrony, prefrontal activation) faces the same question: is it the substrate of awareness, a prerequisite, or a consequence (de Graaf et al., 2012)? The causal studies (Koivisto et al., 2011, TMS; Del Cul et al., 2009, lesions; Rounis et al., 2010, TMS) are the ones that move the argument furthest.
6. **Attention is the gatekeeper.** Attention without consciousness clearly exists (Naccache, Jiang); consciousness without attention mostly turns out not to (Cohen et al., 2011; M.A. Cohen et al., 2012). The same principle explains Scholte's unseen squares, Bayne's switch model and the P3 asymmetry in GH and AC.

## Worked example

*A 24-year-old man is admitted after a motorcycle accident with severe traumatic brain injury. After three weeks his eyes open spontaneously, he has sleep-wake cycles, and he startles to loud noises, but on repeated bedside examination he never follows a command, tracks a face or produces a word. The neurology team records "vegetative state". His mother is sure he squeezes her hand when she talks to him and asks the psychology team two questions: is anyone in there, and will he recover?*

**First, place him on the scale of conscious level.** Eyes open and sleep-wake cycles mean he is not in **coma**, which is defined by the absence of both wakefulness and awareness. No reproducible purposeful response means he does not yet meet the behavioural picture of the **minimally conscious state**. The label "vegetative state" (wakefulness without apparent awareness) is the correct *behavioural* description. Rule out **locked-in syndrome** too (classic background): a fully conscious patient who cannot move could look unresponsive, but locked-in patients usually retain eye movements that can be used to communicate, and their imaging shows intact cortex.

**Second, recognise what the behavioural label cannot tell you.** The vegetative-state definition (Owen & Coleman, 2008) is behavioural by construction. The studies in 16.3 show that behaviour can understate awareness: Owen et al.'s (2006) 23-year-old woman, also injured in a road accident, produced healthy-looking patterns of activity when asked to imagine tennis or walking round her house, and Monti et al.'s (2010) patient answered yes/no questions using the same imagery code. The mother's hand-squeeze impression is not evidence on its own (a grasp can be reflexive), but it is a reason to look harder rather than to dismiss.

**Third, choose an active test.** Owen (2013) argues for **active tasks**, which cannot be passed automatically. An EEG version of the command paradigm (Cruse et al., 2011: "squeeze your right hand", "squeeze your toes") can be done at the bedside; an fMRI imagery paradigm (tennis versus house) is the alternative. Passing either requires sustained attention, comprehension of the instruction, working memory for the task rule and response selection, so a clear positive result is strong evidence of covert awareness.

**Fourth, interpret a negative result with care.** In Cruse et al. (2011), **13 of 16 patients (81%)** showed no response. A negative result might mean no awareness, or it might mean the method was too insensitive, the patient was drowsy that day, or he could not understand the instruction. The chapter is explicit that we do not know which, so a single negative scan must not be reported to the family as proof that "nobody is there".

**Fifth, address prognosis.** Coleman et al. (2009) found, in 41 patients, that neuroimaging signs of consciousness did not match the initial bedside assessment but correlated highly with behavioural recovery **six months later**. A positive covert response would therefore be cautiously encouraging for recovery. Measures of integration point the same way: King et al. (2013) found that information sharing across the brain (wSMI) rises step by step from vegetative to minimally conscious to conscious patients, and Boly et al. (2011) found that vegetative-state patients specifically lacked top-down frontal-to-temporal connectivity, the recurrent processing that Lamme links to awareness.

**Sixth, be honest about the limits.** Even a clearly positive result answers only the first question. As Owen (2013) stressed, we still would not know whether the patient is in pain, depressed, or wants to go on living. A positive scan creates new clinical and ethical duties (pain management, attempts at yes/no communication, involving the family) rather than settling them.

**What the case pulls together.** Conscious *level* (coma, vegetative, minimally conscious) is a behavioural classification; conscious *content* can be probed with brain activity; the neural correlates of consciousness in healthy people (recurrent processing, integration) become clinical tools; and the gap between experience and report, argued over by Block and Kouider for the Sperling array, becomes a life-and-death question at the bedside.

## MCQ traps

1. **Bor and Seth (2012)**: conscious **content** (what you are aware of) versus conscious **level** (coma to alert wakefulness); a non-zero level is needed for any content.
2. **Block (2012)**: **access** (reportable, usable) versus **phenomenal** (raw experience); **Baumeister and Masicampo (2010)**: **basic phenomenal** versus **higher** self-reflective consciousness.
3. **Chalmers (2007)**: the **hard problem** is why physical processing produces experience; the "easy problems" (discrimination, integration, access to internal states, control) are hard but tractable.
4. **Pinker (1997)** gives the "here I am, feeling pain and seeing red" illustration of **higher**-level consciousness.
5. **Kane et al. (2007)**: thoughts wander from the current task about **30%** of the time.
6. **Tononi and Koch (2008)**: consciousness is extraordinarily **informative** because each state rules out vast numbers of alternatives (integration).
7. **Hassin (2013)**: the **"Yes It Can"** principle; **Marien et al. (2012)**: subliminal socialisation goal impaired **proofreading** only when socialising was an **important** goal.
8. **Nisbett and Wilson (1977)**: five identical pairs of **stockings**, most chose the **rightmost**, and denied that position mattered.
9. **Humphrey (1983, 2002)**: consciousness is mainly **social**; **Graziano and Kastner (2011)**: same machinery computes others' and one's own awareness (Abel, Bill and the cup).
10. **Temporo-parietal junction**: TMS impairs self/other switching (**Sowden & Catmur, 2014**); electrical stimulation gave a **43-year-old woman** out-of-body experiences (**Blanke et al., 2002**).
11. **Baumeister et al. (2011a)**: conscious thought is largely **for talking**; **Baumeister et al. (2011b)**: behaviour depends crucially on conscious thought.
12. **Huxley (1874)**: **steam whistle** hypothesis (consciousness reports but does not cause); **Wegner (2003)**: the **illusion** of conscious will.
13. Wegner's principles: **priority, consistency, exclusivity** (not Kelley's consensus, consistency, distinctiveness).
14. **Gollwitzer (1999)**: implementation intentions specify **when, where and how** and create **"instant habits"**; **Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006)** meta-analysis supports them; **Adriaanse et al. (2011)**: they reduce habitual responding.
15. **Wegner and Wheatley (1999)**: cursor, confederate, headphones; false authorship when the word came **1 or 5 s before**, not 30 s before or 1 s after; only **60%** felt authorship even with all three principles (a limitation).
16. **van der Weiden et al. (2013)**: self-agency inflated by a matching goal **and** by a matching **subliminal prime**.
17. **Libet et al. (1983)**: readiness potential about **350 ms before** awareness of intention; awareness about **200 ms before** movement.
18. **Haggard (2005)**: the experience of intending is "thin and evasive"; **Banks and Isham (2009)**: a **120 ms** video delay shifted reported decision time **44 ms** later.
19. **Soon et al. (2008)**: **what** (left/right) predicted **7 s** ahead from **frontopolar BA10** and parietal cortex; **when** predicted **5 s** ahead from **SMA/pre-SMA**.
20. **Bode et al. (2011)**: **left** frontopolar cortex up to **7.5 s** ahead, but accuracy **rarely above 55%** (chance 50%); **Fahle et al. (2011)**: unrecorded decision-relevant thoughts; **Mele (2013)**: arbitrary choices invite **automatic tie-breaking**.
21. **Lamme (2006, 2010)**: report is limited by attention, memory and inner speech, so experience may be richer than report.
22. **Objective** threshold = forced-choice performance; **subjective** threshold = self-report.
23. **Sperling (1960)**: **3 rows of 4** letters for **50 ms**; whole report **4 to 5** letters; partial report high only with an **immediate** cue.
24. **Kouider et al. (2010, 2012)**: no phenomenal consciousness separate from access; **de Gardelle et al. (2009)**: rotated and flipped **pseudo-letters** went unnoticed; **Levin et al. (2002)**: people overestimate their resistance to change blindness. Book's verdict: **misreporting** more than under-reporting.
25. **Coma**: no wakefulness, no awareness; **vegetative state**: wakefulness without awareness; **minimally conscious state**: some awareness.
26. **Owen et al. (2006)**: **23-year-old woman**, road accident, **tennis** (SMA) versus **house** imagery; **Monti et al. (2010)**: yes = tennis, no = house ("Is your father's name Thomas?").
27. **Cruse et al. (2011)**: **16** patients, **EEG**, hand and toe commands, **3** responded, so **81%** did not; **Coleman et al. (2009)**: **41** patients, imaging predicted recovery at **6 months**; **Monti et al. (2013)**: face/house attention switching with an unchanging display.
28. **Lamme**: **feedforward sweep** within about **100 to 150 ms** is unconscious; **recurrent** (feedback) processing accompanies awareness.
29. **Fahrenfort et al. (2007)**: masking spares feedforward, abolishes recurrence (correlational); **Koivisto et al. (2011)**: **TMS** to disrupt recurrence reduced awareness (causal); **Boly et al. (2011)**: impaired frontal-to-temporal top-down connectivity **only in vegetative** patients.
30. **Koivisto et al. (2014)**: animal detection **86% masked versus 88% unmasked**, so recurrence **not necessary** for easy tasks; **Scholte et al. (2006)**: **50%** missed squares despite recurrence, so **not sufficient** under inattention.
31. **Baars (1988)** = global workspace (behavioural); **Dehaene and Naccache (2001)**, **Dehaene and Changeux (2011)** = global **neuronal** workspace (brain areas: **DLPFC, ACC**, later prefrontal, cingulate and parietal).
32. Theatre metaphor: audience = unconscious processors, **bright spot** = consciousness, **control of the spot** = attention; channel analogy: **choosing the channel** = attention, **picture** = consciousness (Baars, 1997).
33. **Lamy et al. (2009)**: early ERPs unaffected; awareness = widespread **P3 at 400 to 600 ms**; **Melloni et al. (2011)**: seen/unseen differences at **200 ms expected** versus **300 ms unexpected**.
34. **Melloni et al. (2007)**: synchrony for seen words at **40 to 182 ms**; **Gaillard et al. (2009)**: implanted electrodes, synchrony at **300 to 500 ms**, frontal dominance from about **350 ms**; **King et al. (2013)**: **wSMI**, four groups, auditory stimuli.
35. **Wyart and Tallon-Baudry (2008)**: stimuli seen **50%** of the time; attention and awareness had **independent** correlates; **Del Cul et al. (2009)**: PFC lesions need a **longer mask delay**, scaled to damage; **Rounis et al. (2010)**: PFC TMS lowers **visibility**, not **performance**.
36. **van Gaal et al. (2010)**: an **unconscious** white-square stop signal slowed responses and activated **inferior frontal cortex and pre-SMA**.
37. **Jiang et al. (2006)**: invisible nudes captured attention according to sexual orientation; **Naccache et al. (2002)**: invisible prime affected **cued** but not uncued trials; **Troiani et al. (2014)**: unseen fearful faces drive the **amygdala**.
38. **Cohen et al. (2011)**: **23%** identified the scene object, **50%** saw nothing, **93%** when attending; **M.A. Cohen et al. (2012)**: pop-out fails under a competing task; attention is **necessary but not sufficient** (routes A, B, C).
39. Corpus callosum: **250 million** axons in Eysenck and Keane (Pinel: about 200 million); **Uddin et al. (2008)**: subcortical coordination after full callosotomy; **Tyszka et al. (2011)**: **8** developmental cases with largely normal networks.
40. **Sperry (1968)**: two consciousnesses; **Gazzaniga (2013)**: left-hemisphere **interpreter**; **Bayne (2008, 2010)**: **switch** model (its problem: rapid content shifts are rarely reported); **Schechter (2012)**: two streams of activity, one conscious at a time.
41. **VP**: "break" plus "fast" gives "breck" (**self-cueing**); **VJ**: right-hemisphere writing, left-hemisphere speech; **NG** (70): own face recognised by both hemispheres; **Keenan and Gorman (2007)**: self-awareness more **right**-lateralised.
42. **Paul S (Gazzaniga & Ledoux, 1978)**: Scrabble letters; right hemisphere **racing driver**, left **draughtsman**; chicken claw to **left** hemisphere, snow scene to **right**; the shovel "cleans the chicken shed".
43. **GH (Verleger et al., 2011)**: **69**, anarchic **left** hand takes money back; smaller **P3 (300 to 400 ms)** to right-hemisphere stimuli. **AC (Hesselmann et al., 2013)**: SOAs **100, 300, 800 ms**; P3 to left-hemisphere stimuli, **none** to right.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Conscious content** | The information a person is aware of at a given moment |
| **Conscious level** | The overall state of consciousness, from coma to alert wakefulness |
| **Access consciousness** | Consciousness whose contents can be reported and used by other processes such as memory and attention (Block) |
| **Phenomenal consciousness** | Raw, private experience (Block); for Baumeister and Masicampo, the basic level of sensation, feeling and present-moment orientation |
| **Higher-level consciousness** | Reflective consciousness involving reasoning and a sense of self extending beyond the present (Baumeister & Masicampo, 2010) |
| **Hard problem** | Explaining why and how physical brain processes give rise to subjective experience (Chalmers) |
| **Easy problems** | Explaining the functions linked to consciousness (discrimination, integration, access, control); tractable, though not actually easy |
| **Introspection** | Examining and describing one's own thoughts (Part V opener) |
| **"Yes It Can" principle** | Hassin's claim that unconscious processes can perform the same high-level functions as conscious ones |
| **Out-of-body experience** | A vivid feeling of being outside, and detached from, one's own body |
| **Temporo-parietal junction (TPJ)** | Region where temporal and parietal lobes meet; linked to self/other representation and out-of-body experiences |
| **Free will** | The idea that we freely or voluntarily choose what to do from among options |
| **Steam whistle hypothesis** | Huxley's view that consciousness signals what the brain is doing without influencing it |
| **Priority, consistency, exclusivity** | Wegner's three conditions under which a thought is experienced as causing an action |
| **Implementation intention** | A plan specifying when, where and how a goal-directed response will be made |
| **Meta-analysis** | Statistical combination of the results of many studies on the same question |
| **Self-agency** | The belief that one has caused a particular outcome |
| **Readiness potential** | Slow ERP build-up thought to reflect preparation of a voluntary movement |
| **Event-related potential (ERP)** | Brain electrical activity time-locked to a stimulus or response, averaged over trials |
| **Electroencephalogram (EEG)** | Recording of the brain's electrical activity from scalp electrodes |
| **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** | Magnetic pulses that briefly disrupt activity in a targeted cortical area |
| **Objective / subjective threshold** | Awareness judged by forced-choice performance / by the person's own report |
| **Neural correlates of consciousness** | Patterns of brain activity that differ between conscious and unconscious processing of comparable stimuli |
| **Partial report** | Sperling's procedure of cueing recall of only part of a brief display |
| **Change blindness** | Failure to detect a change in a visual scene |
| **Inattentional blindness** | Failure to notice an unexpected object when attention is engaged elsewhere |
| **Coma** | Most severe degraded state: no wakefulness and no awareness |
| **Vegetative state** | Brain-damage condition with some wakefulness but apparently no awareness or purposeful behaviour |
| **Minimally conscious state** | Degraded state with some evidence of consciousness |
| **Active task** | A task requiring a self-generated response that cannot be produced automatically (Owen) |
| **Feedforward sweep** | Fast, essentially automatic processing from lower to higher visual areas, within about 100 to 150 ms |
| **Recurrent processing** | Feedback from higher to lower areas producing integrated, top-down-shaped processing; linked to awareness (Lamme) |
| **Masking** | Suppressing processing and conscious perception of a stimulus by following it quickly with a second stimulus |
| **Global workspace** | System in which information from specialised processors is integrated and made globally available, constituting consciousness (Baars) |
| **Global neuronal workspace** | Dehaene's brain-based version, emphasising prefrontal, cingulate and parietal regions |
| **Ignition** | Sudden, widespread synchronised brain activity produced by bottom-up input plus top-down attention, making content globally available |
| **P3** | Late positive ERP component, widespread with awareness (about 400 to 600 ms in Lamy et al., 2009) |
| **Weighted symbolic mutual information (wSMI)** | EEG index of information shared between brain regions (King et al., 2013) |
| **Visual pop-out** | Rapid detection of a target that differs obviously from its distractors |
| **Split-brain patients** | Patients in whom most direct connections between the hemispheres have been severed |
| **Corpus callosum** | Bundle of about 250 million axons linking the two hemispheres |
| **Self-cueing** | A split-brain strategy of passing information between hemispheres via an external route, such as overt speech |
| **Interpreter** | Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere system that constructs coherent explanations of behaviour, even from incomplete information |
| **Switch model** | Bayne's view that a single stream of consciousness moves between hemispheres according to attention |
| **Anarchic-hand syndrome** | Complex, goal-directed hand movements that the patient does not voluntarily initiate and cannot interpret |
