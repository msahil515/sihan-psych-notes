# Chapter 4: Perception, Motion and Action

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapters 2 and 3 mostly studied vision the way a photographer would: show a person a still image, then ask what they made of it. Real seeing does not work on still images. You walk, turn your head, drive round bends, catch balls, reach for cups, watch other people move, and the world in front of you is never the same from one moment to the next. This chapter adds the missing dimension, **time**, and asks what a constantly changing visual input is good for. The answer it builds is that change is not noise to be filtered out. Change is information. The way the visual scene flows past you as you move tells you where you are heading; the rate at which an approaching ball swells on your retina tells you when it will arrive; the pattern of a few moving dots tells you that a person is walking, whether they are male or female, and whether they are afraid.

The chapter then turns that optimism on its head. If change carries so much information, you might expect us to notice every change around us. We do not. People miss a gorilla walking through a basketball game, fail to see that the person they were talking to has been replaced, and swear they would have spotted changes that nobody spots. So the chapter has two halves that pull against each other: the visual system is extraordinarily good at *using* change to guide action and to read other people, and surprisingly poor at *consciously registering* change when attention is elsewhere. That second half is the bridge into Chapter 5 on attention.

Running underneath both halves is one theoretical argument. James Gibson claimed that perception is **direct**: the light reaching a moving observer is so rich that it specifies the layout of the world, the observer's own movement and even the uses of objects, with no need for internal pictures, memory or inference. Every section of the chapter is, in effect, a test of how far that claim can be pushed before it breaks.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 4.1 sets out the four themes. Section 4.2 is Gibson's direct (ecological) theory: optic array, optic flow and the focus of expansion, invariants and affordances, then a strengths and limitations evaluation that Indian papers like to turn into "which is NOT a criticism of Gibson" questions. Section 4.3 applies Gibson's ideas to moving through the world: how we judge heading, how drivers steer round bends (tangent point versus future path), and how we judge **time to contact** using Lee's **tau**, with the evidence that tau is used but is never the whole story. Section 4.4 is Glover's **planning-control model** of reaching and grasping, which you must keep distinct from Milner and Goodale's two visual systems from Chapter 2. Section 4.5 covers the perception of human and biological motion (Johansson's point-light displays) and the **mirror neuron system**, with a careful evaluation of what mirror neurons can and cannot explain. Section 4.6 covers **change blindness** and **inattentional blindness**: how they differ, every cause the book identifies, and the magic-trick box. Every major theory ends with an evaluation, and the limitations are as examinable as the claims.

## 4.1 Introduction

### The missing dimension: time

Most perception experiments flash a picture and measure how it is processed. That method leaves out something that dominates ordinary seeing: in daily life either you are moving, or things around you are moving, or both. The resulting changes in the light reaching your eyes are not a nuisance; they are a major source of accurate information about the environment. And once you take movement seriously, you are forced to think about **action**, because you move in order to do things, and you need vision to guide what you do. So the organising question of the chapter is how we process, and respond to, a visual world that never stops changing.

### The four themes

The book announces four themes, and the section structure follows them:

1. **Perceiving movement in order to move.** How do we travel successfully through the environment, and how do we predict when an approaching object will reach us? This is Gibson's direct perception (4.2) and visually guided action (4.3).
2. **Acting on objects.** How do we use vision to act appropriately on the things around us, for example reaching for and grasping a cup? Chapter 2 already introduced theories that separate vision-for-perception from vision-for-action (Milner and Goodale's perception-action model and the dual-process approach). This chapter adds a more detailed account of vision-for-action and of the dorsal pathways thought to support it: Glover's planning-control model (4.4).
3. **Making sense of moving things, especially people.** Theme 1 treats a moving object mainly as something whose arrival time must be predicted. Theme 3 asks how we *interpret* movement, with the emphasis on biological motion seen under impoverished conditions (point-light displays) and on the role of the mirror neuron system (4.5).
4. **Detecting change.** How well do we notice changes in the visual environment over time? The answer, that attention largely decides which changes reach awareness, is placed last because it links the perception chapters to the attention chapter (4.6).

## 4.2 Direct Perception

### Gibson's break with recognition-centred vision

**James Gibson** (1950, 1966, 1979) proposed an approach to perception so different from the mainstream that it was largely ignored when he put it forward. Until roughly 30 years before the 7th edition, the dominant view was that the central job of vision is **object recognition**: identifying what the things around you are. On that view seeing is heavily cognitive. Information extracted from the retinal image has to be matched against stored knowledge of objects, which is the kind of processing Chapter 3 describes in detail.

Gibson thought this missed the point of vision. Eyes did not evolve so that our ancestors could name objects; they evolved so that our ancestors could respond quickly and appropriately to their surroundings, for example killing prey or not walking off a cliff edge. His own summary was that perception is a matter of "keeping in touch with the environment" (Gibson, 1979, p. 239). And keeping in touch, he argued, does not need elaborate inference, because the information available in the light is far richer than earlier theorists had assumed.

Two labels attach to the theory, and both are examinable:

- It is **ecological**. Gibson emphasised that perception exists to support the individual's interactions with his or her environment, so it should be studied in natural environments with moving, acting observers.
- It is **direct**. For Gibson, perceiving the environment is not mediated by any picture, whether on the retina, in the nervous system or in the mind. Perception is the **pickup of information** from the ambient light, an activity carried out by looking around, moving around and looking at things (Gibson, 1979, p. 147).

Gibson's claim about the close link between perception and action has an obvious modern relative. Milner and Goodale's (1995, 2008) **vision-for-action** system (Chapter 2) likewise treats a major part of vision as existing to guide behaviour, quickly and without needing stored knowledge.

> **Trap.** Gibson's theory is the textbook example of a **bottom-up** (data-driven) account of perception. *Classic background:* the standard contrast in older syllabi and exam keys is with Richard Gregory's **constructivist** (indirect, top-down) theory, in which perception is an active hypothesis built from impoverished sensory data plus stored knowledge and expectation. If a question pairs "direct, ecological, information pickup, affordances" it wants Gibson; if it pairs "hypothesis testing, inference, past experience, impoverished stimulus" it wants the constructivist view. The 7th edition's own criticism of Gibson (below) is precisely that he underplayed the top-down processes the constructivists stressed.

### The optic array

Gibson's starting assumption is that the pattern of light arriving at the eye, the **optic array**, contains all the visual information from the environment that strikes the eye. The optic array is structured: light reflected from surfaces of different textures, orientations and distances arrives in a highly organised pattern, not as a random wash of brightness.

His second assumption is the radical one. The optic array supplies **unambiguous, invariant information** about the layout of objects in space. That information comes in several forms, which Gibson listed as **texture gradients**, **optic-flow patterns** and **affordances**.

*Classic background:* a **texture gradient** is the way the surface texture of the ground (pebbles, grass, floor tiles) appears progressively denser and finer as it recedes from the viewer. The rate at which density changes specifies distance and slant directly, without any need to reason about it; texture gradients are treated as a depth cue in Chapter 2.

### Optic flow and the focus of expansion

Gibson's ideas grew from a very practical problem. During the Second World War he prepared training films about the difficulties pilots faced when taking off and landing, and he asked what visual information a pilot actually has available during those manoeuvres. His answer was **optic flow** (Gibson, 1950): the changes in the pattern of light reaching an observer that are created whenever the observer moves, or parts of the environment move.

Picture the pilot coming in to land (Figure 4.1 in the book shows the optic-flow field drawn as arrows radiating from the middle of the runway). One point in the scene, the place the aircraft is heading towards, does not appear to move at all. This is the **focus of expansion**. Everything else in the visual field seems to stream outward, away from that point. And the streaming is graded: the further a part of the scene lies from the focus of expansion, the faster it appears to move. So the flow field itself carries two useful facts. Where the stationary point sits tells you your direction of travel; how fast the surroundings rush outward tells you about your speed relative to the surfaces around you.

The focus of expansion is not merely a geometrical curiosity; people's attention is drawn to it. Wang et al. (2012) simulated the optic flow a person would see when travelling forward through a stationary environment, and found that observers' **attention was pulled towards the focus of expansion**. That is behavioural evidence that the visual system treats this point as important.

> **Trap.** Optic flow is produced by movement of the observer **and/or** movement of parts of the environment. Do not define it as "the pattern of light on the retina when the eyes move": eye and head movements add a separate rotational component (4.3), and the change on the retina is the **retinal flow field**, which is not the same thing as Gibson's optic flow. Also note who sees the focus of expansion as stationary: it is the point *you are moving towards*, not the point you are fixating (although in ideal straight-line travel you may well be looking at it).

### Invariants

Gibson (1966, 1979) added that certain **higher-order properties** of the optic array stay the same as observers move around. He called them **invariants**. They matter precisely because they survive changes in viewpoint: if a property of the light stays constant whatever angle you view a scene from, it can specify something stable about the scene itself. The focus of expansion is one example of an invariant feature of the optic array, since it continues to mark your heading however the rest of the flow pattern shifts.

### Affordances

A theory that rejects stored knowledge faces an obvious problem: where does **meaning** come from? A chair is not just a set of surfaces; it is something to sit on. Gibson's (1979) answer was that the potential uses of objects, their **affordances**, are themselves directly perceivable. A ladder affords climbing up or down; a chair affords sitting. Withagen et al. (2012) clarify what Gibson meant: affordances are opportunities for action that exist in the environment itself, independent of the animal's mind, and they do not cause behaviour; they only make particular behaviours possible.

Two refinements follow:

- **One object, many affordances.** Most objects afford more than one action, and which affordance actually shapes behaviour depends on the perceiver's current psychological state. The book's example: an orange affords eating to someone who is hungry and throwing to someone who is angry.
- **Learning.** Gibson said little about how we learn which affordances will serve which goals. He did, however, accept that perceptual systems can be tuned by practice, so that after experience a person orients, listens and looks more precisely than before (Gibson, 1966). Notice what this concession is *not*: it is improved pickup of information that is already in the light, not the building of internal knowledge structures.

> **Trap.** For Gibson an affordance is a property of the **environment relative to the animal**, picked up **directly**. It is not a stored association, not a memory of what the object was used for last time, and not something the object *makes* you do. "Affordances cause behaviour" is wrong (they make behaviour possible); "affordances are learned representations in memory" is wrong (that is the opposite of Gibson's view, although later theorists such as Barsalou do bring memory in).

### Findings: evidence for affordances

The book gives three studies that support the idea that affordances are perceived, and perceived quickly.

**Di Stasi and Guardini (2007): climbability matches energy cost.** Observers judged how "climbable" steps of different heights looked. The step height judged most climbable was the one that would have required the **least expenditure of energy** to climb. That is what you would expect if people perceive an affordance scaled to their own body and its capacities, rather than a neutral physical height.

**Pappas and Mack (2008): affordances without awareness.** Gibson's claim that affordances are perceived directly predicts that they can be picked up even without conscious recognition of the object. Pappas and Mack presented pictures of objects so briefly that they were **not consciously perceived**. Even so, each object's main affordance produced **motor priming**: a hammer, for instance, activated brain areas involved in preparing to use a hammer. The action-relevant meaning got through even when the object's identity did not reach awareness.

**Wilf et al. (2013): graspability speeds the motor system.** On each trial participants saw a colour picture of either a **graspable** object or a **non-graspable** one; Figure 4.2 in the book shows pairs matched for similar asymmetrical features, so the difference is graspability and not crude shape. Their task was a simple reaction-time response in which they lifted the arm in a reach-like movement. Muscle activity began **faster for graspable than for non-graspable objects**. The affordance of graspability therefore triggers rapid activity in the motor system, even when the task does not require grasping.

### A modern relative: Barsalou's situated simulation theory

Other theorists have developed ideas that resemble affordances. Barsalou's (2009, 2012) **situated simulation theory** (Chapter 7) holds that understanding a concept typically involves the motor system: to grasp what "hammer" means you partly re-enact, or simulate, the actions associated with hammers. This extends Gibson in an important way. For Barsalou, motor processes can be switched on by a **word** alone, with no object in front of you. That is useful for seeing the boundary of Gibson's theory: Gibson's affordances are picked up from the light coming from an object that is actually present, whereas Barsalou's simulations are drawn from stored conceptual knowledge.

### Evaluation of Gibson's approach

**Strengths.** The book credits the ecological approach with three achievements.

1. **Natural scenes as the right stimulus.** Bruce and Tadmor (2015, in press) single out Gibson's realisation that **natural scenes** are the ecologically valid stimulus for studying vision as being of fundamental importance.
2. **Moving observers, not frozen ones.** Following from the first point, Gibson rejected traditional laboratory research in which a static observer looks at a sparse, impoverished display. He insisted that the moment-by-moment changes in the optic array, produced as we move, are rich in useful information. Much of the rest of this chapter is built on that insight.
3. **Ahead of his time on two visual systems.** It is now widely accepted (for example Milner and Goodale, 1995, 2008) that there are two visual systems, one for perception and one for action. Gibson's claim that the perceptual system lets us respond rapidly and accurately to the environment without using memory describes, almost exactly, the properties now attributed to the **vision-for-action** system.

**Limitations.** The book lists four.

1. **Perception is far more complicated than Gibson implied.** Chapters 2 and 3 document many processing complexities (feature analysis, figure-ground, object recognition, face processing) that a theory of simple information pickup does not address.
2. **Internal representations cannot be dispensed with.** Gibson argued that we do not need to assume internal representations such as object memories to explain perception. Bruce et al. (2003) show where that logic leads: it would mean there are invariants in the light that specify a friend's face, a performance of *Hamlet* or the sinking of the *Titanic*, so that no knowledge of the friend, the play or maritime history would be needed to perceive them. Put that way, the claim is plainly untenable. Recognising your friend requires knowing your friend.
3. **Top-down processes were minimised.** Related to the second point, Gibson greatly underplayed the role of knowledge and expectation in perception. Chapter 3 shows that top-down processes matter most when the visual input is **impoverished** or **ambiguous** (for example ambiguous figures), which are exactly the conditions under which "the information is all in the light" stops being true.
4. **Motion is used in more complex ways than he assumed.** Even for the case Gibson cared most about, moving towards a goal, people draw on many more sources of information than optic flow alone, as Section 4.3 shows.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of Gibson's theory?" Correct limitations: underestimated processing complexity; wrongly rejected internal representations; minimised top-down processes; oversimplified the use of motion. **Not** a limitation (in fact a strength): that he insisted on natural scenes and moving observers, or that he anticipated the vision-for-action system. Students sometimes list "ignored movement" as a weakness; Gibson's whole theory is built around movement. The criticism is that he *oversimplified* how movement information is used, not that he ignored it.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition partly rehabilitates Gibson. Older keys tend to present him as simply refuted by the constructivists. The current position is a division of labour: Gibson's account describes something close to **vision-for-action** well (fast, online, memory-free, driven by optical variables), and fails as an account of **vision-for-perception** (recognition, which needs stored knowledge). An answer that says "Gibson was completely wrong" is as outdated as one that says "Gibson was completely right".

## 4.3 Visually Guided Action

### Why moving through the world is a perception problem

From an ecological point of view, the most important thing vision does is help us move around safely. Three everyday examples frame the section. To cross a road and survive, you must judge whether an approaching car will reach you before you reach the other side. To drive, you must avoid hitting the cars coming the other way. To play tennis, you must predict precisely when and where the ball will meet your racquet. All three involve two separable questions, and the section takes them in turn:

- **Where am I going?** This is the problem of **heading** (the direction you are moving in) and **steering** (keeping that direction aligned with where you want to go, including round bends).
- **When will I get there, or when will it get to me?** This is the problem of **time to contact**.

### Heading and steering: Gibson's global radial outflow hypothesis

Suppose you want to walk to a gate at the far end of a field. Gibson (1950) argued that optic flow solves the problem. When you move forward in a straight line, the point you are heading towards (the focus, or point, of expansion) looks stationary while the surrounding region appears to expand outward from it. Gibson's **global radial outflow hypothesis** says that this overall, global pattern of radial outflow specifies the observer's heading. Steering then becomes a simple matching rule: if the focus of expansion does not coincide with the goal, adjust your movement until it does.

### Why the real case is harder: retinal flow, rotation and efference copy

The hypothesis works neatly for someone moving straight from point A to point B. Two kinds of complication spoil it.

The first is that we often **cannot** travel straight at a goal: roads bend and obstacles must be avoided. The second is that observers constantly move their **eyes and head**, and each of those movements changes the pattern of light on the retina even when the body's path is unchanged. The pattern that actually lands on the retina, the **retinal flow field**, is therefore a mixture of two components:

1. **Linear flow**, which contains a focus of expansion and reflects the observer's translation through the scene.
2. **Rotary flow**, a rotation of the retinal image produced both by following a curved path and by eye and head movements.

Because the two are superimposed, it is often hard to recover heading from retinal flow alone. The rotation has to be subtracted out somehow. One mechanism that helps is **efference copy**, which Bridgeman (2007) describes as an internal brain signal that tells the visual system about the commands sent to move the eyes. Because the brain knows what eye movement it ordered, it can use that copy to compensate for the eye movement's effect on the retinal image (Chagnaud et al., 2012) and so separate "the world moved" from "I moved my eyes".

> **Trap.** Keep three terms apart. **Optic flow** (Gibson) = the change in the optic array produced by observer or environmental movement. **Retinal flow field** = what actually falls on the retina, which is optic flow *plus* rotation from curved paths and eye/head movements. **Efference copy** = an internal copy of a motor command (for example to the eyes) used to compare intended with actual movement and to cancel the self-generated part of retinal motion. Efference copy is a *motor* signal, not a visual one.

### Findings: heading

**A brain area for optic flow.** Much evidence shows that the **medial superior temporal area (MST)** responds strongly to optic flow (for example Smith et al., 2006). Responsiveness alone is correlational. Stronger, causal evidence came from Britten and van Wezel (1998), who **electrically stimulated parts of MST in monkeys** and thereby produced **biases in the monkeys' heading judgements**. If artificially activating an area shifts where an animal thinks it is going, the area is plausibly part of the machinery for computing heading.

**MST copes with eye movements.** Bremmer et al. (2010) addressed the eye-movement problem directly. They showed macaque monkeys distorted flow fields that simulated the combined effect of moving forward and making an eye movement at the same time. Many cells in MST **successfully compensated for the distortion**, signalling heading correctly despite the rotational contamination. So the brain does have neurons that solve the retinal-flow problem described above.

**Prisms and the walker's correction.** On Gibson's account a walker tries to make the focus of expansion line up with straight ahead of the body. Now suppose the walker wears prisms that shift perceived visual direction by **9 degrees** while walking to a target. The prediction is that retinal motion will reveal a focus of expansion out of line with what the walker expects, and the walker will correct. Herlihey and Rushton (2012) confirmed this. Equally important, walkers who were **denied information about retinal motion showed no correction at all**, which ties the correction specifically to flow information rather than to some other cue.

**More than optic flow: binocular disparity.** Given how much information a normal scene contains, it is not surprising that heading judgements use cues beyond optic flow. Van den Berg and Brenner (1994) noted that optic flow can be used with **one eye**. Yet heading judgements were **more accurate with both eyes**, because **binocular disparity** (the slight difference between the two eyes' views; see Chapter 2) supplied extra information about the relative depths of objects in the display.

**Heading without motion at all.** Gibson assumed that motion-generated flow is fundamental to heading. Hahn et al. (2003) showed that it is **not essential**. Observers saw two photographs of a real scene in fairly quick succession, with no apparent motion and therefore no optic flow, and still judged heading direction reasonably accurately.

**What replaces flow: retinal displacement.** Snyder and Bischof (2010) proposed that observers in studies like Hahn et al.'s use the **retinal displacement of objects**. As you approach a target, objects close to your direction of heading shift less on the retina than objects off to the side. Their data supported this, and showed that **nearer objects** were more useful than distant ones. But they also found that displacement information had **limited value**. It often did not allow accurate judgements, and when heading was complex (for example along a **curved path**) it was of little or no use.

### Two systems for heading (Snyder and Bischof, 2010)

To reconcile these findings, Snyder and Bischof (2010) argued that heading information comes from **two systems**:

| | Movement-based system | Displacement-based system |
|---|---|---|
| Information used | Motion (optic flow), as Gibson proposed | Retinal displacement of objects between views |
| Speed | Rapid | Slower |
| Demands | Fairly automatic | Uses more processing resources |

The theory makes a testable dual-task prediction. A second, attention-demanding task performed at the same time should have **little effect** on heading judgements when movement information is available (because the automatic system can do the work), but should **impair** judgements when only displacement information is available (because the resource-hungry system is being starved). Both halves have support: Royden and Hildreth (1999) found little dual-task cost with movement information, and Hahn et al. (2003) found a dual-task cost when only displacement information was present.

> **Trap.** Hahn et al. (2003) is cited for two separate points: heading can be judged from **two static photographs** (no optic flow), and **displacement-only** heading **is** hurt by a secondary task. Royden and Hildreth (1999) is the study where a secondary task does **not** hurt heading based on **movement** information.

### Heading versus future path

Wilkie and Wann (2006) argued that "heading", the instantaneous direction of travel, is the wrong quantity to study when someone is moving along a **curve**, because on a bend your instantaneous direction keeps pointing off the road. What matters is the **path**: the points you will pass through next. Their participants' **path judgements** on curved paths were much more accurate than their **heading judgements** (mean errors of about **5 degrees** versus **13 degrees**).

If path is what matters, drivers on a bend should look at some point on their **future path**. This is the **future-path strategy**. Land and Lee (1994) proposed, with supporting evidence, a different strategy: drivers approaching a bend fixate the **tangent point**, the point on the **inside edge** of the road where, from the driver's seat, the edge's direction appears to reverse.

Figure 4.3 in the book (from Wilkie et al., 2010) shows a road in perspective with three elements: the tangent point as a filled circle on the inside road edge, the desired future path as a dotted line along the lane, and the line of "active gaze" predicted by future-path theory, which runs out along that future path rather than to the inside edge.

The tangent point has two attractions. It is **easy to identify and track**, because it is a visually distinctive point on a road edge. And the **angle between your heading and the tangent point** gives you the road's curvature directly, which is exactly what you need to set the steering angle. That is a very Gibsonian idea: a single optical variable specifies the needed action.

### Findings: tangent point or future path?

**For the tangent point: Kandil et al. (2009).** Drivers negotiated **270-degree bends** at a motorway junction. When free to use any strategy, they looked at the tangent point far more than at the future path (**75% versus 14%** of the time). When they were then instructed to use only one strategy, driving (for example **lane position**) was **better with the tangent-point strategy**.

**Against: Wilkie et al. (2010).** Drivers were told to drive in the centre of the road, towards the outside, or towards the inside. Their gaze followed the instructions closely. Rather than looking at the tangent point, they fixated **where they wanted to be on the road about 1.36 seconds later**, which is the future-path strategy. Only drivers told to take the **racing line** (cutting the corner) looked often at the tangent point, which makes sense because the racing line actually passes close to it.

**Against: Mars and Navarro (2012).** Participants typically gazed at a point about **48 cm** from the tangent point, displaced towards the **centre of the road**. That point corresponded closely to where the car's **inner wheel** would pass. In other words, drivers were looking at the patch of road they were about to drive over, not at the tangent point itself.

**Against: Kountouriotis et al. (2012).** This study used the edge-removal logic. If drivers steer by the tangent point, then degrading or removing the **inside** road edge, where the tangent point lives, should badly impair steering, while degrading the **outside** edge should not matter. The results were the reverse on both counts: steering remained effective when the inside edge was degraded or removed, but was impaired when the outside edge was. In general, drivers fixated points on the road they intended to drive over.

### Resolving the conflict: Lappi et al. (2013)

The apparently contradictory findings can be reconciled by asking *when* in a bend the driver is looking. Lappi et al. (2013) argued that drivers fixate the **tangent point while approaching and entering a bend**, then switch to the **future path further into it**. A closer look at the original data of Land and Lee (1994) showed exactly this changing pattern. Lappi et al. also recorded drivers' eye movements along a long curve of **constant radius** (a motorway slip road); after the **first few seconds**, fixations were mainly on the path ahead rather than the tangent point.

Why switch? The tangent point gives relatively **precise** information about curvature. It is therefore most useful when uncertainty about the bend is greatest, which is on approach and entry. Once the curvature is known and the car is settled into the curve, drivers can go back to watching the road they are about to cover.

> **Trap.** "Drivers steer by fixating the tangent point (Land and Lee, 1994)" is the older textbook answer. The 7th edition's answer is conditional: the tangent point is used mainly on **approach and entry** to a bend and by drivers taking the **racing line**; for most of the bend drivers look along the **future path** (Lappi et al., 2013).

### Evaluation of research on heading

**Strengths.** Gibson's emphasis on optic flow has rightly been highly influential. Flow information is especially useful when people can move **directly** towards a goal rather than along a curved or indirect path, and the evidence suggests optic flow is generally the **dominant** source of information for heading judgements. The tangent-point idea, a single optical feature specifying the required action, is very much in Gibson's spirit and has some support.

**Limitations.**

1. Even when moving straight at a target, people use kinds of information Gibson ignored, such as **binocular disparity** and **retinal displacement** of objects.
2. On curved paths the tangent point is used relatively **infrequently**; people mostly fixate points along the **future path**.
3. More generally, Gibson's approach gives an **oversimplified** account of heading.

### Time to contact: the problem

Many everyday situations require you to predict the moment of **contact** between yourself and something else. Sometimes you are moving towards the object (walking up to a wall, driving towards a stopped car); sometimes it is moving towards you (a ball, a car at a crossing). The obvious way to calculate time to contact is to divide the object's **distance** by its **speed**. That calculation would be complicated and error-prone, because neither distance nor speed is directly available in the retinal image. Both would have to be inferred, and each inference adds error.

### Lee's tau

David Lee (1976, 2009) argued that we do not need distance or speed at all. Provided the approach happens at **constant velocity**, a single optical quantity specifies time to contact directly. That quantity is **tau**:

> **tau = the size of the object's retinal image ÷ the rate at which that image is expanding**

The logic is easy to see. As an object approaches, its image grows. Early in the approach the image is small and grows slowly; just before contact the image is large and is ballooning rapidly. The ratio of current size to rate of growth falls steadily as contact nears, and at constant velocity it equals the time remaining. The faster the image expands relative to its size, the **less** time there is before contact. Nothing about the object's real size, real distance or real speed needs to be known.

Lee extended the idea to **braking**. The rate at which tau changes over time is **tau-dot**, and it indicates whether there is enough braking time to stop at the target. Lee (1976) proposed that drivers brake so as to **hold tau-dot constant**. This **tau-dot hypothesis** is thoroughly Gibsonian: it assumes that information about time to contact is available in optic flow, so the observer can work it out from variables the eye can measure directly.

*Classic background:* the example often used to introduce tau is the diving gannet. Lee and Reddish (1981) filmed gannets plunging into the sea and found that the birds fold their wings at a roughly constant time before entry, regardless of the height of the dive, which is what you would expect if the timing were triggered by tau rather than by computed distance and speed.

> **Trap.** Tau is **retinal image size divided by rate of expansion**, not the other way round. Rate of expansion divided by size would *increase* as contact approaches; tau *decreases* towards zero. And tau specifies time to contact only at **constant velocity**. Tau-dot (rate of change of tau) is the braking variable; tau itself is the time-to-contact variable. Lee proposed drivers keep **tau-dot constant**, not tau constant (tau must fall to zero as you stop at the target).

### Three limits on tau (Tresilian, 1999)

Lee's approach has been highly influential, but Tresilian (1999) pointed out three ways in which tau is of limited use in the real world:

1. **It ignores acceleration.** Tau is accurate only at constant velocity, and real objects (and cars) speed up and slow down.
2. **It gives time to contact with the eyes.** For a driver braking to avoid an obstacle, what matters is time to contact with the **front of the car**, which is some distance ahead of the eyes. A driver relying purely on tau would stop too late, and the book drily notes the front of the car could end up smashed in.
3. **It works only for spherically symmetrical objects.** If the object's image changes shape as it rotates, its rate of expansion no longer tracks its approach. The book's example: do not rely on tau to catch a tumbling **rugby ball**.

### Findings: catching

**Savelsbergh et al. (1993): the deflating ball.** Lee (1976) assumed that judging when a ball will arrive depends crucially on the rate at which its retinal image expands. Savelsbergh et al. tested this cleverly by using a **deflating ball**, which expands on the retina more slowly than a normal ball approaching at the same speed, so tau should signal a later arrival. As predicted, **peak grasp closure came later** for the deflating ball. But the difference was only about **30 ms**, whereas Lee's hypothesis predicts about **230 ms**. The direction of the effect supports tau; its small size shows that participants were also using **other information** (for example depth cues) that largely cancelled the distortion.

**Hosking and Crassini (2010): familiar size.** Participants judged time to contact for familiar objects, a **tennis ball** and a **football**, shown either at their **standard size** or with their sizes **reversed** (for example a tennis ball as big as a football), and also for **unfamiliar black spheres**. Tau is blind to what an object is, so if tau were all that mattered, familiarity should have no effect. In fact judgements were **influenced by familiar size**. Figure 4.4 in the book plots errors for the smaller and larger objects in standard-size, off-size and no-texture conditions, with positive values meaning responses were too late; the largest distortion was with the very small tennis-ball object, which made participants **overestimate** time to contact (respond too late). Stored knowledge of how big an object normally is gets into the judgement, which is exactly what a pure Gibsonian account denies.

**Rushton and Wann (1999): tau versus binocular disparity.** In a virtual-reality catching task, tau and **binocular disparity** were manipulated independently so that they could signal different contact times.

| Conflict created | Effect on response timing |
|---|---|
| Tau signals contact **100 ms earlier** than disparity | Responses about **75 ms earlier** |
| Tau signals contact **100 ms later** than disparity | Responses delayed by only about **35 ms** |

Two conclusions follow. Tau and disparity are **combined**, since neither cue simply wins. And the combination is **asymmetric**: whichever source specifies the **shortest** time to contact is given the **greatest weight**. That is a sensible safety rule, because acting too early on a ball costs you little while acting too late can hurt.

**DeLucia (2013): the size-arrival effect and switching cues.** DeLucia reviewed evidence that observers use time-to-contact information **flexibly**. In the **size-arrival effect**, observers wrongly judge that a **large** approaching object far away will hit them **sooner** than a **small** approaching object that is actually closer. This happens because, at a distance, they give more weight to **relative size** than to tau. But as both objects come closer, observers **switch to tau** to decide which will arrive first. So the information used to judge time to contact changes as objects approach.

> **Trap.** The **size-arrival effect** (large far object judged to arrive before small near one) reflects **relative size** and fades as objects approach. It is not Hosking and Crassini's **familiar-size** effect, nor Rushton and Wann's **disparity** effect, though all three show that tau is one cue among several.

### Findings: braking

**Yilmaz and Warren (1995).** Participants in a driving simulator were told to stop at a stop sign. As Lee's tau-dot hypothesis predicts, braking generally produced a **linear reduction in tau**. However, some participants showed **large, abrupt changes in tau** shortly before stopping rather than the smooth pattern the hypothesis requires.

**Tijtgat et al. (2008): stereo vision and braking.** Drivers with **weak stereo vision** started braking **earlier** than drivers with normal stereo vision, and their **peak deceleration** also came earlier. Because weak stereopsis made distance harder to calculate, they underestimated time to contact and so braked early. If braking were governed by tau alone, which can be computed with one eye, stereo ability should not have mattered. Deciding when to brake therefore depends on more than tau.

> **Trap.** Weak stereo vision led to braking **earlier**, not later (Tijtgat et al., 2008). The mechanism is underestimating time to contact. A question offering "drivers with poor stereo vision brake later because they cannot see depth" has the direction wrong.

### Evaluation of tau and time-to-contact research

**Strengths.** The idea that tau underlies time-to-contact judgements is **simple and elegant**. A good deal of evidence shows that such judgements are often **strongly influenced by tau**, and even when competing cues also have an effect, tau frequently has the **largest** influence. Tau is also often used when drivers decide when to brake.

**Limitations.**

1. **Laboratory bias.** Time-to-contact judgements are more strongly driven by tau or tau-dot in relatively **uncluttered laboratory displays** than under naturalistic conditions (Land, 2009), so laboratory studies may overestimate tau's importance.
2. **Tau is one cue among many.** Land (2009) argued that the brain accepts every valid cue available for an action and weights each according to its current reliability. The cues reviewed above include **familiar size**, **binocular disparity** and **relative size**. Using all available information in this way is clearly sensible.
3. **Emotion is ignored.** Tau takes no account of what the approaching object *means*. Brendel et al. (2012) found that time-to-contact judgements were **shorter for threatening pictures** than for neutral ones. That makes evolutionary sense: overestimating how long a lion will take to reach you could be fatal, so a bias towards early responding for threats is adaptive.
4. **Too narrow a theory.** The tau and tau-dot hypotheses are limited in scope, and there is still **no comprehensive theory** explaining how the various factors that influence time-to-contact judgements are combined and integrated.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of the tau hypothesis?" The distractor to reject is "tau requires knowing the object's distance and speed": the whole point of tau is that it does not. Every other criticism above (acceleration, eyes not car, spherical objects, lab bias, other cues, emotion, no integrative theory) is genuine.

## 4.4 Planning-Control Model

### The question: how vision guides a reach

Section 4.3 was about moving the whole body through space. Section 4.4 narrows to a single action on a single object: reaching out for a cup of coffee. How is visual information used to produce that movement? Glover's (2004) **planning-control model** answers that two systems are involved, used one after the other: a **planning system** first and then a **control system**, although the two often **overlap in time**.

### The two systems

**The planning system** does its work mostly **before** the movement starts.

- Its jobs are choosing the **target** (the cup), choosing the **grasp** to use on it, and setting the movement's **timing**.
- Many things feed into it: what the person is trying to achieve (**goals**), what kind of **object** the target is, the **visual context** surrounding it, and assorted **cognitive processes**.
- Because it draws on a great deal of information and is open to **conscious** influence, it is **relatively slow**.
- It depends on a visual representation in the **inferior parietal lobule (IPL)**, working with motor processes in the **frontal lobes** and **basal ganglia**. The IPL is thought to integrate information about what an object is and what context it sits in with motor planning, which is why it matters for **tool and object use**.

**The control system** does its work **during** the movement.

- It keeps the movement **accurate**, adjusting it where necessary on the basis of **visual feedback**. **Efference copy** is used to compare the actual movement with the desired one, and **proprioception** (the sense of where the parts of one's body are and how they are oriented) also contributes.
- It is influenced by the target's **spatial characteristics** (size, shape, orientation) but **not by the surrounding context**.
- Because it uses little information and is **not open to conscious influence**, it is **fairly fast**.
- It depends on a visual representation in the **superior parietal lobe (SPL)** combined with motor processes in the **cerebellum**.

Figure 4.5 in the book (from Glover, 2004) is a brain diagram of the two systems, with labels for the IPL, the inferotemporal lobe (IT), primary motor cortex (M1), prefrontal cortex (PFC) and SPL. The layout to carry in your head is: planning links IPL with frontal (PFC) and object-identity (IT) regions; control links SPL with the cerebellum and motor output.

| | Planning system | Control system |
|---|---|---|
| When used | Mostly **before** movement onset | **During** movement |
| Jobs | Choose target, grasp type, timing | Keep movement accurate; online correction |
| Inputs | Goals, object nature, **visual context**, cognition | Target's **spatial** properties only (size, shape, orientation); visual feedback, efference copy, proprioception |
| Context-sensitive? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Conscious influence | Yes | No |
| Speed | **Slow** | **Fast** |
| Key parietal region | **Inferior** parietal lobule | **Superior** parietal lobe |
| Partner regions | Frontal lobes, basal ganglia | Cerebellum |

### What the model predicts

Three predictions follow, and the findings below are organised around them.

1. **Errors come mainly from planning.** Planning is slow, context-laden and cognitive, so most errors in action should originate there. The control system's job is to catch and correct them, so it typically makes actions accurate and successful.
2. **Visual illusions should fool planning but not control.** Many illusions (for example the Ebbinghaus and Müller-Lyer illusions from Chapter 2) work through the influence of **visual context**. The model says context reaches only the planning system. So responses that depend on planning should show the illusion, and responses that depend on control should be largely immune.
3. **Planning and control live in different parietal regions.** A crucial assumption is that the two systems are anatomically separate within the parietal lobe: **inferior** for planning, **superior** for control.

### How it relates to Milner and Goodale

There is an obvious family resemblance with Milner and Goodale's two-systems model (Chapter 2). Roughly, Glover's **control** system plays the part of their **vision-for-action** system, while his **planning** system shares a good deal with their **vision-for-perception** system. Two differences matter, however. Glover is concerned with how processing **changes over the course of a single action**, from planning to control, whereas Milner and Goodale describe two streams operating in parallel. And the two models place the key systems in **different brain areas**: Milner and Goodale contrast a ventral (occipitotemporal) stream with a dorsal (occipitoparietal) stream, whereas Glover puts **both** of his systems inside the **parietal lobe**.

> **Trap.** Do not merge the two models. Milner and Goodale: **ventral** "what/perception" versus **dorsal** "how/action", two streams running **in parallel**, differing in *purpose*. Glover: **planning** then **control**, two stages **in sequence** within one action, both **parietal** (IPL versus SPL). The correspondences are only approximate (control resembles vision-for-action; planning overlaps vision-for-perception). Older keys that label the dorsal stream "where" rather than "how" are using Ungerleider and Mishkin's earlier terminology, not Milner and Goodale's.

> **Trap.** Direction error to avoid: **planning = inferior** parietal lobule; **control = superior** parietal lobe. One way to hold it: planning is the *first* and lower-level ("inferior") stage that must happen *before* anything moves; control sits "on top", supervising the moving hand. Ideomotor apraxia (planning) goes with the **inferior** region; optic ataxia (control) goes with the **superior** and posterior parietal region.

### Findings: brain areas

**Neuroimaging: Glover et al. (2012).** Participants either **planned** (prepared to reach and grasp an object but stayed still) or performed **control** (reached immediately for the object). Planning engaged a network including the **middle intraparietal sulcus** and the **posterior medial parietal area**. Control engaged a separate network including the **superior parietal lobe**, the **cerebellum** and the **supramarginal gyrus**; Figure 4.6 in the book shows the planning regions, the control regions (supramarginal gyrus, primary sensorimotor area, SPL) and their separation within posterior parietal cortex. Most importantly, there was **practically no overlap** between the areas active during planning and during control, which broadly supports the model.

*Classic background:* anatomically, the supramarginal gyrus is part of the inferior parietal lobule. So although the 2012 data support the core claim of two separate networks, the regions they identify are not a perfect match to the original 2004 "IPL for planning, SPL for control" labels. This links to the fifth limitation in the evaluation below.

**TMS: Glover et al. (2005).** Neuroimaging shows which areas are active, not which are necessary. **Transcranial magnetic stimulation** can test necessity by creating a brief "virtual lesion" in a small region. Glover et al. applied TMS to the **superior parietal lobe** while participants grasped an object that **changed size** during the reach, so online correction was needed. TMS **disrupted the control process**, exactly as the model predicts for the SPL.

**TMS: Striemer et al. (2011), a failed prediction.** Participants received TMS to either the **inferior** or the **superior** parietal lobe while **planning** to reach out and touch a target. The model predicts more disruption from IPL stimulation, because the IPL is the planning region. The opposite happened: TMS to the **SPL** was far more disruptive. Striemer et al. concluded that the IPL may be needed for certain planning functions, such as **choosing the goal** and **selecting the target**, but is not involved in **detailed programming** of the action.

**Neuropsychology: ideomotor apraxia (planning).** If the IPL supports planning, IPL damage should impair planning. Patients with **ideomotor apraxia**, a condition in which brain damage makes it hard to plan and carry out learned movements, often have IPL damage, although usually other areas are also affected. Mutha et al. (2010) found that such patients were **poor at initiating movements in the direction of the target** but nevertheless **reached the target accurately** in the end. Their problem therefore lay more in **planning** than in **control**, which fits the model.

**Neuropsychology: optic ataxia (control).** If the SPL supports control, SPL damage should impair control. Damage to the **superior and posterior parietal cortex** often produces **optic ataxia**, a severe impairment in making accurate visually guided movements despite intact visual perception (Chapter 2). Two cases are given.

- **IG** (Gréa et al., 2002), a woman with optic ataxia, reached for and grasped a **stationary** object as well as healthy controls. But when the target **suddenly jumped** to a new location, her performance was much poorer. Normal planning with impaired online correction is exactly what damage to the control system should produce.
- **CF** (Blangero et al., 2008), a man with optic ataxia, was very **slow to correct** his movement when a target suddenly moved, again suggesting damage to the control system. However, CF was also **slow when pointing to stationary targets in peripheral vision**, which is not purely a control problem. Blangero et al. concluded that CF was deficient both in processing **hand location** and in detecting **target location for peripheral targets**.

> **Trap.** IG is **Gréa et al. (2002)** and is female; CF is **Blangero et al. (2008)** and is male. Both have optic ataxia and both are slow or poor when the target **jumps** (control deficit). Only CF also shows a deficit with **stationary peripheral** targets. Do not confuse IG with DF (Milner and Goodale's visual form agnosia patient from Chapter 2), who has the *opposite* pattern: poor perceptual report, good visually guided action.

### Findings: processes

**Cognition enters planning: Glover and Dixon (2002).** Participants reached for objects with the word **LARGE** or **SMALL** printed on them. Any effect of the words on grasping must come from the cognitive (semantic) system. **Early** in the reach, when planning dominates, grip aperture was **wider** for objects labelled LARGE. **Later** in the reach, under the control system, the word effect was **smaller**. That is what the model predicts: cognitive influences act on planning and are corrected during control.

**Attention is needed for both: Hesse et al. (2012).** The model says planning involves conscious processing while control is rapid and non-conscious. If so, a demanding **second task** should disrupt planning but leave control alone. Hesse et al. found that a secondary task disrupted **both planning and control** of grasping. So both phases can require attentional resources.

**Initial movements are not always less accurate: Danckert et al. (2002).** The model predicts that the early, planning-driven part of a movement is generally less accurate than the later, control-driven part. Findings are mixed. Danckert et al. discussed a study of grasping in the **Ebbinghaus illusion** in which there was **no illusory effect on maximum grip aperture even at movement onset**, when planning should still have dominated.

**Context affects control too: Mendoza et al. (2006).** A central assumption is that visual context influences **only** planning. Participants pointed to the end of a horizontal line presented alone, with **inward-pointing** arrowheads, or with **outward-pointing** arrowheads (the **Müller-Lyer** figure). Crucially, the display often **changed** between planning and movement, so the arrowheads could be present during planning, during online control, or both. The arrowheads produced movement errors **regardless** of whether they were present during planning or during control. Planning and control therefore look **less different** than the model assumes.

**Late vision is worse, not better: Roberts et al. (2013).** Participants made rapid reaches to a Müller-Lyer figure, with vision available only during the **first 200 ms** of the movement or only during the **last 200 ms**. The model predicts better accuracy with **late** vision, since late vision feeds the context-immune control system. The result was the reverse: accuracy was better with **early** vision. Visual context had a **large effect on late control processes**.

> **Trap.** Of the process studies, only **Glover and Dixon (2002)** supports the model. Danckert et al. (2002) found **no** illusion even at movement onset; Mendoza et al. (2006) found context errors during **control** as well as planning; Roberts et al. (2013) found **late** vision **less** accurate. Each contradicts a different prediction.

### Evaluation of the planning-control model

**Strengths.**

1. It develops the common and plausible assumption that movements towards objects involve **successive planning and control** processes.
2. Much neuroimaging and TMS research supports the idea that regions within the **inferior** and **superior** parietal cortex are important for planning and control, respectively.
3. The assumption that **cognitive processes** are involved in action planning appears correct. Brain areas such as the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, the **anterior cingulate** and the **pre-supplementary motor area** are involved both in action planning and in many cognitive tasks (Serrien et al., 2007).

**Limitations.**

1. **The sequence is too tidy.** Planning and control interact in complex ways during an action, and in practice it is often hard to say where planning stops and control begins (Ramenzoni and Riley, 2004).
2. **"Planning" is not one thing.** According to Glover et al. (2012) themselves, planning covers goal determination, target identification and selection, analysis of the object's affordances, timing, and computing the target's metrical properties (size, shape, orientation, position relative to the body). Such diversity casts doubt on the idea of a **single** planning system.
3. **Control is not always more accurate.** The prediction that planning-based actions are less accurate than control-based ones is sometimes wrong; various complex factors decide which phase yields more accurate action (Roberts et al., 2013).
4. **Eye movements are left out.** The model is mainly about body movements, yet coordination between eye movements and body movements is very important for precise, accurate action.
5. **The brain localisation is doubtful.** The model's assignment of planning and control to particular brain areas is questionable (Striemer et al., 2011).

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of Glover's model?" Reject "cognitive processes contribute to planning" (Serrien et al., 2007) and "imaging and TMS implicate inferior and superior parietal cortex": both are strengths. Note that Glover et al. (2012) sits on both sides: its imaging data support the model, while its own list of planning sub-processes undermines the idea of a single planning system.

## 4.5 Perception of Human Motion

### The questions

People are remarkably good at reading other people's movements: within moments we can tell whether someone is walking, running or limping. The section asks three questions. How well can we interpret human movement when visual information is extremely limited? Are the processes for perceiving **human** motion different from those for perceiving motion in general? And if they are different, **why** is human motion special?

Most of the research concerns human movement, but the perception of human and animal movement has much in common. **Biological motion** is the umbrella term for the perception of the movement of living creatures in general.

### Point-light displays: Johansson's method

It is no surprise that we can interpret people's movements when we can see them clearly. Johansson (1973) asked what happens when nearly all the information is removed. In his **point-light displays**, actors dressed completely in black had small lights fixed to their **joints** (wrists, knees, ankles and so on) and were filmed moving about in a darkened room, so that the film showed nothing but a handful of moving dots. Figure 4.7 in the book (from Shiffrar and Thomas, 2013) shows such a sequence twice, once with the walker's body drawn in and once with only the dots visible. A single frame of dots looks like a meaningless scatter. Set in motion, the dots are seen at once as a person.

Johansson found that observers perceived a moving person accurately with **only six lights** and a **short segment** of film. Later work pushed the limits further:

- **Johansson et al. (1980):** observers watching a point-light display for just **one-fifth of a second** (200 ms) perceived human motion without apparent difficulty.
- **Runeson and Frykholm (1983):** actors performed actions either **naturally** or **pretending to be the opposite sex**. Observers identified the actor's **gender** correctly **85%** of the time for natural movement, and still **75%** of the time in the **deception** condition. Deliberate disguise reduced accuracy only modestly, which shows how much information about a person is carried by the dynamics of movement.

> **Trap.** **Six lights** is Johansson (1973); **one-fifth of a second** is Johansson et al. (1980). Summaries that say "six lights in 200 ms" merge two separate studies.

### Bottom-up or top-down processes?

**The case for innate, bottom-up processing.** Johansson (1975) argued that the ability to perceive biological motion is **innate**, describing the underlying processes as **spontaneous** and **automatic**. Simion et al. (2008) supported this with **newborns aged one to three days**, who preferred looking at a point-light display of biological motion over one that was not biological. Remarkably, the displays showed **chickens**, an animal the babies could never have seen. Such early, experience-free preference suggests relatively basic **bottom-up** processes.

**But development refines it.** Pinto (2006) found that **3-month-olds** were **equally sensitive** to point-light motion of humans, cats and spiders, whereas by **five months** infants were **more sensitive to human** motion. So the infant visual system becomes progressively **specialised** for human movement during the first months.

**Attention matters when the input is degraded: Thornton et al. (2002).** Observers had to report the **direction** (left or right) a point-light walker was moving in, with the walker embedded among masking dots. There were two kinds of mask:

1. **Scrambled mask**, in which each masking dot copied the motion of one of the walker's dots, so the mask was made of the same local movements as the walker.
2. **Random mask**, in which each masking dot moved randomly.

The scrambled mask made the task harder, because the walker was difficult to separate from a mask built from walker-like motions. The task was done either alone (baseline) or together with an attention-demanding secondary task (**dual-task** condition). Figure 4.8 in the book plots percentage correct for each combination. The key result: the dual task **greatly impaired** direction judgements **only with the scrambled mask**. With the random mask, divided attention made little difference. So **top-down processes such as attention** become important for detecting human motion when the visual information is **degraded**.

**Review: Thompson and Parasuraman (2012).** Their review of attention and biological motion concluded that attention is especially likely to be needed when the visual information is **ambiguous** or when **competing information** is present.

**Putting it together.** Basic aspects of human and biological motion perception may be **innate**; such perception **improves during infancy**; and **attentional and other top-down processes** are recruited when the task is relatively complex or viewing conditions are poor.

> **Trap.** "Biological motion perception is purely bottom-up and automatic" is an overstatement. The 7th-edition position is both/and: newborn preferences (Simion et al., 2008) point to innate bottom-up mechanisms, but development (Pinto, 2006, 3 versus 5 months) and attentional demands with **scrambled** masks (Thornton et al., 2002) show top-down contributions, especially with degraded input. Note the mask that hurts under dual-task conditions is the **scrambled** one, not the random one.

### Is human motion perception special? Behavioural evidence

A good deal of behavioural evidence suggests we detect **human** motion better than the motion of other species (Shiffrar and Thomas, 2013). Cohen (2002) measured sensitivity to point-light displays of **human**, **dog** and **seal** motion. Performance was **best for humans** and **worst for seals**. The decisive twist was that the **same pattern** appeared in **seal trainers** and **dog trainers**, people with extensive visual experience of those animals. So the key factor is probably not just the amount of visual experience. Instead, we are most sensitive to observed movements that **resemble our own repertoire of actions**, and a seal's movements are furthest from anything we can do.

### Is human motion perception special? The brain

Figure 4.9 in the book (from Thompson and Parasuraman, 2012) shows the regions involved in **action observation**: the inferior parietal lobule (IPL), anterior intraparietal sulcus (aIPS), ventral premotor cortex (vPMC), superior temporal sulcus (STS), the area labelled EBA, the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and the motion-sensitive area **MT+**. (The caption expands EBA as "extrastriate temporal sulcus"; *Classic background:* the standard name is the **extrastriate body area**, a region responsive to images of the human body.) Two regions are singled out:

- The **superior temporal sulcus (STS)** is of particular importance in **recognising human actions**.
- The **inferior frontal gyrus (IFG)** is involved in working out the **goals** of observed actions.

The evidence for the STS is strong and comes from several methods:

- **rTMS: Grossman et al. (2005).** Repetitive TMS applied to the STS, producing a temporary lesion, substantially **reduced sensitivity to biological motion**. This is causal evidence.
- **Structural correlation: Gilaie-Dotan et al. (2013a).** **Grey matter volume** in the STS correlated positively with detection of **biological** motion but **not** with detection of **non-biological** motion. That specificity matters: the STS is not just a general motion area.
- **Timing: Virji-Babul et al. (2008).** Brain activity while watching point-light displays of **human** versus **object** motion was very similar for the **first 200 ms**. After that, human motion produced **more activity in the temporal lobe**, including the STS. So the special processing of human motion comes in after an initial shared stage.

**Patients.** Brain-damaged patients provide evidence that biological and general motion processing can come apart.

- **AF** (Vaina et al., 1990) performed **poorly on basic motion tasks** yet was **reasonably good at detecting biological motion** from point-light displays.
- **Saygin (2007)** studied stroke patients with lesions in **superior temporal** and **premotor frontal** areas and found that their perception of **biological** motion was **more impaired** than their perception of non-biological motion.

Taken together these two findings form the pattern Chapter 1 calls a **double dissociation**: one patient with general motion impaired and biological motion relatively spared, and a patient group showing the reverse. (Strictly, Saygin's evidence is a difference in degree of impairment across a group rather than a clean sparing, so it is a weaker half of the dissociation than AF.) Either way it suggests that biological motion is processed partly by systems distinct from those for motion in general.

> **Trap.** Low-level motion processing is classically associated with **MT/V5** (MT+), while **biological** motion depends especially on the **STS**. AF (Vaina et al., 1990) is the patient with **poor basic motion but good biological motion**; Saygin's (2007) stroke patients show **worse biological than non-biological motion**. Do not swap them.

### Why is biological motion perception special?

Shiffrar and Thomas (2013) offer three explanations, and the book takes each in turn.

**Reason 1: it is the only motion we can both produce and perceive.** If perceiving an action draws partly on the systems for performing it, then motor ability should affect perception. Three studies test this.

- **Price et al. (2012)** studied people with **Asperger's syndrome**, a condition involving severe problems in social communication and often impaired movement. They performed much worse than controls on **biological motion perception** and on **motor skill** tests, and, most importantly, **poor motor skills were strongly associated with poor biological motion perception**.
- **Serino et al. (2010)** tested patients with **hemiplegia** (paralysis) of **one arm**. They watched point-light animations of a left or right arm and named the action. Identification was much **worse when the animated arm corresponded to their paralysed arm** than when it did not. Damage to the relevant motor cortex therefore reduces sensitivity to the fine detail of corresponding human movements.
- **DC** (Vannuscorps et al., 2013) is a caution against overstating the motor contribution. Born **without upper limbs**, he identified manual actions shown in **videos and photographs** as well as healthy controls, so motor experience with hands is not necessary for understanding hand actions. However, he **was** impaired with **point-light** animations of manual actions when important visual information, namely the **body part** involved, was missing. Motor experience seems to help most when the visual input is impoverished.

**Reason 2: we spend more time watching people than any other moving thing.** Jacobs et al. (2004) asked observers to identify walkers from point-light displays. Performance was much better for walkers the observer had watched for **20 hours a week or more** than for walkers observed for **five hours a week or less**. Visual experience therefore has a substantial effect on sensitivity to biological motion. (Contrast Cohen, 2002, where experience with seals did not help; experience improves discrimination among *human* walkers, but cannot make a non-human action repertoire as accessible as our own.)

**Reason 3: people's movements carry social and emotional information.** Charlie Chaplin's silent films show convincingly that bodily movement alone can convey emotion and social meaning. Atkinson et al. (2004) tested how well observers identify emotions from point-light displays; performance was especially high, around **80%**, for **fear**, **sadness** and **happiness**. Barliya et al. (2013) found that emotion recognition from point-light walkers is partly driven by **speed**: **angry** people walk especially **fast**, while **fearful** or **sad** people walk very **slowly**.

The social reason also predicts that people with impaired social skills should be worse at perceiving human motion. **Adults with autistic spectrum disorders** are significantly worse than controls at detecting human motion in point-light displays, but do **not differ on control tasks** (Shiffrar and Thomas, 2013). The control tasks matter: they rule out a general visual or task-performance deficit and isolate the problem to human motion.

> **Trap.** Match reason to evidence. Producing as well as perceiving: Price et al. (2012), Serino et al. (2010), and DC as the limit. Time spent watching: Jacobs et al. (2004). Social and emotional content: Atkinson et al. (2004), Barliya et al. (2013), autism spectrum findings. The chapter summary singles out the first two reasons (producing as well as perceiving, and time spent making sense of human movement).

### The mirror neuron system

**Discovery in monkeys.** Research on monkeys in the 1990s opened a new way of thinking about biological motion. Gallese et al. (1996) recorded brain activity in monkeys in two situations: when the monkey **performed** an action (for example grasping), and when it **watched another monkey** perform the same action. About **17%** of the neurons in **area F5** of the **premotor cortex** fired in **both** situations. The researchers called them **mirror neurons**. *Classic background:* this work came from Giacomo Rizzolatti's laboratory in Parma, the group usually credited with discovering mirror neurons.

Findings like these led to the proposal of a **mirror neuron system**: neurons that are active both when an animal carries out an action and when it sees another animal carry out the same action. The system is claimed to support **imitation** and **understanding** of other people's actions, and the idea has also been applied to aspects of **speech understanding** (Chapter 9).

**Looking for it in humans.** Finding the system in monkeys triggered an intense search in humans. Molenberghs et al. (2012a) carried out a **meta-analysis** of **125 human studies**. The most important results came from "classical" studies in which participants viewed images of human actions and/or executed actions themselves. The regions most associated with the mirror neuron system were the **inferior frontal gyrus** and the **inferior parietal lobule**, and these are the human equivalents of the regions most associated with mirror neurons in monkeys. Figure 4.10 in the book lists the full set of areas found in the classical studies: inferior parietal lobule, posterior inferior frontal gyrus, ventral premotor cortex, dorsal premotor cortex, superior parietal lobule, middle temporal gyrus and cerebellum.

**Same areas or same neurons?** Imaging shows that the same **areas** are active for perceiving and producing actions, but the mirror claim is about the same **neurons**. That has occasionally been shown in humans: Mukamel et al. (2010) identified **individual neurons** in several regions, for example the **supplementary motor area**, that responded both to **seeing** and to **performing** hand-grasping actions.

**A misleading name.** The book warns that "mirror neuron system" is somewhat misleading. Mirror neurons are usually assumed to help work out **why** someone is acting as well as **what** they are doing. But they do **not** provide an exact motor copy of an observed action. Williams (2013) makes the point with a joke: if watching were the same as precise motor coding, simply watching experts would turn us into Olympic ice-skaters or concert pianists.

> **Trap.** Area **F5** is in the monkey **premotor** cortex (not primary motor cortex, not the STS), and the figure is **17%** of F5 neurons (Gallese et al., 1996). The human equivalents are mainly the **inferior frontal gyrus** and **inferior parietal lobule** (Molenberghs et al., 2012a, **125** studies). Same-*neuron* evidence in humans is Mukamel et al. (2010), supplementary motor area. Imaging alone shows same-*area* activation, which is weaker evidence.

### Findings: what the mirror neuron system does

**Necessary for imitation? Mengotti et al. (2013).** Activation of mirror-system regions while watching others act is well established (Cook et al., 2014), but that is only **correlational**; it does not show the system is **necessary** for imitation. Mengotti et al. used **TMS** to disrupt areas within the mirror neuron system and found, as predicted, that participants' ability to **imitate** another person's actions was impaired.

**Sensitive to meaning, not just movement: Umiltà et al. (2001).** In a **fully visible** condition, monkeys watched an experimenter perform an action directed at an object. In a **hidden** condition, they watched the same action but its crucial final part took place **behind a screen**; before each trial the monkeys saw food being placed behind the screen, so they knew what the hand was reaching for. Three results:

1. **More than half** of the mirror neurons tested **fired in the hidden condition**.
2. About **half** of those fired **as strongly** as in the fully visible condition.
3. In a third condition, identical to the hidden one except that the monkeys **knew no food** had been placed behind the screen, the neurons that had responded in the hidden condition **did not fire**, even though the experimenter's visible movements were the same.

So mirror-neuron activity depended on the **meaning** of the observed action (a reach for food) rather than on its visible surface.

**Sensitive to context and intention: Iacoboni et al. (2005).** The same physical action can have different intentions depending on context. Iacoboni et al. compared two conditions in humans:

1. **Intention condition:** film clips of a scene with a teapot, mug, biscuits, a jar and so on, shown either **before use** (a drinking context) or **after use** (a cleaning context), with a hand grasping a cup in a different way in each scene.
2. **Action condition:** the same grasping actions with **no context**, so the purpose of the grasp could not be worked out.

There was **more mirror-system activity in the intention condition**. Since only in that condition could participants infer why the cup was being grasped, the result suggests the mirror neuron system contributes to understanding the **intentions** behind actions.

**But goal understanding needs more: Lingnau and Petris (2013).** Two groups saw the same point-light displays of human actions; only one group was asked to **identify the goal** of each action. Areas in **prefrontal cortex**, associated with high-level cognition, were **more active** when goal identification was required. Understanding another person's goals from their movements seems to involve more complex cognitive processes than those carried out within the mirror neuron system itself.

### Evaluation of research on human motion and mirror neurons

**Strengths.**

1. Our ability to perceive human or biological motion from **very limited visual information** is impressive.
2. The brain areas involved in human motion perception **differ somewhat** from those for motion in general.
3. Human motion perception is special because it is the **only motion we can both perceive and produce**.
4. There is **some support** for a mirror neuron system that helps us imitate and understand other people's movements.

**Limitations.**

1. **Interaction of bottom-up and top-down processes.** Much remains unknown about how they combine when we perceive biological motion.
2. **Exaggerated claims.** Eagle et al. (2007) described the mirror neuron system as producing automatic, unconscious, non-inferential simulation in the observer of the other person's actions, emotions and sensations. The evidence does not support claims that broad: understanding someone's goals from their actions involves more than mirror-system processes (Lingnau and Petris, 2013). Csibra (2008) put the alternative crisply: mirror neurons more probably "reflect action understanding rather than contribute to it".
3. **Mirror neurons cannot explain all of action understanding.** Gallese and Sinigaglia (2014) point out that understanding an action means representing which goals it is aimed at, identifying the beliefs, desires and intentions that explain why it happened, and seeing how those reasons connect to the agent and the action. That is far more than motor resonance.
4. **The definition varies.** Mirror neurons respond to both observing and executing actions, but researchers disagree about whether the observed and executed actions must be **the same** or only **broadly similar** (Cook et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** The line that mirror neurons "reflect action understanding rather than contribute to it" is **Csibra (2008)**, not Williams (2013), whose point was the joke about not becoming an ice-skater. The book's verdict is that the system is **important but oversold**; popular claims that mirror neurons explain empathy, autism or language are not its position.

## 4.6 Change Blindness

### The illusion of a rich visual world

Everything so far in the chapter has shown change as a gift: it tells us where we are going, when things will arrive and what other people are doing. This last section shows the other side. The perceptual system does **not** always respond appropriately to changes in the visual environment.

Look around you. You almost certainly feel that you are seeing a vivid, detailed picture of the whole scene, and so you probably believe you would immediately notice any reasonably large change in it. That belief is usually wrong. Two phenomena show how wrong.

- **Change blindness** is, in Jensen et al.'s (2011) phrase, "the surprising failure to detect a substantial visual change". Something in the scene alters between one view and the next, and the observer does not notice.
- **Inattentional blindness** (also defined by Jensen et al., 2011) is the failure to notice an **unexpected** item that is **fully visible**, because attention is directed to other aspects of the display. Nothing changes; something new simply appears and goes unseen.

### Inattentional blindness: the gorilla and the cross

**Simons and Chabris (1999): the gorilla.** Observers watched a video of students dressed in **white** passing a ball to one another, and had to **count the passes**. Partway through, a woman in a **black gorilla suit** walked into the shot, faced the camera, thumped her chest and walked off; she was on screen for **nine seconds** (Figure 4.11 in the book shows a frame of this). Almost everyone is certain they would see her. In fact **50%** of observers **completely failed to notice the gorilla**. It is the most famous demonstration of inattentional blindness. *Classic background:* the study was a modern version of Neisser's 1970s demonstrations in which a woman carrying an umbrella walked through a filmed ball game and was often missed.

**Mack and Rock (1998): the original research.** The original research on inattentional blindness was Mack and Rock's (*Classic background:* their 1998 book also gave the phenomenon its name). In their early experiments observers fixated the point where the two arms of a **cross** met; the cross was shown for **200 ms**, and the task was to say which arm was **longer**. Then, without warning, on the **third or fourth** trial a critical stimulus (a coloured spot, for example) was added inside one quadrant of the cross, no more than **2.3 degrees** from where the observer was looking. About a quarter of observers (**25%** on average) never noticed it.

Mack and Rock then rearranged the display: the critical stimulus appeared **at the fixation point** itself, and the cross was centred about **2 degrees away** from fixation. They expected that putting the unexpected stimulus exactly where the eyes were pointing would abolish inattentional blindness. Instead, **detection fell to between 40% and 60%**, so inattentional blindness actually got *worse*. Their interpretation: objects at fixation are normally the focus of attention, but when the task requires attending **away** from fixation (to the arms of the off-centre cross), attention to whatever is at fixation is **actively inhibited**.

> **Trap.** Moving the unexpected stimulus **onto fixation** made it **less** likely to be seen (detection **40% to 60%**, versus 25% missed in the original version). Where the **eyes** point and where **attention** goes are not the same thing, a lesson that runs through the whole section.

### Why change blindness matters

Change blindness has received more research than inattentional blindness. The book gives four reasons why it is an important phenomenon:

1. The findings are **striking and counterintuitive**, so they demand new theoretical thinking.
2. Change blindness research has greatly clarified the **role of attention in scene perception**, which is why the topic sits at the end of the perception chapters, directly before the chapter on attention.
3. It has illuminated the processes behind our **conscious awareness** of the visual world.
4. Most perception studies look at processing of **single** stimuli, whereas change blindness studies focus on **dynamic processes over time**.

### Change blindness in everyday life

You have almost certainly experienced change blindness in the cinema. When a scene is shot more than once, **continuity errors** creep in between takes, and audiences rarely notice. In the Bond film *Skyfall*, a car following Bond is white in one shot, black in the next and white again after that. In *Avatar*, Princess Neytiri's pigtail lies down her back in one shot and over her right shoulder in the next.

### Change blindness blindness

We greatly **overestimate** our ability to detect changes. Levin and Simons (1997) showed participants videos of two people talking in a restaurant; in one video the **plates changed from red to white**, in another a **scarf** worn by one of the diners **disappeared**. **None** of their participants detected any of the changes. Levin et al. (2002) later showed the same videos and asked people whether they thought they would have noticed the changes **without being warned**. **46%** claimed they would have noticed the change in plate colour, and **78%** the disappearing scarf. Levin et al. called this wildly optimistic belief **change blindness blindness**.

Two explanations are offered.

- **Filling in.** Basic visual processes give clear, detailed information only near the **point of fixation**. Top-down processes then **fill in the gaps**, producing the illusion that the whole scene is seen clearly and in detail (Freeman and Simoncelli, 2011; Chapter 5). Since we never experience the gaps, we do not realise how little is actually registered.
- **Overconfidence.** Loussouarn et al. (2011) found that change blindness blindness was most common when observers were led to believe they had detected changes **quickly** and **successfully**. It therefore depends partly on **overconfidence**.

### Change blindness versus inattentional blindness

**Similarities.** In both, an observer fails to detect a visual event that is in plain sight, and in both a **failure of attention** often (but not always) plays an important part.

**Differences.** Jensen et al. (2011) identify three major differences:

1. **Effect of warning.** Telling observers to look for changes does not make change detection easy; it can still be hard. Telling observers to look for an unexpected object makes inattentional blindness tasks **trivially easy**.
2. **Memory.** Change blindness involves **memory**, because the pre-change and post-change versions must be compared across time. Inattentional blindness does **not**: the unexpected object is simply there to be seen.
3. **A demanding primary task.** Inattentional blindness arises when attention is engaged by a **demanding task** (counting passes, judging arm length). Change blindness does not require such a task.

| | Change blindness | Inattentional blindness |
|---|---|---|
| What is missed | A **change** to something in the scene | An **unexpected new** object, fully visible |
| Memory needed? | **Yes** (compare before and after) | **No** |
| Demanding distractor task? | Not required | Typically **required** (in lab studies) |
| Effect of being told to look | Detection can **still be hard** | Detection becomes **trivially easy** |
| Classic demonstrations | Rensink et al. (1997); Levin and Simons (1997); continuity errors | Mack and Rock (1998); Simons and Chabris (1999) gorilla |

**The five processes needed to detect a change.** Because change detection is more complex, Jensen et al. (2011) set out five separate processes that must all succeed:

1. **Attend** to the place where the change occurs.
2. **Encode** what was there before the change.
3. **Encode** what is there after the change.
4. **Compare** the before and after representations.
5. **Become consciously aware** of the mismatch.

Failure at **any** of these stages produces change blindness, which is why there is no single cause.

**Why we usually do notice changes.** In real life many changes are noticed because they produce a **motion signal**: something visibly moves or flickers as it changes. Experimenters prevent this by making the change **during a saccade** (a rapid eye movement), **during a brief blank interval** between the original and the altered picture, or **during an eyeblink**. In each case the transient that would normally draw attention to the change is masked.

*Classic background:* the brief-blank technique is known as the **flicker paradigm**; it is the method associated with Rensink and colleagues' 1997 work, in which the original and changed images alternate with a blank in between until the observer spots the difference. A real-world version is Simons and Levin's (1998) study in which a stranger asking a passer-by for directions was swapped for a different person while a door was carried between them, and many passers-by did not notice.

> **Trap.** The cleanest discriminator between the two blindnesses is **memory**: change blindness needs a **comparison** across time, inattentional blindness does not. A question describing "an unexpected object appears while the person is busy with a task and is not seen" is **inattentional blindness**, however striking the object. A question describing "something in the scene is altered across a cut, blink or blank and not noticed" is **change blindness**. Both are failures of **conscious detection**, not of the eyes.

### What causes change blindness?

There is no single, simple answer, because change detection needs all five processes to succeed. The book reviews evidence for failures at each point.

**Attention goes to what matters: Rensink et al. (1997).** We tend to attend to the parts of a scene most likely to hold interesting or important information. In Figure 4.12 in the book, one picture pair changes the position of a **railing** and the other changes the position of a **helicopter** by a comparable amount. Observers took on average **10.4 seconds** to spot the railing change but only **2.6 seconds** to spot the helicopter change. The railing's height is of marginal interest; the helicopter's position is of central interest. Changes to central-interest items are detected far faster.

**Attention is necessary but not sufficient: Hollingworth and Henderson (2002).** Eye movements were recorded while observers looked at scenes (a kitchen, a living room) for several seconds, on the assumption that the object being fixated at any moment was being attended. Two kinds of change could occur:

1. A **type change**: an object replaced by one from a **different category** (a plate becomes a bowl).
2. A **token change**: an object replaced by a different member of the **same category** (one plate becomes another plate).

Four findings emerged (Figure 4.13 in the book plots them):

1. **Fixation before the change mattered a great deal.** Detection was much better when the changed object had been **fixated before** the change, and there was very little accurate detection for objects not fixated beforehand.
2. **But fixation was not enough.** There was still change blindness for **60%** of objects that **had** been fixated before they were changed. Attention to the object was therefore **necessary but not sufficient**.
3. **The representation persisted.** The **number of fixations on other objects** between the last fixation on the target and the change had **no systematic effect**. A visual representation of an attended object lasts for some time after attention leaves it.
4. **Type beats token.** Detection was much better for **type** changes than **token** changes, which makes sense because type changes are more dramatic and obvious.

> **Trap.** **Type** change = a different **category** (plate to bowl); **token** change = another member of the **same** category. Students often reverse them. Type changes are detected better.

**The changed object is processed even when the change is missed: Busch (2013).** Participants who showed either change blindness or change detection took a **recognition memory** test. Even those who were change blind showed **some memory** for both the pre-change and post-change objects. So the changed object was processed to some degree despite the failure to detect the change.

**Failure to compare: Angelone et al. (2003).** If change blindness sometimes occurs because the two representations are not **compared** (process 4), then change-blind observers might still retain the pre-change information. In one experiment observers watched a video in which the **identity of the central actor changed**, then picked the pre-change actor from photographs of **four people**. Observers who were **change blind** did **as well** on this line-up as those who **detected** the change (**53% versus 46%**). Information about the pre-change actor was available; it simply had not been compared with the post-change actor.

**Two different failures in one real-world study: Varakin et al. (2007).** A **coloured binder** was swapped for one of a different colour while participants' **eyes were closed**. Among those who showed change blindness, some nevertheless **remembered both colours**, so they had failed at the **comparison** stage. Others had **poor memory** for both colours, so they had failed to **represent** the information in memory at all (the encoding stages). One phenomenon, two distinct causes.

**Rapid decay and overwriting: Landman et al. (2003).** Landman et al. argued that we do initially form **detailed** representations of a scene, but these **decay quickly** or are **overwritten** by what comes next. Each trial began with **eight rectangles** in mixed orientations (horizontal or vertical); **1,600 ms** later a second set of eight appeared, and the observer judged whether any rectangle's orientation was now different. There was very little change blindness **provided attention was cued** to the rectangle that might change **within 900 ms** of the first array disappearing. So we have access to fairly detailed information about the current scene for **almost one second**. What we see now must, however, not be disrupted by what we see next. When the previous scene is **overwritten** by the current one, change blindness is a common consequence.

**Sensing versus seeing: Busch et al. (2009).** Busch et al. distinguished two kinds of change detection: **sensing** that something has changed without conscious awareness of **which** object changed, and **seeing** the object that has changed. Using **event-related potentials (ERPs)**, they found that ERP components linked to **selective attention** and to **conscious processing** of visual changes were associated with **seeing** but **not** with sensing. Much research is problematic because it does not separate the two.

**Global change can be sensed: Howe and Webb (2014).** Howe and Webb argued that sensing often happens when observers detect a **global** change across a scene. Observers saw an array of **30 discs** (**15 red, 15 green**). On some trials, **three discs of the same colour** switched colour (red to green or green to red). On **24%** of trials, observers knew the array had changed without being able to identify any of the discs that had changed. Sensing a global change without seeing which objects changed was therefore common.

**Stability over accuracy: Fischer and Whitney (2014).** This is offered as a new theoretical approach. Fischer and Whitney argued that the visual system **sacrifices some accuracy** in order to give us **continuous, stable** perception of the environment. Observers reported the perceived **orientation of black-and-white gratings** presented several seconds apart. The perceived orientation of each grating was **biased towards the orientation of the previous grating**, even when that grating had appeared **ten seconds** earlier. If the system is built to smooth the present towards the recent past, it will tend to absorb changes rather than flag them, which makes us susceptible to change blindness. *Classic background:* this kind of carry-over is now widely called **serial dependence**, and Fischer and Whitney described the window over which it operates as a "continuity field".

| Cause of change blindness | Process that fails (of Jensen's five) | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Attention not at the change location | 1 (attention) | Rensink et al. (1997); Hollingworth and Henderson (2002) |
| Pre- or post-change not encoded | 2 and 3 (encoding) | Varakin et al. (2007), poor-memory subgroup |
| Representations not compared | 4 (comparison) | Angelone et al. (2003); Varakin et al. (2007), good-memory subgroup; Busch (2013) |
| Representation decays or is overwritten | 2 to 4 | Landman et al. (2003) |
| Change sensed but not consciously seen | 5 (conscious recognition) | Busch et al. (2009); Howe and Webb (2014) |
| System favours stability over accuracy | Comparison biased towards past | Fischer and Whitney (2014) |

### What causes inattentional blindness?

Jensen et al. (2011) point out that whether an unexpected object is detected in inattentional blindness studies depends heavily on the **probability that it attracts attention**. Two factors are especially important:

1. The **similarity** of the unexpected object to the **task-relevant** stimuli.
2. The observer's **available processing resources**.

**Similarity of features: Simons and Chabris (1999), second experiment.** In the gorilla study, similarity (or rather dissimilarity) was at work: the gorilla was **black**, while the team whose passes were counted wore **white**. In a further experiment, observers counted passes by **either** the white team **or** the black team. When the attended team wore **white**, only **42%** detected the gorilla, replicating the earlier result. When the attended team wore **black**, **83%** detected it. An unexpected object that shares features (here colour) with what you are attending to is much more likely to break through.

**Similarity of category: Most (2013).** Most argued that similarity in **semantic category** also matters. One group tracked a set of **numbers** (2, 4, 7, 9), the other a set of **letters** (A, H, L, U). On the critical trial something unexpected stayed on screen for **seven seconds**: either the letter **E** or the number **3**. The clever detail is that the E and the 3 were **visually identical** apart from being **mirror images** of each other, so any difference in noticing them cannot come from visual features. Observers were **far more likely to notice** the unexpected item when it came from the **category they were tracking** (Figure 4.16: an E was noticed more by letter-trackers, a 3 by number-trackers). Inattentional blindness can therefore depend on **attentional sets** defined by semantic categories.

**Processing resources: Richards et al. (2012).** **Working memory capacity**, which is related to the availability of processing resources and attentional control, was examined. People **high** in working memory capacity were **less** likely to show inattentional blindness than low scorers.

**Attentional set can matter more than resources: Eitam et al. (2013).** Richards et al.'s findings suggest that inattentional blindness reflects **insufficient attentional resources**, but that is not the whole story. Eitam et al. used very simple stimuli: a **coloured circle** surrounded by a **differently coloured ring**. When observers tried to identify **both** colours, performance was close to perfect, so they clearly had the resources to process both. When they were told to focus on **only** the circle or **only** the ring, clear inattentional blindness appeared: **20%** failed to identify the **task-irrelevant** colour. With resources plainly sufficient, it was the **attentional set**, not a shortage of capacity, that caused the failure.

> **Trap.** Richards et al. (2012) points to **resources**; Eitam et al. (2013) shows that **attentional set** can matter more, since observers who could process both colours still missed the irrelevant one. The 7th edition's overall verdict is that inattentional blindness is **mainly due to attentional sets**.

### In the real world: it's magic!

Magicians have exploited change blindness and inattentional blindness for centuries (Kuhn and Martinez, 2012). The popular explanation for a successful trick is that "the hand is quicker than the eye", but that is usually **not** the main reason. Most tricks rely on **misdirection**, which Kuhn and Martinez describe as disguising the method so that the audience cannot detect it while still experiencing the effect.

Misdirection is often assumed to mean simply steering the audience's attention away from the crucial action. Sometimes it is more complicated than that.

**Kuhn and Findlay (2010): the vanishing lighter.** Participants watched a video of a trick (Figure 4.14 in the book shows the sequence). The magician picks up a lighter in his **left** hand and lights it, pretends to take the flame with his **right** hand while looking at that hand, and, while revealing that the right hand is empty, drops the lighter from his left hand into his lap **in full view**; he then shows that the left hand is empty too. Three main findings:

1. Of the participants who **detected** the drop, only **31%** were fixating near the **left hand** when the lighter fell. The other **69%** were fixating some distance away and detected the drop with **peripheral vision** (Figure 4.15 plots fixation positions for detectors and non-detectors).
2. The average **distance between fixation and the drop** was the **same** for those who detected the drop peripherally and those who missed it. So where the eyes were pointing did not decide who saw it.
3. After the drop, participants who detected it with peripheral vision moved their eyes to the left hand **much faster** than those who missed it (**650 ms versus 1,712 ms**).

The interpretation turns on the difference between two kinds of attention. The drop could be detected by **overt attention** (attention directed at the point of fixation) or by **covert attention** (attention directed away from where the eyes are pointing). Covert attention proved surprisingly effective, because the visual system is very good at detecting **movement in the periphery** (Chapter 2). The quick eye movement to the left hand in detectors suggests their covert attention had caught the drop and then pulled their eyes to it.

**Misdirection versus laboratory inattentional blindness (Kuhn and Tatler, 2011).** Many studies, including Kuhn and Findlay's, show inattentional blindness caused by misdirection. But there is an important difference from the usual laboratory paradigm. Most inattentional blindness studies (for example the gorilla) need an explicit **distractor task** to lower the chance of noticing the unexpected object. Misdirection achieves inattentional blindness **without any explicit distractor task**, which makes it both more impressive and more realistic.

**Smith et al. (2012): change blindness for a fixated coin.** In this trick a coin was passed from one hand to the other and dropped on the table, and participants had to guess whether it would land heads or tails. On a critical trial the coin was **switched**: a UK **1p** for a **2p**, a **50p** for an **old 10p**, or a US **quarter** for a **Kennedy half dollar**. **Every participant fixated the coin** the whole time it was visible, yet **88% or more failed to notice** the change. Change blindness can therefore occur even for an object that is **fixated and attended**. More precisely, an object can be attended without its **task-irrelevant features** (here its identity, since the task was about heads or tails) being processed thoroughly.

> **Trap.** The two magic findings look contradictory but make one point. You can **see** what you are **not looking at** through **covert** attention (Kuhn and Findlay, 2010), and you can **miss** a change in what you **are** looking at, because attending to an object does not guarantee processing its task-irrelevant features (Smith et al., 2012). Fixation is not attention, and attention is not complete processing.

### Evaluation of research on change blindness and inattentional blindness

**Strengths.** Both phenomena are robust and matter in **everyday life**, from films to magic tricks. Most research shows that **attentional processes** are important, although finer distinctions, such as that between **overt and covert attention**, are also needed. Research has increasingly mapped the **various ways** change blindness can arise, including a failure to **compare** pre- and post-change representations. Inattentional blindness is mainly due to **attentional sets** that reduce processing of task-irrelevant stimuli falling outside the set.

**Limitations.**

1. Research has generally **failed to separate** change detection that involves **seeing** the change from detection that involves only **sensing** it.
2. Since **five** processes are required for change detection involving seeing, failure of any of them should cause change blindness. Yet only a **few studies** (for example Varakin et al., 2007) have tried to distinguish clearly among these potential causes.

> **Trap.** Older treatments present change blindness as proof that "we store no visual detail at all". The 7th edition disagrees: Landman et al. (2003), Angelone et al. (2003), Varakin et al. (2007) and Busch (2013) show that detail **is** registered, at least briefly, and that the failure often lies in **comparison**, **overwriting** or **conscious access**. Its summary names attention (covert and overt) as the single most important factor.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through the five main sections. Tracing them is the quickest way to see the chapter as one argument rather than five topics.

1. **Change is information.** Optic flow gives heading; the swelling retinal image gives time to contact; a few moving dots give a person's action, gender and emotion. Gibson's core insight, that a moving observer's light is rich, survives every section.
2. **Gibson's direct theory is pushed until it breaks.** In each domain a single optical variable (focus of expansion, tangent point, tau) does real work, and in each domain people turn out to combine it with other cues: binocular disparity and retinal displacement for heading, future-path fixation for steering, familiar size, relative size, disparity and emotional value for time to contact. Land's (2009) principle, use every valid cue weighted by its reliability, is the chapter's replacement for "one invariant specifies one action".
3. **Vision-for-action is fast, online and context-resistant, but less so than the tidy models claim.** Gibson anticipated it; Milner and Goodale (Chapter 2) gave it a dorsal stream; Glover split action into planning and control. In each case the data (Mendoza et al., 2006; Roberts et al., 2013; Hesse et al., 2012) show more interaction and more cognitive and contextual influence than a strict division allows.
4. **Bottom-up and top-down always interact.** Biological motion has innate roots (newborns, chickens) yet needs attention when input is degraded (scrambled masks). Heading uses an automatic flow system and an effortful displacement system. Change blindness blindness arises because top-down filling in hides how little is seen in detail.
5. **Perceiving actions draws on the ability to perform them, but not completely.** Cohen's seals, Serino's hemiplegic patients and Price's motor-skill correlations support a motor contribution; DC, born without arms, and Lingnau and Petris's prefrontal findings show its limits. The mirror neuron system is the same story at the neural level: important, but more likely to reflect understanding than to constitute it.
6. **Fixation is not attention, and attention is not awareness.** Mack and Rock's inhibited fixation point, Hollingworth and Henderson's fixated-but-missed changes, Kuhn and Findlay's covert detection and Smith et al.'s fixated coin all make the same point, which is the theme Chapter 5 takes up.

## Worked example

*A 61-year-old retired bus driver is referred for neuropsychological assessment eight weeks after a stroke affecting his posterior parietal cortex. His speech and vision tests are normal and he can describe objects accurately. His wife reports three problems: he knocks things over when he reaches for something she has just moved; he swears he would "see anything that changes on the road" and wants to resume driving; and when they cross a busy road he sometimes steps out late and then hurries. The team asks which deficits are likely, how to test them, and what to advise.*

**Is the reaching problem planning or control?** Use Glover's planning-control model. A reach that fails only when the target moves *after* the movement has started points to the **control** system (superior parietal lobe with the cerebellum), as in **IG** (Gréa et al., 2002), who grasped stationary objects normally but failed when the target jumped. A **target-jump** task distinguishes this from a **planning** deficit, in which, as in Mutha et al.'s (2010) **ideomotor apraxia** patients (inferior parietal damage), the movement is launched in the wrong direction but still ends on target. Add a check of **stationary peripheral** targets: if those are also slow, his profile resembles **CF** (Blangero et al., 2008), with deficits in coding hand and peripheral target location as well as in online correction. Intact description of objects alongside poor visually guided reaching is the classic **optic ataxia** pattern from Chapter 2.

**Why does he step out late?** Time to contact on a road cannot rest on tau alone. Tau assumes **constant velocity**, so an accelerating car will arrive sooner than tau implies (Tresilian, 1999). Healthy observers protect themselves by weighting whichever cue signals the **shortest** time (Rushton and Wann, 1999) and by giving threatening objects shorter estimates (Brendel et al., 2012). Ask about stereo vision too: Tijtgat et al. (2008) found weak stereopsis changes braking timing, so depth-cue integration should be assessed rather than assumed. A clinician would test road-crossing judgements with approaching vehicles at varied speeds and accelerations, not only at constant speed.

**Is his confidence about noticing changes justified?** Almost certainly not, and the chapter predicts his certainty. **Change blindness blindness** (Levin et al., 2002) is the normal state: people who detect none of a set of changes still believe they would have caught most of them, and the belief is strongest in those who feel quick and successful (Loussouarn et al., 2011). Driving is a setting where changes often occur during **saccades** and **blinks** or while attention is captured by a demanding task, and where an unexpected object (a cyclist, a pedestrian) can go unseen if it does not match his **attentional set** (Simons and Chabris, 1999; Most, 2013). His parietal damage adds a further risk if it impairs the control of attention.

**Which evidence would answer the brain question?** Imaging can show which parietal regions are active during planning and control (Glover et al., 2012), but only a lesion or a TMS "virtual lesion" shows necessity (Glover et al., 2005; Striemer et al., 2011). In this patient the lesion itself supplies the necessity evidence, and behavioural dissociations (stationary versus jumping targets, initiation direction versus endpoint accuracy) locate the deficit within the model.

**What would you advise?** Do not accept his self-report about noticing changes as evidence of safety; formal assessment of visually guided action, time-to-contact judgements and change detection is required before any return to driving. For road crossing, advise waiting for larger gaps than feel necessary, since both the tau limitation (acceleration) and any control-system deficit push errors in the dangerous direction.

## MCQ traps

1. **Gibson = direct, ecological, bottom-up** (1950, 1966, 1979); the classic contrast is Gregory's **constructivist** (indirect, top-down, hypothesis-testing) theory.
2. **Optic flow** (observer or environment moves) is not the **retinal flow field** (optic flow plus rotary flow from curved paths and eye/head movements) and not the **optic array** (the static structured light pattern).
3. **Focus of expansion** = the point you move *towards*; it appears stationary and the scene streams away from it, faster with distance (Gibson's Second World War pilot films); it draws attention (Wang et al., 2012).
4. **Affordances make behaviour possible; they do not cause it** (Withagen et al., 2012), and Gibson held they are perceived **directly**, not retrieved from memory. Orange: food if hungry, missile if angry.
5. **Affordance evidence:** least-energy step judged most climbable (Di Stasi and Guardini, 2007); **unseen** objects still give motor priming (Pappas and Mack, 2008); faster muscle onset for **graspable** objects (Wilf et al., 2013).
6. **"Gibson ignored movement" is not a valid criticism**; the four real limitations are processing complexity, rejecting internal representations (Bruce et al., 2003, friend's face, *Hamlet*, *Titanic*), minimising top-down processes, and oversimplifying how motion is used.
7. **Efference copy** is a copy of a **motor** command (Bridgeman, 2007), used to cancel self-produced retinal motion; it is not a visual signal.
8. **MST causality:** stimulating MST **biases heading** in monkeys (Britten and van Wezel, 1998); MST cells compensate for eye-movement distortion (Bremmer et al., 2010); responsiveness alone is only correlational (Smith et al., 2006).
9. **Hahn et al. (2003)** appears twice: heading from **two static photographs**, and a dual task impairs **displacement-only** heading; **Royden and Hildreth (1999)** found no dual-task cost with movement information.
10. **Snyder and Bischof (2010):** movement-based system **fast and automatic**; displacement-based system **slow and resource-demanding**, nearer objects more useful, useless on curved paths.
11. **Tangent point** is on the **inside** edge (Land and Lee, 1994); **Kandil et al. (2009)**: **270-degree** bends, **75% vs 14%** looking time, better lane position with tangent strategy.
12. **Future path wins mid-bend:** gaze about **1.36 s** ahead, tangent point only for the **racing line** (Wilkie et al., 2010); **48 cm** towards road centre, the inner wheel's path (Mars and Navarro, 2012); removing the **outside** edge, not the inside, impairs steering (Kountouriotis et al., 2012).
13. **Lappi et al. (2013)** reconciles: tangent point on **approach and entry** (most uncertainty), future path **further in**.
14. **Tau = image size ÷ rate of expansion** (Lee, 1976, 2009), not the inverse; valid only at **constant velocity**; drivers hold **tau-dot** (not tau) constant.
15. **Tresilian (1999):** tau ignores **acceleration**, gives contact with the **eyes** not the car, and needs **spherically symmetrical** objects (not a rugby ball). "Tau needs distance and speed" is **not** a limitation.
16. **Rushton and Wann (1999):** tau 100 ms early, response **75 ms** earlier; tau 100 ms late, only **35 ms** later; the **shortest** time-to-contact cue gets most weight.
17. **Size-arrival effect** (DeLucia, 2013) = **large far** object judged to arrive before **small near** one (relative size); observers switch to tau as objects approach. Familiar size is a different effect (Hosking and Crassini, 2010).
18. **Weak stereo vision brakes earlier**, not later (Tijtgat et al., 2008); **threatening** pictures give **shorter** time-to-contact estimates (Brendel et al., 2012); tau effects are strongest in **uncluttered labs** (Land, 2009).
19. **Planning = inferior** parietal lobule (with frontal lobes, basal ganglia), slow, conscious, **context-sensitive**; **control = superior** parietal lobe (with cerebellum), fast, non-conscious, spatial properties only (Glover, 2004).
20. **Glover is not Milner and Goodale:** Glover's two systems are **sequential** stages of one action and both **parietal**; Milner and Goodale's are **parallel** ventral and dorsal streams. The proposer of the planning-control model is **Glover (2004)**.
21. **Glover et al. (2012):** planning and control networks show **practically no overlap** (supports); **Striemer et al. (2011):** TMS to the **SPL**, not the IPL, disrupts planning most (against).
22. **Ideomotor apraxia (IPL)** = wrong initial direction, accurate endpoint (Mutha et al., 2010) = planning deficit; **optic ataxia (SPL)** = control deficit: **IG** (Gréa et al., 2002, female) fails on **jumping** targets; **CF** (Blangero et al., 2008, male) also slow on **stationary peripheral** targets.
23. **Only Glover and Dixon (2002)** (LARGE/SMALL words affect early grip more than late) supports the process predictions; **Hesse et al. (2012)** (dual task hurts both phases), **Danckert et al. (2002)** (no Ebbinghaus effect at onset), **Mendoza et al. (2006)** (Müller-Lyer errors at planning **or** control) and **Roberts et al. (2013)** (last-200-ms vision **worse**) challenge them.
24. **Point-light numbers:** **six lights** (Johansson, 1973); **one-fifth of a second** (Johansson et al., 1980); gender **85%** natural, **75%** deception (Runeson and Frykholm, 1983). "Six lights in 200 ms" merges two studies.
25. **Newborns aged 1 to 3 days** prefer point-light **chickens** moving biologically (Simion et al., 2008); **3-month-olds** equal for humans, cats, spiders, **5-month-olds** favour humans (Pinto, 2006).
26. **Thornton et al. (2002):** dual task hurts walker-direction judgements **only with scrambled masks**, not random ones (attention matters with degraded input).
27. **Cohen (2002):** human motion best, **seal** worst, the **same** in seal and dog trainers; sensitivity follows our own action repertoire, not mere exposure.
28. **STS, not MT+, is the biological-motion hub:** rTMS reduces sensitivity (Grossman et al., 2005); grey matter predicts biological but not non-biological motion (Gilaie-Dotan et al., 2013a); human and object motion diverge after **200 ms** (Virji-Babul et al., 2008). **AF** (Vaina et al., 1990): poor basic motion, good biological motion; **Saygin (2007)**: the reverse tendency.
29. **Jacobs et al. (2004):** **20+ hours a week** vs **5 or fewer**; **Atkinson et al. (2004):** about **80%** for fear, sadness, happiness; **Barliya et al. (2013):** angry walk fast, fearful and sad slowly; **DC** (Vannuscorps et al., 2013), born without arms, normal on videos and photos, impaired only on degraded point-light actions.
30. **Mirror neurons:** **17%** of neurons in monkey **premotor area F5** (Gallese et al., 1996); human **IFG and IPL** in a **125-study** meta-analysis (Molenberghs et al., 2012a); same **single neurons** only in Mukamel et al. (2010), supplementary motor area.
31. **Umiltà et al. (2001):** mirror neurons fire when the grasp is **hidden** but food is known to be there, and **not** when the monkey knows there is no food (meaning, not surface movement); **Iacoboni et al. (2005):** more activity with **intention** context.
32. **Csibra (2008)**, not Williams, said mirror neurons **reflect** rather than **contribute to** action understanding; Williams (2013) made the ice-skater and pianist joke; Eagle et al. (2007) is the exaggerated claim.
33. **Gorilla** (Simons and Chabris, 1999): on screen **9 s**, **50%** missed; detection **42%** attending to white team vs **83%** attending to black team.
34. **Mack and Rock (1998):** **25%** missed a stimulus within **2.3 degrees**; moving it **onto fixation** cut detection to **40% to 60%** (attention at fixation actively inhibited).
35. **Change blindness blindness** is metacognitive: **none** detected the changes (Levin and Simons, 1997), yet **46%** (plates) and **78%** (scarf) believed they would have (Levin et al., 2002).
36. **Only change blindness needs memory and comparison**; warning makes inattentional blindness detection **trivially easy** but not change detection (Jensen et al., 2011); five processes: attend, encode pre, encode post, compare, consciously recognise.
37. **Hollingworth and Henderson (2002):** **60%** change blindness even for **fixated** objects (attention necessary, not sufficient); **type** = different **category** (plate to bowl), detected better than **token**; Rensink et al. (1997): railing **10.4 s** vs helicopter **2.6 s**.
38. **Change blindness is not "no visual memory":** change-blind observers pick the pre-change actor as well as detectors (**53% vs 46%**, Angelone et al., 2003); detail lasts almost **1 s** if cued within **900 ms** (Landman et al., 2003).
39. **Sensing is not seeing:** ERPs for attention and awareness track **seeing** only (Busch et al., 2009); **24%** of trials sensed without seeing among **30** discs (Howe and Webb, 2014); orientation biased to a grating **10 s** earlier (Fischer and Whitney, 2014).
40. **Attentional set can beat resources:** mirror-image **E** and **3** noticed more within the tracked category (Most, 2013); high WMC protects (Richards et al., 2012), yet **20%** miss an irrelevant colour despite spare capacity (Eitam et al., 2013).
41. **Magic is misdirection, not speed** (Kuhn and Martinez, 2012): **69%** of lighter-drop detectors used **covert** peripheral attention, looking to the hand in **650 vs 1,712 ms** (Kuhn and Findlay, 2010); **88% or more** miss a coin switch while **fixating** it (Smith et al., 2012); misdirection needs **no distractor task** (Kuhn and Tatler, 2011).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Direct perception** | Gibson's claim that perception is information pickup from the ambient light, not mediated by retinal, neural or mental pictures |
| **Ecological approach** | Gibson's view that vision serves the animal's interaction with its natural environment and should be studied with moving observers in natural scenes |
| **Optic array** | The structured pattern of light reaching the eye, containing all the visual information striking it |
| **Optic flow** | The changes in the light reaching an observer when the observer and/or parts of the environment move |
| **Focus of expansion** | The point a moving observer is heading towards; it appears motionless while the rest of the scene seems to stream away from it |
| **Invariants** | Higher-order properties of the optic array that stay constant while other aspects change with viewpoint |
| **Affordances** | The potential uses of an object or surface, which Gibson claimed are perceived directly |
| **Situated simulation theory** | Barsalou's view that understanding a concept partly re-enacts the perceptual and motor states linked to it, even from a word alone |
| **Global radial outflow hypothesis** | Gibson's proposal that the overall outward flow pattern specifies the observer's heading |
| **Retinal flow field** | The changing light pattern on the retina, combining linear flow from self-motion with rotary flow from curved paths and eye/head movements |
| **Efference copy** | An internal copy of a motor command (for example to the eyes), used to compare actual with intended movement and discount self-produced retinal motion |
| **Medial superior temporal area (MST)** | Motion area strongly responsive to optic flow and causally involved in heading perception |
| **Binocular disparity** | The difference between the two eyes' images, a cue to relative depth |
| **Tangent point** | From the driver's seat, the point on the inside edge of a bend where that edge appears to reverse direction |
| **Future-path strategy** | Steering by fixating points along the path one is about to travel |
| **Time to contact** | The time remaining before an observer and an object meet |
| **Tau** | Retinal image size divided by its rate of expansion; specifies time to contact at constant velocity (Lee) |
| **Tau-dot** | Rate of change of tau over time; Lee proposed drivers brake to keep it constant |
| **Size-arrival effect** | The error of judging that a large distant approaching object will arrive before a smaller, nearer one |
| **Planning-control model** | Glover's (2004) account in which actions are prepared by a slow, context-sensitive planning system (IPL) and corrected online by a fast, context-free control system (SPL) |
| **Proprioception** | The sense of the position and orientation of one's own body parts |
| **Ideomotor apraxia** | Brain-damage condition (often involving the inferior parietal lobe) in which learned movements are hard to plan and carry out |
| **Optic ataxia** | Impaired visually guided reaching and grasping after superior/posterior parietal damage, with visual perception intact |
| **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) / rTMS** | Magnetic pulses over the scalp that briefly disrupt a small cortical area, a "temporary lesion"; rTMS uses repeated pulses |
| **Biological motion** | The perception of the movement of living creatures, human or animal |
| **Point-light display** | A display showing only lights attached to a moving actor's joints, filmed in darkness (Johansson, 1973) |
| **Superior temporal sulcus (STS)** | Temporal-lobe region especially important for recognising human actions and biological motion |
| **Autistic spectrum disorders** | Neurodevelopmental conditions marked by impaired social interaction and communication; adults show poorer point-light human motion detection |
| **Mirror neuron system** | Neurons active both when performing an action and when observing someone else perform it, claimed to support imitation and action understanding |
| **Area F5** | Region of monkey premotor cortex where Gallese et al. (1996) found mirror neurons |
| **Meta-analysis** | Statistical combination of the results of many studies on the same question |
| **Change blindness** | Failure to detect a substantial change in the visual scene |
| **Inattentional blindness** | Failure to notice an unexpected, fully visible object while attention is engaged elsewhere |
| **Change blindness blindness** | Overestimating one's own ability to detect visual changes |
| **Saccade** | A rapid eye movement between fixations; changes made during one escape detection |
| **Type change / token change** | Replacing an object with one from a different category / with a different member of the same category |
| **Sensing versus seeing** | Detecting that something changed without knowing what (sensing) versus consciously identifying the changed object (seeing) |
| **Event-related potentials (ERPs)** | Averaged EEG responses time-locked to events, with millisecond temporal resolution |
| **Attentional set** | The features or categories an observer is set to attend to; objects outside it are processed less |
| **Working memory capacity** | Individual differences in the ability to hold and control information in mind; high capacity reduces inattentional blindness |
| **Misdirection** | Magicians' technique of disguising a trick's method so the audience misses it while still experiencing the effect |
| **Overt versus covert attention** | Attention at the point of fixation versus attention directed away from where the eyes are pointing |

## Likely exam points

1. **Gibson's direct theory:** optic array, optic flow, focus of expansion, invariants, affordances; perception as information pickup; ecological; bottom-up; contrast with constructivist theories.
2. **Optic flow origin:** Gibson's Second World War training films on pilots taking off and landing; focus of expansion stationary, surroundings stream outward faster with distance.
3. **Affordances:** opportunities for action that make behaviour possible; state-dependent (orange example); evidence Di Stasi and Guardini (2007), Pappas and Mack (2008), Wilf et al. (2013); extended by Barsalou.
4. **Evaluation of Gibson:** three strengths (natural scenes, moving observers, foresaw vision-for-action) and four limitations (complexity, internal representations, top-down processes, oversimplified motion); Bruce et al. (2003) Titanic argument.
5. **Heading:** global radial outflow hypothesis; retinal flow = linear + rotary flow; efference copy; MST evidence (Britten and van Wezel, 1998; Bremmer et al., 2010).
6. **Beyond optic flow:** binocular disparity (van den Berg and Brenner, 1994); heading without motion (Hahn et al., 2003); retinal displacement and the two-system account (Snyder and Bischof, 2010) with dual-task tests.
7. **Steering round bends:** tangent point (Land and Lee, 1994; Kandil et al., 2009, 75% vs 14%) versus future path (Wilkie et al., 2010, 1.36 s; Mars and Navarro, 2012, 48 cm; Kountouriotis et al., 2012); resolution by Lappi et al. (2013).
8. **Tau:** image size ÷ rate of expansion; constant velocity; tau-dot and braking; Tresilian's (1999) three limits.
9. **Tau findings:** Savelsbergh et al. (1993) 30 vs 230 ms; Hosking and Crassini (2010) familiar size; Rushton and Wann (1999) 75 and 35 ms; size-arrival effect (DeLucia, 2013).
10. **Braking:** Yilmaz and Warren (1995) linear tau decline; Tijtgat et al. (2008) weak stereo brakes earlier.
11. **Tau evaluation:** simple and elegant, often the strongest cue; but lab bias, multiple cues weighted by reliability (Land, 2009), emotion (Brendel et al., 2012), no integrative theory.
12. **Planning-control model:** full comparison table of the two systems, including brain areas (IPL with frontal lobes and basal ganglia; SPL with cerebellum).
13. **Model predictions:** errors from planning; illusions affect planning not control; separate parietal regions.
14. **Glover versus Milner and Goodale:** control resembles vision-for-action, planning overlaps vision-for-perception; Glover is sequential within an action and wholly parietal.
15. **Brain evidence for Glover:** Glover et al. (2012) no overlap; Glover et al. (2005) TMS to SPL; Striemer et al. (2011) contrary TMS result.
16. **Patients:** ideomotor apraxia (Mutha et al., 2010) as planning deficit; optic ataxia IG (Gréa et al., 2002) and CF (Blangero et al., 2008) as control deficits.
17. **Process evidence:** Glover and Dixon (2002) supports; Hesse et al. (2012), Danckert et al. (2002), Mendoza et al. (2006), Roberts et al. (2013) challenge.
18. **Evaluation of Glover:** three strengths (incl. Serrien et al., 2007) and five limitations (Ramenzoni and Riley, 2004; heterogeneous planning; accuracy reversal; eye movements ignored; localisation doubtful).
19. **Point-light displays:** Johansson (1973), Johansson et al. (1980), Runeson and Frykholm (1983) numbers.
20. **Innate versus learned biological motion:** Johansson (1975), Simion et al. (2008), Pinto (2006); attention with degraded input (Thornton et al., 2002; Thompson and Parasuraman, 2012).
21. **Specialness of human motion:** Cohen (2002) seals; STS evidence (Grossman et al., 2005; Gilaie-Dotan et al., 2013a; Virji-Babul et al., 2008); AF versus Saygin (2007).
22. **Three reasons biological motion is special** (Shiffrar and Thomas, 2013), each with its evidence, including DC as a limit.
23. **Mirror neurons:** Gallese et al. (1996) F5 17%; human IFG and IPL (Molenberghs et al., 2012a); same-neuron evidence (Mukamel et al., 2010).
24. **Mirror-system functions:** imitation (Mengotti et al., 2013); meaning (Umiltà et al., 2001); intention in context (Iacoboni et al., 2005); prefrontal contribution to goals (Lingnau and Petris, 2013).
25. **Mirror evaluation:** important but oversold (Eagle et al., 2007; Csibra, 2008; Gallese and Sinigaglia, 2014; Cook et al., 2014).
26. **Inattentional blindness classics and change blindness blindness:** gorilla (50%, 9 s), Mack and Rock (1998) inhibited fixation, Levin et al. (2002) 46% and 78% with its two causes.
27. **Change versus inattentional blindness:** Jensen et al.'s (2011) three differences and five processes; how motion signals are suppressed (saccade, blank, blink).
28. **Causes of change blindness:** attention (Rensink et al., 1997; Hollingworth and Henderson, 2002), comparison failure (Angelone et al., 2003; Varakin et al., 2007), overwriting (Landman et al., 2003), sensing versus seeing (Busch et al., 2009; Howe and Webb, 2014), stability (Fischer and Whitney, 2014).
29. **Causes of inattentional blindness:** similarity of features (gorilla 42% vs 83%) and category (Most, 2013); resources (Richards et al., 2012); attentional set over resources (Eitam et al., 2013).
30. **Magic box:** misdirection; overt versus covert attention (Kuhn and Findlay, 2010); no distractor task needed (Kuhn and Tatler, 2011); fixated coin switch (Smith et al., 2012).
31. **Evaluation of blindness research:** attention central (overt and covert); limitations are the sensing/seeing confusion and too few studies separating the five processes.
