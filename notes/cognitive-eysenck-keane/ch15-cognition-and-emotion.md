# Chapter 15: Cognition and emotion

**What this chapter is really about.** For fourteen chapters the book has studied a person who is, by design, emotionally flat: experimenters keep participants neutral so that mood does not add noise to the data. Chapter 15 opens Part V, "Broadening horizons", by dropping that convenience. The Part opener explains why emotion (this chapter) and consciousness (Chapter 16) come last: both reach into almost every topic already covered. Emotion colours perception, memory, language comprehension and decisions, and cognitive activity in turn changes how we feel. The idea that feeling depends on thinking is ancient. The authors cite Aristotle (via Power and Dalgleish, 2008), who treated fear as a pain arising from the *imagination* of coming danger and noticed that people who expect nothing bad, and people who have nothing left to lose, both feel little fear. The modern twist is that influence runs both ways, and that predicting *when* emotion will change cognition is hard.

The chapter is built around two arrows. From **cognition to emotion**: how appraising a situation decides whether and which emotion we feel, and how we deliberately change feelings once they arise (regulation). From **emotion to cognition**: how moods widen or narrow attention, shape learning and recall, and push judgements and decisions in directions that differ sharply between anxiety, sadness, anger and happiness. The last third is clinical. Anxious and depressed people show characteristic **cognitive biases** in attention, interpretation and memory, and the key question is whether those biases *cause* the disorders or merely accompany them; the answer comes from **cognitive bias modification**, which trains biases up or down and watches symptoms. That is where the chapter meets cognitive-behaviour therapy.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 15.1 sets up the vocabulary (emotion, mood, affect, valence), the structure of emotions, and the bottom-up versus top-down contrast that runs through everything else. Section 15.2 covers appraisal theories, including emotional responses to stimuli that never reach awareness. Section 15.3 is Gross's process model of emotion regulation and the evidence on which strategies work and when. Sections 15.4 and 15.5 are the "emotion changes cognition" half: attention and memory, then judgement, decision making and moral dilemmas. Section 15.6 sets out the four cognitive biases and Williams et al.'s (1997) predictions for anxiety and depression; 15.7 tests the causal claim with cognitive bias modification. The one organising idea: **each mood state has its own pattern of effects**, and the pattern makes sense once you ask what the mood is *for*.

> **Trap.** The Part V opener also contains material that belongs to Chapter 16 but is easy to see in an MCQ. Behaviourists such as John Watson wanted "consciousness" out of psychology and dismissed **introspection** (examining and reporting one's own thoughts), yet Watson (1920) recommended having people **think aloud** while solving problems, which is introspection under another name; he accepted it because he could treat it as verbal behaviour. The "Tulving test" anecdote: Endel Tulving judged textbooks partly by how much they said about consciousness; the 4th edition of Eysenck and Keane gave it **2 of 525 pages** (a fail), the 6th edition **22 pages**.

## 15.1 Introduction

### Why cognitive psychology was slow to study emotion

Cognitive psychology still carries traces of the **computer analogy**, describing the mind as an information-processing device. Computers do not get anxious or cheerful, so a computer-style theory has no natural slot for emotion, and most experimenters simply kept participants neutral. Interest has since grown fast: emotional effects on everyday and eyewitness memory (Chapter 8) and on decision making (Chapter 13) are examples.

### Two questions and four topics

Two questions define the field. **How do cognitive processes shape emotional experience?** And **how do emotional states shape cognitive processes** such as learning and memory? The chapter takes them in four blocks:

1. How **cognitive appraisals** (interpretations) of a situation produce emotional experience.
2. **Emotion regulation**: the mostly deliberate processes by which people manage feelings so as to stay reasonably content and pursue their goals.
3. The effects of emotion **on cognition** (attention, memory, judgement, decision making), with each emotional state producing its *own* pattern of effects.
4. The **cognitive biases** of anxiety and depression in healthy people and patients, and whether they contribute to the disorders or are a by-product of them, tested by studies that try to remove the biases.

### Emotion, mood and affect

The book separates emotions from moods on three grounds:

| Feature | Emotion | Mood |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Usually brief | Longer lasting |
| Intensity | Stronger, so more likely to grab attention | Milder |
| Cause | Usually a specific event (for example, passing an exam) | Often no identifiable cause |

The line is not sharp, though. An emotion can settle into a mood, and a mood can flare into an emotion. For that reason the chapter mostly uses the umbrella term **affect**, which covers both. **Positive affect** means positive emotions and moods, **negative affect** negative ones, and **valence** is the dimension that runs from strongly negative to strongly positive affect: the "good or bad" character of an emotional experience.

> **Trap.** Three discriminators separate emotion from mood (duration, intensity, specific cause), and the book stresses that the distinction is **not** sharp. An option claiming emotion and mood are "categorically distinct states that never convert into each other" is wrong. "Affect" is the superordinate term, not a synonym for mood alone.

### The structure of emotions: categories or dimensions?

There are two schools of thought (Fox, 2008).

The **categorical approach** (for example, Izard, 2007) says there is a set of distinct emotions such as happiness, anger, fear, disgust and sadness, each a separate kind of state. This matches how emotion feels from the inside, which is part of its appeal.

The **dimensional approach** says emotional states are positions in a small space defined by a few continuous dimensions. Two versions are named, and the trick is that they describe the same space:

- **Barrett and Russell (1998)** proposed two uncorrelated dimensions: **misery to pleasure** (that is, valence) and **sleep to arousal**.
- **Watson and Tellegen (1985)** proposed two uncorrelated dimensions of **positive affect** and **negative affect**.

Figure 15.1 (the image is lost, the caption survives) overlays both sets of axes on one two-dimensional plane. Classic background: in the usual drawing of this "affect circumplex", Watson and Tellegen's positive-affect and negative-affect axes run diagonally, at roughly 45 degrees to the valence and arousal axes, so high positive affect sits between "pleasant" and "aroused" and high negative affect between "unpleasant" and "aroused". The four quadrants of the valence-arousal version hold familiar states:

| Quadrant | Valence | Arousal | Example states |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top right | Pleasant | High | Happy, excited |
| Bottom right | Pleasant | Low | Contented, relaxed, calm |
| Bottom left | Unpleasant | Low | Depressed, bored |
| Top left | Unpleasant | High | Stressed, tense |

**Evidence for the dimensional view.** Watson and Tellegen (1985) reanalysed several self-report mood measures and found that the negative-affect and positive-affect dimensions together accounted for **50 to 65 percent** of the variance. Baucom et al. (2012) showed participants many emotional pictures and found brain activity patterns that fitted the idea of separate, independent arousal and valence dimensions.

**Reconciling the two approaches.** The book's resolution has two parts. First, nearly every emotional state can be located somewhere in the two-dimensional space, so the dimensions are a fair description of the raw material. Second, getting from a position in that space ("unpleasant and aroused") to a specific named emotion ("this is anger, not fear") depends on **cognitive processes**. Lindquist et al. (2012), in a meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies, found that regions serving attention, language and long-term memory were active across several different emotions, which is what you would expect if categorising a feeling uses general cognitive machinery.

The strongest evidence for language in particular comes from **Lindquist et al. (2014)**, who tested patients with **semantic dementia**, a condition in which conceptual knowledge is badly eroded (see Chapter 7). The patients sorted photographs of faces showing anger, sadness, fear, disgust, happiness or a neutral expression into whatever piles seemed meaningful to them. They separated positive from negative faces without difficulty, but they could barely tell the four negative emotions apart from one another, even though the sorting task itself made no demand on language. The interpretation is that perceiving emotion has two layers: **core affect** (positive versus negative valence), which survived, and a finer **conceptual, language-based categorisation**, which did not.

> **Trap.** "Categorical versus dimensional" is not a fight with a winner in this edition. The book's position is a synthesis: dimensions (valence and arousal) describe core affect, and cognitive and linguistic processes carve that space into discrete emotions. Also do not swap the two dimensional models: **Barrett and Russell = valence (misery-pleasure) and arousal (arousal-sleep)**; **Watson and Tellegen = positive affect and negative affect**. Both pairs are uncorrelated, and both describe the same space.

### Bottom-up and top-down emotional processing

Emotional experience has two sources. **Bottom-up** (stimulus-driven) processing works through attention and perception of the stimulus itself. **Top-down** processing involves appraising the situation using stored knowledge of similar situations.

**Ochsner et al. (2009)** separated the two with brain imaging. In the bottom-up condition, participants looked at aversive photographs and simply reacted naturally. In the top-down condition, they looked at *neutral* photographs and were asked to interpret them as if they were aversive, so any negative feeling had to be built from the inside.

- **Bottom-up** viewing activated visual-perceptual regions in the occipital, temporal and parietal lobes, and strongly activated the **amygdala**, the structure deep in the front part of the temporal lobe that is closely linked with fear and several other emotions. Self-reported negative affect tracked **amygdala** activity most closely.
- **Top-down** generation recruited the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** and **medial prefrontal cortex** (high-level cognition), along with the anterior cingulate and amygdala. Here self-reported negative affect tracked the **medial prefrontal cortex** most closely, a region associated with constructing cognitive representations of what a stimulus means.

So the amygdala takes part in both routes, but what predicts how bad people feel differs: amygdala for the stimulus-driven route, medial prefrontal cortex for the meaning-driven route. Figures 15.2 and 15.3 showed these maps; their content is the contrast just described.

One important top-down process is **inhibitory control** (Chapter 5). Tang and Schmeichel (2014) found that when anger and anxiety were induced, people with good inhibitory control showed smaller increases in those emotions than people with poor control. Inhibition is therefore a top-down route to damping negative feeling, and it anticipates the emotion-regulation work in 15.3.

The section closes with two cautions. Many theories, appraisal theories above all, give top-down processing the starring role, but bottom-up processing contributes to emotional experience too and should not be forgotten. And the bottom-up versus top-down split is itself a simplification; there may be intermediate processes that are neither (Viviani, 2013).

> **Trap.** In Ochsner et al. (2009), the amygdala was active in **both** conditions. The difference examiners test is which region best predicted self-reported negative affect: **amygdala in bottom-up**, **medial PFC in top-down**. An option saying "the amygdala is active only in bottom-up emotion" is wrong.

## 15.2 Appraisal Theories

### The core claim

Cognitive processes help decide both *whether* we feel an emotion in a given situation and *which* emotion we feel. The most important of those processes, on the appraisal view, is **appraisal**: evaluating what the situation means for us. Brosch (2013) states the central assumption as the idea that emotions arise when an organism judges how a change in its surroundings bears on its own well-being.

There are very many appraisal theories; most share three assumptions:

1. **Each emotion has its own distinctive appraisal pattern.** Because the appraisal is the trigger, top-down processing is crucial.
2. **Appraisal is the chief determinant of emotional state**, more important than any other factor.
3. **Appraisal often sets off the other components of emotion**, such as bodily sensations and action tendencies.

Scherer and Ellsworth (2009) sketched appraisal profiles for the major emotions. Two examples are worth memorising because they show how a single dimension (control) can switch the emotion:

| Emotion | Appraisal profile (Scherer & Ellsworth, 2009) |
|---|---|
| **Anger** | Someone else is to blame **and** the situation is appraised as offering you control and power |
| **Sadness** | The situation is appraised as allowing very little control or power |

Not every theory agrees that appraisal is the master variable; some give it a less central place (Moors & Scherer, 2013).

**Fontaine et al. (2013)** put the centrality claim to a large cross-cultural test. Participants from **27 countries** rated, for a range of emotion words, how probable it was that various features would apply to someone feeling that emotion. The features included appraisal features and the other emotion components (bodily sensations, motor expressions, action tendencies). Using **appraisal features alone**, emotions were classified correctly in **71 percent** of cases, and adding all the other components improved accuracy only a little. That is strong evidence that appraisal carries most of the information about which emotion a person is in.

> **Classic background.** The appraisal tradition traces to Richard Lazarus, who distinguished **primary appraisal** (is this relevant to my well-being, and is it a harm, threat or challenge?) from **secondary appraisal** (what can I do about it, what coping resources do I have?). Lazarus also took one side of the famous early-1980s debate with **Robert Zajonc**: Zajonc argued that affect can come *before* and *without* cognition ("preferences need no inferences"), while Lazarus argued that some appraisal, however rapid, always precedes emotion. The 7th edition does not frame the chapter as that debate. It resolves much of it by saying appraisal can be **automatic and non-conscious** (Smith & Kirby, below), which lets subliminal emotional effects count as fast appraisal rather than as emotion without cognition.

> **Trap.** Older exam keys often equate "the cognitive theory of emotion" with **Schachter and Singer's (1962) two-factor theory** (undifferentiated physiological arousal plus a cognitive label). That theory is not part of this chapter. The 7th edition's cognitive account is **appraisal theory**, in which the *pattern* of appraisals (blame, control, goal obstruction) selects the emotion, and appraisal can be automatic. If a question is clearly framed in the 7th-edition terms, answer with appraisal and regulation; if it asks about "arousal plus labelling", it is using the older two-factor framing.

### Two routes to appraisal: Smith and Kirby

It is tempting to imagine appraisal as slow, conscious deliberation ("whose fault was this?"). Most theorists reject that. **Smith and Kirby (2001, 2009)** proposed two ways in which appraisal happens, set out in Figure 15.4:

| Route | What it involves | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Appraisal by reasoning** | Deliberate thought about the situation | Slower, more flexible |
| **Appraisal by memory activation** | A situation automatically reactivates memories of similar past situations and the appraisals made then | Fast, automatic, can occur without awareness |

The second route lets appraisal theory handle instant or unconscious emotional reactions.

### Findings: conscious appraisals

**The scenario method.** Most studies ask participants to imagine themselves as the main character in a short story; versions of the story differ in the appraisals they invite.

**Smith and Lazarus (1993)** used a story about a student who had done badly in an examination. When the student blamed unhelpful teaching assistants, participants said he would feel **anger**; when he blamed himself (for leaving the work to the last minute), they said he would feel **guilt**. Other-blame versus self-blame thus switches the emotion, just as appraisal theory predicts, and the appraisal manipulations generally had their predicted effects.

**Parkinson (2001)** was not impressed. He pointed out that the appraisal manipulations in that work accounted for **under 30 percent** of the variance in emotion ratings. The effects are real but leave most of the variation unexplained.

**No single appraisal is necessary.** Kuppens et al. (2003) examined four appraisals relevant to anger in people's recent unpleasant experiences: a goal obstacle, other accountability (someone else to blame), unfairness, and control. **None was essential**; some people felt angry without perceiving unfairness, others without a blocked goal. Tong (2010) extended this to four negative emotions (anger, sadness, fear, guilt) and found that **no appraisal, and no combination of appraisals, was either necessary or sufficient** for any of them. This undercuts the strong version of assumption 1: an emotion can be reached by several appraisal routes.

**Separating situation from appraisal.** Scenario studies such as Smith and Lazarus's vary the situation and the appraisal together, so the emotion might be following the situation rather than the appraisal. **Siemer et al. (2007)** held the situation constant: every participant met the same rude, condescending, critical experimenter, then rated six emotions and five appraisals (such as controllability, other-responsibility, self-responsibility). With the situation fixed, any appraisal-emotion link must come from people appraising the same event differently, and appraisals did predict emotion intensity. For example, appraised **personal control** was **negatively** related to guilt, shame and sadness, but **not** to anger.

**Real emotions, not imagined ones.** Hypothetical scenarios produce little genuine feeling. Bennett and Lowe (2008) instead asked hospital **nurses** to describe their most stressful recent incident at work. **Anger and frustration** were the strongest emotions, and the nurses' appraisals predicted their emotions reasonably well, with one exception: **sadness**, perhaps because people use "sad" loosely as a general word for feeling bad.

**Individual differences.** Ceulemans et al. (2012) gave people brief scenarios (one was a friend spreading gossip about you) and asked what appraisals they would make. How angry a given person became depended on two things: which appraisals the scenario activated, and which particular combination of appraisal components *that individual* needed before anger would appear. Different people have different "anger recipes".

**The causality problem.** Correlations cannot show which causes which. If appraisal causes emotion, appraisal judgements should arguably be made *faster* than emotion judgements, yet they are generally **slower** (Siemer & Reisenzein, 2007). The escape route is that the appraisal itself may be fast and automatic while *reporting* it explicitly is slow, and Siemer and Reisenzein found some support for this.

**Manipulating appraisal directly.** The clearest causal test is to change how people appraise an emotional stimulus and see whether the emotion changes. **Schartau et al. (2009)** showed films of humans and animals in marked distress. Some participants were trained in **positive cognitive appraisal**, for example looking for a "silver lining" in the situation. Compared with people who simply watched, the trained group showed reduced **horror**, reduced **distress** and reduced **physiological arousal** measured by the **galvanic skin response** (Figure 15.5 plotted these pre-training to post-training changes for the watch and appraisal conditions). A related line of work trains anxious people to drop their **interpretive bias** (the tendency to read ambiguous situations as threatening); because such bias amounts to unduly negative appraisal, reducing it should reduce anxiety, and generally it does (Mathews, 2012; see 15.6 and 15.7).

### Findings: non-conscious emotional processing

If appraisal can run automatically, emotional reactions should be possible for stimuli people never consciously see.

**Öhman and Soares (1994)** presented snake phobics and spider phobics with pictures of snakes, spiders, flowers and mushrooms, flashed too briefly to be consciously identified. Each group reacted to its *own* feared object: larger **skin conductance responses** to the phobia-relevant pictures, plus more reported arousal and more negative feeling, even without knowing what had been shown.

**Winkielman et al. (2005)** flashed happy or angry faces **subliminally** (below the threshold of awareness) to **thirsty** participants. People shown subliminal happy faces poured and drank about **twice as much** liquid as those shown angry faces (Figure 15.6 showed mean volume for angry, neutral and happy faces). In a second experiment, thirsty participants were willing to pay on average **38 cents** for a drink after happy faces but only **10 cents** after angry ones. Affective reactions, and their effects on behaviour, can be entirely unconscious.

**Affective blindsight.** Chapter 2 described **blindsight**: patients with damage to primary visual cortex who cannot consciously see stimuli in part of the visual field, yet respond to them above chance. Several such patients also show **affective blindsight**, discriminating emotional stimuli they cannot consciously perceive (Tamietto & de Gelder, 2010).

**Tamietto et al. (2009)** tested **two** blindsight patients with pictures of fearful or happy faces and bodily expressions shown to either the intact or the blind field, recording two facial muscles: the **zygomaticus major** (used in smiling) and the **corrugator supercilii** (used in frowning).

- Happy expressions activated the zygomaticus more than fearful ones, and fearful expressions activated the corrugator more, **whether or not the expression was consciously seen**.
- These facial reactions came **200 to 300 ms faster** for expressions the patients did *not* consciously see than for those they did.

The suggested reason for the speed advantage is that non-conscious emotional processing may **bypass the cortex**, taking a quicker subcortical route.

> **Trap.** Affective blindsight is **not** the same as ordinary blindsight, and neither is the same as neglect. Blindsight: above-chance responding to stimuli in a field blinded by primary visual cortex damage. Affective blindsight: discriminating the *emotional* content of such stimuli. In Tamietto et al. (2009) the non-conscious reactions were **faster**, not slower, by **200 to 300 ms**. Zygomaticus = smile; corrugator = frown.

### Evaluation of appraisal theories

**Strengths.**

- Cognitive appraisal often has a strong influence on emotion: it helps determine whether an emotion occurs and which one.
- It explains some **individual differences**: different people appraise the same situation differently and so feel differently (Siemer et al., 2007; Ceulemans et al., 2012).
- Smith and Kirby's split between **deliberate** and **automatic** appraisal has proved useful, especially for non-conscious effects.
- **Manipulating appraisals changes emotion** (Schartau et al., 2009), which points to a causal role.

**Limitations.**

1. **Overstated centrality.** The assumption that situational appraisal is always the decisive factor is too strong; appraisals predict emotions only moderately well. Appraisal theories tend to inflate top-down processing and play down bottom-up processing (Ochsner et al., 2009).
2. **Asocial research.** Studies mostly show emotional situations to passive people sitting alone. Real emotions usually arise during active social interaction and are shaped by how other people react (Parkinson, 2011).
3. **Fixation on the present.** Theories focus on emotion as a response to the current situation, but emotions often concern the past and the future. Anger may be about blaming someone for something done before, and may also carry an expectation that the other person will make amends later (Parkinson, 2011).
4. **Blurred boundary.** The theory assumes appraisals cause emotional experience, but in practice the two run into each other (McEachrane, 2009); there is no sharp line between cognition and emotion.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of appraisal theory?" Real limitations: overstating appraisal's role, neglecting bottom-up processing, neglecting social context, focusing on the present rather than past and future, and the blurring of appraisal with emotion. **Not** a limitation: "it cannot explain individual differences" (it partly can) or "it assumes all appraisal is conscious" (most modern versions explicitly allow automatic appraisal).

## 15.3 Emotion Regulation

### From one stage to two: generation versus regulation

Section 15.2 treated emotion as a **one-stage** affair: a person meets a situation and an emotional response follows. That spontaneous first response is **emotion generation**. Real life often adds a second stage. Suppose your manager says something cutting and you feel a surge of anger; you then work hard to keep the anger down and act as if nothing happened. The first reaction is generated; the effort to change it is **emotion regulation**. Gross (2013) defines regulation by the presence of a *goal*: a person is regulating when a goal has been activated to push the size or the duration of an emotional response up or down. In practice the chapter treats regulation as using deliberate, effortful processes to override the spontaneous emotional state, usually a negative one.

The distinction is attractive partly because it is easy to study. The standard design shows two groups the same emotional material. One group is told to react naturally (generation); the other is told to regulate using a named strategy (regulation). Self-reports and brain activity are then compared.

But the boundary is fuzzy (Gross et al., 2011). The two kinds of process interact and share brain systems (Ochsner et al., 2009), and generation is itself **auto-regulatory**, or self-correcting (Kappas, 2011): an emotion prompts behaviour that alters the situation and hence the emotion. Without instructions, the two usually occur together. Even so, regulation differs from generation in several clear ways, so the distinction is kept.

### Gross's process model

The most influential framework is the **process model** of Gross and colleagues (for example, Gross & Thompson, 2007), drawn as Figure 15.7. Picture a horizontal timeline of how an emotion unfolds: a situation arises, aspects of it are attended to, they are appraised, and a response follows. Two assumptions drive the model. First, **emotional intensity generally grows as you move along the timeline**. Second, regulation can step in at **different points** on it, and the point of entry defines five families of strategy. The book illustrates each with a socially anxious person:

| Strategy family | Where it acts | Socially anxious example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Situation selection** | Choosing which situations to enter | Avoiding potentially stressful social events |
| 2. **Situation modification** | Changing the situation once in it | Asking a friend to come along |
| 3. **Attention deployment** | Changing what you attend to | Focusing on distracting thoughts |
| 4. **Cognitive change** | Changing how you appraise the situation (reappraisal) | Telling yourself most people are friendly and will not judge you harshly for a slip |
| 5. **Response modulation** | Changing the response after it has formed | Hiding your feelings (suppressing expression), or venting them |

**Antecedent-focused versus response-focused.** Strategies 1 to 4 act **before** appraisals have produced a full-blown emotional response, so they are called **antecedent-focused**. Strategy 5 acts **after** the response has been generated and is **response-focused**.

**A myth about venting.** Many people believe that expressing anger gets it "out of your system". Bushman (2002) found the opposite: venting **increases** anger, because acting angry makes angry thoughts easier to retrieve. Venting is a response-modulation strategy that backfires.

> **Trap.** Reappraisal belongs to **cognitive change**, an **antecedent-focused** strategy, even though people often use it after an emotion has started. Suppression belongs to **response modulation**, the only **response-focused** family. And the "catharsis" idea that expressing anger reduces it is contradicted by Bushman (2002).

### Distraction and reappraisal: the two workhorses

Most regulation in practice happens at two stages:

- **Distraction** (attention deployment): pulling attention away from emotional processing and onto neutral material.
- **Reappraisal** (cognitive change): first elaborating the emotional information, then changing what it means.

**Sheppes et al. (2014)** built a theory of strategy choice on the timeline idea. Left alone, an event's emotional intensity tends to climb. Distraction is cheap in cognitive terms and can be used early to "nip the emotion in the bud" before the information is fully processed. Reappraisal is more demanding, because you must engage with the emotional content in order to reinterpret it, but its advantage is that a reinterpretation lasts. Two predictions follow:

1. In **high-intensity** negative situations, people will choose **distraction** more than reappraisal, because distraction blocks emotional information before it builds.
2. People will choose **reappraisal** more for stimuli they expect to **meet again** than for one-off stimuli, because reinterpretation reduces the emotional impact of future encounters too.

### Explicit and implicit regulation

So far regulation has meant deliberate, conscious effort: **explicit emotion regulation**. **Gyurak et al. (2011)** argued that **implicit emotion regulation** matters just as much. Implicit processes, on their account, are triggered automatically by the stimulus, run to completion without monitoring, and can operate without insight or awareness. A strategy used often enough can become implicit through practice. Implicit regulation is necessary for a simple resource reason: consciously regulating every unpleasant moment of the day would exhaust our cognitive capacity.

### Findings: which strategies work?

**Cognitive versus behavioural strategies.** In a meta-analysis, **Augustine and Hemenover (2009)** compared cognitive strategies (ones that involve thinking) with behavioural strategies (ones that involve physical action). **Cognitive strategies, especially reappraisal and distraction, worked better.**

**Clinical relevance.** Patients with anxiety disorders or major depressive disorder have difficulty regulating emotion (for example, Campbell-Sills & Barlow, 2007). **Aldao et al. (2010)** meta-analysed how different strategies related to anxiety and depression symptoms over time:

| Helpful (reduced symptoms) | Harmful (increased symptoms) |
|---|---|
| Acceptance, problem solving, reappraisal | Rumination (obsessive dwelling on issues), avoidance |

**The biggest meta-analysis: Webb et al. (2012).** Webb and colleagues analysed **306 experimental comparisons** of regulation strategies, grouped by the process model's families:

| Family | Overall effect on emotion |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive change** | **Moderate** |
| **Response modulation** | **Small** |
| **Attentional deployment** | **Non-significant** |

The headline ranking is easy to learn, but Webb et al. themselves warned that lumping strategies into families hides crucial differences within them:

- Within **attentional deployment**, the family as a whole had no significant effect, yet **distraction** on its own had **clearly beneficial** effects.
- Within **cognitive change**, **reappraising the situation** helped more than **reappraising the emotional response**.
- Within **response modulation**, **suppressing emotional expression** had a **moderate** effect, but **suppressing emotional experience** had **no** effect.

The moral is that the specific strategy matters more than its broad category.

> **Trap.** Two classic MCQ errors. (1) "Attentional deployment is ineffective, so distraction is ineffective." Wrong: the *family* was non-significant in Webb et al. (2012), but *distraction* specifically was clearly beneficial. (2) "Suppression has no effect." Half-wrong: suppressing **expression** had a moderate effect; suppressing **experience** had none. The overall ranking is cognitive change (moderate) > response modulation (small) > attentional deployment (non-significant).

**It depends on the situation: Troy et al. (2013).** Any strategy's effectiveness varies with circumstances. Troy and colleagues reasoned that **reappraisal** should help when stress is **uncontrollable** (you cannot change the event, so changing how you feel about it is sensible) but should be a poor choice when stress is **controllable**, where problem-focused coping (fixing the problem) would be better than adjusting one's feelings. They measured participants' reappraisal ability, how stressful their recent negative life events had been, and how controllable those events were. Under **high, uncontrollable stress**, people with high reappraisal ability were **less depressed** than those with low ability. Under **high, controllable stress**, high reappraisal ability went with **more** depression (Figure 15.8). Reappraisal ability is a strength or a liability depending on whether the problem can be fixed.

> **Trap.** "Reappraisal is always the best regulation strategy" is false. Troy et al. (2013): good reappraisers were *more* depressed when high stress was **controllable**. Sheppes and Gross (2011, below): reappraisal backfires at **high** emotional intensity.

### Findings: brain mechanisms

The general picture (Ochsner & Gross, 2008) is that effective regulation strategies involve **effortful cognitive processing in the prefrontal cortex**, which **reduces amygdala activation** and with it negative affect. Think of the prefrontal cortex as turning down an emotional alarm generated deeper in the temporal lobe.

**Kohn et al. (2014)** meta-analysed the brain areas involved and proposed a **three-stage neural network model** (Figure 15.9):

| Stage | Network centred on | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Emotion evaluation** | **Ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC)**, receiving affective arousal relayed via the amygdala and basal ganglia | Starts the appraisal and signals that regulation is needed |
| 2. **Initiation of regulation** | **Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)** | Processes the regulation itself |
| 3. **Execution of regulation** | **Superior temporal gyrus, supplementary motor area, angular gyrus** | Changes the emotional state, producing the regulated state |

> **Trap.** Keep Kohn et al.'s order straight: **VLPFC evaluates** (and signals need), **DLPFC initiates**, and a temporo-parietal-motor network (STG, SMA, angular gyrus) **executes**. Exams swap VLPFC and DLPFC.

**Reappraisal versus distraction in the brain.** **McRae et al. (2010)** had participants view very negative pictures while either reappraising (reinterpreting the picture more positively) or distracting themselves (holding a **six-letter string** in memory). Reappraisal produced larger increases in **medial prefrontal cortex** and **anterior temporal** activity (areas involved in processing affective meaning) and was **more effective** at reducing negative affect. This suggests reappraisal gave more control over the emotional state, at least in this paradigm.

**Prefrontal-amygdala coupling.** If prefrontal processes really cause the drop in amygdala activity, the people whose prefrontal areas are most strongly connected to the amygdala should reappraise best. **S.-H. Lee et al. (2012)** asked participants to reappraise while viewing very negative pictures and found exactly that.

### Findings: timing and strategy choice

**Sheppes et al. (2014)** confirmed both of their predictions: people chose **distraction** more often than reappraisal when the situation was **highly intense** and when the negative situation was **unlikely to be met again**.

**Sheppes and Gross (2011)** used the same timeline logic to predict *effectiveness* rather than choice. Because emotional intensity tends to rise over time, timing should not matter much for a cheap strategy such as distraction, but a demanding strategy such as reappraisal should work best early, while intensity is still low. Their review bore this out:

| Strategy | Low intensity (early) | High intensity (late) |
|---|---|---|
| **Distraction** | Effective | **Equally** effective |
| **Reappraisal** | Works well | **Counterproductive** |

### Findings: implicit regulation

**Mauss et al. (2007)** put participants through an anger provocation in which the experimenter acted impatient and irritated. Beforehand, some had completed a sentence-unscrambling task containing reappraisal-related words (for example, "restrains", "stable"), a priming procedure that activates the idea of emotional control without any instruction to use it. The primed participants felt **less anger** and less general negative emotion than controls, through implicit regulation triggered when they were provoked.

**Williams et al. (2009)** compared **implicit** and **explicit** reappraisal before an anxiety-provoking task (preparing to give a speech). **Both** held down physiological activity **equally well**.

### Evaluation of emotion-regulation research

**Advances.**

1. Effective strategies often depend on **effortful prefrontal processing** that **reduces amygdala activation**.
2. We know why some strategies lose effectiveness when used late rather than early (the rising-intensity logic of Sheppes and Gross).
3. Regulation can be **explicit or implicit**.
4. Some of the factors that decide **which** strategy a person picks (intensity, likelihood of re-encounter) have been identified.

**Limitations.**

1. Several theorists doubt that **generation and regulation** can be cleanly separated (Gross et al., 2011).
2. Research concentrates on strategies **inside the individual's head**. People also regulate with **behavioural** strategies such as drinking alcohol, arguing and avoiding situations, and these are associated with symptoms of mental disorders including **social phobia and eating disorders** (Aldao & Dixon-Gordon, 2014).
3. Effectiveness probably depends on the **situation** (for example, controllable versus uncontrollable stress), but the strategy-by-situation interaction has rarely been studied.
4. The **detailed processes** inside complex strategies such as reappraisal are still poorly understood.
5. Little is known about **individual differences** in the ability to use a strategy. A start: McRae et al. (2012) found that people who reappraised successfully tended to have **higher well-being** and **greater working memory capacity**.

> **Classic background.** Gross's earlier work (for example, Gross & John, 2003) contrasted habitual **reappraisal** with habitual **expressive suppression**, and older keys reduce the field to "reappraisal good, suppression bad". The 7th edition is more conditional: suppressing expression has a moderate effect (Webb et al., 2012), and reappraisal can backfire (Troy et al., 2013; Sheppes & Gross, 2011).

## 15.4 Affect and Cognition: Attention and Memory

### Emotion in everyday performance

Laboratory participants are kept neutral, but in daily life we think and decide while happy, sad, angry or anxious. Two driving-simulator studies show that this matters:

- **Pêcher et al. (2009):** **sad** music left lane-keeping intact and slightly lowered speed, while **happy** music was the more distracting, impairing lane-keeping and cutting speed by about **8 mph** relative to neutral music.
- **Stephens and Groeger (2011):** drivers made angry by being stuck behind a slow vehicle then overtook dangerously and approached hazards recklessly, laboratory support for "road rage".

Emotion influences perception, attention, interpretation, learning, memory, judgement, decision making and reasoning (Blanchette & Richards, 2010). This section takes attention and memory; 15.5 takes judgement and decisions.

**Inducing affect in the lab.** Lench et al. (2011) tallied mood-induction methods: emotional **films** (**24%** of studies, the most common), **autobiographical recall** of an intense experience (**20%**), staged real-life situations (**15%**) and emotional music (**7%**).

### Attention: the breadth question

Eriksen and St James (1986) likened visual attention to a **zoom lens** whose attended area can widen or shrink (Chapter 5). Breadth matters for memory too, because we remember mainly what we attended to. Three accounts of how affect sets the zoom have been proposed.

**1. Easterbrook (1959): negative affect narrows attention.** As **arousal or anxiety** rises, the **range of cues** used shrinks. Initially the shrinkage sheds irrelevant peripheral cues and performance improves; beyond a point it starts to shed relevant cues and performance falls. High negative affect thus produces **"tunnel vision"**. The idea may help explain **eyewitness memory** under stress (Chapter 8), and anxious drivers do seem to attend less to peripheral information (for example, Janelle et al., 1999).

> **Classic background.** Easterbrook's two-stage argument is often taught as a cognitive account of the **Yerkes-Dodson (1908)** inverted-U between arousal and performance; the narrowing ("cue-utilisation") hypothesis itself is **Easterbrook (1959)**.

**2. Fredrickson and Branigan (2005): positive affect broadens attention.** Positive emotions enlarge the range of percepts, thoughts and actions currently in mind, the mirror image of Easterbrook's claim (background: part of Fredrickson's "broaden-and-build" theory).

**3. Harmon-Jones et al. (2011): motivational intensity decides.** Both earlier accounts consider only **valence**. Harmon-Jones and colleagues add **motivational intensity**, the strength of the drive to approach or avoid a stimulus:

| | Low motivational intensity | High motivational intensity |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Pleasant music | An attractive member of the opposite sex |
| **Negative** | Sad situations | Threatening stimuli |
| **Predicted attention** | **Broadening** | **Narrowing** |

The functional logic: strong approach or avoidance motivation is served by **narrowing** onto the object to be obtained or escaped, whereas low motivation is served by **broadening**, which keeps a person open to new opportunities.

**A separate mechanism: the eyes (Lee et al., 2014).** **Fear widens** the eyes, improving **detection** of visual stimuli but lowering **acuity**; **disgust narrows** them, with the reverse effects. Both look functional: fear helps detect and locate threat, disgust helps examine the offending object (such as contaminated food).

#### Findings on breadth

- **Anxiety or fear** is negative and high in motivational intensity, so both Easterbrook and Harmon-Jones predict narrowing. In **dual-task** studies with a central primary task and a peripheral secondary task, anxiety impairs the peripheral task more, as predicted (reviewed by Eysenck et al., 2007).
- **Gable and Harmon-Jones (2010b):** **disgust** (high intensity) **narrowed** attention but **sadness** (low intensity) **broadened** it, which contradicts Easterbrook's blanket claim about negative affect.
- **Positive affect** usually broadens attention (review by Friedman & Förster, 2010), but **Gable and Harmon-Jones (2011)** separated the two intensities: the **chance to win** money (high intensity) **narrowed** attention, while learning that money **had been won** (low intensity) broadened it. Only Harmon-Jones et al. predict the narrowing. (The source misspells the rival authors here; they are Fredrickson and Branigan.)

> **Trap.** **Sadness broadens** attention even though it is negative; **positive affect narrows** it when motivational intensity is high. The predictor is **motivational intensity** (high narrows, low broadens), not valence.

#### From attention to memory

Emotion generally **improves memory for goal-relevant, central information** and **worsens it for peripheral information** (Levine & Edelstein, 2009); **weapon focus**, eyewitnesses fixating a weapon and losing surrounding detail, is one example (Chapter 8). It is tempting to put this memory narrowing down to attentional narrowing at encoding, but there are two problems.

**First**, memory narrowing exceeds attentional narrowing. **Riggs et al. (2011)** measured both: a central emotional stimulus reduced fixations on neutral peripheral items, but the poor memory for those items was **not** directly due to the reduced attention. Emotional material may be processed more distinctively or more for meaning, whatever attention it receives.

**Second**, on Harmon-Jones's account low-intensity emotions should **broaden memory**. **Talarico et al. (2009)** had people recall **eight** emotional autobiographical memories (Figure 15.10). Peripheral detail was **poor** for **anger, fear and negative surprise**, fitting Easterbrook, but reasonably **good** for **sadness** (low motivational intensity) and **good for all positive memories**, fitting Harmon-Jones if most positive moods are low in intensity. Similarly, **Gable and Harmon-Jones (2010a)** found positive affect **improved memory for peripheral details** only under **low** motivational intensity.

**Conclusions.** The evidence mostly supports **Harmon-Jones et al. (2011)** (review: Harmon-Jones et al., 2013), but breadth is more flexible than that theory says: it also depends on currently **accessible thoughts and response tendencies** (Huntsinger, 2013). The findings still help predict behaviour in real emotional situations.

### Memory

#### Mood congruity

When you are low, unpleasant memories come to mind; when you are cheerful, happy ones do. **Mood congruity** is the finding that emotional material is **learned and retrieved best when its tone matches the person's mood**. It may help explain why bad moods persist: the mood makes negative information easier to learn and recall, which sustains the mood.

- **Hills et al. (2011):** after a happy mood induction, people **recognised happy faces better than sad ones**; after a sad induction the reverse advantage was slight and **non-significant**.
- **Loeffler et al. (2013):** mood congruity occurs in daily life, but depends on **arousal at learning**: congruity for **negative** words was greater under **high** arousal, for **positive** words under **low** arousal.
- **Miranda and Kihlstrom (2005):** in **musically induced** moods, happy mood aided retrieval of happy autobiographical memories (childhood and recent) and sad mood aided sad ones.

**The positive-negative asymmetry.** Holland and Kensinger (2010) found clear congruity in **positive** moods but less often in **negative** ones, and Rusting and DeHart (2000) found the same with non-autobiographical material. The likely reason is **mood repair**: people in negative moods try to lift them, which makes negative memories less accessible. In **Rusting and DeHart (2000)**, participants studied positive, negative and neutral words and then underwent a negative mood induction; those who claimed to be good at reducing bad moods showed **less** mood congruity.

#### Explaining mood congruity

The 7th edition uses **Tulving's (1979) encoding specificity principle** (Chapter 6): recall depends on the overlap between information available at retrieval and information stored in the trace, and mood-congruent material overlaps more with the rememberer's state. Danker and Anderson (2010) extended this to brain states, arguing that remembering an episode amounts to returning to the brain state present during it. **Lewis et al. (2005)** tested this for mood congruity: participants learned positive and negative words and took a recognition test in a happy or sad mood, and memory was congruent.

| Region | Active at encoding for | Reactivated at test in |
|---|---|---|
| **Subgenual cingulate** | Positive stimuli | Positive mood |
| **Posterolateral orbitofrontal cortex** | Negative stimuli | Negative mood |

> **Trap.** Older textbooks explain mood congruity and mood-state-dependent memory with **Bower's (1981) associative network theory** (emotions as nodes that spread activation to linked memories). The 7th edition uses **encoding specificity** and **brain-state reinstatement** (Danker & Anderson, 2010; Lewis et al., 2005). Bower is the older-key answer, not this edition's explanation.

#### Mood-state-dependent memory

**Mood-state-dependent memory (MSDM)** is better memory when **mood at retrieval matches mood at learning**, whatever the material's tone. **Ucros (1989)** found **moderate** evidence, **stronger for positive moods**, again probably because of mood repair.

**Kenealy (1997)** used music to create happy or sad moods while participants learned a route from a map and again at test the next day. There was strong MSDM in **free recall** but **none in cued recall**, where the cue was the map's outline (Figure 15.11). Encoding specificity explains the free-recall result (mood stored in the trace raises overlap when it matches) but not the cued-recall null. **Eich (1995)** proposed that mood matters less when the crucial information or good cues are **supplied**, as in cued recall; his **"do-it-yourself" principle** says memory is most mood-dependent when **effortful** processing is needed at learning and/or retrieval. **Eich and Metcalfe (1989)** confirmed it: when participants **generated** the second word of each pair (river-v____) rather than simply **reading** it (river-valley), the MSDM effect was **four times** larger.

> **Trap.** **Mood congruity** = mood matches the **material's tone**. **MSDM** = mood at **learning** matches mood at **retrieval**. Both are stronger for **positive** moods. Kenealy (1997): MSDM in **free**, not **cued**, recall; Eich and Metcalfe (1989): **four times** larger for **generated** items.

#### The amygdala and emotional memory

The **amygdala** is central to emotional memory, partly because it is a **hub** connected to about **90 percent of cortical areas**. **Murty et al. (2010)** showed in a meta-analysis that good long-term memory for emotional material goes with stronger encoding activity in a network including the **amygdala** and memory-related **temporal lobe** regions.

**Patients.** **Adolphs et al. (2005)** found controls remembered **gist** better from emotional than neutral contexts, whereas amygdala-damaged patients were impaired at **gist only in emotional contexts** and **not** at background detail, suggesting the amygdala is needed to focus on central information under emotion, consistent with Easterbrook. In **Urbach-Wiethe disease** the amygdala and neighbouring tissue are destroyed. **Cahill et al. (1995)** found that patient **BP** recalled a highly emotional event in a story **worse** than a neutral one, reversing the normal emotional advantage, and **Siebert et al. (2003)** found Urbach-Wiethe patients most impaired for **emotional pictures**, both positive and negative.

The amygdala's influence comes through its links with memory regions such as the **hippocampus and prefrontal cortex** (LaBar & Cabeza, 2006), especially areas involved in **long-term consolidation**. But cognition contributes too: Talmi (2013) argued that emotional events command more **attention, distinctive processing and organisation** than neutral events, and that this helps explain their memory advantage.

> **Trap.** Patient **BP** (Cahill et al., 1995) had **Urbach-Wiethe disease** (amygdala destruction) and lost the emotional memory advantage; do not confuse him with HM. Adolphs et al. (2005): **gist** impaired, **background detail** spared.

### Evaluation

**Established:** high-intensity moods narrow and low-intensity moods broaden attention, with some effect on memory; mood congruity and MSDM are well supported and usually explained by encoding specificity; the amygdala's role is increasingly clear. **Limitations:** (1) attentional breadth affects long-term memory only **partly directly** (Riggs et al., 2011); (2) congruity and MSDM are **harder to obtain with negative moods**, mainly because people try to end them; (3) why MSDM is stronger on some memory tasks than others is not fully understood; (4) the relative weight of **amygdala activation** versus **cognitive processes** in the emotional memory advantage is unresolved.

## 15.5 Affect and Cognition: Judgement and Decision Making

### Judgement, decision making and why personality is included

Chapter 13 studied decisions made in a neutral state. **Decision making** is choosing among options, trivial or life-changing; **judgement** is one of its components, estimating how likely each outcome is and how you would feel if it happened. Someone who expects the worst will tend to decide differently from an optimist.

**Angie et al. (2011)** reviewed the area and concluded that (1) the major moods (**sadness, anger, fear or anxiety, happiness**) each have significant and somewhat **different** effects, and (2) mood effects are on average **larger for decision making than for judgement**.

The section also uses **personality** research, because personality and mood are moderately strongly linked. People high in **trait anxiety** (individual differences in susceptibility to anxiety) are in an anxious mood far more often than low scorers, so studying them is a way of studying habitual anxious mood.

### Two plausible predictions, both only partly right

1. **Valence rules:** negative moods (fear, anger, sadness) should make people pessimistic and risk averse; positive moods should make them optimistic and risk taking.
2. **Consistency:** a given mood should push towards risk or caution whatever the situation.

Both turn out to be only partly correct, and the findings below are best read as a list of exceptions to them.

### Integral versus incidental emotions

Han and Lerner (2009) distinguish **integral emotions**, produced by thinking about the decision itself (anxiety while considering a large, risky investment), from **incidental emotions**, carried over from earlier, unrelated events (elation at passing an exam colouring later, unrelated choices). Most research studies **incidental** emotions (Lench et al., 2011): participants write about an emotional experience and then do an unrelated task. Real decisions, by contrast, often involve **integral** emotions, such as choosing what to do to lift a low mood.

### The somatic marker hypothesis and interoception

**Damasio (1994)** proposed that emotional events automatically trigger **bodily arousal responses** (somatic markers) that tag those events emotionally and then steer later choices. The key capacity is **interoception**, sensitivity to subtle internal bodily changes, which may or may not be conscious.

**Dunn et al. (2010)** used a version of the **Iowa gambling task** (Bechara et al., 1994). Participants repeatedly drew cards from **four decks**, two profitable on average and two unprofitable. Their choices improved over trials although they understood little consciously about how the decks differed. Bodily responses were recorded before each choice, and **interoceptive ability** was measured by accuracy at **counting one's own heartbeats**. Participants whose bodily responses differed most between bad and good decks decided best, and people with **high interoceptive ability** relied on their bodily signals much more than people with low ability (Figure 15.12). Perceiving one's own emotional bodily responses can therefore guide decisions. **Werner et al. (2013)** identified the **right anterior insula** as a key region linking interoception with decision making, alongside the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex** and **amygdala**.

> **Classic background.** Damasio built the hypothesis on patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage (his patient EVR is the standard example) whose intellect was intact but whose real-life decisions were disastrous, and who typically fail to develop anticipatory skin conductance responses to the bad decks.

### Anxiety

#### Judgement

Fear or anxiety is the negative emotion most consistently linked with **pessimistic** judgements about the future. Shortly after the **9/11** attacks, **Lerner et al. (2003)** had participants dwell on aspects of the attacks that made them **afraid**, **angry** or **sad**; the fearful group gave the **highest** estimates of future attacks.

Most people show an **optimism bias**, believing good events are more likely, and bad events less likely, to happen to them than to others. Shepperd et al. (2013) call this **unrealistic comparative optimism**. The less studied **unrealistic absolute optimism** compares a person's estimate with an objective standard (thinking your risk of a disease is **4 percent** when it is really **7 percent**).

Anxious people show **less** optimism bias. In **Lench and Levine (2005)**, students put into a **fearful** mood judged themselves more at risk, relative to the average student, than students in a happy or neutral mood. At the personality level, **Harris et al. (2008)** found that the usual belief that others are more vulnerable than oneself was **weaker** in high trait-anxious people. This may be partly realistic: anxious people really do **experience more negative life events** (van Os et al., 2001).

> **Trap.** The chapter's KEY TERM definition of optimism bias is the **comparative** one (relative to other people). **Absolute** optimism is relative to an objective risk figure (4 versus 7 percent).

#### Decision making

Anxious people generally take **less risk**.

- **Lorian and Grisham (2011):** anxiety-disorder patients scored lower than controls on the **Domain-Specific Risk-Taking Scale**, **30 items** on the likelihood of risky behaviour (betting a day's income on horses, unprotected sex).
- **Raghunathan and Pham (1999):** choosing between **job A** (high salary, low security) and **job B** (average salary, high security), **anxious** participants were much less likely than neutral ones to take risky **job A** (Figure 15.13).
- **Gambetti and Giusberti (2012):** in **real** finances, people with **anxious personalities** had made more conservative choices, favouring interest-bearing accounts over large investments in stocks and shares.

### Sadness

#### Judgement

Sadness, which becomes depression when intense, is tied more to an **absence of positive affect** than anxiety is. Sad people find the environment relatively **unrewarding**, which may make them unusually keen on rewards even at some risk.

Waters (2008) found sad people's estimates of health hazards and life events **more pessimistic** than those of people in a positive mood, and several studies show a **smaller optimism bias** in sad or depressed people. This is often called **depressive realism**, the claim that depressed people see the future more accurately. The book is cautious: it is usually very hard to know how optimistic anyone *ought* to be about their own future, so "realism" is hard to demonstrate (Harris & Hahn, 2011).

> **Classic background.** The term is usually traced to **Alloy and Abramson (1979)**, whose mildly depressed students judged their lack of control over an outcome more accurately than non-depressed students. The 7th edition treats depressive realism as controversial.

#### Decision making

In **Raghunathan and Pham (1999)**, most **sad** participants, unlike anxious ones, chose the **high-risk job A**, which the authors attribute to sad people's motivation to obtain rewards.

**The misery-is-not-miserly effect** (Cryder et al., 2008) is the tendency of sad people to **pay more** for things. The proposed mechanism is that sadness, especially with **self-focus**, shrinks the sense of self and increases the urge to acquire possessions that enhance it. Sad participants offered **almost four times** as much as neutral participants for a sporty insulated water bottle, and the crucial result was that **sad people high in self-focus** offered much more than sad people low in self-focus (Figure 15.14).

**Sadder but wiser?** Lerner et al. (submitted at the time of writing) tested the "sadder-but-wiser" idea on impatience for immediate rewards. The median **neutral** participant treated **$19 today** as equivalent to **$100 in a year**; the median **sad** participant needed only **$4 today**. Sad people wanted an immediate self-enhancing reward, and many were thinking about purchases before choosing. Sadder was not wiser here.

> **Trap.** Two negative moods, two opposite decisions in Raghunathan and Pham (1999): **anxious = safe job**, **sad = high-risk, high-salary job**. Anxiety seeks certainty; sadness seeks reward. The misery-is-not-miserly effect is about **paying more**, and depends on **self-focus**.

### Anger

#### Judgement

Anger counts as negative but can feel **pleasant**, because it brings a sense of control and of dominance over disliked others (Lerner & Tiedens, 2006); it also increases **schadenfreude**, pleasure at disliked people's misfortune (Hareli & Weiner, 2002). The triggering events are still remembered as unpleasant, and anger can lead to aggression that causes much negative affect (Litvak et al., 2010).

Its effect on judgement is the reverse of the other negative moods. **Anxiety and sadness** produce **pessimistic** estimates of negative events; **anger** produces **optimistic** ones (Waters, 2008). Angry people rate themselves **less at risk** than others although they are actually **more likely** to divorce, have trouble at work and develop heart disease (Lerner & Keltner, 2001). The explanation is that anger, unlike other negative moods, carries **certainty** about what happened and **perceived control** over the situation (Litvak et al., 2010).

#### Decision making

**Risk seeking.** Feeling in control, angry people take more risks. On the **Asian disease problem** (Chapter 13), where most people are risk averse, **Lerner and Keltner (2001)** found **fearful** participants risk averse and **angry** participants risk seeking. Gambetti and Giusberti (2012) found people with **angry personalities** had made **riskier** real investments (more stocks and shares, larger sums).

**The pairs reversal.** **Kugler et al. (2012)** first replicated the effect on a real-money lottery: **alone**, angry participants were much less risk averse than happy or fearful ones. In **pairs**, however, the risky option made one's outcome depend on the partner's choice, and now angry participants chose it far **less** than fearful ones (**56 versus 93 percent**). Angry people would not give up control. What anger protects is control, not risk as such.

> **Trap.** "Anger always increases risk taking" is wrong. Alone, yes (Lerner & Keltner, 2001); in pairs, when risk meant depending on someone else, angry people avoided it (**56% vs 93%**, Kugler et al., 2012).

**Anger and reasoning.** Emerson's line that anger "blows out the light of reason" has empirical support, because anger promotes **shallow, heuristic** processing instead of systematic analysis (Litvak et al., 2010).

- **Bright and Goodman-Delahunty (2006):** mock jurors shown **gruesome photographs** of a murdered woman convicted her husband far more often (**41 versus 9 percent**), partly because the photographs made them angrier.
- **Stephens and Groeger (2011):** angry simulator drivers drove recklessly (15.4).
- **Coget et al. (2011):** **seven film directors** relied mostly on **intuitive** decisions when moderately or very angry, but **moderate anxiety** led to **analytic** processing.
- **Small and Lerner (2008):** angry participants awarded less welfare to **Patricia Smith**, a fictitious young divorced mother of three, than neutral or sad participants. Adding a concurrent task (**cognitive load**) to block analytic thought **did not change** the angry participants' decisions, implying they were barely analysing anyway.
- **Ask and Granhag (2007):** angry **police investigators** judging two witness statements and a suspect's guilt were **less influenced by what the witnesses actually said** than sad investigators were.

### Positive mood

#### Judgement

One would expect stronger optimism bias in a good mood. **Lench and Levine (2005)** found happy participants more optimistic than **fearful** ones but **not** than **neutral** ones. **Drace et al. (2009)** found optimism bias was the **same** in positive, neutral and negative moods induced by pictures and music. Earlier studies may have found mood effects because mood was measured just before optimism, letting participants guess the hypothesis (a **demand characteristic**); Drace et al. minimised this.

#### Decision making

Against the naive valence prediction, positive moods typically make people **risk averse** (Blanchette & Richards, 2010). Gay men with high positive affect showed **fewer HIV risk behaviours** (Mustanski, 2007), and participants in a positive mood placed **less risky** bets on hypothetical horse races (Cahir & Thomas, 2010). The likely reason is **mood maintenance**: avoiding risks that might spoil a good feeling.

Positive affect also increases **heuristic** and reduces analytic processing (Griskevicius et al., 2010). **de Vries et al. (2012)** repeatedly offered a 50 percent chance of **1.20 euro** or a 50 percent chance of **1.00 euro**; the first always dominates, yet **happy** participants stuck to it less consistently than **sad** ones, switching gambles after recent losses. **de Vries et al. (2008)** found people most **satisfied** when their decision strategy fitted their mood: **happy** participants preferred having decided **intuitively**, **sad** participants **deliberatively** (Figure 15.15).

**Positive moods differ.** Research has split negative affect into three states but treated positive affect as one, whereas there is evidence for **eight** positive moods: **awe, amusement, interest, pride, gratitude, joy, love and contentment** (Campos et al., 2013). **Griskevicius et al. (2010)** found that **anticipatory enthusiasm, amusement and attachment love** made people persuadable by **weak** arguments, while **awe and nurturant love** produced **less** shallow processing than a neutral mood (Figure 15.16). Why they differ is unexplained.

> **Trap.** Positive mood is **risk averse** and **heuristic**. The chapter calls **sadness the only mood associated with analytic processing**, though Coget et al. found **moderate anxiety** analytic, so read the wording.

### Overall conclusions: the four mood profiles

The moods differ markedly, especially the three negative ones. Figure 15.17 summarised the pattern in a table lost in conversion; this version is assembled from the chapter's text:

| Mood | Judgement of future negative events | Risk in decisions | Processing | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anxiety** | Pessimistic | Risk averse | (Moderate anxiety analytic, Coget et al.) | Increase certainty in an unpredictable, threatening situation |
| **Sadness** | Pessimistic | Risk taking, reward seeking | **Analytic** | Abandon an unattainable goal and plan new ones (Andrews & Thomson, 2009) |
| **Anger** | **Optimistic** | Risk taking (where control is kept) | Heuristic | Overcome an obstacle by direct action, with a sense of control |
| **Positive mood** | Optimism bias, mood effect inconsistent | Risk averse | Heuristic | Maintain the current mood (Oatley & Johnson-Laird, 1987) |

The functional column does the explanatory work. Anxious people reduce uncertainty by avoiding risk. Sad people, having lost a goal, must think hard about what to do next, hence analytic processing. Angry people feel in control, so they are optimistic and willing to gamble on reaching the goal. Happy people protect their mood by thinking shallowly and avoiding risk.

**Limitations.** (1) Most studies use **incidental** emotions, which may tell us little about **integral** ones. (2) Most laboratory decisions are **trivial**, so heuristic processing is unsurprising; intense emotions during major real-life decisions may work differently. (3) Many findings rest on the **heuristic versus analytic** dichotomy, which is oversimplified since most processing mixes both (Keren & Schul, 2009).

### Moral dilemmas: emotion versus cognition

#### Two problems and an asymmetry

Strong emotion enters some complex judgements, notably **moral dilemmas**. In the **trolley problem**, you can divert a runaway trolley that will kill **five** people onto a side track where it will kill **one**. In the **footbridge problem**, you can save the five only by **pushing a heavy person off a bridge** into its path. The arithmetic is the same, yet about **90 percent** divert the trolley and only about **10 percent** push (Hauser, 2006).

**Greene et al. (2008)** call the footbridge case a **personal** moral dilemma, because you would directly harm someone yourself, and the trolley case **impersonal**, because harm follows less directly from your action; most research uses personal dilemmas. Personal dilemmas provoke a strong emotional reaction and so a severe conflict: a powerful emotional case against killing versus a strong cognitive case that five lives outweigh one.

> **Classic background.** The trolley problem is usually credited to the philosopher Philippa Foot (1967) and the footbridge variant to Judith Jarvis Thomson; the chapter cites Hauser (2006) for the percentages and Greene for the model.

#### Greene's dual-process model

| | Fast system | Slow system |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Automatic, **affective** ("heart") | Effortful, **cognitive** ("head") |
| Judgement in personal dilemmas | **Deontological**: based on moral rules or duties ("do not kill") | **Utilitarian**: based on outcomes, saving the most lives |
| Brain regions (Figure 15.19) | **VMPFC** (emotion generation; about Brodmann areas 10 and 11) | **DLPFC** (about areas 9 and 46) and **ACC** (cognitive control) |

> **Trap.** **Deontological = rule-based = affective = VMPFC = refusing to push. Utilitarian = outcome-based = cognitive = DLPFC = pushing.** Damage to the VMPFC makes people **more** utilitarian, because the emotional brake is weakened.

#### Supporting findings

1. **Cognitive load** slowed utilitarian judgements more than deontological ones (Greene et al., 2008).
2. **Anti-anxiety drugs** increased utilitarian judgements on **personal** dilemmas but left impersonal ones unchanged (Perkins et al., 2013), as expected if emotion drives deontological responses only in personal dilemmas.
3. Utilitarian judgements went with more **DLPFC** activation (Greene et al., 2004).
4. Inhibiting the DLPFC with **rTMS** **reduced** utilitarian judgements in high-emotion dilemmas (Tassy et al., 2012).
5. **VMPFC patients**, with reduced emotional responsiveness, made **more** utilitarian judgements than brain-damaged and healthy controls (Thomas et al., 2011), because of blunted emotion rather than impaired cognition (Young & Dungan, 2012).

#### Problem findings

1. **Utilitarian is not automatically better.** The model seems to treat utilitarian answers as the rational ones, yet people **high in psychopathy** (without a diagnosis) give **more** of them (Bartels & Pizarro, 2011), suggesting they can reflect a blunted reaction to harm.
2. **Both kinds can be rule-based.** Priming the rule "**Saving lives**" rather than "**Do not kill**" before the footbridge problem increased utilitarian judgements (Broeders et al., 2011), so utilitarian answers can also rest on a moral rule.
3. **Counterintuitiveness, not utilitarianism.** Kahane et al. (2012) noted that utilitarian options in earlier studies were usually **counterintuitive**, which could explain the DLPFC activity and slow responses. In their counter-example, a waiter knows a customer plans to infect as many people as possible with HIV before going to prison in **48 hours**; slipping him **poppy seeds**, to which he is severely allergic, would hospitalise him for at least that long, and here the utilitarian act feels intuitive. Crossing intuitive and counterintuitive versions with both judgement types, they found counterintuitive judgements rated **harder** whatever their type, **no** link of utilitarian judgements with DLPFC or of deontological ones with VMPFC, and more **ACC** activity (emotional conflict) for **counterintuitive** judgements. Intuitiveness looked more fundamental than the utilitarian-deontological split.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths:** the research addresses emotionally loaded, everyday questions; there is good support for fast affective and slow cognitive processes in moral judgement; imaging and patient studies have begun to locate them. **Limitations:** treating utilitarian judgements as "better" is dubious given the psychopathy finding; moral rules are not confined to deontological judgements; and **intuitive versus counterintuitive** may matter more than **utilitarian versus deontological**.

## 15.6 Anxiety, Depression and Cognitive Biases

### Studying people who are habitually anxious or depressed

Instead of inducing moods, researchers can study people who are **chronically** in one: patients with an **anxiety disorder** or **major depressive disorder**, or healthy people with anxious or depressive personalities, selected with questionnaires such as **trait anxiety** measures. The catch is that anxiety and depression travel together. **Comorbidity**, two or more mental disorders in the same patient at once, is very common in both conditions, and healthy high trait-anxious people also tend to have depressive personalities. A finding about "anxious people" may therefore partly reflect depression, and vice versa.

### How anxiety and depression differ

The two differ in **time orientation**: **past losses** go mainly with **depression**, **future threats** with **anxiety**. **Eysenck et al. (2006)** wrote each negative scenario (for example, a diagnosis of serious illness) in three versions, as a **past** event, a **possible future** event and a **probable future** event. Participants' anxiety ratings were tied more to future versions, their depression ratings more to past versions. Symptoms agree: **worry** about possible future events is a major symptom of several anxiety disorders and the central one in **generalised anxiety disorder**, with excessive concern across many domains (Hirsch & Mathews, 2012); **rumination**, dwelling on past negative feelings and experiences, is very common in depression.

> **Trap.** **Worry is future-directed (anxiety, especially GAD); rumination is past-directed (depression).** Options that swap them are common.

### Why cognitive biases matter clinically

Anxious and depressed patients differ from healthy people cognitively, behaviourally, physiologically and perhaps biochemically, and therapies differ in which of these they target (Kring et al., 2012), so establishing the role of **cognitive** factors is theoretically central. Many theorists (for example, **Beck & Dozois, 2011**) assume that (1) **vulnerability** to clinical anxiety and depression depends partly on **cognitive biases**, and (2) **cognitive therapy** and CBT should aim to reduce or remove those biases.

> **Classic background.** Beck's cognitive model proposes negative **schemas**, activated by stress, that bias attention, interpretation and memory and produce negative automatic thoughts about self, world and future (the cognitive triad). The 7th edition cites Beck and Dozois (2011) for the vulnerability assumption but builds the section on the four biases and Williams et al. (1997).

### The four cognitive biases

| Bias | What it is | Typical measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Attentional bias** | Selective attention to threat stimuli shown together with neutral ones, through **fast engagement** and/or **slow disengagement** | Dot-probe, emotional Stroop, eye tracking |
| **Interpretive bias** | Taking the threatening meaning of ambiguous stimuli or situations | Homophone spelling, ambiguous sentences, questionnaires, ERPs |
| **Explicit memory bias** | Retrieving relatively more negative information on tests requiring **conscious recollection** | Recall, recognition |
| **Implicit memory bias** | Better performance for negative information on memory tests **not** requiring conscious recollection | Implicit tests such as stem completion |

The umbrella KEY TERM **memory bias** covers both memory forms. A person with all four biases would notice threats everywhere, read ambiguity as danger and remember a past full of unpleasant events, and would plausibly be at raised risk of anxiety or depression. That raises the **causality problem**: do the biases help cause the disorder, does the disorder create the biases, or both?

### Theoretical positions

#### Williams et al. (1997): different functions, different biases

The most influential answer to "which biases should each group have?" rests on two kinds of processing:

| | **Perceptual processes** | **Conceptual processes** |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Data-driven | Top-down |
| Speed and control | Often fast and "automatic" | Slower, more controlled |
| Typical tasks | Basic attention, implicit memory | Explicit memory (also some attention and implicit memory) |

The functional claim: **anxiety** exists to **anticipate danger and future threat**, so it should enhance **perceptual** processing of threat; **depression** exists to **replace failed goals**, so what matters is **conceptual** elaboration of self-generated material about failure or loss, not perceptual vigilance. Four predictions follow:

| Bias | Anxiety | Depression |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Attentional** | Yes, with **perceptual** processing | Only with **conceptual** processing |
| 2. **Interpretive** | Yes | Yes |
| 3. **Explicit memory** | **No** | **Yes** |
| 4. **Implicit memory** (perceptual) | **Yes** | **No** |

The findings below are scored against this table; prediction 4 turns out wrong in both columns.

#### The combined cognitive bias hypothesis

Most theorists had treated the biases as independent. **Hirsch et al. (2006)**, studying **social phobia** (excessive fear of social situations), proposed that biases **influence and interact with each other**, so the effect of one depends on the others. **Everaert et al. (2012)** applied this to depression and posed three questions: are the biases interrelated, can changing one cause change in another, and are their combined effects on symptoms merely additive or greater than additive?

#### Cognitive control accounts

**Joormann's impaired cognitive control account of depression** (Joormann et al., 2007; Gotlib & Joormann, 2010) centres on executive functions. Inhibitory control draws on working memory, which is reduced in depression (Kircanski et al., 2012). The chain (Figure 15.20) runs: impaired control, then difficulty **disengaging** from negative material, then extra time to **elaborate** it, then better memory for it, that is, a **memory bias**. Depressed people are indeed slower to disengage from negative stimuli (De Raedt & Koster, 2010), and **Zetsche and Joormann (2012)** found poor inhibitory control went with more depressive symptoms and **predicted the persistence of symptoms and rumination over six months**.

**Attentional control theory (Eysenck et al., 2007)** makes a parallel claim for anxiety: anxiety **reduces available working memory capacity**, weakening attentional control in two ways, **inhibition** (resisting task-irrelevant stimuli, called **negative** attentional control) and **shifting** (moving attention optimally within and between tasks, **positive** attentional control). Eysenck et al. reviewed many studies showing both impairments, and **Calvo et al. (2012)** found **delayed inhibitory control** over threat distractors in high-anxiety participants. Weak control should make anxious people slow to disengage from threat and inclined to spend longer on their worries (Hirsch & Mathews, 2012).

> **Classic background.** Attentional control theory also distinguishes **performance effectiveness** (quality of outcome) from **processing efficiency** (outcome relative to effort), claiming anxiety harms efficiency more than effectiveness because anxious people compensate with extra effort. The chapter covers only the inhibition and shifting components, but the distinction is a common interview question.

> **Trap.** In attentional control theory, **negative** attentional control = **inhibition** and **positive** attentional control = **shifting**. "Negative" does not mean "control of negative emotion".

### Attentional bias

#### How it is measured

**Dot-probe task** (Figure 15.21): after fixating a central cross, the participant sees two stimuli at once in different locations, on critical trials one negative (the word "failure", an angry face) and one neutral. A **dot** then replaces one of them and must be detected quickly. Since detection is faster where attention already is, consistently **faster** responses when the dot replaces the **negative** stimulus indicate attentional bias.

**Emotional Stroop task** (Figure 15.22): participants name the **ink colour** of negative and neutral words as fast as possible; **slower** naming for emotional words is taken to show that their meaning captured attention. The book flags doubts about this inference (see the evaluation).

> **Classic background.** The dot-probe task is usually credited to **MacLeod, Mathews and Tata (1986)**. Older texts lean mainly on emotional Stroop interference; the 7th edition treats the Stroop as the more indirect measure.

#### Findings

**Anxiety.** **Bar-Haim et al. (2007)** meta-analysed attentional bias in anxious groups, separating **subliminal** from **supraliminal** stimuli. The bias was **strong with both** and of **similar size across all anxiety disorders and in high trait-anxious healthy people**; its presence with subliminal stimuli implies **preattentive** as well as attentional processes. **Rudaizky et al. (2014)** found high trait anxiety linked to both **faster engagement** with and **slower disengagement** from negative stimuli, the two biases being **uncorrelated**, so they appear to make separate contributions to vulnerability. **Cisler and Koster (2010)** concluded that the bias reflects partly a **largely automatic threat-detection mechanism** (triggered even subliminally) and that anxious people's **difficulty disengaging** stems mainly from **poor attentional control**.

**Depression.** **Peckham et al. (2010)** found clear attentional bias in depressed patients and healthy samples on the **dot-probe**, but only a **marginally significant** effect on the **emotional Stroop**, probably because the Stroop measures attention less directly.

**Eye tracking.** Reaction times are a single snapshot and cannot easily separate **engagement** from **disengagement** (Clarke et al., 2013); eye tracking gives a continuous record. **Armstrong and Olatunji (2012)** meta-analysed two paradigms:

| Paradigm | Anxiety | Depression |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual search** (target among distractors) | **Vigilance for threat** and **difficulty disengaging** from it | Much weaker evidence |
| **Free viewing** (look wherever you like; closest to daily life) | Smaller effects | **Larger** effects: **slower** to fixate **positive** stimuli and **briefer** gaze on them |

So anxious people show **heightened sensitivity to threat**, depressed people **reduced sensitivity to positive stimuli**, and neither pattern appears in the other group.

> **Trap.** The depressive eye-tracking profile is **less attention to positive**, not more attention to negative. The anxious profile is **threat vigilance** plus **slow disengagement**.

#### Evaluation

The findings fit Williams et al. reasonably well: anxious people show rapid engagement and slow disengagement with supraliminal threat and bias even with subliminal threat, as expected if anxiety biases fast perceptual processing, and attentional bias is **less common in depression**. Two limitations: the dot-probe and Stroop reveal little about the **time course** of attention (eye tracking helps), and the Stroop may be confounded by **response mode**. Threat naturally prompts **avoidance**, but the Stroop requires an **approach** response such as pressing a lever, which could itself slow responses to threat words; when **Chajut et al. (2010)** had participants respond by pushing a joystick away, threatening words produced **faster** responses than neutral ones.

### Interpretive bias

Everyday life is full of ambiguity: a colleague walks past without greeting you, because they dislike you or because they did not see you? Habitually choosing the threatening reading is **interpretive bias**.

#### Anxiety

**Eysenck et al. (1987)** had people varying in trait anxiety **write down** spoken words, some of them **homophones** with a threatening and a neutral spelling (**die/dye**, **pain/pane**). Trait anxiety correlated **+0.60** with the number of threatening spellings, a fairly indirect, hard-to-fake measure. The bias is selective: **Walsh et al.** (submitted at the time of writing) found high trait anxiety biased interpretation of **social or intellectual** threats but **not physical or health** threats, so in healthy anxious people it concerns **interpersonal** threat. **Eysenck et al. (1991)** played **generalised anxiety disorder** patients spoken sentences with a threatening and a neutral reading (one turned on hearing "weak" or "week", others on a child's "growth" and a priest's "convictions"); patients chose the threatening reading more often than controls.

#### Depression

Evidence is **weaker** than for anxiety (Mathews, 2012). Studies using the **Cognitive Bias Questionnaire** (for example, Gupta & Kar, 2008), which offers **four** interpretations of each briefly described ambiguous event, find depressed patients choosing more negative ones, but this rests on **self-report**. **Mogg et al. (2006)** avoided self-report with the homophone task in patients with **major depressive disorder**: they gave threatening spellings for **81 percent** of homophones against **63 percent** for controls.

#### Automatic or strategic?

Both kinds of process contribute (Mathews, 2012). **Calvo and Castillo (1997)** found high trait-anxious participants showed interpretive bias for ambiguous social-threat sentences **1,250 ms** after the sentence but **not at 500 ms**, implicating slower strategic processing. **Moser et al. (2012b)** recorded the **N4 (N400)** event-related potential, which grows when a word is unexpected, as ambiguous scenarios were resolved positively or negatively by the last word. Participants with **social phobia** or **major depressive disorder** showed **larger N4s to positive endings**, so for them the benign ending was the surprise, meaning the bias was in place within about **400 ms**. Rapid biases are probably harder to inhibit than controlled ones.

> **Trap.** **Homophones** (same sound, different spelling: die/dye) **measure** interpretive bias; **homographs** (same spelling, different meanings: bank, choke) are used to **train** it away (15.7). The source text itself slips between the two words in its CBM box. Calvo and Castillo: **1,250 ms, not 500 ms**; Moser et al.: about **400 ms**.

### Memory biases

Memory biases may **maintain** negative moods, automatically (implicit bias) or with awareness (explicit bias).

- **Explicit memory bias in depression:** Williams et al. found many supporting studies, and later work agrees (for example, Rinck & Becker, 2005).
- **Implicit memory bias in depression:** **Phillips et al. (2010)** found a **small but significant** relationship in a meta-analysis, **even with perceptual processing** at encoding and retrieval, which Williams et al. had said should not happen.
- **Anxiety:** **Mitte (2008)** found **no** implicit memory bias, **directly contrary** to Williams et al. Explicit memory bias depended on the test, appearing with **recall** but **not recognition**, probably because recall's more extensive retrieval gives emotion more room to act.

In sum, implicit memory bias is typical of **depression, not anxiety**, and explicit memory bias is **stronger and more frequent in depression**. That fits the view that depression involves an **inward focus**, **disengagement** from the environment and a link with **past** negative events (Eysenck et al., 2006).

> **Trap.** Williams et al. (1997) predicted implicit memory bias in **anxiety, not depression**; the evidence went the **other way** (Mitte, 2008; Phillips et al., 2010). Do not state the prediction as a finding.

### Testing the combined cognitive bias hypothesis

The cleanest test is to change one bias by training and look for predicted changes in another. **White et al. (2011)** trained attentional bias **up**, and threatening interpretations of ambiguous material **increased**. **Amir et al. (2010)** trained interpretive bias **down**, and disengaging from threat became **easier**. **Salemink et al. (2010)** had participants write their own endings to ambiguous scenarios, then trained their interpretations of other situations in a negative or positive direction; when they later recalled their original endings, negative training made the recalled endings **more negative**. Attention, interpretation and memory seem to share, at least partly, common selective-processing mechanisms.

### The cognitive vulnerability hypothesis

Cognitive therapists (Williams et al., 1997; Beck & Dozois, 2011) hold that healthy people with cognitive biases are more vulnerable to anxiety disorders or depression, so negative life events (such as losing a job) should harm them more. Two prospective studies support this.

- **Lewinsohn et al. (2001)** measured **interpretive bias in adolescents** and a year later recorded their negative life events over those **12 months**. Major depressive disorder developed mostly in those with **both** many negative events **and** strong initial bias; among adolescents low in bias, the number of negative events made **no difference**.
- **J.R. Cohen et al. (2012)** assessed **children's** pessimism about the consequences of negative events and their implications for the self. Over the following **7 weeks**, many negative events increased depressive symptoms more in the cognitively vulnerable children (Figure 15.23).

### Conclusions

1. **Anxiety and depression differ** in the nature and strength of their biases (Figure 15.24 rated each bias for each condition; the ratings are lost with the image). The text's summary: **attentional bias is stronger in anxiety**, **memory biases are stronger in depression**, and interpretive bias appears in both but is better established in anxiety.
2. **Theory:** anxiety's job of anticipating future threat implies an **external focus** and attentional bias; depression's job of reflecting on failure and finding new goals implies an **internal focus** and memory bias. The observed pattern matches **approximately**.
3. **Therapy:** treatment should target each patient's biases, and progress can be tracked by how far they shrink.
4. **Links among biases:** changing one often changes others, but the shared mechanisms are still unidentified.

## 15.7 Cognitive Bias Modification

### Why training biases answers the causal question

Section 15.6 left the crucial question open: do cognitive biases make people vulnerable to anxiety and depression, or do the disorders produce the biases? Correlational and even prospective studies cannot fully settle this. The strongest method is **cognitive bias modification (CBM)**, which Hallion and Ruscio (2011) describe as an experimental paradigm that uses training to *induce* biases, either maladaptive or adaptive. The logic is the logic of any experiment: if you change the bias by training and the emotional state changes as a result, the bias has a causal influence on the emotion. In practice CBM has usually meant training designed to **reduce attentional bias and/or interpretive bias** (MacLeod & Clarke, 2013).

### In the real world: reducing anxiety and depression through cognitive bias modification

If biases make people more susceptible to anxiety and depression, then removing them might be therapeutic. The box describes how CBM is done and how well it works.

**Training away attentional bias.** The standard method adapts the **dot-probe task** (Figure 15.21). In the assessment version, the dot replaces the threatening and the neutral stimulus equally often. In the training version, the dot **always** replaces the **neutral** stimulus. Over many trials participants implicitly learn that attending to the threat word or face is useless for the task, and they learn to **stop allocating attention to the threatening stimulus**.

**Training away interpretive bias.** A common method presents a **homograph**, a word with one spelling but at least two meanings, where one meaning is negative and one benign (the book's example is "choke"). It is followed by a **word fragment** that the participant completes using the homograph as a clue, and the fragment can **only** be completed with a positive or neutral word (the example fragment completes to "engine", the benign, car-related sense of choke). Repeated over many trials, participants learn to access the **benign** meaning of ambiguous words.

**How effective is it?**

- **Hakamata et al. (2010)** combined studies of **attention bias modification** for anxiety (Figure 15.25 plotted **seven** studies). Three findings: (1) the **correlation between the reduction in attentional bias and the reduction in anxiety was +0.75**, which is the causal signature CBM is looking for; (2) effects were **greater in patient groups** than in non-patients; (3) there were clear effects on **trait** anxiety, suggesting the technique may produce **enduring personality change**, not just a temporary mood shift.
- **Hallion and Ruscio (2011)** meta-analysed CBM in people high in anxiety or depression. Training reduced both attentional and interpretive biases, with a **larger reduction for interpretive bias**. When participants faced a **stressor** after training, CBM significantly reduced **anxiety** but **not depression**. Most studies targeted a **single** bias; training attentional and interpretive biases together would probably have produced larger effects.
- **Haeffel et al. (2012)** used attentional bias modification aimed at **depression-relevant adjectives** (for example, failure, unlovable, worthless). Participants whose attentional bias fell most had **few depressive symptoms** and also **persisted longer on a difficult laboratory task**, a behavioural sign of low depression.

**Transfer between biases.** Because training one bias often reduces another (White et al., 2011; Amir et al., 2010; Salemink et al., 2010), CBM's benefits can extend beyond the specific bias trained.

> **Trap.** Three numbers and two directions. **Hakamata et al. (2010): r = +0.75** (reduction in attentional bias with reduction in anxiety; seven studies; bigger effects in **patients**; trait anxiety reduced). **Hallion and Ruscio (2011):** **interpretive** bias reduced **more** than attentional bias; after a stressor, CBM reduced **anxiety but not depression**. CBM is generally **more effective for anxiety than for depression**.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Most established therapies for anxiety and depression bundle several components (cognitive restructuring, exposure, behavioural activation and so on), which makes it hard to know which ingredient does the work. CBM is **narrowly focused**, so its mechanism is much easier to identify. It reduces **anxious** symptoms and, to a lesser extent, **depressive** symptoms (MacLeod & Clarke, 2013), and it is plausible that the reduction in biases is the **cause** of these benefits. The research has therefore clarified the relationship between cognitive biases and emotional states, and it supports the causal role assumed by the cognitive vulnerability hypothesis.

**Limitations** (identified by MacLeod & Mathews, 2012):

1. **Generalisation (transfer).** Large reductions in bias in the laboratory do not guarantee reductions in the very different conditions of everyday life.
2. **Narrow outcome measures.** Symptoms after CBM are measured almost entirely by **self-report**. Physiological and behavioural measures are needed to show the full extent of change.
3. **Patients do not understand the rationale.** Anxious and depressed people are often unclear why the training should help, and it would probably work better if they understood the reasoning behind it.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of CBM?" The three listed by MacLeod and Mathews (2012) are **poor transfer to everyday life**, **reliance on self-report**, and **patients not understanding why it should work**. Not a limitation: "it is too multi-component to identify its mechanism", which is the **strength** of CBM by contrast with standard therapies.

## How the chapter fits together

**The two arrows.** The chapter is organised around causation in both directions. From **cognition to emotion**: appraisals determine whether and which emotion arises (15.2), and regulation strategies, mostly cognitive, change emotions once they arise (15.3). From **emotion to cognition**: moods change attentional breadth and memory (15.4) and push judgement and decision making in mood-specific directions (15.5). The clinical sections (15.6 and 15.7) close the loop: cognitive biases are a cognitive cause of emotional disorder, and emotional disorder is a state that sustains the biases. CBM is the experiment that shows the arrow from bias to emotion is real.

**Top-down versus bottom-up.** The Ochsner et al. (2009) contrast set up in 15.1 recurs throughout. Appraisal theory is a top-down theory, and its main limitation is neglect of bottom-up processes (subliminal phobic reactions, affective blindsight). Regulation is top-down control: prefrontal cortex damping amygdala. Williams et al.'s perceptual versus conceptual distinction is the same idea applied to anxiety and depression.

**Automatic versus controlled, everywhere.** The chapter repeatedly finds a fast, automatic process beside a slow, controlled one: Smith and Kirby's memory-activation versus reasoning appraisal; implicit versus explicit emotion regulation; heuristic versus analytic decision making; Greene's affective versus cognitive moral systems; perceptual versus conceptual processing (Williams et al.); rapid versus strategic interpretive bias (Moser et al. versus Calvo & Castillo). Most of the chapter's evaluations criticise these dichotomies as oversimplified (Viviani, 2013; Keren & Schul, 2009; Kahane et al., 2012).

**Valence is not enough.** A theme running from attention to decision making: the positive-negative dimension does not predict cognitive effects on its own. **Motivational intensity** predicts attentional breadth better than valence (Harmon-Jones et al., 2011). In decision making the three negative emotions diverge: anxiety is pessimistic and risk averse, sadness is pessimistic but reward seeking, anger is optimistic and risk taking. The unifying principle is **function**: anxiety reduces uncertainty, sadness rethinks goals, anger asserts control, happiness protects itself.

**Function explains the clinical biases too.** Williams et al. (1997) applied the same functional reasoning to disorders: anxiety anticipates future threat (external focus, attentional bias, worry), depression replaces failed goals (internal focus, memory bias, rumination). The evidence matches the broad pattern but not every prediction (implicit memory bias went the wrong way).

**Regulation shapes memory.** Mood congruity and mood-state-dependent memory are stronger with positive moods because people regulate negative moods away (Rusting & DeHart, 2000), linking 15.3 to 15.4.

**The amygdala and the prefrontal cortex as the recurring brain pair.** Amygdala: bottom-up emotional response (Ochsner et al., 2009), emotional memory enhancement (BP and Urbach-Wiethe disease), somatic markers. Prefrontal cortex: top-down construction of meaning (medial PFC), regulation (VLPFC, DLPFC), utilitarian moral judgement (DLPFC) versus emotional moral responses (VMPFC).

## Worked example

**Scenario.** Meera, 24, is referred to a hospital psychology OPD with eight months of persistent worry about her job, her parents' health and whether her colleagues think she is incompetent, along with poor sleep and low mood for the last two months. On assessment she says: "When my manager does not reply to my message, I know she is unhappy with my work." She has started avoiding team lunches. She mentions that she vents to her sister every night "to get it out", but feels worse afterwards. Her mother was hospitalised last year; since then she has refused a promotion that would mean relocating, choosing to stay in a lower-paid but secure post. A trainee asks you to explain her presentation using cognitive psychology, and to justify a treatment plan.

**Which disorder profile, and the comorbidity problem.** Her worry is **future-directed** and spread across domains, pointing to **generalised anxiety disorder**. The recent low mood raises **comorbid depression**, so any bias you measure may reflect either condition. Following Eysenck et al. (2006), expect her anxiety to concern the future (her parents' health, being judged) and her low mood the past (her mother's hospitalisation).

**Her cognitive biases, named precisely.**

- *"My manager has not replied, so she is unhappy with me"* is an **interpretive bias**: an ambiguous event read as threatening. It is **interpersonal** threat, the kind Walsh et al. found trait anxiety biases most. Eysenck et al. (1991) found exactly this bias in GAD patients with ambiguous sentences.
- Whether she also has an **attentional bias** could be tested with a **dot-probe** task (faster detection of dots replacing threat words), preferably rather than an **emotional Stroop**, whose interpretation is less clear (Chajut et al., 2010) and which is less sensitive in depression (Peckham et al., 2010). Bar-Haim et al. (2007) predict she will show it for both subliminal and supraliminal threat.
- If the depression deepens, Williams et al. (1997) and the later evidence predict growing **explicit memory bias** (preferential recall of past failures), together with less attention to **positive** stimuli (Armstrong & Olatunji, 2012). Mood congruity predicts that her low mood will make negative memories more accessible, helping the mood persist.
- **Attentional control theory** predicts that her anxiety reduces working memory capacity, weakening **inhibition** (she cannot keep worry-related thoughts out) and **shifting** (she cannot move attention back to the task).

**Her emotion regulation.** Avoiding team lunches is **situation selection** used as **avoidance**, which Aldao et al. (2010) found increases anxiety and depression symptoms. Nightly venting is **response modulation** of the kind Bushman (2002) found increases rather than relieves negative feeling, because it rehearses the thoughts. Her repeated replaying of what went wrong would count as **rumination**, the other harmful strategy in Aldao et al.

**Her decisions.** Turning down a risky but better-paid promotion fits the **anxious** profile of pessimism and **risk aversion** (Raghunathan & Pham, 1999; Gambetti & Giusberti, 2012). A predominantly **sad** client would, on the same study, lean the other way, towards the high-risk, high-reward option.

**Treatment plan, justified.**

1. **Cognitive change (reappraisal)** has the best evidence among strategy families (Webb et al., 2012) and helps symptoms (Aldao et al., 2010). Teach her to generate alternative readings of the unanswered message. But the 7th edition's conditional evidence matters: reappraisal is best for **uncontrollable** stress (her parents' health). For **controllable** problems (an unclear message from her manager), **problem solving**, simply asking, is better; Troy et al. (2013) found that good reappraisers facing controllable stress were more depressed.
2. **Timing.** Introduce reappraisal while emotions are still moderate; at high intensity, reappraisal can be counterproductive and **distraction** works better (Sheppes & Gross, 2011).
3. **Replace venting and avoidance** with **acceptance** and graded return to team lunches.
4. **Cognitive bias modification** as an adjunct: **interpretive bias training** with homographs, the bias most reduced by CBM (Hallion & Ruscio, 2011), and attention bias modification with dot-probe training (Hakamata et al., 2010, r = +0.75 between bias reduction and anxiety reduction, larger effects in patients). Explain the rationale to her, since MacLeod and Mathews (2012) suggest CBM works better when patients understand why it should help; use behavioural and physiological outcome measures as well as self-report; and plan for transfer to real life.
5. **Prognosis reasoning.** The cognitive vulnerability hypothesis (Lewinsohn et al., 2001; Cohen et al., 2012) implies that while her interpretive bias persists, further negative life events (another family illness) carry a higher risk of depression. Reducing the bias is therefore preventive as well as therapeutic.

## MCQ traps

1. **Emotion vs mood:** emotion is briefer, more intense and has a specific cause; the boundary is **not** sharp, and **affect** covers both.
2. **Barrett & Russell (1998)** = valence and arousal; **Watson & Tellegen (1985)** = positive and negative affect (**50 to 65%** of mood variance). Same two-dimensional space, both pairs uncorrelated.
3. **Lindquist et al. (2014):** semantic dementia patients split positive from negative faces but not the **four negative** emotions, on a non-verbal sorting task.
4. **Ochsner et al. (2009):** negative affect tracked the **amygdala** (bottom-up) and the **medial PFC** (top-down); the amygdala was active in both.
5. **Fontaine et al. (2013):** **27 countries**, appraisal features alone classified **71%** correctly. **Parkinson (2001):** appraisal manipulations explained **under 30%** of variance.
6. **Smith & Lazarus (1993):** blaming the teaching assistants = **anger**, blaming oneself = **guilt**. **Kuppens et al. (2003); Tong (2010):** no appraisal is necessary or sufficient.
7. **Siemer & Reisenzein (2007):** appraisal judgements are **slower** than emotion judgements.
8. **Schartau et al. (2009):** "silver lining" training cut horror, distress and **GSR**: appraisal is causal.
9. **Öhman & Soares (1994):** masked feared pictures raised **skin conductance**. **Winkielman et al. (2005):** subliminal happy faces, about **twice** the drink; **38 vs 10 cents**.
10. **Tamietto et al. (2009):** **two** blindsight patients; zygomaticus (smile) and corrugator (frown) reactions **200 to 300 ms faster** when the face was **not** consciously seen.
11. **Gross's process model:** four **antecedent-focused** families (situation selection, situation modification, attention deployment, cognitive change) and one **response-focused** (response modulation).
12. **Bushman (2002):** venting **increases** anger.
13. **Webb et al. (2012), 306 comparisons:** cognitive change **moderate**, response modulation **small**, attentional deployment **non-significant**; yet distraction helped, suppressing **expression** was moderate, suppressing **experience** did nothing.
14. **Aldao et al. (2010):** acceptance, problem solving, reappraisal help; **rumination and avoidance** worsen symptoms.
15. **Troy et al. (2013):** good reappraisers were **less** depressed under uncontrollable stress but **more** depressed under **controllable** stress.
16. **Sheppes & Gross (2011):** reappraisal **backfires at high intensity**; distraction works at both levels. **Sheppes et al. (2014):** distraction is **chosen** for intense and one-off situations.
17. **Kohn et al. (2014):** **VLPFC** evaluates, **DLPFC** initiates, **STG/SMA/angular gyrus** execute. **McRae et al. (2010):** reappraisal beat distraction (a **six-letter string**).
18. **Easterbrook (1959)** narrows, **Fredrickson & Branigan (2005)** broaden, **Harmon-Jones et al. (2011):** **motivational intensity** decides (high narrows, low broadens).
19. **Sadness broadens**, disgust narrows (Gable & Harmon-Jones, 2010b); **anticipated** monetary win narrows (2011). **Fear widens** the eyes, **disgust narrows** them (Lee et al., 2014).
20. **Pêcher et al. (2009):** **happy** music was the distracting one (**8 mph** slower). **Lench et al. (2011):** films **24%**, autobiographical recall **20%**.
21. **Mood congruity** = mood matches **material**; **mood-state-dependent memory** = learning mood matches **retrieval** mood. Both stronger for **positive** moods (mood repair).
22. **Kenealy (1997):** MSDM in **free, not cued** recall. **Eich & Metcalfe (1989):** **four times** larger for **generated** items.
23. **7th edition** explains mood memory effects by **encoding specificity** (Tulving, 1979) and brain-state reinstatement (Lewis et al., 2005); **Bower's (1981) network theory** is the older account.
24. **Amygdala** links to about **90%** of cortex. **Urbach-Wiethe** patient **BP** (Cahill et al., 1995) lost the emotional memory advantage. **Adolphs et al. (2005):** gist impaired, background detail spared.
25. **Integral** = from the decision itself; **incidental** = carried over; most research uses **incidental**.
26. **Dunn et al. (2010):** Iowa gambling task, **four decks**, interoception by **heartbeat counting**. **Werner et al. (2013):** **right anterior insula**.
27. **Optimism bias** is **comparative** (vs others); **absolute** is vs an objective figure (**4% vs 7%**).
28. **Raghunathan & Pham (1999):** **anxious** chose the **secure** job, **sad** chose the **high-salary, high-risk** job.
29. **Cryder et al. (2008):** sad people paid **almost four times** as much, mainly when **high in self-focus**. **Lerner et al. (submitted):** **$19** (neutral) vs **$4** (sad) today = $100 in a year.
30. **Anger** is negative yet **optimistic** and **risk seeking** (Lerner & Keltner, 2001, Asian disease problem), because of **certainty and control**.
31. **Kugler et al. (2012):** in **pairs**, angry people avoided the partner-dependent risk (**56% vs 93%** for fearful).
32. **Anger is heuristic:** gruesome photos **41% vs 9%** guilty (Bright & Goodman-Delahunty, 2006); **seven film directors** (Coget et al., 2011); Patricia Smith welfare case (Small & Lerner, 2008).
33. **Positive mood** is **risk averse** (mood maintenance) and **heuristic** (de Vries et al., 2012); **Drace et al. (2009):** optimism bias unchanged by mood.
34. **Sadness** is the only mood the chapter links to **analytic** processing; but **moderate anxiety** was analytic in Coget et al.
35. **Hauser (2006):** **90%** divert the trolley, **10%** push on the footbridge; footbridge = **personal**, trolley = **impersonal**.
36. **Deontological = affective = VMPFC; utilitarian = cognitive = DLPFC.** VMPFC patients and high psychopathy scorers give **more** utilitarian answers; rTMS to DLPFC gives **fewer**; anti-anxiety drugs raise utilitarian answers on **personal** dilemmas only.
37. **Kahane et al. (2012):** the poppy-seed dilemma; **intuitive vs counterintuitive** matters more than utilitarian vs deontological.
38. **Worry = future, anxiety (GAD); rumination = past, depression** (Eysenck et al., 2006).
39. **Williams et al. (1997)** predicted implicit memory bias in **anxiety** not depression; the evidence went the **other way** (Mitte, 2008; Phillips et al., 2010). Mitte: anxious explicit memory bias in **recall, not recognition**.
40. **Attentional control theory:** **inhibition** = negative attentional control, **shifting** = positive attentional control. **Zetsche & Joormann (2012):** poor inhibition predicted symptoms **six months** later.
41. **Bar-Haim et al. (2007):** attentional bias with **subliminal and supraliminal** threat, similar across **all** anxiety disorders. **Rudaizky et al. (2014):** engagement and disengagement biases **uncorrelated**.
42. **Peckham et al. (2010):** depression bias clear on **dot-probe**, marginal on **Stroop**. **Chajut et al. (2010):** **avoidance** responses made threat words **faster**. **Armstrong & Olatunji (2012):** depression = **less attention to positive** stimuli.
43. **Interpretive bias numbers:** **r = +0.60** (Eysenck et al., 1987); **81% vs 63%** (Mogg et al., 2006); **1,250 ms, not 500 ms** (Calvo & Castillo, 1997); N4 at about **400 ms** (Moser et al., 2012b).
44. **Walsh et al.:** trait anxiety biases **social and intellectual**, not **physical or health**, threat.
45. **Lewinsohn et al. (2001):** adolescents, **12 months**; **Cohen et al. (2012):** children, **7 weeks**; negative events harmed only the cognitively vulnerable.
46. **Homophone** (die/dye) **measures** interpretive bias; **homograph** (choke, bank) is used in CBM **training**.
47. **Hakamata et al. (2010):** **r = +0.75**, **seven** studies, bigger in **patients**, trait anxiety reduced. **Hallion & Ruscio (2011):** interpretive bias fell **more**; anxiety but **not depression** fell after a stressor.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Introspection** | Looking inwards at, and describing, one's own mental contents |
| **Affect** | Umbrella term for emotions and moods, positive or negative |
| **Valence** | Whether an emotional experience is good or bad, from very negative to very positive |
| **Categorical / dimensional approach** | Emotions as a set of discrete kinds / as positions on continuous dimensions such as valence and arousal |
| **Amygdala** | Front temporal-lobe structure tied to fear and other emotions; a hub reaching about 90% of cortex |
| **Bottom-up / top-down emotional processing** | Emotion driven by perceiving the stimulus / built from appraising its meaning |
| **Appraisal** | Evaluating what a situation means for one's well-being |
| **Affective blindsight** | Discriminating emotional stimuli that cannot be consciously seen after visual cortex damage |
| **Emotion generation** | The immediate, spontaneous emotional reaction to a situation |
| **Emotion regulation** | Deliberate, goal-driven effort to change the size or duration of a spontaneous emotion, usually a negative one |
| **Process model** | Gross and Thompson's (2007) model placing five regulation families along the timeline of an emotion |
| **Antecedent- / response-focused strategy** | Acting before a full response forms / after it has formed |
| **Distraction** | Turning attention away from emotional processing towards neutral material |
| **Reappraisal** | Elaborating emotional information and then changing its meaning |
| **Suppression** | Response modulation that hides emotional expression or tries to stifle the feeling itself |
| **Explicit / implicit emotion regulation** | Conscious, deliberate regulation / regulation triggered automatically and run without awareness |
| **Rumination / worry** | Dwelling on past negative experiences (depression) / repetitive concern about possible future events (anxiety) |
| **Motivational intensity** | Strength of the urge to approach or avoid; high narrows attention, low broadens it |
| **Mood congruity** | Better learning and recall of material whose emotional tone matches the current mood |
| **Mood-state-dependent memory** | Better memory when mood at retrieval matches mood at learning |
| **Encoding specificity principle** | Recall depends on overlap between retrieval information and the stored trace (Tulving) |
| **"Do-it-yourself" principle** | Eich's claim that mood-dependence is strongest when effortful self-generated processing is required |
| **Urbach-Wiethe disease** | Rare disease destroying the amygdala and adjacent tissue |
| **Integral / incidental emotions** | Emotions produced by the decision itself / carried over from unrelated events |
| **Somatic marker hypothesis** | Damasio's (1994) idea that bodily arousal signals tag events and guide later choices |
| **Interoception** | Sensitivity to internal bodily signals, conscious or not |
| **Optimism bias** | Believing good events are likelier and bad events less likely for oneself than for others |
| **Unrealistic comparative / absolute optimism** | Optimism relative to other people / relative to an objective risk figure |
| **Depressive realism** | Disputed claim that sad or depressed people judge the future more accurately |
| **Misery-is-not-miserly effect** | Sad people's willingness to pay more for goods than others |
| **Personal / impersonal moral dilemma** | Harm done directly by your own act (footbridge) / harm following less directly (trolley) |
| **Deontological judgements** | Moral judgements based on rules or duties, such as "do not kill" |
| **Utilitarian judgements** | Moral judgements based on practical outcomes, such as saving the most lives |
| **Dual-process model (moral)** | Greene et al.'s (2008) fast affective system (VMPFC) versus slow cognitive system (DLPFC, ACC) |
| **Trait anxiety** | Personality dimension of susceptibility to anxiety |
| **Comorbidity** | Two or more mental disorders in the same patient at the same time |
| **Attentional bias** | Preferential attention to threat stimuli shown alongside neutral ones (fast engagement, slow disengagement) |
| **Interpretive bias** | Tendency to take the threatening meaning of ambiguous stimuli or situations |
| **Explicit memory bias** | Recalling relatively more negative information on tests needing conscious recollection |
| **Implicit memory bias** | Relatively better performance for negative information on memory tests not needing conscious recollection |
| **Memory bias** | General term for retrieving relatively more negative or unpleasant than positive or neutral information |
| **Perceptual / conceptual processes** | Williams et al.'s fast, data-driven processes / slower, top-down, controlled processes |
| **Combined cognitive bias hypothesis** | Hirsch et al.'s (2006) claim that cognitive biases influence and interact with each other |
| **Attentional control theory** | Anxiety reduces working memory capacity, impairing inhibition and shifting (Eysenck et al., 2007) |
| **Dot-probe task** | A dot replaces one of two simultaneous stimuli; faster detection at the threat location shows attentional bias |
| **Emotional Stroop task** | Naming the ink colour of emotional and neutral words; slower naming for emotional words is read as bias |
| **Homophone** | Words that sound alike but are spelled differently (die/dye) |
| **Homograph** | A word with one spelling and two or more meanings (bank, patient) |
| **Cognitive vulnerability hypothesis** | Healthy people with cognitive biases are more likely to develop anxiety or depression after negative life events |
| **Cognitive bias modification** | Training, usually to reduce attentional or interpretive bias, used to test causality and as therapy |

## Likely exam points

1. **Part V framing:** emotion and consciousness cut across all of cognition; Aristotle on fear; the Tulving test; Watson's think-aloud paradox.
2. **Emotion, mood, affect, valence**, and the two-way relation between cognition and emotion.
3. **Categorical vs dimensional** approaches, reconciled through Lindquist et al. (2012, 2014).
4. **Ochsner et al. (2009)** bottom-up vs top-down emotion; inhibitory control damps emotion (Tang & Schmeichel, 2014).
5. **Appraisal theory's assumptions**; Scherer and Ellsworth's anger and sadness profiles; Fontaine et al. (2013).
6. **Smith and Kirby's two routes**; Lazarus's primary and secondary appraisal and the Zajonc-Lazarus debate as background.
7. **Conscious-appraisal evidence:** Smith and Lazarus, Parkinson's critique, Kuppens and Tong, Siemer et al. (2007), Bennett and Lowe, Ceulemans.
8. **Causality in appraisal:** Siemer and Reisenzein (2007) timing; Schartau et al. (2009) training.
9. **Non-conscious emotion:** Öhman and Soares, Winkielman et al., affective blindsight, Tamietto et al. (2009).
10. **Four limitations of appraisal theory.**
11. **Generation vs regulation** (Gross, 2013) and why they blur.
12. **Gross's process model:** five families, antecedent vs response focus, Bushman on venting.
13. **Distraction vs reappraisal:** choice and timing (Sheppes et al., 2014; Sheppes & Gross, 2011).
14. **Effectiveness:** Augustine and Hemenover; Aldao et al.; Webb et al. with its within-family exceptions; Troy et al. on controllability.
15. **Neural regulation:** PFC damps amygdala; Kohn et al.'s three networks; McRae et al.; S.-H. Lee et al.
16. **Implicit regulation:** Gyurak et al.; Mauss et al. (2007); Williams et al. (2009).
17. **Breadth of attention:** Easterbrook vs Fredrickson and Branigan vs Harmon-Jones, tested by Gable and Harmon-Jones; Lee et al. (2014).
18. **Attention to memory:** weapon focus, Riggs et al. (2011), Talarico et al. (2009).
19. **Mood congruity and its positive-negative asymmetry** (mood repair, Rusting & DeHart, 2000).
20. **Encoding specificity and reinstatement** (Lewis et al., 2005) versus Bower's older network theory.
21. **Mood-state-dependent memory:** Ucros, Kenealy, Eich's do-it-yourself principle, Eich and Metcalfe.
22. **Amygdala and emotional memory:** Murty, Adolphs, Urbach-Wiethe and BP, Siebert, Talmi.
23. **Integral vs incidental emotion; somatic markers and interoception** (Damasio, 1994; Dunn et al., 2010).
24. **Anxiety:** pessimism, reduced optimism bias, risk aversion (lab and real-life evidence).
25. **Sadness:** pessimism, depressive realism with caveats, reward seeking, misery-is-not-miserly, impatience.
26. **Anger:** optimism and risk through control; Kugler's reversal; heuristic processing evidence.
27. **Positive mood:** risk aversion, heuristic processing, de Vries strategy-mood fit, Drace et al., the eight positive moods.
28. **The four mood profiles, their functions and the research's limitations.**
29. **Moral dilemmas:** trolley vs footbridge, Greene's dual-process model, its support and its three problems.
30. **Anxiety vs depression:** comorbidity, future vs past, worry vs rumination.
31. **The four cognitive biases**, their measures and the causality question.
32. **Williams et al. (1997):** perceptual vs conceptual processing, the four predictions, which failed.
33. **Cognitive control:** Joormann's model of depression and attentional control theory of anxiety.
34. **Bias evidence:** attentional (Bar-Haim, Rudaizky, Peckham, Armstrong & Olatunji), interpretive (Eysenck 1987 and 1991, Mogg, Calvo & Castillo, Moser) and memory (Phillips, Mitte).
35. **Combined bias, vulnerability and CBM:** transfer studies, Lewinsohn and Cohen, CBM methods and meta-analyses, MacLeod and Mathews's three limitations.
