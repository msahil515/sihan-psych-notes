# Chapter 12: Problem solving and expertise

**What this chapter is really about.** This chapter opens Part IV, "Thinking and reasoning". The part opener contrasts two snapshots of thought: a drowsy, drifting interior monologue from Joyce's *Ulysses* (Molly Bloom) and the first author talking himself through a stubborn PowerPoint slide. Together they show three things. Thinking is usually conscious, but we are aware mostly of its *products*, not its processes, and our later reports of it are unreliable. Thinking varies in how **directed** it is (the monologue drifts; the slide has a sharp goal). And it varies in how much **knowledge** it uses (a little hard-won technical know-how for the slide; a lifetime of knowledge about people for the monologue). The opener adds a warning for Chapters 12 to 14: one cognitive system does all kinds of thinking, so the divisions between problem solving, judgement, decision making and reasoning are partly arbitrary and the same brain regions keep reappearing. It also notes a recent shift in reasoning research from formal deduction to **informal reasoning**, which draws on knowledge and experience much as judgement and decision making do.

Within that frame, Chapter 12 asks three questions. How do people solve problems they have never met before, especially when the answer arrives as a sudden "aha"? How do people use an old problem to solve a new one (analogy)? And what makes an expert, such as a chess grandmaster or a radiologist, different from a novice, and is that difference built by practice alone? A single tension ties the three together: **past experience** is the engine of good problem solving (it supplies analogies, templates and fast pattern recognition) and also its main saboteur (it produces functional fixedness and mental set, and even makes chess masters miss the shortest win).

**How to read this chapter.** Sections 12.1 and 12.2 give the vocabulary every later section uses (well-defined versus ill-defined, knowledge-rich versus knowledge-lean), with the Monty Hall box previewing why people fail. Section 12.3 is the Gestalt tradition: insight, hints, incubation, Ohlsson's representational change theory, functional fixedness and mental set. Section 12.4 is Newell and Simon's information-processing tradition: problem spaces, heuristics, planning, the prefrontal cortex and the cognitive miser. Section 12.5 covers analogy. Sections 12.6 to 12.10 cover expertise: chess (template theory), medicine (fast versus analytic diagnosis), brain plasticity (taxi drivers, musicians) and Ericsson's deliberate practice theory, which the chapter cuts down to "necessary but not sufficient". Most theories end with an evaluation, and the limitations are as examinable as the claims.

## 12.1 Introduction

### Where this chapter sits: the forms of thinking

The Part IV opener includes a box defining the forms of thinking covered across Chapters 12 to 14. You will meet these terms again in the next two chapters, so fix them now.

| Form of thinking | What it involves (in brief) | Main chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem solving** | Getting from the recognition that a problem exists, through a series of steps, to a solution; most other forms of thinking contain some problem solving | 12 |
| **Decision making** | Choosing one option out of several, where the choice has personal consequences | 13 |
| **Judgement** | The part of decision making that estimates how likely various events are; the emphasis is on accuracy | 13 |
| **Deductive reasoning** | Working out which conclusions *must* follow if certain statements are assumed true; supposedly based on logic | 14 |
| **Inductive reasoning** | Deciding whether statements or hypotheses are true from the available evidence; used by scientists and detectives, but never guaranteed to give valid conclusions | 14 |
| **Informal reasoning** | Judging how strong an argument is, using one's own knowledge and experience | 14 |

Chapter 12 concentrates on problem solving, with emphasis on the role of learning and the skills of experts. Chapter 13 covers judgement and decision making, where the running theme is our heavy reliance on **heuristics** (simple, error-prone rules of thumb). Chapter 14 covers inductive, deductive and informal reasoning and the question of whether humans are rational, to which most psychologists give the deliberately unsatisfying answer "yes and no".

> **Trap.** Deductive and inductive reasoning are defined by what they *guarantee*. Deduction asks what follows *necessarily* from premises taken as true; induction asks whether a hypothesis is *supported* by evidence and can never guarantee a valid conclusion. A question that describes a detective or scientist drawing a probable conclusion from clues is describing induction, however "logical" the detective sounds.

### Three everyday problems, three topics

The chapter begins with three ordinary predicaments. You must reach an urgent meeting in another city, but the trains run late, the car is unreliable and the buses are slow. You are trying to recall how to make a new computer do something, and you reach back to what worked on the old one. You are an expert chess player in a hard position with the clock running. Each one previews a topic.

1. **Problem solving.** Goel (2010) gives a four-part definition worth knowing in substance: there must be two situations, a current one and a desired one; the person is in the first and wants to be in the second; the route from one to the other is not obvious to them; and closing the gap takes several steps that are consciously guided. Notice the last two conditions. If the route is obvious, there is no problem; if the gap is closed in a single automatic step, it is not problem solving in this sense.
2. **Analogical problem solving.** In daily life we constantly lean on past experience, noticing that the present problem resembles one we solved before and borrowing its solution.
3. **Expertise.** Experts have extensive specialist knowledge in a domain. Expertise overlaps with problem solving, because experts are very efficient solvers of problems in their domain, but the two research traditions differ in emphasis.

| | Problem-solving research | Expertise research |
|---|---|---|
| Role of knowledge | Usually minimised | Central |
| Individual differences | Mostly ignored | A main focus |
| Typical question | What processes do people use? | What separates experts from novices (knowledge, strategy)? |

## 12.2 Problem Solving: Introduction

### Three defining features

Eysenck and Keane pick out three features of problem solving:

1. It is **purposeful**: it is directed at a goal.
2. It relies on **controlled** processes and is not purely automatic.
3. A problem exists only when the person **lacks the knowledge to produce an immediate solution**. This makes "problem" relative to the solver. A sum that is a genuine problem for most people is not a problem at all for a professional mathematician, who simply knows how to do it.

### Well-defined versus ill-defined problems

A **well-defined problem** has every component clearly specified: the starting state, the moves or strategies allowed, and the goal, which is specified clearly enough that you know when you have reached it (the centre of a maze, for instance). Chess is the standard example. It is enormously complex, but it is well-defined: the starting position is fixed, the rules define every legal move, and the goal is checkmate.

An **ill-defined problem** is underspecified in one or more of these respects. The book's example is deciding to become happier. The number of possible strategies is unlimited, you cannot tell in advance which will work, and because happiness fluctuates and resists definition, you cannot even be sure when the problem has been solved.

Most real-life problems are ill-defined, yet psychologists have mostly studied well-defined ones (Goel, 2010). There are two reasons, and both are about experimental convenience rather than real-world importance. Well-defined problems have an **optimal solution strategy**, and the researcher **knows the right answer**, so it is easy to see exactly where and how a participant's strategy goes wrong.

> **Key study, Goel & Grafman (2000): patient PF.** PF had damage to the **right prefrontal cortex**. His IQ was **128**, well above average, and he did well on well-defined laboratory problems. In everyday life, though, he coped very badly with ill-defined problems. The reason was planning: he could not construct the rough preliminary plan that gives an unstructured problem some shape, and without that structure he could not get started. Well-defined laboratory tasks supply their own structure, so they never exposed the deficit.

PF is the chapter's first clinical lesson and recurs in 12.4. Standard tests are mostly well-defined, so a frontal patient can score normally, even brilliantly, on them while failing at running a life. The well-defined versus ill-defined distinction therefore predicts where such patients will and will not show their deficit.

> **Trap.** PF's lesion was in the **right** prefrontal cortex, and his IQ was **high** (128). Questions sometimes offer "left frontal damage" or "low IQ explains his everyday failures". The point of the case is the dissociation: intact intelligence on well-defined tests, impaired planning on ill-defined real-life problems.

### Knowledge-rich versus knowledge-lean problems

The second distinction cuts across the first. A **knowledge-rich problem** can be solved only by someone who has a great deal of relevant background knowledge; chess problems are the example. A **knowledge-lean problem** can be solved without specialist knowledge, because nearly all the information needed is in the problem statement itself. Traditional problem-solving research used knowledge-lean problems on purpose, because they keep individual differences in knowledge out of the results. Expertise research (12.6 onwards) does the opposite and uses knowledge-rich problems.

> **Trap.** "Well-defined" and "knowledge-lean" are not synonyms. Chess is **well-defined** (clear start, rules and goal) and also **knowledge-rich** (you need a vast store of chess knowledge to play it well). The Tower of Hanoi is well-defined *and* knowledge-lean. Becoming happier is ill-defined. Keep the two dimensions separate.

### In the real world: the Monty Hall problem

The Monty Hall problem is named after the host of an American television game show and shows, in one puzzle, most of the reasons people fail at problems.

**The problem.** There are three closed doors; a car is behind one and goats are behind the other two. You choose a door. The host, who knows where the car is, then opens one of the doors you did not choose and reveals a goat. He offers you the chance to switch to the remaining closed door. Should you?

About **85%** of people stick with their original choice (**Burns & Wieth, 2004**). That is the wrong decision: switching wins **two-thirds** of the time.

**Why switching is right, first argument.** Your first pick was made at random among three doors, so it had a one-third chance of hiding the car. Whatever you picked, the host can always open a goat door, because at least one of the two unchosen doors has a goat. His action therefore tells you nothing new about *your* door, which stays at one-third. The remaining two-thirds now sits entirely on the other closed door.

**Why switching is right, second argument (Krauss & Wang, 2003; Figure 12.1).** List the three possible arrangements, assuming you picked door 1:

| Scenario | Where the car is | What the host must do | Result of switching |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Door 2 | Open door 3 (the only goat door left) | Win |
| 2 | Door 3 | Open door 2 (the only goat door left) | Win |
| 3 | Door 1 (your door) | Open either 2 or 3 | Lose |

Switching wins in two of three scenarios. In scenarios 1 and 2 the host has no choice about which door to open, and that constraint is exactly the information people overlook.

**Humans versus pigeons.** **Herbranson and Schroeder (2010)** found that humans, even after extensive practice, switched on **66%** of trials, whereas pigeons switched on **96%**: the pigeons simply maximised reward, while the humans tried more elaborate strategies.

**Why do people do so badly?** The box gives four reasons, each previewing a theme of the chapter:

1. **A heuristic.** People apply the **uniformity fallacy** (Falk & Lann, 2008), assuming that all available options are equally likely whether or not they are. Two doors left, so it must be fifty-fifty.
2. **Limited processing capacity.** The problem loads the **central executive**, the attention-like controller of working memory (Chapter 6). **De Neys and Verschueren (2006)** found that only **8%** solved it while doing a demanding central-executive task at the same time, against **22%** without the extra load.
3. **Misrepresenting the problem.** Most people treat the host's choice as random. **Burns and Wieth (2004)** made the causal structure visible with a boxing version: of three boxers, one is certain to beat anyone; you pick one boxer, the other two fight, and the winner then fights your boxer, and you win if you picked the winner of that second bout. Here **51%** correctly switched, against only **15%** in the standard door version, because it is obvious that whoever won the first bout won through skill, not luck.
4. **Real-world suspicion.** Television hosts sometimes play tricks, so a contestant may reasonably wonder whether the host opened a door only *because* the first choice was right (Schuller, 2012).

In one line: we fail because we use heuristics, because our capacity is limited, and because we misrepresent the problem (here, its causal structure). The correct answer is **switch**, not "it makes no difference".

## 12.3 Gestalt Approach: Insight and Role of Experience

### Reproductive and productive thinking

The first serious research on problem solving came from the **Gestalt psychologists**, a group of German researchers active from roughly the 1920s to the 1940s. They drew a distinction that still organises the field:

- **Reproductive thinking** re-applies past experience in a systematic way. You have met this kind of problem before, and you do what worked last time.
- **Productive thinking** requires a **new restructuring** of the problem, seeing it in a way you have not seen it before. It is the more complex of the two.

Classic background: the best-known Gestalt texts are Wertheimer's *Productive Thinking* (1945) and Köhler's chimpanzee studies of the 1920s, in which Sultan suddenly joined two short sticks to reach fruit. Older keys link "insight learning" to Köhler and contrast it with Thorndike's trial and error; the 7th edition skips both and goes straight to modern tests of the Gestalt claim.

### Insight

The Gestaltists claimed that problems needing productive thinking are often solved by **insight**: a sudden restructuring of the problem, sometimes accompanied by the "**ah-ha experience**". Kounios and Beeman (2014) give a more technical definition whose core is that insight is a sudden understanding or solution that comes from **reorganising the elements of one's mental representation** so as to reach an interpretation that was **not obvious or not dominant** before. The KEY TERM box gives the plain version: insight is the experience of suddenly realising how to solve a problem.

**The mutilated draughtboard problem (Figure 12.2)** is the chapter's demonstration insight problem. Start with a draughtboard (checkerboard) of 64 squares covered by **32 dominoes**, each domino covering two adjacent squares. Now cut off two squares at diagonally opposite corners. Can the remaining **62 squares** be covered by **31 dominoes**?

Almost everybody starts by mentally laying dominoes, which is hopeless: there are **758,148** possible arrangements, and very few people solve the problem unaided. The insight uses something you already know. Every domino covers **one black and one white square**, and the two removed corners lie on one diagonal, so they are the **same colour**. The board now has two more squares of one colour than the other, so 31 dominoes cannot cover it. Once the problem is represented by colour instead of geometry, the proof takes one sentence.

**Is insight special?** The theoretical debate is whether insight involves a distinct process or the same processes as any other thinking (the "**business-as-usual**" view). Eysenck and Keane's own position is that insight problems probably involve a **mixture** of both (citing Weisberg, 2014). Keep this middle position in mind; it is the 7th edition's answer.

> **Trap.** The Gestalt account presents insight as a sudden, all-or-none restructuring. The 7th edition keeps the *experience* of suddenness but argues, with evidence below (Ellis et al., 2011), that the underlying *processes* can be gradual and unconscious, and it treats insight as a mixture of special and business-as-usual processes. An older key may mark "insight is sudden and discontinuous" as simply true; the modern answer is that the *feeling* is sudden while the *processing* need not be.

### Findings: is insight really sudden?

> **Key study, Metcalfe & Wiebe (1987).** Participants rated their feeling of "**warmth**", how close they felt to the solution, while working on insight and non-insight problems. On **non-insight** problems warmth rose **steadily**, as you would expect if the solution is reached through a sequence of steps. On **insight** problems warmth stayed **flat and low** and then shot up **just before** the solution appeared.

This is the classic behavioural evidence that insight *feels* different, but it is subjective, and the findings below show why feelings cannot settle what the underlying process is.

### Findings: brain activity and insight

Researchers have looked for a brain signature of insight (reviewed by Kounios & Beeman, 2014).

> **Key study, Bowden et al. (2005).** Participants did the **Remote Associates Test (RAT)**: they saw three words (the book's example is *fence, card, master*) and had to find one word that forms a compound with each (here, *post*). On each trial they reported whether the solution came with insight. Brain differences between insight and non-insight solutions were concentrated in the **right hemisphere**, specifically the **right anterior superior temporal gyrus (aSTG)**, which was active **only** for insight solutions (Figure 12.3). This region is linked to processing **distant semantic relations** between words, and to reinterpretation and semantic integration.

Why the right hemisphere? Bowden and Jung-Beeman (2007) argue that **integrating weak, distant associations**, which is what an insight solution needs, happens mostly in the right hemisphere, while the **left** mainly produces **strong activation of closely related associations**: the left gives you the obvious associate, the right keeps remote ones faintly alive.

Two further regions are often linked to insight:

- The **anterior cingulate cortex** (within BA24, BA32 and BA33), which is involved in **detecting cognitive conflict and breaking mindsets**. That fits insight, which means abandoning one way of representing a problem for a better one.
- The **prefrontal cortex**, active in several insight studies (Kounios & Beeman, 2014), as you would expect for a high-level cognitive process.

Eysenck and Keane add the essential methodological caveat: this neuroscience evidence is **correlational**. Showing that an area is active during insight does not show that the area is *necessary* for it.

Classic background: the RAT was devised by Mednick (1962) as a test of creative, associative thinking. Papers sometimes ask for its originator; the 7th edition uses it only as a laboratory insight task.

### Findings: insight without awareness of the process

> **Key study, Ellis et al. (2011).** Participants solved anagrams presented as five letters in a circle: four consonants and a vowel, where four letters made a word and one consonant was a distractor (the book's example is K A F M S). On most trials participants said the solution came **suddenly**. But their **eye movements** told a different story. Over the course of each such trial, they spent a **steadily shrinking proportion of time looking at the distractor letter**. They were gradually homing in on the relevant letters without knowing it.

So insight may rest on processes **below conscious awareness**: knowledge accumulates gradually, and only its arrival in consciousness is sudden.

**Cushen and Wiley (2012)** point the same way. They classified participants as insight or non-insight solvers by the processes actually used and found **no relationship** between those processes and how insightful people judged their own solutions, presumably because people have little access to their own solution processes (the part opener's point about products versus processes).

### Facilitating insight: hints

Hints help with insight problems, which is unsurprising. The surprise is how **subtle** an effective hint can be.

**Maier's (1931) two-string (pendulum) problem.** Participants enter a room containing various objects (poles, pliers, extension cords and so on) and two strings hanging from the ceiling (Figure 12.4). They must tie the strings together, but the strings are too far apart to hold one and reach the other. The insightful and rarely found solution is to tie the **pliers** to one string as a weight, set it **swinging like a pendulum**, hold the other string, and catch the swinging one as it comes towards you.

Classic background: in Maier's original study the experimenter, as if by accident, brushed against one string to set it moving, and this casual hint helped many participants, often without their noticing that they had been helped.

> **Key study, Thomas & Lleras (2009).** Participants working on the pendulum problem took occasional exercise breaks. One group's exercise involved **swinging** their arms; the other group **stretched** their arms. The swinging group solved the problem more often, yet participants were **unaware** of any link between their arm movements and the problem.

A hint can therefore work **without awareness of its relevance**, the third finding in a row in which the key processing happens outside awareness.

### Facilitating insight: incubation and sleep

**Wallas (1926)** argued that **incubation**, setting a problem aside for a while, helps solve it, because the **subconscious mind keeps working** on the problem during the break.

Classic background: Wallas proposed four stages of creative problem solving: **preparation**, **incubation**, **illumination** (the insight) and **verification**. The order is a favourite MCQ; the 7th edition discusses only the incubation stage.

The standard design compares an experimental group given an incubation period away from an unsolved problem with a control group that keeps working on it continuously.

> **Key study, Sio & Ormerod (2009), meta-analysis.** Three findings:
> 1. Incubation effects, generally **small**, appeared in **73%** of studies.
> 2. They were **stronger** for **creative problems with multiple possible solutions** than for **linguistic and verbal problems with a single solution**. A plausible reason is that incubation **broadens the search** through knowledge, which pays off when there are many possible answers.
> 3. They were **larger after a long preparation period** before the break, probably because a long preparation makes it more likely that the solver has reached an **impasse**, and an impasse is what incubation helps to break.

**Sleeping on a problem.** **Wagner et al. (2004)** gave participants mathematical problems that each contained a simple but hard-to-spot short cut, then retested them several hours later. Of those who **slept** between training and test, **59%** found the short cut, against **25%** of those who stayed awake. The benefit has limits: **Sio et al. (2013)**, using the RAT, found sleep improved performance on **difficult** problems but not on **easy** ones.

**Why does incubation work?** **Simon (1966)** proposed that during incubation the problem solver **forgets control information**, the record of which strategies have already been tried. Losing that record frees the solver to approach the problem afresh. **Penaloza and Calvillo (2012)** supported the forgetting idea. Participants solved insight problems with or without **misleading clues**; some worked continuously and others had a **two-minute** incubation break. Incubation helped **only when the break allowed the misleading information to be forgotten**.

> **Trap.** Wallas (1926) explained incubation by **continued subconscious work**; Simon (1966) and Penaloza and Calvillo (2012) explain it by **forgetting** (of control information or misleading information). If asked which account the 7th-ed evidence supports, choose forgetting.

### Representational change theory

**Ohlsson (1992)** asked why problem solvers so often get stuck. His answer was that an **impasse** (a block) usually happens because the problem has been **represented wrongly**; the mutilated draughtboard, represented as geometry instead of colour, is a perfect example. According to his **representational change theory**, insight requires the representation to change, and there are **three ways** this can happen:

| Route | What changes | Draughtboard example |
|---|---|---|
| **Constraint relaxation** | Self-imposed restrictions on what counts as a permissible move are lifted | (see the matchstick and nine-dot problems below) |
| **Re-encoding** | Some element of the representation is reinterpreted | Each domino is re-encoded as "one black plus one white square" |
| **Elaboration** | New information is added to the representation | The board is elaborated as "now missing two squares of the same colour" |

Changing the representation is clearly close to the Gestalt notion of **restructuring**. Ohlsson's contribution was to specify **how** the restructuring happens, which the Gestaltists had left vague.

**The Öllinger et al. (2014) extension (Figure 12.5).** Öllinger and colleagues turned Ohlsson's account into a flow of stages:

1. Prior knowledge and the perceptual features of the problem produce an initial **problem representation**.
2. That representation is **searched** using heuristics.
3. If the search keeps failing, the solver hits an **impasse**.
4. The impasse prompts a **new representation**.
5. The new representation is **searched again**, and the cycle continues until a solution is found or the solver gives up.

The new element is step 5: even after insight has broken an impasse, **search may still be needed**. Insight changes the space you are searching; it does not necessarily deliver the answer.

### Findings: constraint relaxation in matchstick arithmetic

> **Key study, Knoblich et al. (1999).** Participants saw false equations written in **Roman numerals** made of matchsticks and had to move **one** stick to make the equation true (Figure 12.6).
> - **Type A** problems required changing **values**. For example, VI = VII + I (6 = 7 + 1) becomes VII = VI + I (7 = 6 + 1).
> - **Type B** problems required a less obvious change to the **operators**. For example, IV = III − I (4 = 3 − 1) becomes IV − III = I (4 − 3 = 1).
>
> Type B problems were **much harder**.

The prediction came from arithmetic experience: most operations we learn change the numbers, hardly any change the operators (+, − and =), so "leave the operators alone" is a deeply ingrained constraint and harder to relax.

**Knoblich et al. (2001)** added **eye-tracking**. On both problem types, participants initially looked much longer at the **values** than at the **operators**, showing that their starting representation assumed the values were what needed changing.

### Findings: the prefrontal cortex imposes constraints

If constraints on insight problems are imposed by the **lateral prefrontal cortex**, then damage there should, paradoxically, *help*.

> **Key study, Reverberi et al. (2005).** Patients with **lateral prefrontal damage** solved **82%** of the hardest matchstick problems, against **43%** of healthy controls.

> **Key study, Chi & Snyder (2011).** Using the same problems, they applied brain stimulation that **reduced excitability of the left lateral prefrontal cortex** and **increased excitability of the right** homologous area. **60%** of stimulated participants solved the hardest matchstick problem, against **20%** of controls.

The interpretation: lowering left lateral prefrontal excitability probably weakened the **inhibitions** that block constraint relaxation, and raising right-sided excitability may have boosted **insight processes**, consistent with Bowden's right-hemisphere findings.

> **Trap.** Direction: frontal **patients outperformed** controls here, and so did stimulated participants (with the **left** dampened, the **right** boosted). Contrast the planning evidence in 12.4, where frontal damage makes performance *worse*.

### Findings: when hints help most

Representational change theory makes a timing prediction. Before an impasse, the solver has not yet built a firm representation, so there is nothing for the hint to change. Long after an impasse, the solver is too fixated on the faulty representation. **At** the impasse, the representation is formed but not yet rigid, and a hint should do the most good. **Moss et al. (2011)** confirmed this: hints given **at the point of impasse** improved performance more than hints given before or after it.

### In the real world: constraint relaxation and magic tricks

**Danek et al. (2014)** treated working out a magic trick as an insight problem needing representational change. In their ice-cube trick (Figure 12.7), water is poured into an apparently empty mug, the mug is upturned, and only a large ice cube drops out. That seems impossible if you assume ordinary objects, real water, an empty mug and a real ice cube. In fact a napkin glued inside the mug, alongside the cube, absorbs the water. A **verbal cue relaxing the assumption that the mug was empty** significantly improved solution: constraint relaxation in a real-world setting.

### Findings: the nine-dot problem and the limits of insight

In the **nine-dot problem** (Figure 12.8) you must draw **four straight lines** through all nine dots, arranged in a 3 by 3 square, without lifting your pencil. Most people assume, without being told, that the lines must stay **inside the square** formed by the dots, so the key insight is to relax that constraint.

A long-standing puzzle is that success rates stay fairly low **even when people are told explicitly that they may go outside the square**. **Öllinger et al. (2014)** explained why: relaxing the constraint helps, but it still leaves a huge number of possible four-line combinations. As their theory predicts, solvers needed **efficient search after the insight** to find the solution.

### Evaluation of representational change theory

**Strengths.**

- It extended the Gestalt approach by stating **precisely** the mechanisms of insight (constraint relaxation, re-encoding, elaboration).
- It is a productive marriage of **Gestalt ideas** with the **information-processing approach**.
- Öllinger et al.'s (2014) version improved it by stressing that efficient **search** is often needed **after** as well as before the impasse.

**Limitations.**

1. We usually cannot **predict when or why** a representation will change.
2. Ohlsson played down **individual differences** in problem-solving ability, such as **working memory capacity**.
3. The theory wrongly implies that **constraint relaxation is usually enough** to solve an insight problem; the nine-dot problem shows it is not (Öllinger et al., 2014).

> **Trap.** "Constraint relaxation is sufficient to solve insight problems" is a stated **limitation** of the original theory, not one of its strengths. The nine-dot problem is the evidence: telling people they may go outside the square does not by itself solve it.

### Past experience: functional fixedness

Past experience normally makes us better problem solvers, but the Gestaltists showed convincingly that it can also mislead, and many failures on insight problems happen for exactly that reason. The first negative effect is **functional fixedness**: rigidly thinking of an object only in terms of its usual function(s), so that we miss other uses for it.

> **Key study, Duncker (1945): the candle problem.** Participants received a candle, a book of matches, a box of tacks and some other objects (Figure 12.9), and were asked to fix the candle to the wall beside a table so that it would not drip onto the table. Most tried to tack the candle straight to the wall or to stick it on with melted wax. Few found the right solution: **empty the tack box, tack the box to the wall, and use it as a platform** for the candle. Duncker's interpretation was that people were **fixated on the box's function as a container** and so could not see it as a platform. More people solved the problem when the box was presented **empty** at the start, because it then looked less like a container.

> **Key study, Ye et al. (2009).** A more direct demonstration that prior experience causes fixedness. Participants judged whether each of nine objects could serve one function (for example, as packing material to protect an egg in a box), and then immediately judged whether the same objects could serve a second function (for example, being thrown and caught over a distance of **15 feet**). Some objects, such as a **ski cap** or a **pillow**, could do both. **Having decided that an object served the first function made people less likely to notice that it could also serve the second.**

### Overcoming functional fixedness

**Challoner (2009)** studied the creation of **1,001 important inventions** along with the solutions to insight problems. Nearly every case followed two steps: (1) notice a **rarely noticed or new feature**, and (2) build a solution on that **obscure feature**.

**McCaffrey (2012)** argued that the crucial obscure features are overlooked because we attend to an object's typical function, as cued by its shape, size, material and so on. He proposed the **generic-parts technique** to counter this: (1) describe each part of the object in **function-free** terms (a candle becomes "wax" and "string", not "a candle"), then (2) ask whether each description suggests a use. Participants trained in the technique solved **83%** of insight problems such as Duncker's candle problem, against **49%** of controls.

**Chrysikou et al. (2013)** proposed that functional fixedness is partly caused by **too much cognitive control**. Participants generated either a common or an uncommon use for objects. Transcranial stimulation of the **left prefrontal cortex**, designed to **reduce** cognitive control, improved performance **only** when **uncommon** uses had to be generated. This fits neatly with Reverberi et al. and Chi and Snyder above: the prefrontal cortex that keeps us on task also keeps us inside conventional constraints.

### Past experience: mental set

The second negative effect is **mental set**: continuing to use a strategy that worked before even when it is no longer appropriate, or when a better one is available. Mental set is not purely a flaw. It lets us solve a run of problems of the same type quickly and with little effort; the cost appears only when the problem type quietly changes.

> **Key study, Luchins (1942): the water-jar problems.** Participants used three jars of different capacities to obtain an exact quantity of water. In the sample problem, Jar A holds **28** quarts, Jar B **76** and Jar C **3**, and the target is **25** quarts. The easy solution is to fill A and pour from it into C once, leaving 25 in A. Of participants who had previously had similar (simple) problems, **95%** solved it. Of participants who had first been trained on a series all solvable by the same complex formula (**fill B, then from it fill C twice and A once**), only **36%** solved the easy final problem.

Classic background: Luchins called this effect **Einstellung** (German for "setting" or "attitude"), and older textbooks and keys use "Einstellung effect" and "mental set" interchangeably.

> **Trap.** **Functional fixedness** is about an **object** (you cannot see a new use for the tack box). **Mental set / Einstellung** is about a **procedure** (you keep applying the formula that worked before). Duncker's candle problem is the classic case of the first; Luchins's water jars of the second. Both are negative effects of past experience, and both were Gestalt themes, which is why papers like to pair them in a "match the following".

**Even experts are vulnerable.** You might expect experts working in their own domain to be immune to set. They are not. When chess experts tried to find the quickest way to win from a given position, most missed the shortest solution and instead used a longer one based on a familiar strategy (**Bilalić et al., 2008a**). **Bilalić et al. (2008b)** then tracked the eyes of experts who had found the familiar solution and were trying hard to find a better one. Their eyes kept returning to features of the board **related to the familiar solution**: their attention was still partly steered by the processes that had generated the first answer, even while they believed they were looking for something else.

**Reducing set with real materials.** **Vallée-Tourangeau et al. (2011)** gave Luchins's water-jar problems either **on paper**, as in the original, or with **real jars at a sink**. After a series of problems requiring the complex formula, participants faced a problem that could be solved only by a simpler method. Only **40%** of the paper group solved it, against **68%** of those using real jars. The authors attribute the advantage to the richness and dynamism of the **perceptual input** when people manipulate real objects.

## 12.4 Problem-Solving Strategies

### Newell and Simon: problem solving as search under limited capacity

The landmarks of the information-processing tradition are an article by **Newell et al. (1958)** and, above all, the 1972 book *Human Problem Solving* by **Allen Newell and Herb Simon**. Their central idea was that the **strategies** people use on complex problems are shaped by the **limits of our ability to process and store information**. They assumed that **short-term memory capacity is very limited** and that complex processing is usually **serial** (one process at a time). Both assumptions were built into their **General Problem Solver**, a computer program designed to solve well-defined problems. (Gobet and Lane, 2015, review what this approach achieved and where it fell short.)

Newell and Simon worked mainly with **well-defined, knowledge-lean** problems. The best known is the **Tower of Hanoi** (Figure 12.10). Up to five discs sit on the first of three pegs, largest at the bottom and smallest at the top. The goal is to rebuild the same pile, in the same order, on the last peg. Only one disc may be moved at a time, and a larger disc may never sit on a smaller one.

Classic background: the minimum number of moves for *n* discs is 2ⁿ − 1, so 7 moves for three discs and 31 for five. Papers occasionally ask this.

**The problem space.** For each problem Newell and Simon defined a **problem space**: the **initial state**, the **goal state**, all the **mental operators** (moves) that can turn one state into another, and all the **intermediate states** that those operators can produce. Solving a problem is then a **search** through this space for a path from start to goal.

**Heuristics versus algorithms.** A limited-capacity mind cannot search a large problem space exhaustively, so Newell and Simon argued that we rely on **heuristics**: cheap rules of thumb that often give reasonably good answers, without guarantee. **Algorithms** are generally complex procedures **guaranteed** to reach the solution (for example, systematically trying every move sequence).

> **Trap.** The word "heuristic" appears in both Chapter 12 and Chapter 13 with the same core meaning (a shortcut traded off against accuracy), but the examples differ. In problem solving the heuristics are **means-ends analysis, hill climbing and progress monitoring**; in judgement (Chapter 13) they are **representativeness and availability**. A question listing "availability" as a Newell and Simon problem-solving heuristic is wrong.

### Means-ends analysis

Newell and Simon regarded **means-ends analysis** as the most important heuristic. It has three steps:

1. Note the **difference** between the current state and the goal state.
2. Set a **subgoal** that would reduce that difference.
3. Choose a **mental operator** (a move or series of moves) that achieves the subgoal.

It is usually very helpful. Its weakness is that it pulls you towards the goal, which is exactly wrong when the path to the goal temporarily leads away from it.

> **Key study, Sweller & Levine (1982).** Participants solved a maze (Figure 12.11) of which most was hidden. One group could see where the **goal** was; the other could not. Means-ends analysis needs knowledge of the goal's location, so only the goal-information group could use it. The maze was built so that means-ends analysis would mislead: **every correct move turned away from the goal**. The goal-information group did very badly: only **10%** had solved it after **298 moves**. The no-goal-information group solved it in an average of **38 moves**.

People stuck with means-ends analysis even while it crippled them: knowing *more* (where the goal was) made them perform *worse*, because it switched on a heuristic the maze was built to punish.

### Hill climbing

**Hill climbing**, also identified by Newell and Simon, means making whichever move changes the present state into one that looks **closer to the goal**. It is **simpler** than means-ends analysis (it sets no subgoals) and is used mainly when the solver has **no clear grasp of the problem's structure**.

Because it looks only one step ahead, hill climbing often fails. The book's analogy is a climber who always steps uphill to reach the highest summit and is likely to end up stranded on a lower peak, cut off from the summit by valleys the rule forbids entering. (Classic background: the river-crossing missionaries-and-cannibals problem is the usual illustration, since solvers stall at the move that brings people *back* across.)

> **Trap.** Means-ends analysis and hill climbing both move you towards the goal, but they differ. **Means-ends analysis** compares current and goal states and sets **subgoals**; **hill climbing** sets no subgoals and simply takes whatever move looks locally best. Hill climbing is the simpler, more short-sighted one and is used when the problem structure is not understood. Neither is an algorithm.

### Progress monitoring

**MacGregor et al. (2001)** proposed a further heuristic, **progress monitoring**: solvers keep track of how fast they are moving towards the goal, and if progress looks too slow to succeed within the moves available, they switch strategy.

Their evidence used the **nine-dot problem**, with participants given one line of the solution as a start. Performance was **worse** in a condition where participants had an **illusion of progress**, which delayed their switch of strategy, than in a condition where it was obvious that little progress was being made. Paradoxically, a problem that looks hopeless sooner is solved better, because it triggers the strategy change sooner.

**Payne and Duggan (2011)** extended the idea using an **unsolvable** water-jar problem with either a **small** or a **large** number of possible states. With a small state space, people revisited the same states more often and so realised sooner that progress was impossible; as predicted, they **abandoned the problem in fewer moves** when the problem had **few states**.

### Planning

It is natural to assume that people facing a complex problem do some planning first. If so, prefrontal regions linked to planning and other complex processes should be involved.

**Evidence from brain-damaged patients.**

> **Key study, Goel & Grafman (1995).** Patients with **prefrontal damage** did worse than healthy controls on the **Tower of Hanoi**, although both groups used basically the **same strategy**. The patients were especially disadvantaged on a difficult move that required moving **away from the goal**. Prefrontal damage makes it harder to **plan ahead**.

> **Key study, Colvin et al. (2001).** On **water-jar** problems, both prefrontal patients and controls used a fairly crude **hill-climbing** strategy, but the patients did worse because they found it harder to make moves that **conflicted with** that strategy. Patients with **left dorsolateral prefrontal** damage did worse than those with **right** damage, which fits neuroimaging evidence (below) that the left DLPFC is involved in **initial planning**.

Prefrontal damage also causes problems in everyday life. PF (**Goel & Grafman, 2000**; see 12.2) had right prefrontal damage, high intelligence and very poor planning, and coped extremely badly with life. **Goel et al. (2013)** used a realistic **travel-planning task**, arranging a trip to Italy for an American couple, and found that patients with **right prefrontal damage** planned poorly, partly because they made **premature commitments** to decisions.

**Evidence from neuroimaging.**

> **Key study, Dagher et al. (1999).** Participants did the **Tower of London** task (Figure 12.12): coloured balls or discs must be moved one at a time from a start arrangement to match a goal arrangement, and participants are often told to plan the whole sequence mentally before moving anything. **More complex** versions of the task increased activity in several regions, notably the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**.

Classic background: the Tower of London was devised by Shallice (1982) specifically to measure planning in patients with frontal lesions, and it remains a standard neuropsychological test of executive function, which is why it matters for clinical psychology.

> **Trap.** Two towers. The **Tower of Hanoi** (pegs, discs of different sizes, no larger on smaller) is Newell and Simon's problem-space task and was used by Goel and Grafman (1995) and Crescentini et al. (2012). The **Tower of London** (coloured balls, match a goal arrangement) is the planning task used by Dagher et al. (1999), Nitschke et al. (2012), Ruh et al. (2012) and Koppenol-Gonzalez et al. (2010). Papers swap them.

### Sequential processing stages

Tasks like the two towers need the sequence of moves to be planned, but planning is only one stage in a chain that includes **plan production** and **plan execution**. On a complex problem only some of the moves are planned at first; the first plan is executed, then a further plan is produced and executed, and so on.

- **Crescentini et al. (2012)**, using simple Tower of Hanoi problems, found the **DLPFC** more active during **initial planning** than during execution, whereas **posterior temporal areas, inferior frontal regions and dorsolateral premotor cortex** were more active during **execution**.
- **Nitschke et al. (2012)** argued that Tower of London problems need **problem representation** first and **planning** second, and supported this with eye movements. On problems that made heavy demands on building a representation, participants **switched their gaze more often between the start and goal states**. On problems that made heavy demands on planning, the **final fixation on the start state was unusually long**.
- **Ruh et al. (2012)**, also with the Tower of London, found **left DLPFC** activation greatest during **problem representation** and **right DLPFC** activation greatest during **planning** itself.

> **Trap.** The sides are easy to scramble. **Ruh et al. (2012)**: **left** DLPFC peaks in **representation**, **right** DLPFC in **planning**. **Colvin et al. (2001)**: **left** DLPFC damage worse than right. PF and the Goel et al. (2013) travel planners: **right** prefrontal damage. The safe summary statement is that the prefrontal cortex, especially the **DLPFC**, is heavily involved in planning.

### How much planning do people actually do?

**Newell and Simon (1972)** expected only **modest** planning, because of limited short-term memory capacity. The evidence suggests that people *can* plan but often do not.

- **Delaney et al. (2004)**: on water-jar problems, participants free to choose their own strategy showed little sign of planning. Those **instructed** to work out the complete solution before moving showed clear planning and solved the problem **much faster** than controls.
- **Koppenol-Gonzalez et al. (2010)** found marked **individual differences** among students on the Tower of London. Some planned efficiently: they spent a long time pre-planning and performed very well. Others showed little or no effective planning: they spent little time pre-planning and made many errors.
- **Paynter et al. (2010)** recorded **event-related potentials (ERPs)** during complex problem solving, focusing on early trials when there was no behavioural sign of progress. ERPs nevertheless differed between **correct and incorrect moves**, pointing to **non-conscious learning mechanisms** that owe little to explicit plans.

> **Key study, Patsenko & Altmann (2010).** The strongest evidence that most solvers plan very little. While participants did the Tower of Hanoi, the researchers **added, removed or moved discs during participants' saccades** (eye movements), so that participants did not consciously notice the changes. If people were following a pre-formed plan, such changes should have wrecked their performance. In fact, disruption was **minimal**. The next move seemed to be driven by the **current visible state** of the problem, through immediate perceptual and attentional processes, rather than by a stored plan.

The book's summary: people engage in deliberate pre-planning **some** of the time, some problem-solving processes run **below conscious awareness**, and many people make **little use** of pre-planning.

### Cognitive miserliness

**Dual-process theories** (Evans, 2008) explain performance on judgement and reasoning tasks by two kinds of thinking. **Kahneman (2003)** distinguished **System 1**, which is fast and effortless, from **System 2**, which is slow and effortful (developed further in Chapter 13). Most dual-process theorists hold that many people are **cognitive misers**: economical with the time and effort they spend on thinking. Kahneman claimed that System 2 is supposed to **check** the answers System 1 produces, but often fails to, even when the System 1 answer is wrong.

**The Cognitive Reflection Test (Frederick, 2005)** measures how far people behave as cognitive misers. It has three short items, each with a tempting fast answer that is wrong:

| Item (paraphrased) | Tempting wrong answer | Correct answer |
|---|---|---|
| A bat and a ball cost $1.10 together, and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. What does the ball cost? | 10 cents | **5 cents** |
| If 5 machines make 5 widgets in 5 minutes, how long do 100 machines take to make 100 widgets? | 100 minutes | **5 minutes** |
| A patch of lily pads doubles in size every day and covers the whole lake in 48 days. When did it cover half the lake? | 24 days | **47 days** |

Check the bat and ball: if the ball cost 10 cents, the bat would cost $1.10 and the total would be $1.20, not $1.10. The intuitive answer survives only because nobody checks it, which is exactly the System 2 failure Kahneman describes.

When the test was given at **Harvard and Princeton**, **75%** of participants got **at least one** item wrong and **20%** got **all three** wrong.

**What does a low CRT score mean?** Low scorers do relatively badly on a wide range of judgement and reasoning tasks (**Toplak et al., 2011**). Part of the reason is cognitive miserliness, but part is that CRT scores **correlate positively with intelligence**. **Toplak et al. (2014)** used an expanded CRT and found it still predicted performance on several thinking tasks **after intelligence was statistically controlled**. So the CRT captures something beyond intelligence: a disposition to check one's intuitions.

> **Trap.** The book's text attaches the wrong answer "24 days" to "problem 2"; it belongs to the **lily-pad** item (problem 3), a misprint. The key is **5 cents, 5 minutes, 47 days**.

**Cognitive misers versus Newell and Simon.** The two ideas overlap, because in both cases people fall back on simple and sometimes inaccurate strategies. The difference is the **reason**. For Newell and Simon, we use heuristics because our **limited capacity forces us to**. Cognitive misers use heuristics because they are **unwilling** to put in the effort, not because they are unable to.

> **Trap.** "Can't versus won't." **Newell and Simon**: heuristics forced by **limited capacity**. **Cognitive miser**: heuristics chosen out of **reluctance**, so misers fail problems they have the **ability** to solve.

## 12.5 Analogical Problem Solving

### What an analogy is and why it matters

**Analogical problem solving** means solving a problem by drawing on an **analogy**: a comparison between two objects or systems (or between a current and a previous problem) that brings out the ways in which they are similar. (The book quotes a definition from the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2013, to this effect.) Analogy is central to everyday life, because most of the time we handle a new situation by relating it to one we have already met.

Analogy has also mattered in science: **Ernest Rutherford** modelled the atom on the **solar system**, with electrons orbiting the nucleus like planets. **Chan et al. (2012)** recorded discussions among **Mars Rover Mission** scientists and found that **uncertainty** about an issue predicted analogy use, and that the analogies **reduced uncertainty** and allowed fast, approximate problem solving.

### Three kinds of similarity (Chen, 2002)

To use an old problem on a new one, you must first see that they are similar. **Chen (2002)** distinguished three types of similarity:

| Type | What is shared | Example (radiation and fortress problems, below) |
|---|---|---|
| **Superficial** | Details irrelevant to the solution, such as the specific objects | Both involve rays and a patient (if the source story is also medical) |
| **Structural** | Causal relations among the main components | A strong central force must be split into weak converging forces |
| **Procedural** | The procedures that turn the solution principle into concrete actions | Exactly how to divide and aim the forces |

Chen gave participants a problem plus an analogy sharing either **structural and procedural** similarity or **only structural** similarity. Performance was significantly better with **both**, because those participants more often found the **right concrete actions**: seeing the principle is not enough if you cannot turn it into steps.

The section then asks when people use a relevant analogy, what processes solve an explicit analogy, and why individuals differ.

### Findings: detecting the analogy

> **Key study, Gick & Holyoak (1980).** Participants received the **radiation (tumour) problem**. A patient has a malignant stomach tumour that can be destroyed only by a particular kind of ray. A ray strong enough to destroy the tumour will also destroy the healthy tissue it passes through; a ray weak enough to spare healthy tissue is too weak to destroy the tumour. On its own, the problem was solved by only **10%** of participants.
>
> The analogy was a story participants had **memorised earlier**: a general wants to capture a fortress, but the roads leading to it are mined, so that sending the whole army along any one road would be disastrous; the mines, however, let small groups pass safely. The general splits his army into small groups that **converge on the fortress at the same moment along different roads**. The parallel solution to the radiation problem is to send several **weak rays from different directions** that converge on the tumour.
>
> When told the story was relevant, **80%** solved the radiation problem. When **not** told, only **40%** did.

The striking figure is 40%: half the people with the solution in memory never **retrieved** it, because the fortress story shares the problem's structure but none of its surface content. When the source story was made **superficially similar** (a surgeon using rays on a cancer), **88%** spontaneously recalled it on meeting the radiation problem (**Keane, 1987**).

> **Trap.** The tumour/radiation problem was originally devised by **Duncker (1945)**, who also devised the candle problem. Older keys may credit Duncker for the radiation problem and the 7th edition itself calls it "Duncker's radiation problem" when describing Kurtz and Loewenstein; the **fortress analogy study** is **Gick and Holyoak (1980)**. The numbers form a ladder: **10%** alone, **40%** with the story but no hint, **80%** with the hint, **88%** spontaneous recall with a superficially similar story (**Keane, 1987**, not Gick and Holyoak).

### Reception versus production paradigms

Gick and Holyoak used the **reception paradigm**: participants are **given** a possible analogy in detail before the problem. In everyday life, people usually **generate** their own analogies, which is the **production paradigm**. **Blanchette and Dunbar (2000)** confirmed that in the reception paradigm people often choose analogies on the basis of **superficial** similarity, but found that in the production paradigm people mostly produced analogies that shared **structural** features with the current problem.

**Dunbar and Blanchette (2001)** took this into real laboratories, analysing what leading **molecular biologists and immunologists** said in lab meetings. When they used analogies to **fix experimental problems**, the source was often **superficially similar** to the current problem. When they used analogies to **generate hypotheses**, the analogies had **fewer superficial and many more structural** similarities. The take-home point is that the kind of analogy people use depends on their **current goal**.

> **Trap.** "People only use surface similarity" is a **reception**-paradigm result. In the **production** paradigm, and when scientists **generate hypotheses**, analogies are **structural**. Surface similarity mainly drives **retrieval** (Keane, 1987).

### Bridging dissimilar problems: concrete mental models

How do people use an analogy when the old and new problems look very different? **Day and Goldstone (2011)** argued that this works when people have **interacted extensively** with the first problem and built a **concrete mental model** of it. Participants who interacted with a simulation of a **ball oscillating between two elastic bands** were reasonably successful on a later task that involved **regulating a city's population**, a problem with completely different surface content but a similar underlying dynamic.

### Improving analogy use: compare the problems

Because analogy depends on **retrieving** a relevant case from memory, Loewenstein (2010) argued that retrieval should improve if the **underlying structure of the current problem is made explicit**.

> **Key study, Kurtz & Loewenstein (2007).** All participants had earlier read the fortress story. Some were then asked to **compare the underlying structure of two similar problems**, one of them Duncker's radiation problem; others considered the two problems **separately**. The comparison group solved the tumour problem more often (**54% versus 38%**), and the advantage came from **better retrieval of the fortress story**. Good encoding of the *target* problem is what unlocks the *source* analogy.

**Gentner et al. (2009)** found the same with **management consultants**. They studied two examples of a type of contract structure, either one at a time or with instructions to **compare** them, and then retrieved a similar example from their own experience. The comparison group (1) found it **easier to recall** a relevant example, and (2) recalled examples of **higher quality**, in the sense that they better embodied the key principle shared by the two studied examples.

The practical conclusion: a solver who forms a **full description of the deep structure** of the current problem is more likely to retrieve an analogy, and to retrieve a better one.

### Findings: processes in four-term analogies

Much research on the processes of analogy uses **four-term analogy problems** of the form **A:B::C:D** ("A is to B as C is to D"). In one version from Schmidt et al. (2012), participants judge whether FLAME:CANDLE::STEEPLE:CHURCH expresses the same relation in both pairs. These problems are popular because they are **tightly controlled**: all the necessary information is presented and there is **one correct answer**, which makes the underlying processes easier to isolate.

**Unconscious detection.** **Reber et al. (2014)** asked participants to rate how closely related the words in a pair were (for example, *desk-bus*). Pairs were judged **more closely related** if participants had just been shown, **subliminally**, a pair expressing the **same relation** (for example, *table-car*). Analogies, it seems, can be detected **without awareness**.

**Three sequential stages.** Four-term analogies involve:

1. **Encoding** the first pair in terms of the relationship between its words;
2. **Mapping**: linking the first word of each pair and inferring what the fourth word should be;
3. **Response**: deciding whether the fourth word offered is correct.

**Knowlton et al. (2012)** reviewed computational models that treat analogical reasoning as **sequential** and argued it must be **serial** because of its processing demands. Their model has the stages **analogue retrieval, mapping, analogical inference and schema induction**, where the induced **schemas** are abstract structures formed by comparing the analogy with the current problem. They noted that the long-standing gap between such computational models and cognitive neuroscience is now narrowing.

**Brain activity across the stages.**

- **Krawczyk et al. (2010)** used **fMRI** at each of the three stages. **Encoding** engaged several frontal regions: the **inferior frontal gyrus**, the **DLPFC (BA9/46)** and the **rostrolateral prefrontal cortex (BA10)**. **Mapping** engaged similar regions, centred on the inferior frontal gyrus and rostrolateral prefrontal cortex. The **response** stage showed **less** prefrontal activation, because most of the work had already been done.
- **Maguire et al. (2012)** found the same pattern with **ERPs**: heavy processing at encoding, similar but briefer processing at mapping, and less at response. Participants often distinguished correct from incorrect fourth terms within about **400 ms**, again because most processing had happened earlier.

### The brain network for analogical reasoning (Krawczyk, 2012)

Krawczyk (2012) summarised the patient and neuroimaging evidence (Figure 12.13) as a network in which each region contributes a different function:

| Region | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Occipital and parietal** cortex | Visual and spatial processing of the problem |
| **Rostral / rostrolateral prefrontal cortex** (about **BA10**) | **Integrating** information within the analogy; one BA10 region is active throughout analogical reasoning, a second only during mapping and response (Volle et al., 2010) |
| **DLPFC** and **inferior frontal gyrus** | **Inhibition** of distraction and interference |
| **Temporal lobes** | Stored **meanings** of concepts (semantic memory) |
### Inhibiting distractors: frontal versus temporal patients

**Krawczyk et al. (2008)** argued that analogy often requires **executive inhibition** of tempting but wrong answers. Their picture analogy was *sandwich : lunchbox :: hammer : ?*, with four options:

- **toolbox**: correct (a container for the item);
- **nail**: a **semantic** distractor (associated with hammers);
- **gavel** (an auctioneer's hammer): a **perceptual** distractor (looks like a hammer);
- **ribbon**: an **irrelevant** distractor.

They tested patients with **prefrontal** damage, patients with **temporal** damage (frontotemporal lobar degeneration of frontal and temporal variants; Figure 12.14) and healthy controls. As predicted, **frontal** patients more often chose the **semantic or perceptual distractors** than temporal patients did, and **only the frontal patients improved** when no relevant distractors were present. An intact prefrontal cortex is needed to **inhibit answers that are related to the correct answer**.

**Cho et al. (2010)** narrowed the region: activity in the **inferior frontal gyrus** rose as the amount of **interfering information** rose, that is, as the need for inhibitory control increased.

### Associative versus categorical analogies (Schmidt et al., 2012)

**Schmidt et al. (2012)** argued that the processes and regions depend on the **kind** of analogy. In **associative** analogies (for example, CAR:TRAILER::DONKEY:CART) the relationship can be experienced directly. In **categorical** analogies (for example, RODENT:MOUSE::APPLIANCE:TOASTER) the relationship is more **abstract** (category to member).

- Patients with **left-hemisphere** damage were **equally poor** on both kinds, as expected given left-hemisphere dominance for language and the verbal format.
- Patients with **right-hemisphere** damage were **more impaired on categorical** than associative analogies, reflecting the more complex, abstract nature of categorical relations.

> **Trap.** Direction check: it is the **right-hemisphere** patients who show the **selective** deficit (categorical worse than associative). Left-hemisphere patients are poor on **both**.

### Working memory and analogy

> **Key study, Morrison et al. (2001).** Participants judged **verbal** analogies true or false (for example, BLACK:WHITE::NOISY:QUIET) and also **pictorial** analogies built from cartoon characters, either alone or while doing a secondary task that loaded one component of **working memory**. Three findings:
> 1. A **central executive** load impaired **both** verbal and pictorial analogies: analogy needs the central executive's limited capacity.
> 2. A **phonological loop** load impaired **verbal** analogies only, because both tasks involved verbal processing.
> 3. A **visuo-spatial sketchpad** load impaired **pictorial** analogies only.

**Cho et al. (2007)** varied both **problem complexity** and the need for **interference resolution**. Performance was especially poor when **high complexity** and **interference resolution** were combined, because the combination overloaded the central executive.

### Individual differences: intelligence, Raven's matrices and working memory capacity

Analogical reasoning correlates about **+0.7** with intelligence (**Spearman, 1927**), suggesting it depends heavily on higher-level processes; the working-memory evidence above points the same way (reviewed by Morrison, 2005).

The link becomes tighter with **Raven's Progressive Matrices** (Raven et al., 1998; a sample item is in Figure 12.15). Raven's is used as a measure of **fluid intelligence** (non-verbal intelligence applied to novel problems), but it is also, in effect, a test of **geometrical analogies**, since each answer is chosen by analogical reasoning across rows and columns. That makes it important to know which individual differences drive Raven's performance.

- **Ackerman et al. (2005)** pooled studies in a meta-analysis: people with higher **working memory capacity** (holding information while simultaneously working on it) tended to score higher on Raven's **Advanced** Progressive Matrices, with an overall r of **+0.50**.
- **Wiley et al. (2011)** showed the relationship is driven mainly by Raven's items that require **new combinations of rules**, not by items that reuse rules from earlier items.

Why should high working memory capacity help? Two candidate explanations: better **attentional (executive) control**, or greater **attentional capacity**.

- **Jarosz and Wiley (2012)** supported **attentional control** with eye-tracking: high-capacity individuals spent **less time fixating the most common incorrect answer**, so they were less pulled in by the most attractive distractor.
- **Chuderski and Nęcka (2012)** measured both. **Attentional capacity** correlated **highly** with Raven's performance; **attentional control** correlated too, but **less**. Each made a **separate** contribution, so both help solve geometrical analogies.

> **Trap.** Analogy with intelligence: **+0.7 (Spearman, 1927)**. Raven's APM with working memory capacity: **+0.50 (Ackerman et al., 2005)**. In Chuderski and Nęcka (2012) **capacity** correlated more strongly than **control**, although Jarosz and Wiley's eye-tracking favoured control.

## 12.6 Expertise

### From short laboratory tasks to years of learning

Most of the studies so far gave people a short time to learn, used narrow tasks and required no specialist knowledge. Real life is different. People spend years building knowledge and skill in a field such as psychology, law, medicine or journalism, and the end point of that long learning is **expertise**: a very high level of thinking and performance in a particular domain, achieved through **many years of systematic practice**.

Expertise resembles problem solving, since experts solve the problems of their domain with great efficiency. The research traditions differ in their materials: classic problem-solving research used **knowledge-lean** problems that need no special training, while expertise research uses **knowledge-rich** problems that require a great deal of knowledge beyond what the problem itself states.

### Why study chess?

Gobet et al. (2004) give three reasons why chess has been the favourite domain:

1. The **Elo rating system** (named after the chess master **Arpad Elo**) gives a **precise** measure of each player's level.
2. Expert players develop specific cognitive skills, such as **pattern recognition** and **selective search**, that are useful in other kinds of expertise too.
3. Findings on chess experts' **remarkable memory for chess positions** generalise well to most other forms of expertise.

The section then takes, in order, **chess** expertise, **medical** expertise (especially diagnosis), the possible role of **brain plasticity**, and **Ericsson's** view that **deliberate practice** is the main requirement for expertise.

## 12.7 Chess-Playing Expertise

### Measuring skill and what makes the difference

Chess skill can be measured unusually precisely from results against other players, and because tournament records are kept across whole careers, **longitudinal data** are available. Why are some players so much better than others? The obvious answer is practice: it takes about **10,000 hours** to become a grandmaster. More specifically, expert players have far more **detailed information about chess positions stored in long-term memory** than non-experts.

> **Key study, De Groot (1965).** Chess players saw board positions from real games for a brief time; the board was then removed and they reconstructed the positions. **Masters** recalled them far more accurately than weaker players (**91% versus 43%**). The difference lay in **stored knowledge of chess positions**, not general memory ability, because the groups did **not differ** in recalling **random** board positions.

Classic background: **Chase and Simon (1973)** extended De Groot's method and explained the masters' advantage by **chunking**: experts perceive a position as a small number of meaningful clusters of pieces (chunks) stored in long-term memory, rather than as many separate pieces. Gobet's template theory, below, is the modern descendant of chunking theory. Older keys may name chunking or Chase and Simon where the 7th edition names templates and Gobet.

> **Trap.** De Groot (1965) found **no expert advantage for random positions**; Gobet and Waters (2003) later found a **small** advantage for **truly random** ones (see below). If asked for De Groot's finding, the answer is "masters better on real positions, no difference on random ones".

**Humans versus computers.** Human experts' strategies are completely unlike those of chess computers. **Deep Blue**, which beat the then world champion **Garry Kasparov in May 1997**, evaluated up to **200 million positions per second** and looked up to about **six moves ahead**. No human does anything like this; human expertise rests on knowledge that tells the player which few moves deserve consideration.

### Template theory (Gobet)

Experts plainly store more chess information than novices; the hard question is what form that information takes. **Fernand Gobet**'s **template theory** (for example, Gobet & Waters, 2003) is the most influential answer: much of the knowledge is stored as **templates**.

A **template** is an **abstract, schematic structure** that is more general than any single board position. Each has:

- a **core** of fixed information, and
- **slots** holding variable information about pieces and their locations.

A template typically holds information about **ten or so pieces**, though it can be larger. The slots are what make templates **flexible**: one template can match many specific positions by filling its slots differently.

**Four predictions.**

1. Chess positions are stored in about **three templates**, some of them quite **large**.
2. Outstanding players owe their excellence mostly to **template-based knowledge**, which is accessed rapidly and narrows the moves they need to consider, rather than to **slow, strategy-based search**. So top players should still play very well under **severe time pressure**.
3. Experts store the **precise board locations** of pieces after studying a position, and pieces **close together** on the board are most likely to belong to the same template (Gobet & Simon, 2000).
4. Experts should recall even apparently **random** positions better than non-experts, because some patterns arise by chance even in random positions, and those patterns match templates.

### Findings, prediction by prediction

**Prediction 1 (number and size of templates).** **Gobet and Clarkson (2004)**: experts recalled positions much better than novices, but the **number of templates did not vary with playing strength**, averaging about **two**. What varied was **size**: the maximum template size was **13 to 15** pieces for **masters** against only about **6** for **novices**.

**Prediction 2 (fast template access versus slow search).** The evidence here is **mixed**.

*For the prediction:*

- **Charness et al. (2001)** recorded the **first five fixations** (about **one second** in total) of expert and intermediate players asked to find the best move. Even this early, experts were more likely to fixate **tactically relevant pieces** (**80% versus 64%** of fixations).
- **Burns (2004)** studied **blitz chess**, in which the whole game must be finished in **five minutes**, less than **5%** of the normal time. With so little time there is no room for slow search, so blitz performance should depend mainly on templates. Blitz performance correlated **highly** with performance in normal chess (between **+0.78 and +0.90**).

*Against the prediction:*

- **van Harreveld et al. (2007)** found that skill differences between players predicted game outcome **less** well as the **time available decreased**. If strong players' advantage shrinks under time pressure, slow processes must matter **more for strong players** than for weak ones.
- **Moxley et al. (2012)** gave experts and tournament players **five minutes** to choose the best move on several problems while **thinking aloud**, so the first move mentioned could be compared with the move finally chosen. The final move was generally **much stronger** than the first, for both groups and at every level of difficulty (Figure 12.16). **Slow, strategy-based processing** improves the choice substantially.

**Prediction 3 (precise locations).** Most studies show that players recall the exact squares of pieces that are near one another within a template. But **McGregor and Howes (2002)** pointed out that these studies asked players to **memorise** positions, whereas real play is about **evaluating** them. They asked expert and unskilled players to **evaluate** positions (for example, which side was winning) and then gave a surprise **recognition** test. Test positions were **identical**, **shifted** (every piece moved one square sideways, so all locations changed but the relations were preserved), or **distorted** (only **one** piece moved one square, but in a way that changed the **attack and defence relations**). Experts remembered **attack and defence relations** much better than precise **locations**. **Linhares et al. (2012)** found the same in reconstruction errors: **masters** differed from **grandmasters** more on **abstract, strategic** features (significant attacks and defences) than on **superficial** features such as exact locations.

**Prediction 4 (random positions).** A meta-analysis by **Gobet and Simon (1996)** found a **small** skill effect on memory for random boards. **Gobet and Waters (2003)** noticed, however, that those "random" boards were not completely random, because the **pieces** were not chosen at random (both kings were always there, for example). Using **truly random positions and pieces**, they found the predicted effect: pieces recalled ranged from **14.8** for the most expert players to **12** for the least expert.

### Evaluation of template theory

**Strengths** (as the book lists them):

1. Much of what experts store from a position is in the form of a **few large templates** (citing McGregor & Howes, 2002).
2. Outstanding players have much more **knowledge of chess positions**, which gives them a substantial advantage in play.
3. Experts' success at **blitz** chess is due mainly to superior **template-based knowledge** (Burns, 2004).
4. Experts recall **random** positions better than non-experts (Gobet & Waters, 2003).

**Limitations:**

1. **Slow search** matters more to experts than the theory allows. Experts **look further ahead** than non-experts (**Charness, 1981**), and skill predicts outcome **less** well when time per move is cut (**van Harreveld et al., 2007**).
2. The best players are better at strategies that take them **beyond stored knowledge**. In **Bilalić et al. (2008a)**, a problem could be solved in **five moves** with a familiar strategy or in **three** with a less familiar one. **No candidate masters** found the shorter solution; **50% of international masters** did. The very top players can escape the familiar, template-driven answer.
3. **What exactly is stored** is still disputed. Template theory says precise **locations**; the evidence suggests **attack and defence relations** may matter more (McGregor & Howes, 2002; Linhares et al., 2012).

> **Trap.** "Experts use **more** templates" is wrong; Gobet and Clarkson (2004) found about **two** regardless of skill, with **size** growing. And keep the roles straight: **Burns (2004)** blitz and **Charness et al. (2001)** early fixations **support** template theory; **van Harreveld et al. (2007)**, **Moxley et al. (2012)** and **Charness (1981)** (experts look further ahead) count **against** it. Same first author, opposite roles.

## 12.8 Medical Expertise

### Why diagnosis, and why radiology

Medical expertise matters because diagnostic decisions can be a matter of life or death. The section concentrates on **radiology**, where expertise shows in diagnoses made from **X-ray** images, with some work on **dermatology** (skin disease). Even expert radiologists make errors: in some areas the **false negative rate** (failing to detect disease that is present) can reach **30%** (**Krupinski, 2011**).

### Explicit versus implicit reasoning

Theories differ about how expert and novice diagnosticians differ, but many rest on the distinction between two kinds of reasoning (Engel, 2008):

| | **Explicit reasoning** | **Implicit reasoning** |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Slow | Fast |
| Control | Deliberate | Automatic |
| Awareness | Conscious | Not conscious |
| Who relies on it, according to the theory | **Novices** | **Experts** |

The central assumption is that **experts reason mainly implicitly** and **novices mainly explicitly**. This is the same broad contrast that the dual-process theories of judgement (Chapter 13) and reasoning (Chapter 14) draw between deliberate, explicit processing and rapid, implicit processing, and it echoes System 1 and System 2 from 12.4.

Two qualifications come before the evidence. First, the evidence mainly concerns the **visual** specialties (**pathology, radiology, dermatology**) more than technical specialties such as **surgery** or **anaesthesiology** (Engel, 2008). Second, even when experts begin with fast, automatic processing, they usually **cross-check** their diagnoses with slow, deliberate processing (**McLaughlin et al., 2008**).

### Findings: eye-tracking

Eye-tracking reveals where a doctor's attention goes while examining an image, which makes it a natural tool here.

> **Key study, Krupinski et al. (2013).** A **longitudinal** study of **pathologists** viewing **breast biopsy** slides at the start of each of their first four years of residency. As residency progressed (one participant's data are shown in Figure 12.17), there was a **marked fall in the number of fixations per slide** and **less inspection of non-diagnostic regions**. Expertise made viewing more economical and more focused on what matters.

> **Key study, Kundel et al. (2007).** Doctors experienced in **mammography** examined difficult mammograms with or without breast cancer. The **mean search time** for mammograms showing cancer was **27 seconds**, but the **median time to first fixate the cancer** was only **1.13 seconds**, and for the experts it was typically **under one second**. The correlation between time to first fixation on the cancer and diagnostic performance was about **−0.9**: the sooner the eyes landed on the cancer, the better the performance. The most expert doctors fixated the cancer almost immediately, which suggests **holistic or global** processing; the least expert relied on a slower strategy.

**Kundel and Nodine (1975)** had shown much earlier that experts detect abnormalities very fast. **Chest radiographs** were shown for only **200 milliseconds**, too short for any visual search, and experienced radiologists still interpreted them correctly **70%** of the time.

> **Trap.** The Kundel et al. (2007) correlation is **negative** (about **−0.9**) because the variable is **time** to first fixation: the shorter the time, the better the performance. **27 seconds** is mean total search time; **1.13 seconds** is median time to fixate the cancer.

**The type of image matters as well as expertise.** **Kok et al. (2012)** compared images of **focal** (localised) diseases, such as tumours, with images of **diffuse** diseases. **Both** experts and non-experts made similarly **long** fixations on focal diseases and **shorter, more scattered** fixations on diffuse diseases. Eye-movement patterns therefore depended on the image more than on expertise, and the main reason experts were more **accurate** was their superior **knowledge**.

**Does expertise change eye movements in the same way across domains?** **Gegenfurtner et al. (2011)** ran a **meta-analysis** of expert visual interpretation in domains including **medicine, sport and transportation**, and found five expert-novice differences common to all of them. Compared with non-experts, experts' eyes **reached the relevant information sooner** and **landed on it more often**, **wasted fewer fixations** on irrelevant regions, made **briefer individual fixations**, and **jumped further** between fixations (**longer saccades**).

### Two theoretical accounts of the eye-movement findings

Both accounts fit the Gegenfurtner pattern; they explain it differently.

| Account | Claim | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Information-reduction hypothesis** (Haider & Frensch, 1999) | With expertise, attention is allocated **more efficiently and selectively**: experts learn to ignore what is irrelevant | Fewer fixations on irrelevant areas |
| **Holistic model** (Kundel et al., 2007) | Experts extract information from a **wider area with each fixation** | Experts fixate relevant areas **faster**, and their successive fixations are **further apart** (longer saccades), as predicted |

> **Trap.** **Information reduction** (Haider & Frensch, 1999) is about **selective** attention; the **holistic model** (Kundel et al., 2007) is about a **wider field per fixation**. "Longer saccades and faster first fixation on the target" points to the holistic model.

### Is expert diagnosis like perceiving the gist of a scene?

The speed and accuracy of expert diagnosis resemble our everyday ability to grasp the **gist** of a visual scene. **Prass et al. (2013)** showed photographs for only **30 milliseconds** and observers could still tell whether an **animal or a vehicle** had been shown on **more than 90%** of trials. It is tempting to call such performance "automatic", but the evidence (for example, Cohen et al., 2011; Chapter 16) indicates that some **attention** is still needed for conscious perception of a scene's gist.

> **Key study, Melo et al. (2012).** Tested directly whether expert diagnosis uses the same processes as everyday visual recognition. Diagnosing lesions in **chest X-rays** and **naming animals** took comparably short times (**1.33 versus 1.23 seconds**), and, most importantly, both tasks activated **very similar brain regions** (Figure 12.18). Diagnosis produced **greater** activation in the **frontal sulcus** and **posterior cingulate cortex**, suggesting it is more cognitively demanding than naming animals. **Naming letters** activated similar regions to both, but less strongly.

Melo et al. concluded that medical experts use **rapid pattern recognition**: each image is matched against stored patterns from past cases, a predominantly **visual** strategy. Non-experts may instead use a more **analytic** approach, working through the clinical features before reaching a diagnosis.

### Can extra information hurt experts?

> **Key study, Kulatunga-Moruzi et al. (2004).** Three groups differing in expertise diagnosed **skin diseases** from case **photographs**. Some saw only the photographs; others received a **comprehensive verbal description** before each photograph. You would expect everyone to do better with the extra description. That was true for the **least expert** group only. The **more expert** groups, including **dermatologists**, did **better without** the verbal descriptions. Experts were using a fast visual strategy, and the verbal descriptions **interfered** with it.

> **Trap.** In Kulatunga-Moruzi et al. (**2004**), verbal descriptions **helped novices** and **hurt experts**. Kulatunga-Moruzi et al. (**2011**), below, is a separate study about the **order** of fast and analytic processing.

### But slow analysis still matters: Mamede et al. (2010)

The evidence so far suggests that experts diagnose accurately without slow, analytic processing. Eysenck and Keane call that an **oversimplification**. **Mamede et al. (2010)** compared experts and non-experts who gave diagnoses either **immediately** or after a period of **analytic thinking**. Analytic thinking **improved experts' diagnoses on complex cases** but **not on simple ones**. **Non-experts gained nothing** from analytic thinking.

The pattern is the reverse of what a simple "experts are intuitive, novices are analytic" story would predict. Analysis helps those who have the **knowledge to analyse with**, and only when the case is hard enough to need it.

### Evaluation of research on medical expertise

**Strengths.**

- Expert and non-expert diagnostic strategies often **differ considerably**.
- **Eye-tracking** supports the idea that experts use **fast, almost automatic** processes more effectively than non-experts.
- There is support for an important distinction between such fast processes and **deliberate, analytic** processes.
- There is interesting evidence that expert diagnosis resembles the **visual recognition of everyday objects** (Melo et al., 2012).

**Limitations.**

1. Most studies simply **compare experts with non-experts**. That is useful, but it reveals little about the **learning processes** by which expertise develops.
2. More research is needed on how experts and non-experts **combine fast and analytic processing**. **Kulatunga-Moruzi et al. (2011)** found that non-experts benefited from the combination when **fast processing came first and analytic processing second**, but **not** in the reverse order. This matters because real doctors usually combine both.
3. The claim that experts' fast processes are "**automatic**" is shaky. Automaticity is a complex and imprecise concept (Chapter 5), and there is **no compelling evidence yet** that medical experts use automatic processes.

### Chess expertise versus medical expertise

**Similarities.**

1. Both require **several years of intensive training**.
2. Training builds **huge stores of relevant knowledge** that can be **accessed rapidly**.
3. Experts in both are better than non-experts at using **rapid** (possibly automatic) processes.
4. Experts in both use **analytic or strategy-based** processes **flexibly** when needed.

**Differences.**

| | Chess expertise | Medical expertise |
|---|---|---|
| Form of stored knowledge | Fairly **abstract** templates | Probably **less abstract**, more **visual** |
| What the task demands | Relate the position to stored knowledge, **then plan** one's own future moves and the opponent's | More **narrowly focused**: relate the case to stored knowledge to reach a **diagnosis** |

## 12.9 Brain Plasticity

### The question

Nobody doubts that becoming an expert means acquiring vast knowledge and specialised processes. The controversial question is whether developing expertise also **changes the brain**. The key concept is **plasticity**: experience- or training-related changes in the brain's structure and function that affect behaviour (Herholz & Zatorre, 2012). It is likely that structural changes produced by plasticity in turn **make further learning easier**, so plasticity may feed the growth of expertise.

### London taxi drivers and "The Knowledge"

To get a licence to drive a London black cab, a driver must master **"The Knowledge"**: detailed knowledge of the **25,000 streets** within **six miles of Charing Cross**, plus the locations of thousands of hospitals, tube stations and other landmarks. Acquiring it takes about **three years**.

High intelligence is not the key; the **mean IQ** of London cab drivers is around the **population average**. The structure that matters is the **hippocampus**, in the medial temporal lobe, which is crucial for long-term memory (Chapter 6).

> **Key study, Maguire et al. (2006): patient TT.** TT, an older taxi driver, had recently suffered **extensive hippocampal damage**. He still knew London's **landmarks** and their **spatial relationships** well, but his **navigation** had deteriorated badly: he relied too much on **main roads** and got **lost on minor roads**. Stored large-scale knowledge survived; flexible route-finding through the detailed street network did not.

**Does learning The Knowledge change the hippocampus?** Probably yes.

- Compared with trainees and other comparison groups, **experienced** drivers show extra **posterior hippocampal grey matter**, and among older drivers those still working **full time** have more of it than **retired** drivers of the same age (**Woollett et al., 2009**).
- There is a cost. Taxi drivers did poorly at learning and remembering new **word-word** or **object-place associations** (**Woollett & Maguire, 2009**), and they had **less** grey matter than other people in the **anterior hippocampus**, a region important for processing **novel stimuli** and **encoding** new information. The heavy use of the hippocampus in learning The Knowledge may impair the ability to take in other new information.

> **Trap.** Posterior gain, anterior loss: **more** posterior and **less** anterior hippocampal grey matter, with **worse** learning of new associations. Their mean IQ is **average**.

### Is the relationship causal?

Everything above is **correlational**: it cannot show that acquiring The Knowledge **causes** the hippocampal differences (perhaps people with a larger posterior hippocampus are drawn to, or better at, the job).

- **Woollett and Maguire (2011)** gave **less indirect** evidence. They followed adults who had spent several years learning The Knowledge. Those who **qualified** as London taxi drivers showed a **selective increase in posterior hippocampal grey matter**; those who **failed** did not.
- **Scholz et al. (2009)** gave more convincing **longitudinal** evidence. After **six weeks** of learning to **juggle**, trainees' **visual motion area** had gained about **5%** in both **grey and white matter**, and most of that gain remained at follow-up.

### Musical training

Many studies have looked at the effects of musical training on brain structure and function (Herholz & Zatorre, 2012; Zatorre, 2013). Changes such as greater **volume or thickness of auditory cortex** occur at **all ages** but are generally **larger in children and young adults** than in older adults.

> **Key study, Hyde et al. (2009).** After **15 months** of lessons on a musical **instrument**, **6-year-olds** differed significantly from untrained children in relative **voxel size** (a voxel being one tiny cube of tissue in the brain image) within the **primary motor area** (Figure 12.19) and the **primary auditory area** (Figure 12.20). The children with the **greatest brain changes** showed the **greatest improvement** in musical skills (motor-test performance and a melody-rhythm test).

> **Key study, Foster & Zatorre (2010).** Cortical volume was greatest in adults with the **most musical practice** (up to **58,000 hours**). But cortical structure **still predicted musical performance when the effects of training were statistically removed**. This suggests that **pre-existing anatomical features** influence how quickly people learn music, which is a warning against reading every expert-novice brain difference as an effect of practice.

### Evaluation of plasticity research

**Strengths.**

- Many studies show **predictable** differences in brain structure between people with different amounts of training in a domain (Zatorre, 2013).
- **Controlled training studies**, with brain structure measured **before, during and after** training, give reasonable support to the hypothesis that developing expertise **causes** structural change.
- Because plasticity is **greatest in the young**, it may help explain differences in how fast expertise develops at different ages.

**Limitations.**

1. It is hard to show **definitively** that practice caused brain changes that are **relevant to** the performance improvement.
2. The evidence for plasticity is strongest for **musical** training; it is **less clear** for other forms of expertise.
3. There is **no coherent theory** of how plasticity affects expertise. Cognitive neuroscience stresses that cognition depends on **complex brain networks** (for example, Bullmore & Sporns, 2012), but how specific structural changes relate to those networks is unclear.

## 12.10 Deliberate Practice

### Ericsson's theory

Prolonged practice over several years is essential for every kind of expertise, but that observation is not a theory. What is needed is an account of what **effective** practice consists of. **Ericsson** (for example, Ericsson & Towne, 2010) supplied one: expertise is developed through **deliberate practice**, which has **four** features:

1. The task is at an **appropriate level of difficulty**, neither too easy nor too hard.
2. The learner receives **informative feedback** on performance.
3. The learner has **adequate opportunities to repeat** the task.
4. The learner has the **opportunity to correct errors**.

Classic background: the founding study is Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) on violinists at a music academy, in which the best performers had accumulated the most hours of solitary deliberate practice (around 10,000 by about age 20). It is the source of the popular "10,000-hour rule". The 7th edition cites Ericsson's later work rather than this study.

### Long-term working memory

What does years of deliberate practice actually change? According to **Ericsson and Kintsch (1995)**, experts learn to get round the limited capacity of **working memory**. Their notion is **long-term working memory**: with expertise, domain knowledge structures let the expert transfer information **rapidly into long-term memory** and keep it accessible through **retrieval cues held in working memory**. The effect, as Guida et al. (2013) put it, is that working memory capacity *appears* to expand, although what has really happened is that long-term memory is being used during a working-memory task.

**An example.** Suppose an expert and a novice both try to memorise a chess position. The novice must depend almost entirely on working memory, which holds little for long. The expert uses extensive chess knowledge to encode much of the position **directly into long-term memory**, so their recall is far better. The expert is using long-term working memory; the novice is not.

### Two predictions, one of them controversial

1. Expertise should depend more on the amount of **deliberate** practice than on the sheer number of hours spent practising.
2. Much more controversially, **deliberate practice is all that is needed**: it is both **necessary and sufficient** for outstanding performance, so **innate talent** contributes little or nothing.

> **Trap.** Ericsson's claim, as the 7th edition presents it, is that deliberate practice is **necessary and sufficient** and talent is irrelevant. The 7th edition's **conclusion** is that deliberate practice is **necessary but not sufficient**. A question asking "according to Ericsson" wants "necessary and sufficient"; a question asking what the evidence shows wants "necessary but not sufficient".

### Findings: positive

**Long-term working memory exists.**

> **Key study, Ericsson & Chase (1982): SF.** SF was an American student who practised the **digit-span** task (recalling random digits immediately in order) for **one hour a day for two years**. His span rose from **7 digits** to **80 digits**, about **ten times** the average. How? SF was a keen runner with detailed knowledge of **running times**, so he recoded groups of digits as times. A sequence beginning **3594**, for example, became **Bannister's world-record mile** time (3 minutes 59.4 seconds) and was stored as a **single chunk**. He then organised the chunks into a **hierarchical retrieval structure**. This is long-term working memory in action: domain knowledge turns meaningless digits into long-term memory codes reachable through retrieval cues.

**Guida et al. (2012, 2013)** reviewed brain-imaging studies of experts on working-memory tasks. When experts built and used chunks, **prefrontal and parietal** activity went **down**; when they built and used knowledge structures, activity in the **medial temporal** lobe, the long-term memory region, went **up**. Non-experts did not show this profile, which implies that heavy use of long-term working memory is an **expert** trait.

**Practice correlates with skill.**

- **Campitelli and Gobet (2011)** reviewed the chess literature. Every study they found linked accumulated practice hours to chess skill at r **above +0.50**. Players with no international standing averaged **under 10,000 hours** of practice; **international masters** averaged **more than 25,000**.
- **Tuffiash et al. (2007)** studied tournament-rated **Scrabble** players. **Elite** players spent much more time on deliberate practice activities, such as **analysing their own past games** and **solving anagrams**, and **lifetime accumulated Scrabble study** was a good predictor of expertise.

**Deliberate versus less deliberate practice.** Few studies compare the two directly. **Coughlan et al. (2014)** let **expert** and **intermediate Gaelic football** players choose how to practise. The experts concentrated more on their **weaker skill** and found practice **more effortful and less enjoyable**, which strongly suggests their practice was more deliberate. As the theory predicts, only those using deliberate practice (the experts) showed any **benefit after a 6-week retention interval**.

**Why do experts drop out?** **Ericsson and Moxley (2012)** proposed a sequence: deliberate practice **declines**, performance then **falls**, and the fall leads to **dropout**. **de Bruin et al. (2008)** found support in **elite young chess players** who had left competitive chess.

### Findings: negative

The 7th edition is blunt that expertise does not depend only on deliberate practice.

> **Key study, Hambrick et al. (2014).** A reanalysis of studies of chess and music. Variation in deliberate practice explained on average only **34%** of the variance in **chess** performance and **29.9%** in **music** performance. Most of the variance is left unexplained by practice.

**Correlation is not causation.** The amount of deliberate practice a person does is **not under experimental control**. People with high **innate talent**, or who have **early success**, are the ones most motivated to practise heavily. So the correlation between practice and skill probably reflects two causal paths at once: (1) deliberate practice **improves** skill, and (2) early success (and talent) **leads to** more practice.

**Campitelli and Gobet's (2011) three predictions.** If deliberate practice theory were right:

| Prediction | What was found in chess |
|---|---|
| 1. Everyone who does massive deliberate practice should reach a very high level | **Not supported**: several players have practised for **over 20,000 hours** without becoming masters |
| 2. The number of hours needed to reach high expertise should vary little between individuals | **Not supported**: hours needed to reach **master** level ranged from **3,000 to 23,600** |
| 3. Everyone should benefit **comparably** from a given amount of practice | **Not supported**: see Howard (2009) below |

> **Key study, Howard (2009).** Compared three tiers of chess player: **candidates** (the very top, who have played in the qualifying contest for a world-title match), **non-candidate grandmasters** (elite, one tier lower) and **non-grandmasters**. Plotting rating against number of games played (Figure 12.21) showed (1) **clear differences** between the groups **early on**, which **widened** with more games, and (2) **no improvement in any group after about 750 games**. Five players who had played **over 2,300 games** (more than **8,000 hours** of play) showed **no improvement at all** over their last **1,000 or more** games. There is a **ceiling** on what practice can achieve.

Howard's findings suggest that future top players can be identified **early** and that they have very high **natural talent**, and that each person's talent sets a **ceiling** on attainable performance. The book notes the same appears true in sports such as tennis and golf (Federer, Tiger Woods, the Williams sisters).

### Intelligence as an index of talent

How can natural ability be measured? When the expertise is **broad**, such as success in a career, **intelligence** is very important.

- **Gottfredson (1997)**, reviewing IQ and job success, reported that in **high-complexity** occupations IQ predicted **work performance** at r = **+0.58**. People in very demanding jobs (accountants, lawyers, doctors) average an IQ of roughly **120 to 130**, against a population mean of 100.
- **Grabner et al. (2007)**: every chess master in their sample had a **verbal IQ of at least 110** and a **non-verbal IQ of at least 115**, and IQ correlated significantly with chess rating.
- **Meinz and Hambrick (2010)** studied **piano sight-reading**. Sight-reading correlated **+0.67** with total deliberate practice. **Working memory capacity**, which correlates highly with intelligence, correlated **+0.28** with sight-reading, rising to **+0.37** when deliberate practice was controlled. Ability predicted performance **over and above** practice.

For a **very specific** skill, intelligence counts for less. **Ceci and Liker (1986)** studied experts who estimate **odds in harness racing** by juggling interactions among as many as **seven variables** (track size, a horse's lifetime speed and so on). Their IQs spanned **81 to 128**, yet IQ was **unrelated** to how well they handicapped.

> **Trap.** Intelligence matters for **broad** expertise (Gottfredson) and little for **narrow** expertise (Ceci and Liker). The Meinz and Hambrick working-memory correlation goes **up** (+0.28 to +0.37) when practice is controlled, the opposite of what "practice explains everything" predicts.

### Evaluation of deliberate practice theory

**Strengths.**

- Strong support for the idea that domain memory can be developed through **long-term working memory**; most experts develop superior long-term working memory, easing the limits on processing capacity.
- Strong evidence that deliberate practice is **necessary** for outstanding expertise.

**Limitations.**

1. **Measurement.** Deliberate practice is hard to measure precisely. Hours of study are the usual proxy, but hours can be spent practising non-deliberately, and a chess player's study time partly reflects how many tournament games they play. **Howard (2012)** matched two groups on games played; one had studied **five times** as many hours as the other, yet their playing strength was **similar**, so study hours themselves seem to matter relatively little.
2. **Causality.** The key supporting finding, a strong correlation between expertise and amount of practice, cannot establish causation, because naturally talented people may simply be **more motivated** to practise.
3. **Not sufficient.** Deliberate practice is **necessary but not sufficient**: people with comparable amounts of practice in the same domain still differ substantially (Howard, 2009).
4. **Talent.** The claim that innate talent is unimportant is unconvincing. Sternberg and Ben-Zeev (2001, p. 302) asked, "Is one to believe that anyone could become a Mozart if only he or she put in the time?" A more plausible view is that **talent sets the ceiling** and deliberate practice determines **how close** each person gets to it (Howard, 2009).
5. **Intelligence.** Individual differences in talent measured by intelligence tests matter, especially for **broad and complex** skills such as becoming an outstanding lawyer.
6. **Longitudinal research needed.** Deliberate practice, talent and motivation are probably causally linked, but the **strengths and directions** of those links have not been established.

> **Trap.** "Which is **not** a limitation of deliberate practice theory?" The listed limitations are measurement, causality, insufficiency, talent, intelligence and the need for longitudinal work. **"Deliberate practice is unnecessary for expertise"** is **not** one of them; the 7th edition accepts that practice is **necessary**. Likewise "long-term working memory has no empirical support" is wrong; it is listed as a strength.

## How the chapter fits together

**Past experience cuts both ways.** This is the chapter's spine. Experience supplies analogies (Gick & Holyoak), templates (Gobet), rapid diagnostic pattern recognition (Kundel, Melo) and long-term working memory (SF). The same experience produces functional fixedness (Duncker, Ye et al.), mental set (Luchins), experts missing shorter solutions (Bilalić), and experts being hurt by extra verbal information (Kulatunga-Moruzi). Whenever a question asks whether knowledge helps or hinders, the 7th-edition answer is "both, depending on whether the problem matches what was learned".

**Representation comes first.** Gestalt restructuring, Ohlsson's constraint relaxation, re-encoding and elaboration, the Monty Hall boxer version, Nitschke's and Ruh's representation stage before planning, Kurtz and Loewenstein's structural comparison, and McCaffrey's generic-parts technique all make the same point: most failures are failures to represent the problem in the right terms, not failures to search hard enough. Öllinger et al. add the necessary correction that once the representation is right, search is still needed.

**Much of the important processing is not conscious.** Ellis et al.'s eye movements, Cushen and Wiley's mismatch between process and felt insight, Thomas and Lleras's arm-swinging hint, Paynter et al.'s ERPs, Patsenko and Altmann's invisible disc changes, Reber et al.'s subliminal analogies and experts' sub-second fixations all show it. This continues the part opener's point that we know the products of thinking, not its processes.

**The prefrontal cortex is double-edged.** It is needed for planning (Goel & Grafman, Colvin, Dagher, Ruh), for inhibiting distractors in analogy (Krawczyk, Cho) and for working memory, yet it also imposes the constraints that block insight, so damping it can help (Reverberi, Chi & Snyder, Chrysikou). Clinically, this explains why a frontal patient can be both worse and, on a narrow class of problems, better than normal.

**Fast versus slow, again.** Kahneman's System 1 and System 2, the cognitive miser, implicit versus explicit diagnosis, and template access versus slow search in chess are the same contrast in four settings. In every case the chapter ends in the same place: experts rely more on fast processes, but slow, deliberate processes still matter, especially on hard problems (Moxley; Mamede), and the "automatic" label is not yet proven.

**Practice is necessary, not sufficient.** Plasticity research shows that training changes the brain, and deliberate practice research shows that practice is indispensable, but Hambrick, Campitelli and Gobet, Howard, Foster and Zatorre, Grabner and Meinz all leave a large place for pre-existing ability.

## Worked example

*A 46-year-old civil engineer is referred to a clinical psychology service eight months after surgery for a right frontal meningioma. His wife reports that he "can't organise anything": he has failed to plan a family holiday, books things and then has to cancel them, and has lost his job managing site projects. On testing his full-scale IQ is 124 and his memory scores are normal. On the Tower of London he performs normally on easy items but fails five-move problems, starting to move pieces almost immediately. On a set of matchstick arithmetic problems he does unusually well on the hard items. On a picture-analogy task he often chooses a semantically related distractor (for "sandwich : lunchbox :: hammer : ?" he chooses "nail"). A trainee on the team suggests that the high IQ means the complaints must be motivational.*

**Is the trainee right that a high IQ rules out a genuine cognitive deficit?** No. The pattern is PF's (Goel & Grafman, 2000): right prefrontal damage, IQ of 128, good performance on well-defined tests and poor handling of ill-defined everyday problems, because he cannot construct the preliminary plan that gives an unstructured problem its shape. IQ tests and most laboratory tasks are well-defined and supply their own structure. Goel et al. (2013) found that right prefrontal patients on a realistic trip-planning task made premature commitments, which matches the booking and cancelling.

**What does the Tower of London result add?** Planning load. More complex Tower of London problems recruit the DLPFC (Dagher et al., 1999), and planning, as opposed to building a representation, peaks in the right DLPFC (Ruh et al., 2012). His near-zero pre-planning time on hard problems looks like Koppenol-Gonzalez et al.'s (2010) low-planning group, who pre-planned briefly and made many errors. Goel and Grafman (1995) would predict particular difficulty with any move that leads away from the goal.

**Why would he be better than normal at matchstick problems?** The lateral prefrontal cortex imposes the conventional constraints of arithmetic. Reverberi et al.'s (2005) lateral prefrontal patients solved 82% of the hardest matchstick problems, against 43% of controls. Reduced top-down constraint is a deficit in daily life and an advantage on problems that need constraint relaxation.

**Why "nail"?** Krawczyk et al. (2008) found that frontal patients, more than temporal patients, chose semantic and perceptual distractors and improved when no related distractors were present. The deficit is in inhibiting a related but wrong answer, not in stored meanings (which depend on the temporal lobes).

**What about the team's own reasoning?** The trainee anchored on one familiar rule, "high IQ means no deficit", which is mental set in clinical form (Luchins; Bilalić et al., 2008a). Mamede et al. (2010) is the relevant corrective: for complex cases, deliberate analytic thinking improves expert diagnosis, while a quick first impression is enough only for simple cases. Using ill-defined, real-world tasks alongside standardised tests is the practical implication of the well-defined versus ill-defined distinction.

## MCQ traps

1. **Goel (2010)**: problem solving needs two states, a desire to move between them, a non-obvious route, and a **consciously guided multi-step** process.
2. **PF (Goel & Grafman, 2000)**: **right** prefrontal damage, IQ **128**, good on well-defined lab tasks, fails ill-defined everyday problems through poor planning.
3. **Monty Hall**: **switch** (wins two-thirds); **85%** stay (Burns & Wieth, 2004); pigeons **96%** versus humans **66%** switching (Herbranson & Schroeder, 2010).

6. **Metcalfe & Wiebe (1987)**: warmth rises **gradually** for non-insight problems, stays **flat then jumps** for insight problems.
7. **Bowden et al. (2005)**: RAT insight solutions activate the **right anterior superior temporal gyrus**; right hemisphere = **weak, distant** associations, left = **strong, close** ones.
8. **Ellis et al. (2011)**: anagram eye movements drift **gradually** away from the distractor although solutions feel sudden.
9. **Wallas (1926)**: incubation by **subconscious work** (classic stages: preparation, incubation, illumination, verification); **Simon (1966)**: incubation works by **forgetting control information**.
10. **Sio & Ormerod (2009)**: incubation effects in **73%** of studies, bigger for **multiple-solution creative** problems and after **long preparation**.
11. **Wagner et al. (2004)**: **59%** (sleep) versus **25%** (no sleep) found the short cut; **Sio et al. (2013)**: sleep helps **difficult** RAT items only; **Penaloza & Calvillo (2012)**: **two-minute** break helps only if misleading information is forgotten.
12. **Reverberi et al. (2005)**: lateral prefrontal patients **82%** versus controls **43%**; **Chi & Snyder (2011)**: left dampened, right boosted, **60% versus 20%**.
13. **Moss et al. (2011)**: hints work best **at** the impasse, not before or after.

18. **Cognitive Reflection Test (Frederick, 2005)**: **5 cents, 5 minutes, 47 days**; Harvard/Princeton **75%** at least one wrong, **20%** all wrong; predicts thinking beyond IQ (**Toplak et al., 2014**).
19. **Chen (2002)**: **superficial, structural, procedural** similarity; structural plus procedural beats structural alone.
20. **Gick & Holyoak (1980)**: **10%** alone, **40%** uninformed, **80%** informed; **Keane (1987)**: **88%** recall with a superficially similar story.
21. **Reception** paradigm favours **superficial**, **production** favours **structural** analogies (Blanchette & Dunbar, 2000); scientists fixing problems use superficial, generating hypotheses use structural (**Dunbar & Blanchette, 2001**).
22. **Kurtz & Loewenstein (2007)**: comparing problems **54% versus 38%**, via better **retrieval**.
23. Analogy-intelligence **+0.7 (Spearman, 1927)**; Raven's APM-working memory capacity **+0.50 (Ackerman et al., 2005)**, driven by **new rule combinations** (Wiley et al., 2011).
24. **De Groot (1965)**: masters **91%** versus weaker players **43%**; **no difference for random** positions.
25. **Gobet and Clarkson (2004)**: about **two** templates regardless of skill; maximum size **13 to 15** (masters) versus about **6** (novices).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Problem solving** | Goal-directed, controlled thinking that moves through several steps from recognising a problem to reaching a solution |
| **Decision making / judgement** | Choosing among options with personal consequences / estimating how probable events are (Chapter 13) |
| **Deductive / inductive / informal reasoning** | Deriving conclusions that must follow from premises / judging whether evidence supports a hypothesis, never guaranteed valid / weighing arguments using one's own knowledge (Chapter 14) |
| **Well-defined problem** | A problem whose starting state, permitted methods and goal are all clearly given |
| **Ill-defined problem** | A problem in which the starting state, goal or available methods are vague or unspecified |
| **Knowledge-rich problem** | A problem that only someone with substantial relevant background knowledge can solve |
| **Knowledge-lean problem** | A problem solvable without specialist knowledge because the statement contains what is needed |
| **Reproductive / productive thinking** | Gestalt terms for re-applying past solutions versus restructuring the problem in a new way |
| **Insight** | The sudden realisation of how to solve a problem, typically after the representation is reorganised |
| **Remote Associates Test** | A task in which the solver finds one word that links three given words (fence, card, master: post) |
| **Incubation** | Putting an unsolved problem aside for a period, which is claimed to make later solution more likely |
| **Representational change theory** | Ohlsson's (1992) account that insight follows an impasse when the problem representation is changed |
| **Constraint relaxation / re-encoding / elaboration** | Ohlsson's three routes: dropping a self-imposed restriction / reinterpreting an element / adding new information to the representation |
| **Impasse** | A point at which the solver is stuck because the current representation yields no solution |
| **Functional fixedness** | Rigidly thinking of an object only in terms of its usual use, so that other uses are missed |
| **Generic-parts technique** | McCaffrey's (2012) method of describing each part of an object without reference to function and asking what each description could be used for |
| **Mental set (Einstellung)** | The tendency to keep using a previously successful method when it is no longer appropriate or optimal |
| **Uniformity fallacy** | Assuming that all available options are equally probable whether or not they are (Falk & Lann, 2008) |
| **Problem space** | An abstract map of every state a problem can be in, including start, goal and all intermediate states, linked by operators |
| **Heuristic** | A low-effort rule of thumb that often, but not always, gives a reasonably accurate answer |
| **Algorithm** | A step-by-step procedure guaranteed to reach the solution |
| **Means-ends analysis** | A heuristic that identifies the gap between the current and goal states and sets a subgoal to reduce it |
| **Hill climbing** | A simple heuristic of always making the move that seems to bring you closest to the goal |
| **Progress monitoring** | A heuristic in which noticing slow progress towards the goal prompts a change of strategy |
| **Cognitive miser** | A person who habitually minimises the time and effort spent thinking |
| **System 1 / System 2** | Kahneman's fast, effortless thinking versus slow, effortful thinking that should check System 1's output |
| **Cognitive Reflection Test** | Frederick's (2005) three-item test with tempting wrong intuitive answers, measuring the tendency to check intuitions |
| **Analogy** | A comparison that highlights similarities between two things, or between a current and a past problem |
| **Superficial / structural / procedural similarity** | Chen's (2002) three kinds of similarity: shared surface details; shared causal relations; shared procedures for implementing the solution |
| **Reception versus production paradigm** | Being given a candidate analogy by the experimenter versus generating one's own |
| **Four-term analogy** | A problem of the form A:B::C:D, solved by encoding, mapping and response |
| **Fluid intelligence** | Non-verbal reasoning ability applied to new problems, typically measured by Raven's Progressive Matrices |
| **Working memory capacity** | The ability to process and store information at the same time |
| **Expertise** | A very high level of domain knowledge and performance built up through years of systematic practice |
| **Template** | In chess, an abstract schematic memory structure with a fixed core and variable slots for pieces and locations |
| **Chunking** | Classic background (Chase & Simon, 1973): grouping several pieces into one meaningful unit stored in long-term memory |
| **Explicit versus implicit reasoning** | Slow, deliberate, conscious reasoning versus fast, automatic, non-conscious reasoning |
| **Information-reduction hypothesis** | Haider and Frensch's (1999) view that expertise makes attention more selective and efficient |
| **Holistic model** | Kundel et al.'s (2007) view that experts take in information from a wider area per fixation |
| **Plasticity** | Experience-dependent change in brain structure and function that affects behaviour |
| **The Knowledge** | The London black-cab licensing requirement: 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross plus thousands of landmarks |
| **Deliberate practice** | Practice at a suitable difficulty, with informative feedback and chances to repeat and correct errors |
| **Long-term working memory** | Experts' use of knowledge structures to store information quickly in long-term memory and reach it through retrieval cues held in working memory |
