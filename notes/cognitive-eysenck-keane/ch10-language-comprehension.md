# Chapter 10: Language Comprehension

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 9 stopped once the individual words had been recognised. A string of identified words is not yet a meaning: you still have to work out who did what to whom (grammatical structure), what the speaker actually intended (which may be the opposite of what the words say), what has been left unsaid and must be filled in (inferences), and how the current sentence fits the story built so far. This chapter climbs that ladder from the single sentence to the whole text.

Two questions recur at every rung. The **timing question**: does the system first build a bare grammatical skeleton and consult meaning, context and world knowledge only later, or does it use everything from the first word? The **thoroughness question**: do we build a complete, accurate representation and draw every possible inference, or settle for one that is good enough for the task? At the sentence level these questions divide the garden-path and constraint-based models; for metaphor, the standard pragmatic model and automatic access; for inference, the constructionist and minimalist positions; for whole texts, schema theory and Kintsch's construction-integration model. The 7th edition's verdict is nearly always the same: non-syntactic information is used earlier than the serial, syntax-first theories allowed, but processing is also shallower and more goal-dependent than the "complete and accurate" theories assumed.

**How to read this chapter.** 10.1 maps the territory and fixes the vocabulary. 10.2 introduces parsing and the surprising power of prosody. 10.3 is the sentence-level core: the garden-path, constraint-based and unrestricted race models, the good-enough challenge to all three, and the ERP evidence on timing. 10.4 turns to intended meaning: figurative language, the autism-spectrum box, metaphor models and common ground. 10.5 on working memory capacity is a hinge, because it explains individual differences that reappear in metaphor (10.4) and inference (10.6). 10.6 asks which inferences readers really draw, and 10.7 assembles the large-scale theories of discourse. For each model, be able to say what information it admits at the first moment and how complete a representation it expects.

## 10.1 Introduction

### From words to meaning

The early stages of reading and listening differ (the eye fixates letters, the ear segments a continuous acoustic stream), which is why Chapter 9 treated them separately. The higher-level processes of comprehension are largely shared: whether a story is read or heard, its structure must be built, its intended meaning recovered and its parts integrated. Most research has used reading, so the chapter leans on reading studies, assuming (usually correctly) that the conclusions also hold for listening.

### Two levels of sentence analysis

The first level is **syntactic**. **Syntax** is the set of rules governing word order in a language's sentences. **Grammar** covers similar ground but is broader, taking in sentence structure, punctuation and the parts of speech. Working out a sentence's syntactic or grammatical structure is **parsing** (10.2 and 10.3).

The second level is **meaning**, where the **literal** meaning of a sentence often differs from its **intended** meaning, as in irony, sarcasm and metaphor. The book's example is someone advising a notoriously idle colleague not to overdo it: literally a caution against overwork, actually a dig at laziness. The study of intended meaning is **pragmatics** (10.4), and the context of an utterance shapes it heavily.

> **Trap.** **Syntax** is word-order rules; **grammar** adds parts of speech and punctuation; **semantics** is literal meaning; **pragmatics** is intended meaning in context ("meaning minus semantics"). The book's glossary definitions are narrower than a linguist's: in linguistics "grammar" often spans phonology, morphology and syntax, and Chomsky's "a grammar" is the whole rule system that generates a language. An option defining grammar as "the complete rule system of a language" is the linguist's sense, not the 7th edition's.

### The rest of the chapter

After parsing and pragmatics comes **individual differences** in comprehension, which most theories of sentence processing ignore. Then the focus widens to **discourse**, where the central question is which inferences are and are not drawn, and finally to how readers integrate a whole text, identify its themes and represent the situation it describes.

## 10.2 Parsing: Overview

### What parsing research asks

Parsing research asks how readers and listeners recover sentence structure, and above all **when** each kind of information is used. The book lists four possible relations between **syntactic** and **semantic** analysis:

1. Syntactic analysis generally comes first and influences semantic analysis.
2. Semantic analysis generally comes first.
3. The two occur simultaneously.
4. Syntax and semantics are so closely bound that they work hand in glove (credited to Gerry Altmann, personal communication).

The models in 10.3 are bets on these options: the garden-path model backs option 1, the constraint-based model something close to options 3 and 4.

### Why English is not the whole story

Most parsing studies use English. Grammatical relations can be signalled by **word order** or by **inflection** (changing a noun or verb to mark a grammatical feature, such as *-ed* for past tense or *-s* for plural). English depends heavily on word order; more inflected languages such as **German** allow freer order (Harley, 2013). This may explain the finding that German parsers perform worse than English ones (**Kübler, 2006**). Principles discovered with English may not transfer, a point that returns as a limitation of the garden-path model.

### Syntax, grammar and ambiguity

Every language permits an unlimited number of sentences, yet they are systematic. Linguists such as **Chomsky (1957)** captured this productivity and regularity in sets of rules, and such a set is a **grammar**. Ideally a grammar generates every permissible sentence of a language and rejects every impermissible one.

> **Classic background.** Chomsky's transformational grammar (1957, 1965) separated **surface structure** (the actual word sequence) from **deep structure** (the underlying relations of meaning). "John is easy to please" and "John is eager to please" look alike on the surface but differ underneath: in the first John is pleased, in the second John does the pleasing. Indian papers still ask about this.

Ambiguity comes in two kinds. **Global ambiguity** means the complete sentence has two or more readings ("Kids make nutritious snacks": children who cook, or children as food). **Local ambiguity** means several analyses are possible at some point mid-sentence, until later words settle it; most garden-path sentences are of this type.

Why so much ambiguity? Producing only unambiguous sentences would be very demanding for speakers and writers, and **Piantadosi et al. (2012)** argued that some ambiguity is actually **desirable**: context usually supplies the missing information, so repeating it explicitly would be inefficient and dull.

Researchers favour ambiguous sentences for a methodological reason. Normal parsing is too fast to observe directly; ambiguities make it slow down or fail, and where and how readers stumble reveals the parser's default operations, much as visual illusions reveal normal perception.

### Prosodic cues

Listeners also use **prosodic cues**: stress, intonation, rhythm and word duration. Their value shows when they are removed: monotone speech in which every syllable gets equal weight is hard to understand (**Duffy & Pisoni, 1992**). Prosody can also mislead: a pause placed where it conflicts with the syntax makes a sentence much harder to understand (**Pauker et al., 2012**; details in 10.3). Speakers' production of prosody is covered in Chapter 11.

Prosody matters most for ambiguous speech. In "The old men and women sat on the bench", a speaker who means that only the men are old lengthens "men" and gives the stressed syllable of "women" a steep pitch rise, grouping "old men" apart from "women".

> **Key study, Holzgrefe et al. (2013).** German listeners heard ambiguous sequences such as *Mona oder Lena und Lola* ("Mona or Lena and Lola") with a pause and other boundary cues placed after *Mona* (early) or after *Lena* (late). A late boundary groups the first two names, meaning Mona or Lena, plus Lola. With the late boundary, listeners **integrated the prosody into their parse immediately**. (Figure 10.1 shows the sound spectra of the two versions, the silent pause marked #.)

**Nakamura et al. (2012)** sharpened the timing point: prosody shaped listeners' interpretations **before** the disambiguating words had been heard. Prosody is used predictively, not only for repair.

### Prosody in silent reading

Readers seem to generate prosody even when nothing is heard, with punctuation as its written trigger.

> **Key study, Steinhauer and Friederici (2001).** Participants heard sentences with intonational boundaries or read them with **commas** at the same points. The **event-related potentials** (ERPs) were similar, suggesting that speech-comprehension mechanisms also operate when reading.

> **Key study, Hirotani et al. (2006).** Spoken, "John, go to the library for me" has a pause after "John" that eases understanding. Read silently, **fixations on "John" were longer when a comma followed it**, mirroring the spoken pause.

(This silent phrasing is often called **implicit prosody**, a background term the book does not use.)

### The pattern of boundaries

Traditionally, each prosodic boundary was read as a separate signal. **Frazier et al. (2006)** argued that the **overall pattern** of phrasing matters.

> **Key study, Frazier et al. (2006).** Listeners heard "I met the daughter (#1) of the colonel (#2) who was on the balcony", always with an intermediate boundary at #2 and a boundary at #1 that was larger, equal or smaller. Interpretation depended on the **relation between the boundaries**. Listeners were **most likely** to put the colonel on the balcony when **#1 was larger than #2**, and **least likely** when #1 was smaller.

On the traditional view, a boundary at #2, just before "who was on the balcony", should on its own discourage attaching the clause to the nearest noun (the colonel). But the same #2 boundary had different effects depending on #1: a big break after "daughter" makes the break after "colonel" relatively small, so "the colonel who was on the balcony" is heard as one unit. Frazier et al. even suggested that prosody may provide the framework within which comprehension happens, in silent reading as well as speech.

> **Trap.** Prosodic cues concern grouping words into phrases and so recovering **syntax**. Do not confuse them with Chapter 9's **phonemic restoration** (hearing a sound masked by noise) or the **McGurk effect** (lip movements altering the phoneme heard), which concern identifying sounds. And the silent-reading claim is not about lip movement: commas trigger processing similar to spoken boundaries.

## 10.3 Models of Parsing

### Two families of models

There are countless parsing models, but most belong to one of two families.

| | **Two-stage, serial** | **One-stage, parallel** |
|---|---|---|
| Flagship | **Garden-path model** (Frazier & Rayner, 1982) | **Constraint-based model** (MacDonald et al., 1994) |
| Information used at first | **Syntax only** | **All sources** (syntactic, semantic, contextual) |
| Analyses at once | One | Several, competing |
| When the first analysis fails | A second stage revises it | The competition shifts |

A third model, the **unrestricted race model**, combines features of both. The book warns that findings often look inconsistent because people are highly sensitive to subtle features of language, so apparently trivial differences between test sentences change how they are parsed.

### The garden-path model

The name comes from the idiom "to lead someone up the garden path": ambiguous sentences lure the parser into a structure that later proves wrong. The notorious example, **"The horse raced past the barn fell"**, is almost impossible on first reading because "raced" is taken as the main verb; only at "fell" does it emerge that "raced past the barn" was a reduced relative clause ("the horse *that was* raced..."). Such sentences are extremely rare in natural language (McKoon & Ratcliff, 2003), and commoner ambiguities have been more informative. (Classic background: the sentence is usually credited to **Bever, 1970**.)

#### Assumptions

1. Only **one** syntactic structure is initially considered.
2. **Meaning plays no part** in selecting that first structure.
3. The **simplest** structure is chosen, using two principles.
4. **Minimal attachment**: prefer the structure with the **fewest nodes** (major constituents such as noun phrases and verb phrases), that is, the simplest tree.
5. **Late closure**: attach each new word to the **current** phrase or clause if grammatically permissible.
6. When the principles conflict, **minimal attachment wins**.
7. If the first structure clashes with later information (for example, semantic information from a **thematic processor**), a **second stage** revises it.

The model is serial and syntax-first: a fast, meaning-blind first pass, with meaning entering only at the checking and repair stage.

#### The two principles in action

**Frazier and Rayner (1982)** demonstrated both with eye movements. For **minimal attachment**: "The girl knew the answer by heart" versus "The girl knew the answer was wrong". The fewest-nodes structure makes "the answer" the **direct object** of "knew", which suits only the first sentence; in the second, "the answer" heads an embedded clause, and fixations were **longer**.

For **late closure**, compare a version in which late closure works, "Since Jay always jogs a mile it seems like a short distance to him", with the version in which it fails: "Since Jay always jogs a mile seems like a short distance to him". Late closure keeps the first clause open and makes "a mile" the object of "jogs", which is wrong for the second version, where "a mile" starts the main clause. Readers made **very long fixations on "seems"**, the word that exposes the error. A comma after "jogs" (a written prosodic cue) makes the second version much easier (Rayner et al., 2012).

Why follow principles that mislead? Because both **minimise demands on short-term memory** (the parser commits to one simple structure rather than holding several), and they usually deliver the correct structure quickly.

> **Trap.** **Minimal attachment** concerns *how many nodes* (simplest tree); **late closure** concerns *where* the new word attaches (the current phrase). The girl/answer pair tests minimal attachment; the Jay/jogs and sneezed/doctor sentences test late closure. In conflict **minimal attachment** wins. Both principles are purely **syntactic**.

#### Findings for the model

Readers and listeners use both principles very often (Rayner et al., 2012). **Van Gompel and Pickering (2001)** showed late closure's grip with "After the child had sneezed the doctor prescribed a course of injections": eye movements revealed difficulty after "sneezed" because readers tried to make "the doctor" its object, although "sneeze" cannot take an object.

The model's most distinctive claim, that structure can be computed with almost no semantic information, has neuropsychological support.

> **Key case, DM (Breedin & Saffran, 1999).** DM had **semantic dementia**, the progressive loss of knowledge of word meanings (Chapter 7), yet performed at **essentially normal levels** at detecting grammatical violations and identifying sentence subjects and objects. **Garrard et al. (2004)** found intact **grammaticality judgements** in another semantic dementia patient.

Semantic dementia involves the **anterior temporal lobe**. Syntactic impairment has a different signature: reviewing patient studies, **Wilson et al. (2012)** linked impaired comprehension of complex syntax to damage in the **left inferior and middle frontal gyri** (Figure 10.2). That dissociation is worth remembering clinically.

> **Trap.** Semantic dementia (anterior temporal lobe) is the group with **spared** syntax. Do not attach DM's normal grammaticality judgements to frontal patients, whose deficit is the reverse. DM is a **single** case; Garrard et al. (2004) is the second.

The crucial claim that meaning cannot affect the **initial** structure fares worse.

> **Key study, Trueswell et al. (1994).** Eye movements were recorded on "The defendant examined by the lawyer turned out to be unreliable" and "The evidence examined by the lawyer turned out to be unreliable". Minimal attachment makes "examined" the main verb in both, predicting equal difficulty. But a defendant can examine things and evidence cannot, so if meaning is used at once, only the defendant sentence should mislead. Readers **took longer to disambiguate the defendant sentence**, contrary to the garden-path model.

> **Trap.** The **animate** noun ("defendant") made the sentence **harder**, because it is a plausible agent of "examined". An option claiming the "evidence" version was harder reverses the result.

> **Key study, Pauker et al. (2012).** Listeners heard "When a bear is approaching the people the dogs come running" with pauses (#) placed either at the true clause boundary (after "the people") or, misleadingly, also after "approaching". Late closure predicts easy parsing either way. With the well-placed pause, sentences were **accepted 87%** of the time; with the misleading pause, only **28%**. Misleading prosody can largely override late closure.

Visual context is a further problem. The model says the scene cannot affect the first parse, but studies using the **visual world paradigm** (tracking eye movements to objects in a display while instructions are heard) suggest otherwise.

> **Key study, Spivey et al. (2002).** Participants heard "Put the apple on the towel in the box". The simplest structure takes "on the towel" as the **destination**, which is wrong. With a display that did not resolve the ambiguity, listeners behaved as the garden-path model predicts. When the display showed **two apples**, one on a towel and one on a napkin, listeners used it to identify which apple was meant and made **far fewer looks to the incorrect object** (the empty towel) (Figure 10.3).

Participants had **several seconds** to inspect the display before the instruction. **Ferreira et al. (2013)** replicated the effect when the display came first, but when it appeared **at the same time** as the instruction, listeners found it much harder to use. Prior context helped because it let them **predict** the kind of instruction coming. So processing depends on precise task demands, and disambiguating context works only when available **ahead of** the instruction.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** A simple, coherent account; minimal attachment and late closure genuinely shape initial parsing; and the principles are plausible because they reduce processing demands.

**Limitations.**

1. **Meaning does affect the first structure** (Trueswell et al., 1994), and later sections show rapid effects of semantic and world knowledge.
2. **The principles are easily overridden** by misleading prosody (Pauker et al., 2012), prior context (Spivey et al., 2002) and, below, verb bias.
3. **Hard to test.** Early non-syntactic effects can be explained away by claiming the second stage starts very quickly.
4. **Cross-language variation.** **Spanish, Dutch and French** show a preference for **early** closure (Harley, 2013).

> **Classic background.** The standard cross-language study is **Cuetos and Mitchell (1988)**: in "Someone shot the servant of the actress who was on the balcony", English readers mostly attach the clause to the actress (late closure), Spanish readers to the servant.

### The constraint-based model

**MacDonald et al.'s (1994)** model says the initial interpretation depends on **multiple information sources** (syntactic, semantic, world knowledge), called **constraints** because each limits the possible interpretations. Built on a **connectionist** architecture (Chapter 1), it assumes that all relevant information is **available immediately**, that **competing analyses are activated in parallel**, and that the structure with most support is most activated. Readers are confused when the correct structure is less activated than an incorrect one.

Four characteristics of language help resolve ambiguity:

1. **Grammatical knowledge** limits possible interpretations.
2. The various kinds of information about a word are **not independent** of each other.
3. A word may be **less ambiguous in some respects than others** (ambiguous in tense but not in grammatical category, say).
4. Grammatically permissible interpretations differ greatly in **frequency and probability** in past experience, and the one most consistent with experience is usually chosen.

The fourth is the heart of the model: parsing runs on learned probabilities rather than fixed structural rules.

**MacDonald (2013)** extended it into a **production-distribution-comprehension account**. Speakers reduce their own load with strategies such as **"easy first"** (start with common words and simple phrases while planning the rest) and **plan reuse** (favour well-practised sentence plans). Listeners are sensitive to these strategies, which helps them predict what comes next; many constraints thus reflect listeners' knowledge of how speakers behave.

#### Findings

The rapid use of meaning in Trueswell et al. (1994) fits the model, and Harley (2013) and van Berkum (2009) review many similar findings. The sharper test concerns **parallel versus serial** processing.

> **Key study, Cai et al. (2012).** In "Because it was John that Ralph threatened the neighbour recorded their conversation", "the neighbour" could be the **object** of "threatened" or the **subject** of "recorded their conversation". Readers adopted the subject analysis, yet the **object analysis disrupted processing even though it was not adopted**. An unchosen analysis must have been computed, in parallel, as the constraint-based model assumes.

The model's signature constraint is **verb bias**: many verbs allow two structures but occur far more often in one. "The professor read the newspaper during his break" is easier than "The professor read the newspaper had been destroyed", because "read" usually takes a **direct object** and only sometimes an **embedded clause**. The constraint-based model predicts easier resolution when structure matches bias; the garden-path model predicts **no effect** of bias.

> **Key study, Wilson and Garnsey (2009).** Ambiguity took **longer to resolve when the sentence structure was inconsistent with the verb's bias** (Figure 10.4 plots reading times in the disambiguating region), so experience with particular verbs affects parsing immediately.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** (1) It is efficient to use all information from the outset, and readers do use meaning (Trueswell et al., 1994; Wilson & Garnsey, 2009) and context (Spivey et al., 2002) rapidly. (2) People can consider more than one analysis at once (Cai et al., 2012), which a serial model cannot explain. (3) Because parsing reflects experience, it should vary between people, and **Brysbaert and Mitchell (1996)** found **substantial individual differences among Dutch readers** in parsing decisions.

**Limitations.** (1) **Imprecise predictions**: Rayner et al. (2012) argued that the general claim of interactive, constraint-based parsing is almost unfalsifiable, since it does not say which factors matter, or how and when. (2) Much of its support (early non-syntactic effects) can also be accommodated by a garden-path model with a fast second stage.

> **Trap.** That second limitation mirrors the garden-path model's third. Hence the decisive tests probe **parallelism** (Cai et al., 2012) or **frequency effects** that a structure-only first stage should ignore (verb bias).

### The unrestricted race model

**Van Gompel et al. (2000)** proposed three assumptions:

1. **All sources of information**, semantic and syntactic, are used to select a structure (as in the constraint-based model).
2. **Only one structure is pursued**; alternatives are ignored unless it is disconfirmed.
3. If it must be abandoned, there is **extensive reanalysis** (as in the garden-path model's two stages).

Candidate analyses "race", any information can speed its favoured candidate, and the first to finish is adopted.

> **Key study, van Gompel et al. (2001).** Readers saw three sentence types: **ambiguous** ("The burglar stabbed only the guy with the dagger during the night": either man could hold the dagger), **verb-phrase attachment** ("...the dog with the dagger...": the burglar held it) and **noun-phrase attachment** ("...the dog with the collar...": the dog wore it). The **garden-path** model (minimal attachment favours the verb-phrase reading) predicts fast verb-phrase and ambiguous sentences but slow noun-phrase sentences. The **constraint-based** model predicts fast verb-phrase and noun-phrase sentences (meaning supports only one reading) but slow ambiguous sentences (strong competition). In fact, **ambiguous sentences were read fastest** and the other two **did not differ** (Figure 10.5).

Only the race model predicts this. With ambiguous sentences, whichever structure wins is acceptable, so **reanalysis is never needed**; with the disambiguated sentences, the wrong structure sometimes wins, so **reanalysis is sometimes needed**.

> **Trap.** This **ambiguity advantage** counts **against** the constraint-based model, which predicts ambiguous sentences should be *slower*. It is the race model's signature result. It reappears in Swets et al. (2008), where good-enough processing explains it.

> **Key study, Mohamed and Clifton (2011).** In "The second wife will claim the entire family inheritance for herself" there is an **ambiguous region** ("the entire family inheritance") and a **disambiguating region** ("for herself"), sometimes preceded by context biasing the wrong structure. The **garden-path** model predicts no slowing anywhere (the correct structure is the simplest); the **constraint-based** model predicts slowing in **both** regions (competition, then selection); the **race** model predicts no slowing in the ambiguous region but slowing in the **disambiguating** region (one structure, often wrong). Reading times fitted the **race model** best.

**Evaluation.** The model arguably combines the best features of its rivals: it is reasonable that all information, world knowledge included, builds a structure from the start, and that the structure is kept until contradicted. More research is needed, but like the other two it assumes readers eventually reach the **correct** structure, which, as the next subsection shows, is often untrue.

> **Comparison.**
>
> | | Garden-path | Constraint-based | Unrestricted race |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Authors | Frazier & Rayner (1982) | MacDonald et al. (1994) | van Gompel et al. (2000) |
> | First-pass information | Syntax only | All sources | All sources |
> | Analyses at once | One | Several | One (race winner) |
> | Revision | Second stage | Re-weighting | Extensive reanalysis |
> | Signature evidence | Frazier & Rayner (1982); DM | Trueswell (1994); Cai (2012); verb bias | van Gompel (2001); Mohamed & Clifton (2011) |

### Good-enough representations

Until recently almost every theory of sentence processing, including the three above, assumed that the processor builds representations that are **complete, detailed and accurate** (Ferreira et al., 2002). The models disagree about how the parser reaches the right structure, not about whether it does.

The **good-enough** view rejects that premise: the usual goal is an analysis **good enough to generate a response to the current task** (Swets et al., 2008). If a rough, partly heuristic interpretation will do, that is what is built, so comprehension is often shallow and sometimes wrong.

The **Moses illusion** shows this. Asked how many animals of each sort Moses took onto the ark, many people answer "two"; the correct answer is "none", because the ark was Noah's. The question is processed well enough to retrieve the story but not well enough to notice the wrong name. (Classic background: the illusion was first reported by **Erickson and Mattson, 1981**.) Similarly, **Ferreira (2003)** found that representations of heard sentences were sometimes **inaccurate rather than rich and complete**: "The mouse was eaten by the cheese" was sometimes taken to mean that the mouse ate the cheese.

#### Why such errors happen

**Ferreira (2003)** proposed that we use **heuristics** (rules of thumb) to simplify comprehension, notably the **NVN strategy**: treat the **subject** (first noun) as the **agent** and the **object** as the **recipient or theme**. Most English sentences fit this pattern, so it usually works; implausible passives are where it fails, because heuristic plus world knowledge (mice eat cheese) outvotes the syntax.

**Christianson et al. (2010)** put it as a conflict between the passive's **syntactic structure** and **semantic knowledge** of what usually happens, which is sometimes resolved in favour of semantics.

> **Key study, Christianson et al. (2010).** After hearing a sentence, listeners described an unrelated line drawing. After **plausible passives** ("The fish was caught by the angler") they tended to **produce passives**, showing the structure had been processed (structural priming). After **implausible passives** ("The angler was caught by the fish") they tended **not** to, suggesting they had paid **little attention to the syntax**.

#### Can processing be made more thorough?

**Swets et al. (2008)** argued that readers expecting **detailed** comprehension questions should process more thoroughly than readers expecting superficial ones, so depth depends on goals.

> **Key study, Swets et al. (2008).** Sentences such as "The maid of the princess who scratched herself in public was terribly humiliated" (did the maid or the princess scratch herself?) were read under three conditions: detailed relative-clause questions, superficial questions on every trial, or occasional superficial questions (Figure 10.6). Sentences, especially ambiguous ones, were **read more slowly when detailed questions were expected**. With superficial questions, ambiguous sentences were read **faster** than unambiguous ones (an **ambiguity advantage**), and this advantage **disappeared** when demanding questions were anticipated.

The logic: if you never commit to an attachment, ambiguity costs nothing, because no later word can contradict a commitment you never made.

> **Key study, Dwivedi (2013).** Participants read pairs such as "Every kid climbed that tree. The tree was in the park." Despite knowing they would be questioned on meaning, about **one-third** wrongly said **several trees** were climbed.

So readers and listeners very often process sentences in a shallow, heuristic way and misinterpret them, even when they have reason to be careful.

> **Trap.** **Good-enough** is not **garden-path**. A garden-pathed reader commits to the wrong structure and then revises it, and the garden-path model assumes revision eventually succeeds. A good-enough reader may never compute the full structure and may keep a wrong reading without noticing. The good-enough idea is **Ferreira's** (Ferreira et al., 2002; Ferreira, 2003); the "good enough for the task" formulation is **Swets et al. (2008)**. Moses illusion answer: **none**.

### Cognitive neuroscience: event-related potentials

Because the parsing disputes are about **timing**, researchers have relied on **event-related potentials** (ERPs), which track the brain's response to each word with millisecond precision. The broad result is that **several kinds of semantic information are processed very early**, which fits the constraint-based and unrestricted race models better than the garden-path model (reviews: Kutas et al., 2011; Van Petten & Luka, 2012).

#### The N400

The key component is the **N400**: a **negative** wave with **onset at about 250 ms** and **peak at about 400 ms**. It is **smaller** when a word's meaning matches the meaning built up by the context and **larger** when it does not, so it indexes **semantic processing**.

> **Classic background.** The N400 was discovered by **Kutas and Hillyard (1980)** using semantically anomalous sentence endings (the textbook example is warm bread spread "with socks"). Syntactic violations are classically linked to a later positive wave, the **P600** (Osterhout & Holcomb, 1992). The 7th edition chapter uses only the N400, but papers may expect N400 = meaning, P600 = syntax.

> **Trap.** A **larger** N400 means **worse** semantic fit. The N400 **peaks** at about 400 ms and **begins** at about 250 ms; an option giving 250 ms as the peak is wrong.

#### Prediction

Historically, prediction in language was thought inefficient: natural language seemed too unconstraining for predictions to be right most of the time, and wrong predictions should be costly (the view summarised by Kutas et al., 2011). Cognitive neuroscience has since produced good evidence for **top-down predictive processes** in sentence comprehension (Chapter 9 gives parallel evidence, including DeLong et al.'s 2005 article study).

#### Word meaning and world knowledge together

The traditional view held that word meanings are processed first and **world knowledge** afterwards.

> **Key study, Hagoort et al. (2004).** Dutch participants read sentences whose critical word made them true ("The Dutch trains are **yellow** and very crowded"), false by word meaning ("...are **sour**...") or false by world knowledge ("...are **white**...", since Dutch trains are yellow). The traditional view predicts later detection of the world-knowledge violation. In fact the two violations produced **very similar N400 effects** (Figure 10.7).

Hence word meaning and world knowledge seem to be retrieved and integrated **at the same time**, within about **400 ms**, which suggests (but does not prove) the almost immediate use of all relevant information assumed by the constraint-based theory.

#### Discourse context

The traditional view also held that discourse context is processed after word meanings.

> **Key study, Nieuwland and van Berkum (2006a).** Listeners heard a cartoon-like story in which a woman watches a peanut that dances, smiles and sings about a girl he has met. The story ended with the peanut either "**salted**" (right for real peanuts, wrong for this story) or "**in love**" (right for the story, odd for a real peanut). The **N400 was larger for "salted"**: story context had rapidly overridden the default meaning.

> **Trap.** The larger N400 went to the word that is **normal in the real world** ("salted"), because it violated the **story**. The N400 tracks fit to the **current context**, not real-world plausibility.

#### Inferences about the speaker

**Van den Brink et al. (2012)** argued that listeners also draw inferences about **who is speaking**. "I have a large tattoo on my back" clashes with stereotyped expectations when said in an upper-class accent but not in a working-class one.

> **Key study, van den Brink et al. (2012).** The N400 to "tattoo" was **much larger** in the **upper-class** accent, but **only in women**, attributed to their **greater empathy skills**, which let them process voice-based inferences about the speaker rapidly.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Behavioural measures such as reading time give only indirect evidence about timing; ERPs show clearly that **world knowledge, speaker knowledge and context are used early**, favouring constraint-based theories over the garden-path model. They also suggest that comprehenders anticipate: Van Petten and Luka (2012) concluded that interpretation is continuous and incremental and produces **general expectations** about upcoming meaning.

**Limitations.** (1) A small N400 to a predictable word fits **successful prediction** but also **easy integration**, and the two are hard to separate. (2) Specific predictions should incur costs when wrong, but such costs have proved hard to find, which is why Van Petten and Luka (2012) favoured **general expectations** over specific predictions.

## 10.4 Pragmatics

### What pragmatics adds

**Pragmatics** concerns language as actually used: the meaning a speaker **intends** and a listener **recovers**, rather than the literal meaning, and it often involves inference. Someone who praises the weather after days of non-stop rain is understood to mean that it is dreadful.

The book's formula is **"meaning minus semantics"**. Faced with a sentence in an unfamiliar language, a dictionary plus the grammar would give you the literal meaning; whatever the speaker meant beyond that is pragmatics. Recovering it usually needs context: the speaker's tone, the speaker's behaviour, the surrounding situation.

### Figurative language

Much of pragmatics concerns **figurative language**, language not meant literally:

- **Metaphor**: a word or phrase used to mean something it resembles ("Time is a thief").
- **Irony**: intended meaning far from literal meaning; the book's example is the *Dr Strangelove* line forbidding fighting because the men are in the War Room.
- **Idiom**: a common figurative expression ("kick the bucket").

> **Key study, Bohrn et al. (2012).** A **meta-analysis** comparing figurative and literal processing found (1) **essentially the same brain network** for both and (2) **greater** activation for figurative language in several **inferior frontal gyrus** areas (**BA45/46/47/13**), especially on the **left**.

So figurative language uses no special system but demands more cognitive resources, for example to work out word meanings and integrate them.

> **Trap.** Bohrn et al. (2012) found the **same network** plus extra **left inferior frontal** activation. Older texts stress the right hemisphere for metaphor and humour; an option placing figurative processing in a separate right-hemisphere network is not the 7th edition's finding.

The chapter then takes two topics: **figurative language** (mainly metaphor) and how far listeners adopt the **speaker's perspective** (common ground). First, a box showing why pragmatics matters clinically.

### In the real world: autistic spectrum disorders and pragmatics

The importance of pragmatics can be shown by studying people who are poor at separating literal from intended meaning. **Autistic spectrum disorders** (ASD) involve difficulties in social interaction and communication plus repetitive patterns of behaviour and thinking. People with ASD are very poor at understanding **others' intentions and beliefs**, and they have weak **central coherence** (the ability to integrate all the available information when interpreting an utterance or situation). Both predict severe trouble with figurative language.

There is a confound: most people with ASD have **general learning difficulties**, so all of their language develops slowly. **Asperger syndrome** removes it. This relatively mild ASD involves social-communication problems despite **at least average intelligence** and **no delay in language development**. If such people show impaired pragmatic comprehension alongside normal general comprehension, pragmatic processes must differ from basic comprehension processes, a classic dissociation argument.

**Loukusa and Moilanen (2009)** reviewed the evidence and found impaired pragmatic comprehension on several tasks.

> **Key study, Kaland et al. (2005).** Children and adolescents with Asperger syndrome were poor at drawing appropriate inferences from **jokes, white lies, figurative language, irony and misunderstandings**. In one irony story, a mother cooks her daughter's favourite meal, the daughter watches television without thanking her, and the mother remarks sarcastically on her fine manners. Participants with Asperger syndrome were **less able to explain why the mother said it**, yet were **comparable to controls** on inferences **not requiring social understanding**.

That final control locates the deficit in inferences about minds, not in inference generally.

> **Key study, Zalla et al. (2014).** Participants judged whether a speaker's compliments were **literal or ironic**. The speaker's **occupation** was associated with irony (comedian, chat-show host) or not (accountant, clergyman). **Controls used the occupation** to detect irony; participants with Asperger syndrome **ignored it**, as weak central coherence predicts.

The box concludes that, despite broadly normal language, people with Asperger syndrome have special difficulty inferring others' intentions and motives and so show impaired pragmatic comprehension in social contexts.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition (2015) still treats **Asperger syndrome** as a separate diagnosis, but **DSM-5 (2013)** merged it into **autism spectrum disorder** (as does ICD-11). Older keys follow DSM-IV; newer ones may describe it as ASD without intellectual or language impairment. Keep the two accounts apart: failing to grasp others' **intentions and beliefs** is the theory-of-mind account (classic background: Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, 1985, Sally-Anne task); failing to **integrate all available information** is **weak central coherence** (classic background: Frith, 1989). Zalla et al. (2014) supports the second.

### Figurative language: metaphors

Metaphor processing depends on many factors (Gibbs, 2013), including the listener's language ability, the metaphor's familiarity and the listener's goal (understanding a metaphor versus judging its aptness).

#### The standard pragmatic model

Traditionally metaphors were thought harder than literal statements. The **standard pragmatic model** (e.g., **Grice, 1975**) has three stages: (1) access the **literal** meaning; (2) decide whether it **makes sense** in context; (3) if not, **search for a non-literal meaning**. Figurative meaning is thus a fallback, and metaphors should always be slower than literal statements.

They are not: findings are mixed, but metaphors are often understood **as fast as or faster than** literal statements (Gibbs, 2013).

> **Key study, Glucksberg (2003).** Participants judged whether sentences were **literally true or false**. On the standard model, figurative meaning should never be computed here (the literal meaning is simply false), so metaphors should be as fast as other false sentences. Instead, responses to **metaphors were slower than to literally false sentences**: participants experienced conflict between the metaphor's "true" non-literal meaning and its false literal meaning.

So metaphorical meanings are accessed **fairly automatically**. **Kazmerski et al. (2003)** found this held for **high-IQ** individuals but **not** for low-IQ individuals. (Classic background: this slowing is the **metaphor interference effect**, first reported by Glucksberg, Gildea & Bookin, 1982.)

#### Perceptual grounding

Some theorists (Barsalou, 2012; Gibbs, 2013) argue that sensory experience contributes to understanding metaphors.

> **Key study, Lacey et al. (2012).** Participants read texture metaphors ("Sam had a rough day") and literal equivalents ("Sam had a bad day"). Language and visual areas did **not** differ significantly, but **somatosensory areas involved in texture perception were activated only by the metaphors**, suggesting that metaphor comprehension is **perceptually grounded**.

#### Kintsch's predication model

**Kintsch (2000)** proposed a **predication model** with two components:

1. **Latent semantic analysis**: represents word meanings through their relations with other words.
2. **Construction-integration**: uses those meanings to build interpretations.

In "Lawyers are sharks", the **argument** is "lawyers" and the **predicate** is "sharks". The model **selects predicate features relevant to the argument** (vicious, aggressive) and **inhibits irrelevant ones** (fins, swimming).

**Wolff and Gentner (2011)** agreed that metaphor comprehension involves a **directional** process in which argument and predicate play different roles (the book describes argument information being projected on to the predicate), but argued that it is **preceded by a non-directional process** that finds what the two terms have in common.

> **Trap.** Kintsch's **predication model** of metaphor (2000) is distinct from his **construction-integration model** of discourse (1998; 10.7), although it uses construction-integration as a component. The model with **latent semantic analysis** and an **argument/predicate** distinction is the predication model.

#### Findings

**Non-reversibility** (Chiappe & Chiappe, 2007): "My surgeon is a butcher" (clumsy) means something quite different from "My butcher is a surgeon" (skilful). The predication model explains this because only predicate features relevant to the argument are selected, so swapping the argument changes the selection.

> **Key study, McGlone and Manfredi (2001).** "My lawyer was a shark" took **longer** to understand after a sentence stressing a literal shark property ("Sharks can swim"), because the pre-activated irrelevant feature had to be inhibited.

If metaphor needs inhibition, people high in **working memory capacity** (the ability to process and store information at the same time; 10.5), who inhibit irrelevant information better, should excel.

> **Key study, Chiappe and Chiappe (2007).** High-capacity individuals interpreted metaphors **23% faster** than low-capacity individuals.

> **Key study, Pierce et al. (2010).** When deciding whether metaphors such as "an insult is a razor" are **literally false** (which requires inhibiting their metaphorical truth), high-capacity individuals performed better.

Wolff and Gentner's account predicts that under time pressure the shared non-directional stage should make **forward** ("Some giraffes are skyscrapers") and **reversed** ("Some skyscrapers are giraffes") metaphors equally comprehensible, whereas with more time the directional stage should favour forward metaphors.

> **Key study, Wolff and Gentner (2011).** At a **500 ms** deadline, forward and reversed metaphors **did not differ**; at **1,600 ms**, forward metaphors were **much more comprehensible** (Figure 10.8, which also included literal and scrambled statements).

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Figurative language needs more resources than literal language; there is support for a **non-directional then directional** sequence; high-capacity individuals process metaphors faster partly through better **inhibition**; and **perceptual** involvement (Lacey et al., 2012) is potentially important.

**Limitations.** Differences between **types** of metaphor have been neglected. Gibbs (2013) separates **"A is B" metaphors** ("Lawyers are sharks") from **correlation metaphors** ("My research is off to a great start"), and the predication model fits the former far better.

> **Trap.** The **standard pragmatic model** predicts slower, optional metaphor comprehension; the evidence shows metaphors are often **as fast or faster** (Gibbs, 2013) and **automatic** (Glucksberg, 2003). Numbers: **23%** (Chiappe & Chiappe, 2007); **500 ms** no difference, **1,600 ms** forward advantage (Wolff & Gentner, 2011).

### Common ground

**Grice (1975)** proposed the **cooperativeness principle**: speakers and listeners work together towards mutual understanding. That depends on **common ground**, the knowledge and beliefs speaker and listener share. Listeners expect speakers to refer mostly to common ground and struggle when they do not. This section takes the listener's side (Chapter 11 covers speakers). Common ground is not static: conversation exists partly to **extend** it (Brown-Schmidt & Heller, 2014).

> **Classic background (Chapter 11).** Grice's **four maxims** are **quantity** (as informative as necessary), **quality** (truthful), **relation/relevance** and **manner** (clear). Chapter 11 dates them **Grice (1967)**, this chapter **Grice (1975)**: the lectures were given in 1967 and published in 1975, so it is one theory, not two.

#### Two positions

**Keysar et al. (2000)** argued that computing common ground is effortful, so listeners generally use a fast, effortless **egocentric heuristic**: interpreting what they hear from their **own** knowledge rather than **shared** knowledge, and so treating as possible referents things that only they can see. Because this causes misunderstandings, listeners sometimes follow it with an **effortful** attempt to adopt the speaker's perspective: egocentric first, corrected later.

Others (e.g., **Bezuidenhout, 2013, 2014; Brown-Schmidt & Heller, 2014**) argue that listeners **rarely** use the heuristic and take the speaker's perspective into account **very early**.

#### Findings

> **Key study, Keysar et al. (2000).** A **director** (speaker) and **addressee** (listener) faced a grid in which some slots were closed on the director's side (Figure 10.9). The addressee could see **three candles** of different sizes, the director only **two**. Told to move "the small candle", a listener using common ground should pick the smaller of the two the director could see. Listeners' **initial eye movements were often directed to the candle only they could see**.

But the listener-only candle was a **better fit** to the words than the intended target.

> **Key study, Heller et al. (2008).** With that bias **removed**, participants **rapidly fixated the target** whether or not a listener-only object was present; common ground was used early without difficulty.

> **Key study, Luk et al. (2012).** Chinese-English bilinguals were primed with **Chinese** icons (a Chinese dragon, Confucius) or **American** icons (the American flag, Superman) before Keysar et al.'s task. **45%** of the American-primed participants showed egocentric bias at least once, against only **5%** of the Chinese-primed.

**S. Wu et al. (2013)** found Chinese participants **as egocentric as Americans early** in processing but **quicker to correct** it later. The cultural difference lies in correction.

> **Key study, Lin et al. (2010).** When a listener-only **competitor** resembling the target was present, listeners fixated it **more under a concurrent attention-demanding task** (Figure 10.10). Using common ground draws on attention.

> **Key study, Horton and Slaten (2012).** Pairs of pictures (a cat drinking milk, a cat sitting up) were described by two speakers, each always describing the same picture. Participants formed **speaker-picture associations** and, when a speaker began "the cat that's...", **rapidly fixated** that speaker's picture.

Common ground can therefore arise from cheap **memory associations**, not only from deliberate construction as Keysar et al. assumed.

**Brown-Schmidt (2012)** noted that most studies ask only whether specific items are in common ground, ignoring how **rich** it can be (shared cultural and community knowledge). Pairs who jointly moved game pieces built and maintained rich common-ground representations through discussion.

> **Key study, Savitsky et al. (2011).** Nearly all studies use strangers, and friends might be expected to be **less** egocentric. The opposite was found: friends were **more** egocentric, because they **overestimated** how well they communicate (the authors' **closeness-communication bias**).

#### Evaluation

**Established.** Whether listeners use common ground depends on (1) its **effort and attentional cost**, (2) **culture** (less use in individualistic than collectivistic cultures) and (3) whether simple **long-term memory associations** make it cheap.

**Limitations.** (1) The **richness** of common ground has been neglected. (2) Most studies use **strangers**, which may not generalise to friends (Savitsky et al., 2011). (3) Set-ups are **artificial**: objects in front of two people rarely differ in visibility, and real conversation involves **many turns**, not reactions to single repeated utterances (Bezuidenhout, 2014).

> **Trap.** The **egocentric heuristic** is a **listener** strategy (Keysar et al., 2000) of using **own** rather than **shared** knowledge. **Heller et al. (2008)** removed the original bias; **45% vs 5%** is Luk et al. (2012); **Wu et al. (2013)** put the cultural difference in **later correction**; friends are **more** egocentric (Savitsky et al., 2011).

## 10.5 Individual Differences: Working Memory Capacity

### Why individual differences matter

People differ substantially in almost every complex cognitive activity, so theories that assume everyone comprehends text the same way are unlikely to be right. **Just and Carpenter (1992)** argued that individual differences in **working memory capacity**, the ability to **process and store information at the same time**, shape comprehension. Reading is a natural place for this to show: earlier words and propositions must be held while new ones are processed.

> **Classic background.** Capacity is measured with **complex span** tasks, originally the **reading span** task of **Daneman and Carpenter (1980)**: read a series of sentences (processing) while remembering each one's final word (storage). Reading span predicted comprehension far better than simple word span.

**Engle and Kane (2004)** proposed (Chapter 6) that high-capacity individuals have better **executive attention** (attentional control), seen in better **monitoring of task goals** and better **resolution of response competition**.

> **Trap.** Working memory capacity in this sense is an **individual-difference** measure of **processing plus storage**, interpreted by Engle and Kane as **executive attention**. It is not short-term memory span (storage only, as in digit span) and not a component of Baddeley's working memory model.

### Findings

> **Key study, Daneman and Merikle (1996).** A **meta-analysis** found that working memory capacity correlated about **+0.35** with **global** comprehension measures (e.g., vocabulary) and about **+0.50** with **specific** measures (e.g., making inferences, detecting ambiguity).

Comprehension is therefore moderately strongly linked to capacity, more so for specific skills. Because capacity correlates highly with **intelligence**, **Christopher et al. (2012)** checked and found that capacity **still predicted comprehension with intelligence controlled**. Capacity also predicts processes discussed elsewhere: high-capacity readers draw more **elaborative causal inferences** (**Barreyro et al., 2012**; see 10.6) and form more complete **situation models** (**Dutke & von Hecker, 2011**; see 10.7), and they handle metaphors faster (10.4).

### Explanations

**Better goal maintenance, less mind wandering.** If high capacity means better goal maintenance (Engle & Kane, 2004), high-capacity readers should show less **mind wandering** (task-unrelated thought).

> **Key study, McVay and Kane (2012a).** High-capacity individuals had **better comprehension** and **less mind wandering**, and their comprehension advantage was **partly due to** the reduced mind wandering.

**Unsworth and McMillan (2013)** confirmed this and found that mind wandering also depended on readers' **motivation** and **topic interest**.

**Better discrimination of relevant from irrelevant information.**

> **Key study, Kaakinen et al. (2003).** Reading a text on **rare diseases** that mixed relevant and irrelevant information, **only high-capacity readers** gave extra time to the **relevant** information during the **initial** reading.

> **Key study, Sanchez and Wiley (2006).** The **seductive details effect** is reduced comprehension when a text comes with interesting but **irrelevant** material such as illustrations. **Low-capacity** individuals showed a **larger** effect, partly because (as eye movements showed) they **looked longer at the irrelevant illustrations**.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Strong support for Just and Carpenter's (1992) assumption. High-capacity individuals have **larger vocabularies**, detect **ambiguities** better, draw more **elaborative inferences** and build more effective **situation models**, because they (1) have better **attentional control** and so mind-wander less and (2) better **discriminate relevant from irrelevant** information and **inhibit** the irrelevant.

**Limitations.** (1) Capacity correlates fairly strongly with **intelligence** and **vocabulary**, so it is often unclear whether capacity itself is responsible. (2) High- and low-capacity individuals differ in several ways (Chapter 6), so other, unstudied processes may contribute.

## 10.6 Discourse Processing: Inferences

### From sentences to discourse

Everyday language mostly comes as **discourse**: at least several sentences of text or connected speech. Compared with isolated sentences, discourse is much **less often ambiguous** (context helps) and typically requires **inferences**.

We infer constantly and mostly unawares. In Rumelhart and Ortony's (1977) mini-story, Mary hears the ice-cream van, remembers her pocket money and rushes into the house. Nothing states that she wants an ice-cream, that it costs money, that the money is indoors or that she must hurry before the van arrives, yet every reader understands all of this.

### Three types of inference

| Type | Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Logical** | Word meanings alone | A widow must be female |
| **Bridging** (**backward**) | Links the current text to **earlier** text, making it coherent | "The car" in sentence 2 is the car Ken drove in sentence 1 |
| **Elaborative** | **World knowledge** adds details not in the text | A vase hurled at a wall broke |

**Forward** (**predictive**) inferences, anticipating what happens next, are an important **subtype of elaborative** inference. A major theoretical problem is how the relevant pieces are found in our vast store of world knowledge (Harley, 2013).

The boundary between bridging and elaborative inferences is fuzzy. In a scenario adapted from **Kuperberg et al. (2011)**, Jill has very fair skin, forgets her sunscreen and has sunburn on Monday. After the second sentence a reader can draw the **elaborative** inference that she will burn; after the third, the **bridging** inference that the burn came from the forgotten sunscreen.

> **Trap.** **Bridging = backward** (coherence with earlier text); **elaborative** adds world knowledge, and **forward/predictive** is a kind of elaborative inference. "Widow implies female" is **logical**. **Anaphor resolution** is the simplest **bridging** inference.

### Theoretical perspectives

Logical and bridging inferences are normally drawn because comprehension needs them. The dispute concerns how many **elaborative** inferences are drawn.

**The constructionist approach.** **Bransford et al. (1972)** argued that readers construct a fairly complete **mental model** (an internal representation of a possible situation or event) of what the text describes, so **many elaborative inferences are drawn during reading** even when not needed.

> **Classic background.** **Bransford, Barclay and Franks (1972)**: after hearing "Three turtles rested on a floating log, and a fish swam beneath them", people often falsely recognised "...a fish swam beneath **it**", which describes the same scene. When the turtles had been *beside* the log, the change was readily detected. People remember the situation, not the sentence.

**The minimalist hypothesis.** **McKoon and Ratcliff (1992)** argued that without specific, goal-directed strategies, only **two kinds** of inference are drawn automatically: those making the currently processed parts of the text **locally coherent**, and those based on **quickly and easily available** information. Its assumptions:

1. Inferences are **automatic** or **strategic** (goal-directed).
2. Some automatic inferences create **local coherence** (two or three sentences making sense together or with easily available knowledge), using text held in working memory at the same time.
3. Others use information **readily available because the text states it explicitly**.
4. **Strategic** inferences serve the reader's **goals** and sometimes produce local coherence.
5. **Most elaborative inferences are made at recall, not during reading.**

The main disagreement with constructionism is the **number of automatic inferences**, which minimalists put far lower.

The hypothesis later became a **memory-based framework** (Gerrig & O'Brien, 2005): incoming text acts as a signal to **all of long-term memory**, both inactive parts of the text representation and general knowledge; the signal spreads **autonomously** and without restriction, and the **most active** elements enter the reader's understanding. (Classic background: this is the **resonance** idea of Myers & O'Brien, 1998, which returns in 10.7.)

**Search-after-meaning theory.** **Graesser et al. (1994)** argued that the number of inferences is **flexible**, depending on how far the reader's **goals** involve a **search after meaning**. A proof-reader, attending little to meaning, draws few. (Classic background: the theory also assumes readers seek local and global coherence and try to **explain** events, which favours causal inferences.)

> **Trap.** **Constructionist** = Bransford et al. (1972), many elaborative inferences during reading. **Minimalist** = McKoon and Ratcliff (1992), elaborative inferences mostly **at recall**. **Search after meaning** = Graesser et al. (1994), goal-dependent. Do not confuse Graesser's **search after meaning** with Bartlett's (1932) **effort after meaning**, or Bransford's **constructionist** inference account with Bartlett's **reconstructive** recall.

### Findings: anaphor resolution

An **anaphor** is a word, usually a pronoun, referring back to a previously mentioned noun or noun phrase. In "Fred sold John his lawn mower, and then he sold him his garden hose", a bridging inference identifies "he" as Fred.

- **Gender** helps: "Juliet sold John her lawn mower, and then she sold him her garden hose" is easier. **Arnold et al. (2000)** found that pronoun gender guided looks to the right picture **faster** when it showed a **male and a female** than two same-sex characters.
- **Order of mention** helps. In Harley's (2013) examples, "Vlad sold Dirk his broomstick because he hated it" is easy because "he" is the **first-named** man; "...because he needed it" is harder because "he" is the **second-named**.
- **Working memory capacity**: with **ambiguous** pronouns, high-capacity readers more often considered **both** possible referents (**Nieuwland & van Berkum, 2006b**).
- **Engagement**: unambiguous pronouns were resolved automatically only by readers **highly engaged** with the text (**Love & McKoon, 2011**).

These findings fit **Kaiser et al. (2009)**: anaphor resolution reflects **multiple constraints** (gender, meaning and so on) operating **together and in parallel**, much like the constraint-based model of parsing.

### Findings: causal and predictive inferences

**Causal inferences** are a common bridging inference. **Garrod and Terras (2000)** used pairs like "Ken drove to London yesterday. The car kept overheating." The link could come from "drove" **activating related concepts** such as "car" (context irrelevant), or from a representation of the **whole situation** into which sentence 2 is fitted (context essential).

> **Key study, Garrod and Terras (2000).** Both operate, as **two stages**: **bonding**, a low-level, **automatic** activation of words from the preceding sentence, then **resolution**, which checks the interpretation against the **context**. **Resolution is affected by context; bonding is not.**

Both the minimalist and search-after-meaning theories predict that **goals** shape inference.

> **Key study, Calvo et al. (2006).** Participants told to **anticipate what might happen next** drew **more predictive inferences** than those reading for comprehension, and drew them **faster**.

> **Key study, Murray and Burke (2003).** Readers of **high, moderate and low skill** all showed some evidence of predictive inferences (inferring "break" after an angry husband throws a fragile vase against a wall), but only **high-skill** readers drew them **automatically**.

High-capacity readers also draw more causal inferences (Barreyro et al., 2012).

### Findings: underlying processes

> **Key study, Gras et al. (2012).** After a short text implying a sting (a woman at breakfast waves away bees round the jam pot and one lands on her arm), the word *sting* appeared in blue, red or green at **350, 750 or 1,000 ms**, and participants **named its colour**. Naming was slowed **only at 1,000 ms** (Figure 10.11).

The inference thus took about **one second** to form, and since it interfered with a task that did not need it, it was **automatic**.

> **Key study, Kuperberg et al. (2011).** In ERP recordings, "sunburn" in the simple fair-skin, forgot-sunscreen scenario produced only a **small N400** (the N400 is larger when a word fits its context poorly), so the causal inference began very rapidly. In a **complex** version, where Jill **usually remembered** sunscreen, the N400 was still small but **not as small**: inference processing starts quickly even then.

**Lassonde and O'Brien (2009)** confirmed that readers draw specific predictive inferences when context **strongly** supports them; when context is **weak**, they form a **flexible, general** inference compatible with several outcomes rather than none. And as van den Brink et al. (2012) showed (10.3), listeners rapidly infer what a particular speaker is likely to say.

### Overall evaluation

**Consensus.** (1) Readers routinely draw **bridging** (including causal) inferences. (2) Context and world knowledge are used **rapidly**. (3) Many causal and predictive inferences are **automatic**, depending on factors such as **working memory capacity** and **engagement**. (4) **Goals** determine whether predictive inferences are drawn. (5) **Skilled** and high-capacity readers draw **more** inferences.

Graesser et al. (1997) reconciled the theories: the **minimalist** account probably holds when reading is **very fast**, the text **lacks global coherence** and the reader has **little background knowledge**; the **constructionist** account holds when the reader seeks **enjoyment or mastery** at a **leisurely** pace. Inference drawing is highly flexible.

> **Trap.** The chapter summary concludes that the evidence favours the **minimalist and search-after-meaning** approaches over the **constructionist** one. Older texts that present constructionism as the default are out of date, though Graesser et al. (1997) still give it its own conditions.

**Limitations.** (1) It is hard to predict which inferences will be drawn, because many factors interact and the theories are imprecise (how would one measure whether information is "readily available"?). (2) Measures are limited: the N400 shows only whether a mismatch was detected. (3) The range of inferences may be wider than assumed, extending to inferences about speakers and even authors (the book suggests you would be surprised by cockney slang in a textbook).

## 10.7 Discourse Comprehension: Theoretical Approaches

### Story memory is selective

Describing a book you have read, you give its main events and themes and leave out minor details, reflecting both what you extracted while reading and selective retrieval. Reciting random sentences instead would suggest you had not understood it.

> **Key study, Gomulicki (1956).** One group wrote a **précis** of a story in front of them; another **recalled** it from memory. A third group could barely **tell the précis from the recalls**. Story memory, like a summary, keeps the important information.

What counts as important? **Trabasso and Sperry (1985)** found that statements **causally connected to many others** were judged much more important than unconnected ones.

> **Key study, Margolin et al. (2013).** Since research uses paper texts while readers increasingly use screens, they presented **narrative** (story) and **expository** (factual) texts on **paper, e-reader or computer**. Comprehension was **comparable**, and reading activities (rereading, note-taking) changed only **modestly**, so paper-based findings probably generalise.

> **Trap.** **Narrative** text tells a story; **expository** text informs or explains. The **event-indexing model** is about narrative; the **construction-integration model** was developed mainly for expository text.

Of the many theories (McNamara & Magliano, 2009, review the main ones), the chapter covers schema theory, the construction-integration model, the event-indexing model and event-segmentation theory.

### Schema theory: Bartlett

#### What schemas are

We understand texts by relating them to structured knowledge in long-term memory, much of it held as **schemas**: well-integrated packets of knowledge about the world, events, people and actions (Chapter 7). **Scripts** concern **events** and their consequences; **frames** concern some **aspect of the world**, such as a building. (Classic background: scripts and the **restaurant script** come from **Schank & Abelson, 1977**.)

**Ghosh and Gilboa (2014)** listed four **necessary and sufficient** features:

1. **Associative structure**: interconnected units.
2. **Basis in multiple episodes**: integrated from several similar events.
3. **Lack of unit detail**: because the events vary.
4. **Adaptability**: updated by new information over time.

Definitions vary, and although **Bartlett (1932)** stressed **adaptability**, it is probably the feature **least often** found in recent definitions.

Schemas matter because they supply much of the knowledge needed for understanding and because they generate **expectations** (the usual order of events in a restaurant) that are usually confirmed, making the world predictable.

> **Key study, Bransford and Johnson (1972).** Participants heard a deliberately vague passage of instructions (sort things into piles, go elsewhere if facilities are lacking, do not do too much at once). **Without a title** it was rated incomprehensible and only **2.8 idea units** were recalled. With the title **"Washing clothes" beforehand**, it was easy to understand and **5.8** units were recalled. With the title given **after hearing but before recall**, only **2.6**.

The "title after" group is the key control: if the title were just a retrieval cue, it should have helped then too. It did not, so the schema must be active **during comprehension**.

> **Trap.** **2.8** (no title), **5.8** (before), **2.6** (after). The "after" condition shows a **comprehension** effect, not a **retrieval-cue** effect, which also underlies the sixth limitation of Bartlett's theory below.

#### Bartlett's theory

**Bartlett (1932)** argued that memory for a story depends on both the story and the rememberer's prior schematic knowledge, so comprehension and memory involve **top-down**, schema-triggered processes. His method was to create **conflict** between a story and the reader's knowledge by using stories from an **unfamiliar culture**; prior knowledge should then distort recall towards what is conventional in the reader's own culture.

| Error | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Rationalisation** (most important) | Recall is made more **rational** and consistent with the rememberer's **cultural expectations** |
| **Levelling** | **Unfamiliar details are omitted** |
| **Sharpening** | **Certain details are selected and embellished** |

Schemas could distort **comprehension** or **retrieval**. Bartlett favoured **retrieval**, seeing recall as **reconstructive**, combining story and schema information. Because story information fades quickly but schematic knowledge does not, distortions should **increase over time**.

> **Classic background.** Bartlett called remembering an **"effort after meaning"** and used **repeated reproduction** (one person recalls the story repeatedly) and **serial reproduction** (each recall becomes the next person's story).

#### Findings

Bartlett used **North American Indian** stories, most famously **"The War of the Ghosts"**, but his studies were **poorly controlled** (Roediger, 2010); in particular, he deliberately gave no specific instructions. Many of his distortions were therefore **conscious guesses**.

> **Key study, Gauld and Stephenson (1967).** With "The War of the Ghosts", instructions stressing **accurate** recall **eliminated almost half** of the errors found with Bartlett's instructions.

Bartlett's claim that distortions grow with repeated recall has **often failed to replicate** (Roediger, 2010), with an exception.

> **Key study, Bergman and Roediger (1999).** Recall of "The War of the Ghosts" at **15 minutes, one week and six months** showed **major distortions** rising from **27%** to **59%** (Figure 10.12 scores the story's **42 propositions** and includes control groups with no initial recall).

> **Key study, Sulin and Dooling (1974).** A story about a ruthless dictator named **Gerald Martin** or **Adolf Hitler** was followed by recognition testing that included the unpresented sentence "He hated the Jews particularly and so persecuted them". The Hitler group **falsely recognised** it at the **long** but **not** the short interval: schematic distortion grew over time.

> **Key study, Anderson and Pichert (1978).** Participants read a story from a **burglar's** or a **homebuyer's** perspective, recalled it, then recalled it again from the **other** perspective, producing **more information important only to the new perspective**.

The story had not changed, only the schema guiding the search, so this supports **schema-driven retrieval**. But schemas also affect **comprehension** (Bransford & Johnson, 1972).

> **Key study, Brewer and Treyens (1981).** Rejecting artificial texts learned deliberately, they tested **incidental** memory. Participants spent **35 seconds** in a room set up as a **graduate student's office** (Figure 10.13), then were unexpectedly tested elsewhere. They recalled more **schema-consistent** objects (desk, calendar, pencils) than **schema-inconsistent** ones (such as a skull), and objects **absent** but "recognised" with **high confidence** were nearly all schema-consistent (books, filing cabinet).

Eyewitness memory shows the same pattern (Chapter 8): a **bank-robbery schema** (robbers typically male and disguised) adds schema-consistent details that never happened (**Tuckey & Brewer, 2003a, b**).

**Steyvers and Hemmer (2012)** argued that such research **exaggerates** schemas' harm: a real graduate office probably does contain books and a filing cabinet, and Brewer and Treyens had deliberately left them out.

> **Key study, Steyvers and Hemmer (2012).** With naturalistic scenes (a kitchen, with a stove as highly consistent and a small wooden sailing boat as highly inconsistent), **false recall** was **lower for high-consistency objects (9%)** than **low-consistency objects (18%)**, partly because guesses about consistent objects were usually right. In a second experiment (Figure 10.14: high consistency meant appearing in all **five** scenes of a type, low in only one), **very consistent** objects were **best recalled** and **very inconsistent** objects were also **reasonably well recalled**, because their unexpectedness drew attention.

Schemas can thus be very **beneficial** for real-world memory.

> **Trap.** In Brewer and Treyens' contrived office, false memories were mainly **schema-consistent**; in Steyvers and Hemmer's natural scenes, false recall was **lower** for consistent objects (**9% vs 18%**). The difference is in the materials.

#### Neuroscience

**Van Kesteren et al. (2012)** explained good memory for both kinds of information: **schema-inconsistent** information is processed thoroughly by the **medial temporal lobe**, while **schema-consistent** information is processed by the **medial prefrontal cortex**, which helps store direct links with existing knowledge. **Ghosh and Gilboa (2014)** reviewed studies linking schema processing to the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex**, and patients with **prefrontal damage** have particular trouble with schematic information (e.g., **Cosentino et al., 2006; Farag et al., 2010**).

> **Trap.** Inconsistent → **medial temporal lobe**; consistent → **medial prefrontal cortex**. Swapping them is the usual distractor.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Schematic knowledge aids comprehension and recall, and many memory distortions (very common in the laboratory) reflect it. The theory emphasises **top-down** processing (Wagoner, 2013, gives a history), and evidence increasingly links schema processing to the **prefrontal cortex**.

**Limitations.**

1. **Definition**: theorists disagree about what a schema is (Ghosh & Gilboa, 2014), and its contents are hard to pin down.
2. **Testability**: explanations need **independent** evidence that the relevant schemas exist and were activated, which is rarely available; Harley (2013) noted the charge that such accounts are "nothing more than re-descriptions of the data."
3. **When schemas activate** is unclear: the theory implies many top-down inferences, yet (10.6) they are often not drawn.
4. **How schemas activate** is unclear: "the five-hour journey from London to New York" probably activates a **plane-flight script**, though none of its words is strongly linked to flying (Harley, 2013).
5. **Exaggerated error**: everyday memory is more accurate than the theory implies (Steyvers & Hemmer, 2012); **Wynn and Logie (1998)** found students' recall of their **first week at university** generally **accurate up to six months** later.
6. **Retrieval vs comprehension**: Bartlett located schema effects at **retrieval**, but they often operate at **comprehension** (Bransford & Johnson, 1972).

### Kintsch's construction-integration model

**Kintsch's (e.g., 1998) construction-integration model** combines elements of **schema theories** with **Johnson-Laird's mental models** (Chapter 14).

#### Assumptions

1. Sentences are turned into **propositions**: statements asserting or denying something, which can be true or false.
2. These are **stored briefly** with **associatively related propositions** (such as inferences), including **many irrelevant** ones.
3. **Spreading activation** then selects propositions for the text representation. In this **integration** process, **clusters of highly interconnected propositions** attract most activation and are most likely to be kept. In the final representation, propositions from the text and from inferences are **hard to tell apart**.
4. The result is **three levels** of representation:

| Level | Content | Depends mainly on |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface representation** | The text itself | Construction |
| **Propositional representation (textbase)** | Propositions formed from the text | Construction |
| **Situation representation** | A **mental model** of the situation described | **Integration** (the only level mostly dependent on it) |

Figure 10.15 (adapted from Kintsch, 1992) shows the model. As a flow: **text** → **construction** (a fast, indiscriminate, **bottom-up** generation of text propositions plus associated knowledge, **ignoring** the text's overall theme) → **integration** (spreading activation strengthens connected clusters and lets irrelevant propositions fade; **context** and **top-down** factors act here) → the three-level **text representation**. Construction is cheap and sloppy; integration cleans up.

This differs sharply from schema theory's top-down emphasis. In Kaakinen and Hyönä's (2007) summary, construction is a "dumb" **bottom-up** process in the reader's knowledge base, and top-down factors such as perspective or goals act only at **integration**.

> **Classic background.** The model grew out of **Kintsch and van Dijk (1978)**, in which a text is processed in cycles of **micropropositions** in a limited-capacity buffer and condensed into **macropropositions** (the gist). Older textbooks sometimes present that model under Kintsch's name.

#### Findings

Since inferences enter the representation alongside text propositions, readers should confuse what they read with what they inferred, as often found (e.g., Sulin & Dooling, 1974).

> **Key study, Kintsch et al. (1990).** After reading brief situation descriptions, participants were tested on recognition immediately or up to **four days** later. The three levels had **distinct forgetting functions** (Figure 10.16): the **surface** representation was forgotten **rapidly**, the **situation** representation showed **no forgetting**. The most complete representation was remembered best, the least complete worst.

> **Key study, Dixon and Bortolussi (2013).** Readers of an interesting text (*Interview with the Vampire*) and a duller one (*The History of Pendennis*) rated their **engagement** or their **on-task attention**; recall then assessed their **situation model**. Engagement ratings, reflecting integration fairly directly, predicted recall strongly; attention ratings, mixing construction and integration, predicted it less well, **exactly as predicted**, supporting the construction/integration distinction.

> **Key study, Mulder and Sanders (2012).** **Causal relations** appeared to be represented in the **situation** representation but **not** in the textbase or surface representation, so readers do not always form all three levels.

The model says goals act at **integration**, not construction. Imagine reading about four rare diseases knowing a close friend has one of them and that you must explain it to mutual friends.

> **Key study, Kaakinen and Hyönä (2007).** In such a task, readers spent **most of their time on relevant sentences** (about the friend's disease) and little on irrelevant ones: goals influenced **construction**, contrary to the model.

The model also says text is linked to **world knowledge** before **discourse context**.

> **Key study, Cook and Myers (2004).** A passage described a low-budget film being shot in the Sahara by a tiny crew who all took on extra jobs; then "Action!" was called by **the actress**. World knowledge says directors call "Action!", so readers should have dwelt on "actress", but the context explains the oddity. The word was **not fixated for long**: context was used **before** general world knowledge, as in Nieuwland and van Berkum's (2006a) peanut study.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** The model specifies in **more detail** than earlier theories how text and knowledge combine, and spreading-activation selection is plausible. There is reasonable evidence for the **three levels** (Kintsch et al., 1990), some support for the **construction/integration** distinction (Dixon & Bortolussi, 2013), and the prediction that readers confuse text with inference is correct.

**Limitations.** Most broadly, construction is unlikely always to be inefficient and bottom-up.

1. **Construction is not purely bottom-up**: goals direct attention very early (Kaakinen & Hyönä, 2007).
2. **Context is not held back until integration**: this has been disproved (Cook & Myers, 2004; Nieuwland & van Berkum, 2006a).
3. **Neglected factors**: readers' **goals**, **emotional responses** and **imagery** (McNamara & Magliano, 2009).
4. **Missing levels**: **Graesser et al. (1997)** added the **text genre** level (narrative, description, jokes, exposition) and the **communication** level (how the writer addresses readers, for example as an invisible storyteller).
5. **Which inferences** are drawn is not specified.
6. **Situation-model construction** is underspecified, the gap the event-indexing model addresses.

> **Trap.** Schema theory is **top-down throughout**; construction-integration is **bottom-up at construction, top-down at integration**, and the 7th edition's main criticism is that top-down influences act **earlier**. Kintsch et al. (1990): the **situation model** survives four days, the **surface** form is lost fastest. Graesser et al.'s two missing levels: **genre** and **communication**.

### Event-indexing model and event-segmentation theory

Radvansky and Zacks (2011) argued that events are fundamental units of experience. Two theories build on this: the **event-indexing model** (**Zwaan et al., 1995**) and **event-segmentation theory** (**Zacks et al., 2007**), which extends it. The event-indexing model concerns **narrative** texts, unlike the construction-integration model's focus on **expository** texts, but both centre on **situation models**.

#### The event-indexing model

Its basic assumption (in McNamara and Magliano's 2009 phrasing) is that cognition is more attuned to **dynamic events** (changes of state) than to static information. Readers monitor **five indexes** to decide whether to update their situation model:

| Index | What is tracked |
|---|---|
| **Protagonist** | The central character in the current versus the previous event |
| **Temporality** | How the times of the current and previous events relate |
| **Causality** | The causal relation of the current event to the previous one |
| **Spatiality** | How the current setting relates to a previous one |
| **Intentionality** | How the character's goals relate to the current event |

A **discontinuity** on any index (a change of setting, a flashback) costs **more processing effort**, and since indexes are monitored **independently**, two simultaneous changes should cost more than one.

> **Trap.** The five indexes are **protagonist, temporality, causality, spatiality, intentionality** ("who, when, why, where, what for"). "Object" belongs to Kurby and Zacks' coding scheme, not the model.

**Zwaan and Madden (2004)** contrasted two views of **outdated** information after updating: the **here-and-now view** (current information is more available; **part of the event-indexing model**) and the **resonance view** (new text resonates with all related information in memory, so outdated information can still interfere).

#### Event-segmentation theory

**Zacks et al. (2007)** distinguished **incremental updating** of single dimensions ("**brick by brick**", the event-indexing emphasis) from **global updating**, replacing the whole model ("**from scratch**", the event-segmentation emphasis). We constantly predict the near future; prediction gets harder near the **boundary** between events, which can trigger a new model.

> **Key study, Zacks et al. (2011).** Observers watching films of everyday activities (washing a car) were worse at predicting what would happen **five seconds later** when the film stopped **near an event boundary** than mid-event.

> **Trap.** **Incremental** updating and the **here-and-now** view go with the **event-indexing model** (Zwaan et al., 1995); **global** updating at **event boundaries** goes with **event-segmentation theory** (Zacks et al., 2007); **resonance** is the rival to here-and-now.

#### Findings

> **Key study, Rinck and Weber (2003).** Reading speed fell by **35%** when **one** index changed compared with none.

> **Key study, Curiel and Radvansky (2014).** Updating took longer when **both spatial and protagonist** information changed than when one did, consistent with **independent** indexes.

Readers generally update **intentionality, time and protagonist** but are **less likely to update space** (**Smith & O'Brien, 2012**), unless cues make earlier spatial information more accessible.

> **Key study, McNerney et al. (2011).** Reading a whole novel, ***The Stone Diaries*** by Carol Shields, readers slowed significantly when **protagonist** or **causality** information needed updating, as with short texts, but surprisingly **sped up** for **spatial** and **temporal** updates, perhaps because deep engagement made those easier.

> **Key study, O'Brien et al. (2010).** A passage first said a tree would **not** be cut down, then had lightning force its removal, and finally mentioned that only the stump remained. The **outdated** information disrupted comprehension of the final sentence, as the **resonance view** predicts.

> **Key study, Kendeou et al. (2013).** Mary had been a **vegetarian for ten years** and later ordered a cheeseburger and fries. Readers told **why** (her doctor advised meat because she lacked vitamins) showed **no interference**: a **causal explanation** for the update **eliminates** it.

> **Key study, Ezzyat and Davachi (2011).** In narratives with boundaries marked "**a while later**", memory for information **across** boundaries was **poorer** than **within** an event, evidence for event segmentation.

> **Key study, Kurby and Zacks (2012).** Readers reported their thoughts while reading a long narrative. **Incremental** updating appeared as more mentions of **character, object, space, time and goal** when that aspect changed; **global** updating appeared as more mentions of **character, cause, goal and time** at **event boundaries** (Figure 10.17). Both approaches gain support.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** The approach identifies aspects of situation models that other theories ignore, covers both **incremental** and **global** updating within and between events, and has identified factors (such as causal explanation) that decide whether outdated information disrupts reading.

**Limitations.** (1) It fits **narrative** texts and has little to say about **expository** ones. (2) It works better for **simple** texts: most readers **failed** to form a situation model of a complex murder-scene account (**Zwaan & van Oostendorp, 1993**). (3) It neglects readers' **goals**, **reading skill** (McNamara & Magliano, 2009) and the **motivation** needed to monitor five dimensions. (4) Research mostly uses **short** texts, and long texts may differ (McNerney et al., 2011).

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all seven modules.

1. **The timing question gets one answer at every level.** Serial theories said non-syntactic or top-down information waits its turn, and each was undermined: the garden-path model by Trueswell et al. (1994), Spivey et al. (2002), verb bias and the ERP studies; the standard pragmatic model by Glucksberg (2003); the construction-integration model by Kaakinen and Hyönä (2007) and Cook and Myers (2004). Information arrives earlier than serial accounts allowed.
2. **The thoroughness question also has one answer: it depends on goals.** Good-enough parsing (Ferreira, 2003; Swets et al., 2008), the minimalist and search-after-meaning accounts of inference, Graesser et al.'s (1997) reconciliation, engagement effects (Love & McKoon, 2011; Dixon & Bortolussi, 2013) and anticipation instructions (Calvo et al., 2006) all say comprehension is as deep as the task and the reader's goals make it.
3. **Parallel constraints recur.** The constraint-based model, Kaiser et al.'s (2009) account of anaphor resolution, the memory-based (resonance) framework and the spreading activation of construction-integration all treat comprehension as many cues combining at once.
4. **Prediction is everywhere, but general rather than specific.** Prosody used before disambiguation (Nakamura et al., 2012), visual context helping because it allows prediction (Ferreira et al., 2013), N400 evidence (Van Petten & Luka, 2012), predictive inferences (Gras et al., 2012; Lassonde & O'Brien, 2009) and event boundaries as prediction failures (Zacks et al., 2011) fit one picture.
5. **Working memory capacity explains individual differences at every level**: metaphor (Chiappe & Chiappe, 2007; Pierce et al., 2010), ambiguous pronouns (Nieuwland & van Berkum, 2006b), elaborative inferences (Barreyro et al., 2012) and situation models (Dutke & von Hecker, 2011), via attentional control and filtering. Its confound with intelligence is the standing caveat.
6. **Dissociations show separable parts.** Semantic dementia with spared syntax (DM; Breedin & Saffran, 1999), complex-syntax deficits after left inferior and middle frontal damage (Wilson et al., 2012), and Asperger syndrome with normal language but impaired pragmatics (Kaland et al., 2005; Zalla et al., 2014) are the chapter's clinical hooks.

## Worked example

*A 26-year-old engineering graduate is referred to a clinical psychology outpatient department by his company's counsellor. His technical work is excellent and his written reports are grammatically flawless. The problems are social. When his manager, noticing he had barely started his share of a team task, said "Don't overdo it", he earnestly assured her he was not working too hard. He laughs at none of a colleague's jokes, and he thanked a team-mate who sarcastically called his missed deadline "brilliant timing". In meetings he refers to "the file" or "the issue" without realising that colleagues do not know which one he means. His language milestones were normal and his IQ is above average. His mother asks whether he has "a language problem".*

**Is basic comprehension impaired?** No. Flawless grammar, normal milestones and above-average IQ mean his **syntactic** and **semantic** processing are intact. What fails is the step from literal to **intended** meaning, **pragmatics**. "Don't overdo it" to someone who has hardly started is the book's lazy co-worker jibe, and "brilliant timing" for a missed deadline is plain **irony**; he takes both literally.

**Which profile fits?** The "In the real world" box: **Asperger syndrome** (in DSM-5 terms, ASD without intellectual or language impairment). Because his general language is normal, his pragmatic difficulty cannot be blamed on slow language development, which is the dissociation showing that pragmatic comprehension uses distinct processes.

**Which mechanisms?** Name two. First, difficulty grasping others' **intentions and beliefs**: like Kaland et al.'s (2005) participants, he cannot explain why someone would say the opposite of what they mean, though he copes with non-social inference (his technical reasoning is fine). Second, **weak central coherence**: like Zalla et al.'s (2014) participants, who ignored a speaker's sarcastic occupation, he ignores the context (the missed deadline) that makes "brilliant" ironic.

**And "the file"?** A **common-ground** failure on the speaker side, the production counterpart of Keysar et al.'s (2000) **egocentric heuristic**: he refers from his own perspective rather than from what is shared (Chapter 11 covers speakers).

**How would you test it with the chapter's paradigms?** A **literal-truth judgement task** with metaphors (Glucksberg, 2003): typical adults are *slowed* by metaphors because figurative meaning intrudes automatically. An irony task with **speaker-occupation cues** (Zalla et al., 2014) for contextual integration. A **director task** (Keysar et al., 2000, with Heller et al.'s 2008 control for the better-fit bias) for perspective-taking.

**Differential diagnosis, in the chapter's terms.** **Semantic dementia** (anterior temporal lobe) is the reverse pattern: lost word meanings, spared syntax (DM), and it would not present this way in a young adult with intact vocabulary. **Left inferior and middle frontal damage** impairs complex syntax (Wilson et al., 2012), which he handles perfectly. **Low working memory capacity** would show as weak inferences, poor situation models, mind wandering and seductive-details effects across all texts, not selectively in social ones.

**What do you tell his mother?** That grammar and vocabulary are intact, so it is not a language problem in the usual sense; the difficulty lies in working out what people *mean* rather than what they *say*, especially when that depends on reading intentions or combining contextual cues. Intervention would target social inference and perspective-taking.

## MCQ traps

1. **Pragmatics = "meaning minus semantics"**; syntax = word-order rules; grammar adds parts of speech and punctuation.
2. "Kids make nutritious snacks" is **global** ambiguity; garden-path sentences are **local**. Some ambiguity is **desirable** (Piantadosi et al., 2012).
3. Commas give speech-like ERPs (**Steinhauer & Friederici, 2001**) and longer fixations on "John" (**Hirotani et al., 2006**); monotone speech is hard (**Duffy & Pisoni, 1992**).
4. **Frazier et al. (2006):** the **relative size** of boundaries decides attachment; colonel-on-balcony most likely when boundary 1 > boundary 2.
5. **Garden-path model = Frazier & Rayner (1982)**: serial, syntax-only first pass. "The horse raced past the barn fell": Bever (1970), background.
6. **Minimal attachment** = fewest **nodes**; **late closure** = attach to the **current** phrase; in conflict, **minimal attachment** wins.
7. Girl/answer sentences test **minimal attachment**; Jay/jogs and sneezed/doctor (van Gompel & Pickering, 2001) test **late closure**.
8. **DM (Breedin & Saffran, 1999):** **semantic dementia**, anterior temporal lobe, **normal** grammaticality judgements; complex-syntax deficits = **left inferior and middle frontal gyri** (Wilson et al., 2012).
9. **Trueswell et al. (1994):** the **"defendant"** (animate) sentence was **harder**.
10. **Pauker et al. (2012):** **87%** vs **28%** acceptance (congruent vs misleading pause).
11. **Spivey et al. (2002):** two-apple display removes the garden path; **Ferreira et al. (2013):** only if the display **precedes** the instruction.
12. **Early closure** preferred in **Spanish, Dutch, French** (Harley, 2013).
13. **Constraint-based model = MacDonald et al. (1994)**: connectionist, **parallel**; signature constraint **verb bias** (Wilson & Garnsey, 2009).
14. **Cai et al. (2012):** an analysis **not adopted** still disrupted reading, so analyses run in **parallel**.
15. **Brysbaert & Mitchell (1996):** individual differences in **Dutch** parsing favour the constraint-based model.
16. **Unrestricted race model = van Gompel et al. (2000)**: all information sources, **one** structure, **extensive reanalysis**.
17. **Van Gompel et al. (2001):** **ambiguous** sentences **fastest**; this contradicts the constraint-based prediction.
18. **Mohamed & Clifton (2011):** slowing only in the **disambiguating** region, fitting the race model.
19. **Good-enough** (Ferreira et al., 2002; Ferreira, 2003): mouse eaten by cheese via the **NVN** heuristic; **Moses illusion** answer is **none**.
20. **Christianson et al. (2010):** implausible passives gave **no passive priming**.
21. **Swets et al. (2008):** the **ambiguity advantage** vanishes with detailed questions; **Dwivedi (2013):** about **one-third** said several trees.
22. **N400:** negative, onset **~250 ms**, peak **~400 ms**, **larger** for poor fit. P600 = syntax (background).
23. **Hagoort et al. (2004):** "white" (world knowledge) and "sour" (word meaning) gave **similar** N400s.
24. **Nieuwland & van Berkum (2006a):** **"salted"** gave the **larger** N400 (violated the story).
25. **Van den Brink et al. (2012):** tattoo + upper-class accent, larger N400, **women only**.
26. **Bohrn et al. (2012):** **same network** plus extra **left IFG** (BA45/46/47/13) for figurative language.
27. **Asperger syndrome** (book): at least average IQ, **no language delay**; merged into ASD in **DSM-5 (2013)**.
28. **Kaland et al. (2005):** impaired social inferences, **normal** non-social ones; **Zalla et al. (2014):** occupation cue **ignored**.
29. **Standard pragmatic model (Grice, 1975)**: literal first. Refuted: metaphors often as fast or faster (Gibbs, 2013); **Glucksberg (2003)**: metaphors **slower** than literally false sentences, so access is **automatic**.
30. **Kazmerski et al. (2003):** automatic access in **high-IQ** only; **Lacey et al. (2012):** texture metaphors activate **somatosensory** cortex.
31. **Predication model (Kintsch, 2000):** **LSA** + construction-integration; explains **non-reversibility** (surgeon/butcher).
32. **Chiappe & Chiappe (2007):** high WMC **23%** faster; **McGlone & Manfredi (2001):** "Sharks can swim" slows the metaphor.
33. **Wolff & Gentner (2011):** no forward/reversed difference at **500 ms**, forward advantage at **1,600 ms**.
34. **Egocentric heuristic** (Keysar et al., 2000) is a **listener** strategy; **Heller et al. (2008)** removed its bias.
35. **Luk et al. (2012):** **45%** (American prime) vs **5%** (Chinese prime); **Wu et al. (2013):** difference in **later correction**.
36. **Savitsky et al. (2011):** friends are **more** egocentric than strangers.
37. **Daneman & Merikle (1996):** **+0.35** global, **+0.50** specific.
38. **Bridging = backward**, **predictive = forward** (a kind of elaborative); widow → female is **logical**.
39. **Minimalist (McKoon & Ratcliff, 1992):** elaborative inferences mostly **at recall**; **search after meaning** = Graesser et al. (1994), not Bartlett's "effort after meaning".
40. **Garrod & Terras (2000):** **bonding** (automatic, context-free) **then resolution** (context-sensitive).
41. **Gras et al. (2012):** slowing **only at 1,000 ms**.
42. **Bransford & Johnson (1972):** **2.8 / 5.8 / 2.6** idea units.
43. **Bartlett:** **rationalisation** (most important), **levelling**, **sharpening**; **Gauld & Stephenson (1967):** almost **half** the errors removed.
44. **Bergman & Roediger (1999):** **27% → 59%** major distortions; **Sulin & Dooling (1974):** Hitler effect at the **long** interval only.
45. **Brewer & Treyens (1981):** **35 s**; **Steyvers & Hemmer (2012):** **9% vs 18%** false recall.
46. **Van Kesteren et al. (2012):** inconsistent → **MTL**, consistent → **mPFC**.
47. **Kintsch et al. (1990):** over **four days**, surface forgotten fastest, **situation model** not forgotten.
48. **Event-indexing (Zwaan et al., 1995):** **five** indexes, here-and-now view; **Rinck & Weber (2003):** **35%** slower; **space** least updated (Smith & O'Brien, 2012).
49. **Resonance:** outdated tree information interferes (O'Brien et al., 2010); a **causal explanation** removes interference (Kendeou et al., 2013).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Syntax** | Rules governing the order of words in a language's sentences |
| **Grammar** | Rules on acceptable word orders plus parts of speech; broader than syntax |
| **Parsing** | Working out a sentence's syntactic or grammatical structure |
| **Inflection** | Change to a noun or verb marking a grammatical feature (*-s* plural, *-ed* past) |
| **Global / local ambiguity** | Whole sentence has two readings / two readings possible only partway through |
| **Prosodic cues** | Stress, intonation, rhythm and duration marking phrase structure in speech |
| **Garden-path model** | Frazier and Rayner's (1982) serial model: syntax-only first parse, later revision |
| **Minimal attachment** | Prefer the structure with the fewest nodes |
| **Late closure** | Attach each new word to the phrase or clause currently being processed |
| **Visual world paradigm** | Tracking eye movements to displayed objects while speech is heard |
| **Constraint-based model** | MacDonald et al.'s (1994) connectionist model: all information at once, competing analyses in parallel |
| **Verb bias** | A verb's tendency to occur more often in one of its permitted structures |
| **Unrestricted race model** | Van Gompel et al.'s (2000) model: all information selects one structure, extensive reanalysis if it fails |
| **Ambiguity advantage** | Faster reading of ambiguous than disambiguated sentences |
| **Good-enough representation** | Interpretation adequate for the task but possibly incomplete or wrong |
| **NVN strategy** | Heuristic taking the first noun as agent, the second as recipient or theme |
| **Moses illusion** | Failing to notice the wrong name in a question about a familiar story |
| **N400** | Negative ERP wave (onset ~250 ms, peak ~400 ms), larger for poor semantic fit |
| **Pragmatics** | Study of language as used, especially intended meaning; meaning minus semantics |
| **Figurative language** | Language not meant literally: metaphor, irony, idiom |
| **Autistic spectrum disorders** | Disorders of social interaction and communication with repetitive behaviour and thinking |
| **Central coherence** | Ability to integrate all available information when interpreting an utterance or situation |
| **Asperger syndrome** | ASD with social-communication problems, at least average intelligence and no language delay |
| **Standard pragmatic model** | Grice's (1975) sequence: literal meaning, context check, then search for non-literal meaning |
| **Predication model** | Kintsch's (2000) metaphor model: LSA plus construction-integration, selecting predicate features relevant to the argument |
| **Argument / predicate** | A metaphor's topic ("lawyers") / what is said of it ("sharks") |
| **Cooperativeness principle** | Grice's principle that speakers and listeners work towards mutual understanding |
| **Common ground** | Knowledge and beliefs shared by speaker and listener, easing communication |
| **Egocentric heuristic** | Listener strategy of interpreting from one's own rather than shared knowledge |
| **Working memory capacity** | Ability to process and store information at the same time |
| **Seductive details effect** | Poorer comprehension when a text carries interesting but irrelevant material |
| **Discourse** | Language at least several sentences long, written or spoken |
| **Logical inference** | Inference from word meanings alone |
| **Bridging inferences** | Inferences linking current to earlier text for coherence; backward inferences |
| **Elaborative inferences** | Inferences adding world-knowledge details to the text |
| **Predictive inference** | Forward elaborative inference about what happens next |
| **Mental model** | Internal representation of a possible situation or event |
| **Constructionist approach** | Bransford's view: a full model of the situation, many elaborative inferences while reading |
| **Minimalist hypothesis** | McKoon and Ratcliff's (1992) view: automatic inferences only for local coherence and readily available information |
| **Search-after-meaning theory** | Graesser et al.'s (1994) view: inference number depends on the reader's meaning-seeking goals |
| **Anaphor** | Word, usually a pronoun, referring back to an earlier noun or noun phrase |
| **Bonding / resolution** | Garrod and Terras' automatic context-free first stage / context-sensitive second stage of bridging |
| **Schema / script / frame** | Integrated knowledge packet / schema for event sequences / schema for an aspect of the world |
| **Rationalisation** | Bartlett's error of recall distorted to fit cultural expectations |
| **Levelling / sharpening** | Omitting unfamiliar details / selecting and embellishing details |
| **Proposition** | Statement asserting or denying something, true or false |
| **Construction-integration model** | Kintsch's (1998) model: bottom-up construction, spreading-activation integration, three levels |
| **Surface / textbase / situation model** | Exact wording / propositions from the text / mental model of the situation described |
| **Event-indexing model** | Zwaan et al.'s (1995) model: readers monitor five situational indexes |
| **Here-and-now / resonance view** | Current information more available / all related information reactivated, so outdated information interferes |
| **Event-segmentation theory** | Zacks et al.'s (2007) theory: experience divided into events, global updating at boundaries |
