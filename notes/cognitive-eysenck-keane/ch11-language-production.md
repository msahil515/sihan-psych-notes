# Chapter 11: Language Production

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapters 9 and 10 were about taking language in. This chapter turns the arrow round: you have a thought, and a second later a listener hears a grammatical string of sounds, or a reader sees a correctly spelled string of letters. Speaking feels effortless, but in between lies a chain of operations: choosing a message, building a grammatical frame, picking words to fill it, assembling morphemes, and retrieving and ordering sounds or letters. Eysenck and Keane organise most of the chapter around one argument about that chain. Is it a strict **assembly line**, each stage finishing before handing over to the next (Levelt's **WEAVER++**)? Or an **interactive network** in which all levels are active at once and influence each other (Dell's **spreading-activation theory**)? The answer the chapter reaches is "limited interaction": more than Levelt allowed, less than a free-for-all.

The evidence comes through three windows: **speech errors**, which show the system's structure through the way it breaks; **laboratory timing studies** (mostly picture naming), which show the order and speed of stages when it works; and **brain-damaged patients** (aphasia, dysgraphia), which show which components can be lost independently. The second half widens the lens to speech as a social act (Grice, audience design, gesture, prosody, discourse markers), to writing as a working-memory-hungry skill, and to spelling, where a two-route model and its two dysgraphias give clinical psychology a clean double dissociation.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 11.1 compares speaking with writing and sets out the four levels of speech production that every later section refers to. Sections 11.2 and 11.3 collect the raw data on planning and errors. Section 11.4 is the theoretical core, Dell against Levelt; most of what follows is phrased in terms of that contrast. Section 11.5 takes the same questions into the clinic (aphasia, anomia, agrammatism, jargon aphasia). Section 11.6 treats speech as communication, and 11.7 and 11.8 cover writing and spelling. Two "In the real world" boxes show Alzheimer's disease appearing in speech and in writing years before diagnosis.

## 11.1 Introduction

### Why production is harder to study than comprehension

In a comprehension experiment the researcher chooses the input; in a production experiment the participant chooses the output, and that is much harder to constrain. Production is also more than a linguistic activity. People speak and write to *do* things (inform, be friendly, persuade), so a full account needs motivational and social factors as well as grammar and words. The chapter covers **speech** and **writing** and how brain damage affects each. More is known about speech, which matters more in daily life because almost everyone talks far more than they write.

### Similarities between speaking and writing

Both communicate information about people and the world, and both draw on the **same knowledge base**. Theories of both assume an initial stage at which the producer settles the *overall meaning* before choosing any words (Dell et al., 1997, for speech; Hayes & Flower, 1986, for writing), followed by production that often runs **clause by clause**.

- **Hartley et al. (2003)** studied **Eric Sotto**, who produced academic letters either by word processing or with a voice-recognition system. Despite far less practice at dictating, his letters were comparable in **readability** and **grammatical errors** across the two modes.
- **Gould (1978)** found that even practised dictators rarely dictated more than **35% faster** than they wrote, although people can *speak* **five or six times** faster than they write.
- **Gould (1980)** videotaped people composing letters and found that **planning took two-thirds of the total time** in both modes. When most of the time goes on deciding what to say, a faster output channel saves little.

> **Trap.** Do not reason that dictation should be five or six times faster than writing because speech is. Gould's point is that the shared planning stage, about two-thirds of the time, is the bottleneck, so dictation gains at most about 35%.

### Differences between speaking and writing

**Cleland and Pickering (2006)** note that written language uses longer and more complex constructions, longer words and a larger vocabulary, and more signposting words (*but*, *on the other hand*). The signposts partly compensate for the absence of **prosody** (rhythm, stress, intonation), which does structural work in speech (11.6). Four specific differences:

1. **Speakers usually know exactly who their listener is.**
2. **Speakers get moment-by-moment feedback**, verbal and non-verbal (a puzzled frown), and adjust to it.
3. **Speakers have much less planning time**, which is why spoken language is shorter and simpler.
4. **Writers can see what they have produced; speakers cannot.** This may matter less than it seems: **Olive and Piolat (2002)** found no difference in text quality between writers who could and could not see their earlier text.

So speech tends to be informal, simple and fast, while writing is more formal and complex and, lacking feedback, has to be clear first time.

Brain damage shows the **output** systems can separate. **EB** (**Levine et al., 1982**) had largely intact writing despite being almost unable to speak and lacking **inner speech**; other patients speak fluently but find writing very hard (**Kolb & Whishaw, 2003**). The chapter's position is nevertheless that the **higher-level** processes, planning and use of knowledge, are very similar in the two modes.

### How easy is speech production?

We typically speak at **2 to 3 words a second, about 150 words a minute**, which suggests speaking needs few resources. In fact we rely on strategies that cut the load while we plan ahead.

- **Preformulation** is reusing phrases we have produced before, so that a chunk is retrieved rather than built. About **70% of our speech** consists of word combinations we use repeatedly (**Altenberg, 1990**). **Kuiper (1996)** found heavy use of stock phrases ("They are off and racing now") in **auctioneers and sports commentators**, who must talk very fast.
- **Underspecification** is using simplified expressions that leave the full meaning implicit, such as "or something" and "and things like that" (**De Cock, 2004**). It often travels with preformulation, since many stock phrases are also vague.

Alcohol, which impairs attention, short-term memory and thinking, shows how much work fluent speech involves.

> **Key study, Hollien et al. (2001).** Compared with when sober, intoxicated speakers produced **three times as many dysfluencies** (tripping over words, stammering, stuttering), spoke **more slowly** and at a **higher pitch**.

> **Key study, Reichel and Kisler (2012).** **162 speakers** were recorded sober and intoxicated. In **men**, intoxication reduced the richness and creativity of speech (less **word variety**, fewer **phoneme combinations**). **Women** showed no such decline because they **put in more effort to compensate**, which itself shows that fluent speech normally depends on effortful, resource-limited processing.

### Stages in speech production

Every later section uses the four levels of **Dell's (1986)** spreading-activation theory:

| Level | What is represented | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Semantic** | The meaning or message to be communicated | The **planning** level: what do I want to say? |
| **2. Syntactic** | The grammatical structure of the planned utterance | The sentence frame |
| **3. Morphological** | The **morphemes**, the basic units of meaning | *fork* + *-s* |
| **4. Phonological** | The **phonemes**, the basic units of sound | /f/ /ɔː/ /k/ /s/ |

A word contains one or more morphemes (*unhelpful* = *un* + *help* + *ful*). It is natural to assume the levels run in this order, but real production is untidy: "later" processes can run alongside, or even ahead of, "earlier" ones. How much overlap there is, and whether information flows backwards from sound to meaning, is what divides Dell from Levelt in 11.4.

> **Trap.** **Morphemes** are units of *meaning*; **phonemes** are units of *sound*. Dell's order is **semantic, syntactic, morphological, phonological**: meaning first, sound last. Options that swap the last two levels are distractors.

### In the real world: impoverishment of spontaneous speech

Coherent spontaneous speech is cognitively demanding, so it suffers early when the brain begins to fail. **Alzheimer's disease** is a progressive decline in mental abilities caused by general brain degeneration, often preceded by **mild cognitive impairment (MCI)**, minor problems with memory and thinking. Which aspects of speech go first matters practically (an early marker) and theoretically (which components separate).

> **Key study, Butterworth (1984).** Analysing **Ronald Reagan's** speeches before his re-election in **1984**, Butterworth found subtle signs of impairment, for example in **vocabulary use**, a full **ten years** before Reagan was diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease.

> **Key study, Ahmed et al. (2013).** A **longitudinal** study followed patients from **MCI** to **mild** and then **moderate** Alzheimer's disease, scoring five aspects of speech: (1) **speech production** (rate, distortions, non-words); (2) **fluency errors** (false starts, filled pauses, incomplete sentences); (3) **lexical content** (the share of meaning-carrying **content words**); (4) **syntactic complexity**; (5) **semantic content** (how fully information is conveyed).

The results split cleanly. **Speech production** and **fluency** changed only slightly and non-significantly. **Lexical** and **semantic content** declined significantly, **already at the MCI stage**. **Syntactic complexity** declined **later**. Ahmed et al. described early Alzheimer's speech as **"empty"**: many words, little information. People with MCI therefore speak fluently with few errors yet communicate less than controls, and semantic content offers a way to monitor progression. In terms of the four levels, the earliest damage is at the meaning end, and grammar holds up longer than vocabulary.

> **Trap.** In early Alzheimer's the speech is **fluent but empty**; the first marker is **lexical and semantic content**, not fluency or speech rate, and **syntax declines later**. Do not confuse **Butterworth (1984)** (Reagan, early Alzheimer's signs) with **Butterworth (1985)** (jargon aphasics' neologisms carry correct affixes, 11.5).

## 11.2 Speech Planning

Most of the time we plan what we will say before saying it. The questions are **how far ahead**, **in what units**, and **what decides** the amount.

### Do speakers plan grammar in advance?

> **Key study, Lee et al. (2013).** Speakers produced sentences such as *The student of the teacher who is raising her hand*, which is ambiguous: on the **simpler** structure the **teacher** is raising her hand, on the **more complex** one the **student** is. **Time to speech onset was longer** for the complex structure.

The words are identical in both readings, so the delay can only come from the grammar: at least part of the syntactic structure is prepared before the first word is spoken.

### The scope of planning: clause or phrase?

- A **clause** is a group of words within a sentence containing a **subject and a verb**.
- A **phrase** is a group of words expressing **a single idea**; in *Failing the exam was a major disappointment to him*, the first three words form a phrase.

**Clause-level planning.** In **word-exchange errors** the swapped words often come from **different phrases** but the **same clause** (*My chair seems empty without my room*). For two words to swap, both must be in the plan at once, so the plan spanned the clause. **Holmes (1988)** also found that people speaking spontaneously often **paused just before the start of a clause**, presumably while planning it.

**Phrase-level planning.**

> **Key study, Martin et al. (2004).** Participants described moving pictures with sentences beginning either with a **simple** initial phrase (*The ball moves above the tree and the finger*) or a **complex** one (*The ball and the tree move above the finger*). They took **longer to start** when the first phrase was complex, suggesting they planned that phrase before speaking.

**Distance differs by level.** **Sound-exchange errors** occur over **shorter distances** than word-exchange errors, so the sounds of words are planned only shortly before use, while words and structure are laid out further ahead.

### Flexibility in planning

Speakers want to **start quickly** (little planning) and to **speak fluently** (much planning), and they resolve the conflict flexibly according to their goals and the situation.

> **Key study, Ferreira and Swets (2002).** Participants gave spoken answers to maths problems. **Without time pressure**, problem difficulty affected the **time to start speaking** but **not** the time spent speaking, so answers were planned in full beforehand. When told to answer **very rapidly**, speakers planned **some** of the answer beforehand and the rest **while speaking**, doing as much in advance as the deadline allowed.

**Wagner et al. (2010)** found three factors that set the amount of grammatical advance planning: **slower** speakers planned more than fast ones; speakers planned more before **simple** than before complex sentences; and speakers under **low** cognitive load planned more than those under high load.

All three fit one principle, a trade-off between **avoiding errors** and **cognitive cost**. Accuracy needs costly planning; saving effort means more errors. Speakers plan extensively mainly when it is cheap (simple sentence, no competing task). **Swets et al. (2013)** summarise: planning processes "flexibly adapt to external task goals".

> **Trap.** The 7th edition does not say speech is simply planned clause by clause: planning **can** span a clause and **can** be limited to a phrase, and its span is **flexible**. Wagner et al.'s second factor is counter-intuitive: **more** planning before **simple** sentences, because planning a simple sentence in full is affordable and planning a complex one is not. And **sound** exchanges span **shorter** distances than **word** exchanges.

## 11.3 Speech Errors

### Why study slips?

The average person makes a speech error about **once every 500 sentences** (**Vigliocco & Hartsuiker, 2002**), and errors come in several kinds partly because they can arise at any of the four levels. They are valuable because they are **systematic**, not random: even brain-damaged patients' errors are usually similar to the intended words (**Dell et al., 2014**), who described slips as "more right than wrong". An error that is mostly right shows which parts of the plan were intact and which component went astray.

There is a methodological caveat. Classic error corpora were written down by researchers just after hearing them. **Ferber (1995)** compared such collections with errors found in **tape recordings** and found major differences: spontaneously noticed errors are an incomplete sample. This matters less now that most research uses errors **induced in the laboratory**.

### Error types

**Spoonerisms.** The initial letter or letters of two nearby words swap to make two different words, named after the **Reverend William Archibald Spooner** (*You have hissed all my mystery lectures*). Most of his celebrated examples were, in fact, painstakingly invented.

**Freudian slips.** Errors revealing the speaker's (often unconscious) sexual desires.

> **Key study, Motley (1980); Motley et al. (1983).** Male participants said pairs such as *goxi furl* and *bine foddy* aloud; swapping the initial sounds gives *foxy girl* and *fine body*. When the experimenter was a female presented as attractive and provocatively dressed, such spoonerisms **increased**, as predicted. With pairs such as *tool kits* and *fast luck*, **sexual spoonerisms were more frequent when the situation produced sexual arousal** (Motley et al., 1983).

The point for theory is that the speaker's motivational state, which is outside the linguistic plan, can raise the activation of particular words enough to steer an error.

**Semantic substitution errors.** A word of similar meaning replaces the target (*tennis bat* for *tennis racquet*). In **99%** of cases the substitute is the **same part of speech**, suggesting that speakers lay down the grammatical frame first and then look for words to fill its slots.

**Morpheme-exchange errors.** Inflections stay in place but attach to the wrong stems (*He has already trunked two packs*). So placing **inflections** and placing **stems** are handled by rather separate processes. The stems are settled **before** the inflections are pronounced, because the suffix's sound adjusts to the new stem: in *the forks of a prong*, the plural is pronounced as it should be on *forks*, not as it would have been on *prongs*. (Classic background: voiceless /s/ after *fork*, voiced /z/ after *prong*.)

**Number-agreement errors.** A singular verb with a plural subject or the reverse; collective nouns (*government*, *team*) are grammatically singular but feel plural, so people say *The government have made a mess of things*.

- **McDonald (2008):** an **external working-memory load** made subject-verb agreement judgements especially hard, so avoiding agreement errors needs processing resources.
- **Haskell and MacDonald (2003):** after *The family of mice ...* or *The family of rats ...* (both need a singular verb) many participants used a plural, **more so when the nearest noun was more obviously plural** (*rats* ends in *-s*).
- **Haskell et al. (2010):** **recent experience** matters; after hearing phrases like *a trio of violinists* with plural verbs, speakers used more plural verbs with similar phrases (*a class of children*).
- **Mirković and MacDonald (2013):** **meaning** matters too. In **Serbian**, where both verb forms are acceptable after quantified phrases, speakers used plurals more after *several wolves* than after *many wolves*, because *several* better suggests distinct individuals.

Most of these error types are explained by Dell's (1986) spreading-activation theory (11.4).

### Error detection: two theories

**Perceptual loop theory (Levelt, 1983).** Speakers catch errors by **listening to themselves** and noticing a mismatch with what they intended, using the **comprehension system** just as they would on someone else's speech.

**Conflict-based theory (Nozari et al., 2011).** Detection uses information from **inside the production system**: when candidate words compete at response selection, the system registers the **conflict**, which signals a likely error.

| Prediction | Perceptual loop (Levelt, 1983) | Conflict-based (Nozari et al., 2011) |
|---|---|---|
| What predicts catching one's own errors? | **Comprehension** ability | Quality of the **production** system |
| Speed of detection | **Slow**: the error must be produced and heard | **Fast**: conflict exists before the error is out |

The perceptual loop theory could claim speed by monitoring **inner** speech, but **Huettig and Hartsuiker (2010)** found speakers monitored their **overt** speech.

### Findings

> **Key study, Nozari et al. (2011).** In **29 aphasic patients**, **comprehension** ability was essentially **unrelated** to detecting their own errors (**r = -0.01**). **Production** ability predicted detection, and specifically: patients making many **semantic** errors were much worse at detecting their own semantic errors (**r = -0.59**), and those making many **phonological** errors were worse at detecting phonological ones (**r = -0.43**).

Figure 11.2 shows the crossover: the ability to avoid semantic errors (the "s weight") went with detecting **semantic** but not phonological errors, and the ability to avoid phonological errors (the "p weight") with detecting **phonological** but not semantic ones. A comprehension-based monitor has no reason to produce that pattern; a monitor reading conflict at each production level does.

> **Key study, Blackmer and Mitton (1991).** Among callers to a **radio chat show**, **19%** of overt self-corrections were **immediate**; one caller said *willfiddily* and added *fully* without a pause. Detection that fast fits conflict monitoring better than listening to one's own output.

A complication: high-conflict errors should blend correct and incorrect sounds and so have **low perceptual clarity**. **Nooteboom and Quené (2013b)** found this only for errors detected **late**; errors detected **early** were perceptually **clear**, so conflict may not explain every detection.

> **Trap.** The near-zero **r = -0.01** belongs to **comprehension**; **-0.59** (semantic) and **-0.43** (phonological) belong to **production**. The negative signs just mean more errors of a type went with fewer detections. Also keep each monitor with its camp: perceptual loop = **Levelt**, and WEAVER++ relies on this kind of self-monitoring (the chapter summary says the model **exaggerates the role of comprehension** in error detection); conflict monitoring = **Nozari et al.**, which the book uses to rescue Dell's theory from predicting too many errors.

### Evaluation

Speakers probably detect some errors by monitoring their own speech and others by conflict monitoring, but the evidence favours the production-internal route: patients' detection depends far more on **production** than on **comprehension** ability, and detection is often **very fast**. Against it, errors detected **early** are usually perceptually clear. Three general limitations: errors and corrections are hard to **predict**, so systematic research is difficult; detection is **very fast** and hard to measure precisely; and several processes are probably involved, but what decides **which one operates when** is unclear.

## 11.4 Theories of Speech Production

### The contrast that organises the chapter

The controversy is about how the four levels (semantic, syntactic, morphological, phonological) **relate in time and in the direction of information flow**.

**Dell's (1986) spreading-activation theory**: processing runs **in parallel** at different levels and is **interactive**, so any level can influence any other, including "backwards" from sound towards meaning.

**Levelt et al.'s (1999) WEAVER++**: word production passes through stages in **serial** order, each **completed before the next begins**, and the system is **feedforward**, flowing strictly from meaning to sound.

The real gap is smaller than this sounds. Dell accepted that at any moment processing is further advanced at the meaning end than at the sound end, so both theories agree that early processing is concentrated at the higher levels and later processing at the morphological and phonological levels. They disagree about how much the levels **overlap** and whether they **feed back**. Two framing points:

1. **Goldrick (2006)** concluded there is **"limited interaction"** in speech production. Too much interaction would produce floods of errors; too little would make it hard to produce new sentences and ideas.
2. The theories grew from **different data**. Dell's rests mainly on **speech errors**, WEAVER++ mainly on **response times** for producing words correctly in the laboratory, and interactive effects show up more strongly in error data than in response-time data (Goldrick, 2006).

| | **Spreading-activation theory** | **WEAVER++** |
|---|---|---|
| Author | Dell (1986); developed by Dell et al. (1997, 2008, 2014) | Levelt et al. (1999) |
| Timing of levels | **Parallel**; levels overlap | **Serial**; each stage completes first (**discrete**) |
| Direction of flow | **Interactive** | **Feedforward** (meaning to sound only) |
| Main evidence | **Speech errors** | **Response times** (picture naming) |
| Scope | Sentences and error patterns | **Single words** |
| Keeping errors down | Activation reset, syntactic "traffic cop", conflict monitoring | **Self-monitoring** of own speech |

> **Trap.** **Dell = parallel, interactive, error-based. Levelt/WEAVER++ = serial, discrete, feedforward, response-time-based.** "Limited interaction" is **Goldrick's (2006)** verdict. Classic background: older texts and keys often present **Garrett's (1975)** speech-error model, which separated a level assigning words their grammatical roles from a later level placing their sound forms in order; the 7th edition does not discuss it, but treat it as the serial, error-based ancestor of this debate.

### Spreading-activation theory (Dell, 1986)

#### The machinery

1. **Nodes and spreading activation.** Knowledge is a network of **nodes** for concepts, words, morphemes and phonemes, each with a level of **activation**. Activating a node passes some activation to connected nodes: strongly activating *tree* partly activates *plant*. Sound nodes spread activation too.
2. **Categorical rules** at each level constrain which categories, and combinations of categories, are acceptable; at the syntactic level they specify which grammatical category each slot needs.
3. **The lexicon** is a **connectionist network** of concept, word, morpheme and phoneme nodes; an active node sends activation to everything connected to it.
4. **Insertion rules** fill each slot with **the most highly activated node of the right category** (if a verb is needed, the most active verb). **Immediately after selection, that node's activation is reset to zero**, so it cannot be chosen again and again.

**Dell et al. (2008)** added a learned **"syntactic traffic cop"** that monitors what we intend to say and **inhibits words of the wrong syntactic category**, which is why substitution errors keep the part of speech.

Errors happen when an **incorrect item is more activated than the correct one** at selection; because activation spreads, many nodes are active at once, and that is what makes errors possible. **Dell et al. (2014)** give a detailed account of how this yields anticipations, exchanges and other errors.

#### Findings: five predictions

**1. The mixed-error effect.** In a **mixed error** the wrong word is related to the target **in both meaning and sound**. If semantic and phonological processing were sealed off from each other, mixed errors should occur only at chance rates; if they interact, a word boosted from both sides should intrude more often. The effect therefore suggests **semantic and phonological factors influence selection simultaneously**. Two caveats: a **monitor** that filters out sound-dissimilar errors could produce the same result (**Levelt et al., 1999**), and the chance baseline is hard to estimate.

> **Key study, Ferreira and Griffin (2003).** Participants read an incomplete sentence such as *I thought that there would still be some cookies left, but there were ...* and then named a picture of a **priest**. They tended to say **"none"**: the **semantic** link *priest*-*nun* combined with the **phonological identity** of *nun* and *none*. Neither link alone would produce *none*, so this demonstrates mixed-error logic experimentally and sidesteps the baseline problem.

**2. The lexical bias effect.** Errors tend to form **real words** rather than non-words. Dell's explanation (e.g., **Dell et al., 2008**) is that words have lexical representations and so can gather activation that non-words cannot. The alternative is that speakers **monitor inner speech** and **edit out** non-words. Both have support (**Dell et al., 2014**). **Nooteboom and Quené (2008)** found speakers often stopped themselves just before an error: shown *BARN DOOR*, a speaker might say *D... BARN DOOR*.

> **Key study, Corley et al. (2011).** Participants said tongue twisters quickly. When **all** stimuli were real words, the usual lexical bias appeared. When **half** were pronounceable non-words (*gulk*, *bish*, *nabe*), the bias **disappeared**: with non-words now legitimate targets, the editor stopped filtering them out.

**3. Anticipatory errors**, a sound produced too early (*a Tanadian from Toronto*), are common (**Nooteboom & Quené, 2013a**). Many words in a sentence are active during planning, and a later one is sometimes more active than the current one.

**4. Exchange errors** (*I must send a wife to my email*) are also common (**Nooteboom & Quené, 2013a**), and the reset rule explains them. If *wife* is selected too early, its activation drops to zero, so when its own slot arrives the most active waiting noun is *email*, which fills it. One premature selection yields a swap.

**5. Short distances.** Anticipations and exchanges usually move words only a little way, because words for the part of the sentence being planned are more activated than words for distant parts.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths:** the **mixed-error** and **lexical bias** effects show production can be highly **interactive**; the theory explains **exchange** and **anticipatory** errors; spreading activation links production to other activities such as **word recognition** (**McClelland & Rumelhart, 1981**); widespread activation may underlie our ability to produce **novel sentences**; and the charge that the original version predicted **too many errors** is answered by **Nozari et al.'s (2011)** monitoring mechanisms.

**Limitations:** it **de-emphasises message construction**; it predicts which errors occur but not the **time** taken to produce correct and incorrect words; its interactive effects are **weaker in error-free data** (**Goldrick, 2006**); and it says too little about **what controls the amount of interaction**, which falls when processing demands are **high** (**Mädebach et al., 2011**). The chapter summary's verdict: it explains most errors but risks **exaggerating interactivity**.

### Anticipatory and perseveration errors (Dell et al., 1997)

**Dell et al. (1997)** sorted most errors into two kinds:

1. **Anticipatory**: sounds or words spoken **ahead of time** (*caff of coffee* for *cup of coffee*), mainly reflecting **inexpert planning**.
2. **Perseveratory**: sounds or words spoken **later** than they should be, carried over from earlier (*beef needle* for *beef noodle*), reflecting **planning failure** or failure to monitor what one is about to say.

The key assumption is that **experts plan further ahead**, so their errors should more often be intrusions from the future. In Dell et al.'s words, "Practice enhances the activation of the present and future at the expense of the past." The measure is the **anticipatory proportion** = anticipations ÷ (anticipations + perseverations).

> **Key study, Dell et al. (1997).** After extensive practice at tongue twisters (*five frantic fat frogs*, *thirty-three throbbing thumbs*), total errors **fell**, but the **anticipatory proportion rose from 0.37 to 0.59**.

Errors are likeliest when there is **no coherent plan**, and then the future is weakly activated, so few errors are anticipations. The overall error rate should therefore correlate **negatively** with the anticipatory proportion. Across published data sets the proportion fell from about **0.75 at low error rates** to about **0.40 at high error rates** (Figure 11.3).

> **Key study, Vousden and Maylor (2006).** **8-year-olds, 11-year-olds and young adults** said tongue twisters **slowly or fast**. The anticipatory proportion **rose with age** (more language practice), and **fast** speech gave **more errors** and a **lower** anticipatory proportion.

> **Key study, Wutzler et al. (2013).** **Elderly** people with **significant cognitive impairment** had a significantly **lower** anticipatory proportion than unimpaired peers, presumably because they planned less well.

> **Trap.** The anticipatory proportion goes **up** with practice (0.37 to 0.59), age in development and **low** error rates (about 0.75); it goes **down** with **high** error rates (about 0.40), **fast** speech and **cognitive impairment**. Good planning means errors leap **forward**; poor planning lets the **past** intrude. This "perseveration" is a slip category, not the clinical perseveration of frontal patients.

### Levelt's theoretical approach and WEAVER++

#### The model

**WEAVER++** (**Levelt et al., 1999**) stands for **Word-form Encoding by Activation and VERification** and concerns the production of **individual spoken words**. Its assumptions:

- A **feedforward** activation-spreading network: activation moves forwards, never back, **from meaning to sound**.
- **Three levels**: **lexical concepts** at the top; **lemmas** in the middle, abstract words specified **syntactically and semantically but not phonologically** (Harley, 2013), so that knowing a word's meaning and that it is a noun, but not its pronunciation, means you have its lemma; and **word forms** (morphemes and phonemic segments) at the bottom.
- **Lexical selection is competitive**, depending on how many lexical units are active.
- After selection, processing passes through stages **one at a time**.
- Errors are avoided by a **checking mechanism** in which speakers **monitor what they say**.

The model's figure (Figure 11.4) is lost, but the text gives the route it draws:

1. **Conceptual preparation** yields a **lexical concept**.
2. **Lexical selection** picks a **lemma** (meaning plus syntax), usually the most activated.
3. **Morphological encoding** activates the selected lemma's basic word form.
4. **Phonological encoding** computes the syllables, giving a **phonological word**.
5. **Phonetic encoding** and **articulation** follow, with **self-monitoring** throughout.

The whole conversion is **lexicalisation**, turning the thought behind a word into its sound (Harley, 2013). WEAVER++ is **discrete** (the lemma is identified before any work on the sound begins) and **feedforward** (one direction only).

> **Trap.** The **lemma** carries **meaning and syntax** (including grammatical gender) but **no sound**; "lemma = phonological form" and "lemma = morpheme" are both wrong. **Lexicalisation** is the whole meaning-to-sound conversion, not just lemma retrieval.

#### Findings: timing

> **Key study, van Turennout et al. (1998).** ERPs in **Dutch** speakers showed that a noun's **grammatical gender** (syntax) was available **40 ms before its initial phoneme**, the order WEAVER++ requires.

> **Key study, Indefrey (2011).** A **meta-analysis** of ERP and other brain-activity studies, mostly of **picture naming**, gave approximate stage timings:
>
> | Stage | Approximate time |
> |---|---|
> | Conceptual preparation | about **200 ms** |
> | Lemma retrieval | **75 ms** |
> | Phonological code retrieval | **20 ms per phoneme**; **50 to 55 ms per syllable** |
> | Phonetic encoding, then articulation | articulation begins about **600 ms** after processing starts |
>
> Each stage was linked to particular **left-hemisphere** regions (Figure 11.5).

Two limitations: the data come mostly from **picture naming**, and, as **Riès et al. (2013)** noted, timings are measured from **stimulus onset**, whereas for an action like speaking it would be more sensible to time processes relative to **speech onset**.

#### Findings: the tip-of-the-tongue state

In the **tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state** you have the concept but cannot find the word: semantic processing has succeeded (correct lemma) but phonological processing has failed (Harley, 2013).

- **Harley and Bown (1998):** words that **sound unlike nearly all other words** (*apron*, *vineyard*) produced far more TOT states than words with many sound-alikes (*litter*, *pawn*); unusual phonological forms are hard to retrieve.
- **Vigliocco et al. (1997):** if lemmas carry syntax, gender should be available in a TOT state. **Italian** speakers guessed the noun's **gender correctly 85%** of the time.
- **Biedermann et al. (2008):** **German** speakers in TOT states guessed gender and **initial phoneme**. If gender comes first and phonology later, correct gender should go with better phoneme guesses; it did not, suggesting syntax is **not** necessarily available before phonology.

> **Trap.** **Dutch** = 40 ms gender advantage (van Turennout et al., 1998); **Italian** = 85% gender in TOT (Vigliocco et al., 1997); **German** = gender did not help phoneme guesses, **against** WEAVER++ (Biedermann et al., 2008).

#### Findings: competitive selection

In the **picture-word interference task**, participants name pictures while ignoring distractor words; **same-category** distractors **slow** naming (**Melinger & Rahman, 2013**), plausibly through competition.

> **Key study, Piai et al. (2014).** A picture (a **dog**) was named with a **related** (*cat*), **unrelated** (*pin*) or **identical** (*dog*) distractor. On the model, candidates are first activated and then a **top-down competitive process involving frontal cortex** selects one. **Left temporal** activity (word activation) was greater with related and unrelated distractors than with identical ones (only one word active). **Left superior frontal gyrus** activity (effort of resolving competition) was **greatest with related** and **least with identical** distractors, exactly as competition predicts.

#### Findings: is selection finished before phonology starts?

WEAVER++ says lemma selection is **complete** before phonology is accessed; Dell's theory says phonology can **start before** selection ends, so unselected candidates should leak sound activation. Most evidence favours Dell.

> **Key study, Meyer and Damian (2007).** Participants named a **target picture** while ignoring a **distractor picture** whose name was **phonologically related** (*dog*-*doll*, *ball*-*wall*) or unrelated. On WEAVER++ the distractor's name is never selected, so relatedness should not matter. Targets were named **faster** with related distractors, as spreading activation predicts.

**Oppermann et al. (2014)** found that a distractor object was processed **phonologically when attended**, contrary to WEAVER++, but not when it failed to capture attention.

> **Key study, Mädebach et al. (2011).** A phonologically similar distractor word **slowed** naming when processing demands were **low** but had **no effect** when demands were **high**. Processing is **serial** (Levelt-like) under **high** demands and **interactive** (Dell-like) under **low** demands.

This is the bridge between the theories: the degree of interaction itself varies with available capacity. Interaction is a luxury; when resources are scarce the system narrows to one candidate at a time.

#### Evaluation of WEAVER++

**Strengths:** (1) the sequence from lexical selection to morphological to phonological encoding is reasonably accurate (**Indefrey, 2011**); (2) it shifted research from speech errors to the **precise timing** of word production; (3) it is **simple, elegant** and makes many testable predictions, arguably more testable than interactive theories; (4) lexical selection often is **competitive**.

**Limitations:** (1) its **narrow focus on single words** neglects sentence planning; (2) there is **much more interaction** between levels than it allows (e.g., **Meyer & Damian, 2007**); (3) the **mixed-error**, **lexical bias**, **word-exchange** and **sound-exchange** data imply more **parallel processing** than it predicts; (4) **Harley (2013)** argued that lemmas are not strongly motivated, since most evidence only demands a **semantic** versus **phonological** distinction; (5) anomia research (11.5) supports it **reasonably** but includes inconsistent findings; (6) per the chapter summary, it **exaggerates the role of comprehension** in detecting one's own errors.

> **High-yield.** The 7th edition's verdict: brain-activation patterns and some TOT research support WEAVER++'s stages, but production is **much more interactive** than WEAVER++ assumes, and Dell risks **overstating** interaction. Goldrick's (2006) "limited interaction" plus Mädebach et al.'s (2011) demand-dependence is the best one-line synthesis.

> **Trap.** **Mädebach et al. (2011)**: **high** demands give **serial** processing, **low** demands give **interactive** processing. Students reverse this because "interactive" sounds more effortful.

## 11.5 Cognitive Neuropsychology: Speech Production

### The traditional picture: Broca's versus Wernicke's aphasia

The cognitive neuropsychology of language began in the nineteenth century with **aphasia**, severe brain-damage-induced problems in comprehending and/or producing language. Its best-known result was a two-way split:

| | **Broca's aphasia** | **Wernicke's aphasia** |
|---|---|---|
| Speech | **Slow, non-fluent** | **Fluent**, apparently grammatical, often **lacking meaning** |
| Grammar in production | Poor | Apparently intact (but see problem 4) |
| Comprehension | **Relatively intact** | **Severely impaired** |
| Site | **BA44/45**, **inferior frontal gyrus** | **Posterior BA22**, **superior temporal gyrus** |
| Core deficit (traditional view) | **Production** | **Comprehension** |

There is a **grain of truth** here: **Yang et al. (2008)** found that most stroke patients with damage to **Broca's area** had Broca's-type deficits and most with damage to **Wernicke's area** had Wernicke's-type problems.

### Five reasons the distinction is oversimplified

1. **Same label, different patients.** Patients given the same label show **very different symptoms** (Harley, 2013).
2. **Networks, not centres.** **Berwick et al. (2013)** describe four interconnected left-hemisphere pathways (Figure 11.6): one for **basic syntactic processing**, one **connecting sensory and motor processes**, and two for **semantic processing**. Two of them involve Broca's and Wernicke's areas, which are better seen as hubs in a network.
3. **Lesions ignore the labels.** Broca's patients often have damage to Wernicke's area, and Wernicke's patients sometimes to Broca's (**De Bleser, 1988**).
4. **The pattern depends on the language.** Broca's patients' far greater difficulty speaking grammatically is more common in **English** than in **German** or **Italian** speakers. **Dick et al. (2001)** explain this by **inflection**: inflected languages mark grammatical changes by altering the words themselves, and because English is **less inflected**, English-speaking Wernicke's patients have fewer chances to make visible grammatical errors.
5. **Not only language.** **McNeil et al. (2010)** argued that aphasic patients also have **general attention and memory problems**, which fits the common finding that their performance **varies from occasion to occasion**.

> **Key study, Hodgson and Lambon Ralph (2008).** **Healthy** participants with only **500 ms** to name pictures made errors closely resembling those of **stroke aphasics with semantic impairments**. Many aphasic naming errors may reflect **reduced processing resources and/or semantic control**, not lost knowledge.

Research has therefore shifted to **specific impairments** that can be related to the models in 11.4: **anomia**, **agrammatism** and **jargon aphasia**.

> **Trap.** Older textbooks and exam keys call Broca's aphasia "expressive" or "motor" and Wernicke's "receptive" or "sensory", each tied to one centre (classic background: the Wernicke-Geschwind model linked them through a single fibre bundle, the arcuate fasciculus). The 7th edition keeps the sites (**Broca = BA44/45, inferior frontal gyrus; Wernicke = posterior BA22, superior temporal gyrus**) but calls the scheme **oversimplified**. Any of the five reasons is a correct "limitation". Broca's patients' comprehension is only *relatively* intact: agrammatic patients often struggle with syntactically complex sentences.

### Anomia

**Anomia**, impaired object naming, affects nearly **all** aphasic patients. WEAVER++ predicts two ways lexicalisation can fail: at the **semantic** level (wrong **lemma**, so errors resemble the target in **meaning**) or at the **phonological** level (right lemma, but the **word form** cannot be retrieved).

#### Findings

> **Key study, Howard and Orchard-Lisle (1984): patient JCU.** JCU had a **semantic** impairment. Given the first sound of a word **closely related in meaning** to the pictured object, she often produced that wrong word. But when she produced a name **very different in meaning** from the object, she **rejected it 86%** of the time. Her semantic access was coarse: enough to reject a distant name, too imprecise to reject a near neighbour.

> **Key study, Kay and Ellis (1987): patient EST.** EST could apparently select the right **lemma** but not its **phonological form**. His semantic system seemed unimpaired, yet he struggled to find any but **very common** words, a condition Kay and Ellis likened to a **magnified tip-of-the-tongue state**.

> **Key study, Laganaro et al. (2008).** Aphasic patients classed as **semantic** or **phonological** named pictures while **ERPs** were recorded. The semantic group showed abnormalities **early** (**100 to 250 ms** after picture onset), the phonological group only **later** (**300 to 450 ms**), in different brain areas. Anomia can arise at an **early semantic** or a **later phonological** stage.

Two separable failure points fit serial models but do **not rule out** interactive ones. The deciding question is whether phonology can influence word selection.

> **Key study, Soni et al. (2009).** Aphasic patients named pictures with a **correct** cue (lion + *l*), an **incorrect** cue (lion + *t*, suggesting *tiger*) or **no** cue. On Levelt's model the name is settled before the cue's phonology is used, so a misleading cue should not hurt; on interactive models phonological activation feeds into selection, so correct cues help and wrong ones harm. The results **supported interactive models**.

> **Key study, Soni et al. (2011).** For a picture of a **candle**, the accompanying sound was **related-category** (*l*, suggesting *lamp*), **associate** (*w*, suggesting *wax*), **neutral** (*g*) or **correct** (*k*). Naming was **worse in all three** non-correct conditions than with *k*: semantic processing is **not necessarily complete before phonological processing starts**, contrary to Levelt et al. (1999).

#### Evaluation

Much of the anomia evidence fits Levelt's two failure points (**lemma selection**, **phonological access**). But a **simpler** account may be better, with anomia following fairly directly from a patient's semantic and phonological impairments without any need for lemmas, and **Soni et al. (2009)** suggest **more interaction** between semantic and phonological processing than Levelt allows.

### Agrammatism

#### What it is

If production builds a **syntactic frame** separately from retrieving **content words** (e.g., Dell, 1986), damage should sometimes spare word retrieval and destroy the frame. **Agrammatism** is that pattern: **short sentences** with **content words** (nouns, verbs) but without **function words** (*the*, *in*, *and*) and **word endings**, which carry much of a sentence's grammatical structure. Agrammatic patients often also have trouble **understanding syntactically complex sentences**.

#### Is it a syndrome?

The label implies a **syndrome** whose members share symptoms, which would suggest grammar is **localised**. Agrammatism is fairly strongly correlated with damage to **Broca's area (BA44/45)** (**Cappa, 2012**), but it is **not** a syndrome: many patients have **some but not all** of its symptoms (Harley, 2013).

#### Findings

**Grodzinsky and Friederici's (2006)** model (Figure 11.7) helps explain the diversity by dividing syntactic processing into **three phases in different areas**:

| Phase | What is computed | Brain area |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Local phrase structure** | Local phrases built once **word category** (noun, verb) is known | **Frontal operculum** and **anterior superior temporal gyrus** |
| **2. Dependency relations** | **Who is doing what to whom** | **Broca's area (BA44/45)** |
| **3. Integration** | Syntactic and lexical information combined | **Posterior superior temporal gyrus and sulcus** |

**Friederici et al. (2006)** found greater Broca's-area activation for **syntactically complex** sentences, and Broca's area is typically damaged in agrammatism. A lesion hitting one phase more than the others yields some agrammatic symptoms and not others.

> **Trap.** The figure caption in the 7th-edition source labels the dependency area "BA33/45"; the text says **BA44/45** (Broca's area), which is correct. Phase 1 is **not** Broca's area: local phrase structure is the **frontal operculum plus anterior superior temporal gyrus**.

**Competence or processing?** Agrammatic patients might have **lost** grammatical competence, or **kept** it but be unable to use it in real time. Both probably contribute, but recent work favours the second.

- **Beeke et al. (2007)** argued that artificial tasks (picture description) underestimate agrammatic grammar; their patient's speech was **more grammatical in conversation at home** with a family member than in the laboratory.
- **Burkhardt et al. (2008)** found agrammatic patients **resolved syntactic complexities reasonably well but slowly**, pointing to a **processing limitation** rather than lost knowledge.
- **Rhys et al. (2013)** studied a patient whose output was mostly variants of **"very good"**, yet she used **prosodic cues** (stress, intonation) to convey various meanings and grammatical structures.

**Is it specific to language?** **Christianson et al. (2010)** found agrammatic patients were also impaired at **sequence learning**. Since grammaticality depends heavily on **word order**, a general sequencing deficit could itself produce ungrammatical speech, so in some patients the deficit may be broader than language.

> **Key study, Griffiths et al. (2013).** Patients had damage to the **dorsal** syntactic pathway (Figure 11.6) and/or a **ventral** pathway, both connecting Broca's and Wernicke's areas. They heard sentences such as *The woman is hugged by the man* and chose among the **correct** drawing, a **syntactic distractor** (a woman hugging a man) and a **semantic distractor** (a man painting a woman). Damage to **either or both** pathways produced **many more syntactic errors** than in controls or patients with neither pathway damaged, while **all** groups made **very few semantic errors**. Syntactic processing in comprehension (and probably production) needs **two pathways** linking Broca's and Wernicke's areas.

The design is worth noticing. A **passive** sentence defeats a simple word-order strategy (the woman is mentioned first but is the one hugged), and the two distractor types separate a syntactic failure from a semantic one.

#### Evaluation

Agrammatism supports a **syntactic level** in production; there is preliminary evidence of **reduced processing resources** (Burkhardt et al., 2008); and **two pathways** are needed for syntax (Griffiths et al., 2013). Limitations: symptoms are **too diverse** for a syndrome; patients may have **more grammatical competence** than assumed; and their impairments may be **broader** than language (Christianson et al., 2010).

### Jargon aphasia

#### What it is

**Harley (2013)** describes **jargon aphasia** as an extreme **fluent** aphasia in which **syntax is largely intact** but speech shows **gross word-finding difficulties**: the **mirror image of agrammatism**. Jargon aphasics **substitute words** and produce **neologisms** (made-up words), and their **self-monitoring is deficient**: they are usually **unaware** of their errors and become **irritated** when not understood.

The book's example (from **Sampson & Faroqi-Shah, 2011**) is a picture description of a house in which well-formed sentence frames carry distorted and invented words; the patient works through several near-misses before reaching *weeding*, and ends with an utterance built round neologisms (*a nicoverit spotole for the changer*).

| | **Agrammatism** | **Jargon aphasia** |
|---|---|---|
| Fluency | Reduced; short utterances | **Fluent** |
| Grammar | **Impaired**: function words and endings missing | **Largely intact** |
| Content words | **Largely available** | **Gross word-finding failure**; neologisms |
| Awareness of errors | Usually aware (classic background) | **Typically unaware** |
| Associated region | Broca's area (BA44/45) (Cappa, 2012) | Wernicke-type fluent aphasia (classic background) |

#### Findings

**Grammar in the jargon.** If syntactic processing is intact, neologisms should carry the **prefixes and suffixes** their sentence slots require, and they do (**Butterworth, 1985**).

**What shapes a neologism?** (1) Some are **phonologically related to the target**, suggesting the target was accessed and then distorted during **phonological** processing. (2) **Robson et al. (2003)** found that the neologisms of **LT**, an English-speaking jargon aphasic, consisted mostly of **consonants common in English**. (3) **Goldman et al. (2001)** found that **recently used phonemes**, presumably still partly active, often appeared in neologisms.

Other neologisms are almost **unrelated** to the target. **Olson et al. (2007)** studied **VS**, an **84-year-old woman**, whose related and unrelated neologisms were affected alike by **word frequency**, **imageability** and **word length**, suggesting (not proving) a **single underlying deficit**.

**Self-monitoring.**

> **Key study, Sampson and Faroqi-Shah (2011).** In **five jargon aphasics**, there was a **strong negative correlation** between **self-monitoring** and production of **jargon** (neologisms plus real words unrelated in sound to the target). **Masking noise** that stopped patients hearing their own voice **reduced self-monitoring** and **increased jargon**.

> **Key study, Eaton et al. (2011): patient TK.** Over **21 months**, TK's single-word production (e.g., naming) **improved**, and the improvement **correlated highly with increased self-monitoring**.

The masking result suggests that hearing one's own voice matters for these patients, the perceptual-loop route of 11.3; the 7th edition's position is that both monitoring routes probably exist.

**Therapy.** Impaired **phonological processing** often plays a major role (Harley, 2013), so phonological therapy should help.

> **Key study, Bose (2013): patient FF.** FF, a **77-year-old man**, received therapy on **generating and analysing phonological features of words**. His **naming improved** and his **neologisms decreased**.

#### Evaluation

Many neologisms result from severely deficient **self-monitoring**, and they sometimes incorporate **target** phonemes or **recently used** ones. Limitations: the relative weight of **phonological and semantic** deficits is unclear (Harley, 2013); more research is needed on the **extent and grammaticality** of jargon aphasics' sentences; and whether the **same processes** produce target-like and target-unlike neologisms is unknown.

## 11.6 Speech as Communication

### From monologue to dialogue

The theories so far share one limitation, which **Pickering and Garrod (2004)** stated plainly: mechanistic accounts of language come almost entirely from studying **monologue**, yet "the most natural and basic form of language use is dialogue". In conversation, production and comprehension are **interwoven** (**Pickering & Garrod, 2013**). Since nearly all speech happens as conversation in a social context, many theorists stress **audience design** (partner-specific processing): tailoring what is said, and how, to the listener's needs. This section asks how far speakers do that, and what cheaper tools they use when they do not.

### Grice's Cooperative Principle and four maxims

**Grice (1967)** proposed the **Cooperative Principle** (speakers and listeners should try to cooperate) and four **maxims** for the speaker:

| Maxim | What the speaker should do |
|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Say things **relevant** to the situation |
| **Quantity** | Be **as informative as necessary** |
| **Quality** | Be **truthful** |
| **Manner** | Make the contribution **easy to understand** |

Two issues: the other three maxims are arguably **implied by relevance**, so four may be unnecessary; and some speakers (the book cites second-hand car salesmen and politicians) are guided by **self-interest** and ignore maxims (**Faulkner, 2008**).

Even well-meaning speakers break the **maxim of quantity**.

> **Key study, Engelhardt et al. (2006).** With only **one apple** present, *Put the apple in the box* is enough, yet **30%** of the time speakers said something over-detailed such as *Put the apple on the towel in the box*.

> **Key study, Davies and Katsos (2013).** Arguing that Engelhardt et al.'s displays were **complex**, they used **simpler arrays** (Figure 11.9). Participants asked a hypothetical listener (shown in a photograph) to pass an item: obeying quantity means *Pass me the bag* with one bag (**1-referent**) and *Pass me the closed bag* with two (**2-referent**). Speakers did so on **90%** of trials **in both conditions** and were overinformative on only **8%**.

Adherence to quantity thus depends on **scene complexity**: 30% overinformative with cluttered displays, 8% with simple ones.

> **Trap.** Grice's own name for the relevance maxim is **relation** (classic background); the 7th edition calls it **relevance**. **Quantity** is about how much information, **quality** about truth; an over-detailed instruction violates **quantity**, not manner.

### Audience design and common ground

**Common ground**, the information speaker and listener share, includes what is **physically co-present**, what is **linguistically co-present** (already mentioned) and what comes from shared **cultural or community membership** (**Brown-Schmidt, 2012**). Speakers make **global** assumptions (the listener's language, general knowledge, shared experiences) and **local** ones (what the listener knows or attends to right now); local assumptions are more often wrong because they keep changing (**Arnold, 2008**).

**Horton and Keysar (1996)** proposed the **monitoring and adjustment model**: speakers first plan utterances from information available **to themselves**, ignoring the listener's perspective, and then **monitor and correct** the plan to fit common ground. The reason is that taking the listener's perspective while planning is often **computationally difficult**. Speakers still communicate fairly well because, according to **Shintel and Keysar (2009)**, they rely on cues that are "powerful, simple, and cheap": speaking more clearly to a **new** than an old listener, and (often unintentionally) **looking at** the object being talked about.

> **Trap.** The monitoring and adjustment model is "**egocentric first, correct later**": common ground enters only at the monitoring stage. An option saying it is consulted from the outset describes the opposite view.

#### Findings

Speakers sometimes simply say what is **easiest** for them (**Ferreira, 2008**).

> **Key study, Fukumura and van Gompel (2012).** Speakers normally use a noun phrase (*the red desk*) on first mention and a **pronoun** (*it*) next, but a pronoun only works if the **listener heard** the first sentence. Speakers generally used the pronoun **even when the listener had not**, tracking their own discourse rather than the listener's knowledge.

Perspective-taking is demanding, so it should depend on executive control: **Wardlow (2013)** found a correlation of **+0.42** between **perspective-taking** and **inhibitory control**, an executive function related to intelligence.

Speakers also use cheap strategies, above all **copying** the partner's words, phrases and ideas. **Syntactic priming** is the tendency to reuse the **syntactic structure** of recently heard utterances: after hearing a passive (*The man was bitten by the dog*) you are more likely to produce one. It occurs across **different topics** and **without awareness** (**Pickering & Ferreira, 2008**). It **reduces production demands**, and **Jaeger and Snider (2013)** found it **strongest** after an **unexpected** structure, which helps speakers get "on the same wavelength" as their partner.

**Galati and Brennan (2010)** argued that audience design is likeliest when the listener's needs are **clear and simple** (a bilingual easily keeps to the language a monolingual listener knows).

> **Key study, Galati and Brennan (2010).** Speakers told the same **cartoon story twice to one listener and once to another**. With a listener who had **heard it before**, they used **fewer words** and **less detail**; with a **new** listener they spoke **more intelligibly**. When the listener's needs are easy to read, audience design can be strong in both content and delivery.

> **Trap.** Evidence runs both ways, so "which is NOT evidence for audience design" questions are common. **Against:** Ferreira (2008); Fukumura and van Gompel (2012); the monitoring and adjustment model; Kraljic and Brennan (2005, below). **For:** Galati and Brennan (2010). The reconciling principle: speakers design for the audience when the listener's needs are **clear and cheap to track**.

### Gesture

Speakers gesture believing it helps communication, and listeners do understand gesturing speakers better (comprehension chapters). Gesture may be the older system: human communication probably relied on gesture before vocalisation, and **primate gestures resemble human language much more than primate vocalisations do** (**Cartmill et al., 2012**).

#### Findings

Do speakers gesture more when the listener can see them? Only **50%** of studies found this; in the other **50%** visibility had no significant effect (**Bavelas & Healing, 2013**).

> **Key study, Gerwing and Allison (2011).** Speakers described an **elaborate dress** to a listener who was **visible** or on the **telephone**. The **number** of gestures did **not** differ, but their **informativeness** did: face to face, **74%** of the information was carried by gesture and **26%** by speech; on the telephone, **27%** by gesture and **73%** by speech.

Counting gestures can therefore miss the effect of the listener, which shows up in what the gestures carry.

> **Key study, Holler and Wilkin (2011).** After listener **feedback**, gestures became **fewer** once the listener signalled **understanding**, and feedback asking for **clarification, elaboration or correction** was followed by **more precise, larger or more visually prominent** gestures.

Why gesture when the listener cannot see? Speakers **blind since birth** gesture even to **blind** listeners (**Iverson & Goldin-Meadow, 1998**), so gesture must also help the **speaker**.

> **Key study, Frick-Horbury and Guttentag (1998).** Given definitions of uncommon words (e.g., *tambourine*), participants produced **21% fewer** target words when gesturing was made difficult.

Gestures thus help speakers work out **what to say** and help listeners **understand**. The blind-speaker finding is evidence for the speaker-facilitation function, not the listener one.

### Prosodic cues

**Prosody** is the systematic variation in how words are uttered that specifies or disambiguates meaning (**Cvejic et al., 2012**). **Prosodic cues** include **rhythm, stress, intonation** and **duration**. In *The old men and women sat on the bench*, if the women are **not** old, *men* is lengthened and the stressed syllable of *women* has a **steep pitch rise**; neither feature appears if the women are old.

> **Key study, Snedeker and Trueswell (2003).** Speakers produced ambiguous instructions such as *Tap the frog with the flower* (use the flower, or tap the frog holding it), giving **many more prosodic cues** when the **context fitted both** interpretations.

> **Key study, Kraljic and Brennan (2005).** Speakers used prosodic cues heavily and listeners used them to disambiguate, but speakers produced them **regardless of whether the listener needed them** or whether they realised the listener did.

So prosody here showed no special responsiveness to the listener; it probably **emerged from speaker-centred planning**, with the structure of the speaker's own plan leaving a prosodic trace. Overall, prosody often reflects audience design but sometimes depends mainly on speaker-centred processes (**Wagner & Watson, 2010**).

### Discourse markers

**Discourse markers** are words and phrases that do not add to the content but serve functions such as clarifying the speaker's intentions. Listeners interpret them accurately: *oh* and *um* signal difficulty deciding what to say next (**Tree, 2007**), and *you know* checks understanding and builds connection.

> **Key study, Bolden (2006).** When shifting to a **new topic**, speakers used ***oh* 98.5%** of the time if the topic **concerned the speaker**, and ***so* 96%** of the time if it was most relevant to the **listener**, usually without realising it.

*So* also marks the **consequences** of what has just been said (**Buysse, 2012**).

> **Trap.** **Bolden (2006): *oh* = speaker-related topic shift (98.5%); *so* = listener-related (96%).** Discourse markers are by definition **not** content words.

## 11.7 Writing: The Main Processes

### Writing as a test of the whole cognitive system

**Kellogg and Whiteford (2012)** describe composing an extended text as a severe test of **memory, language and thinking** together: it needs rapid retrieval of topic knowledge from **long-term memory**, high **verbal ability** and **clear thinking**. Writing expertise therefore probably correlates at least **moderately with IQ**.

### Key processes

Theorists differ in minor ways about the number and nature of writing processes (**Hayes & Chenoweth, 2006; Hayes, 2012**), but most accept **Hayes and Flower's (1986)** three: **planning** (producing and organising ideas to meet the writer's goals), **sentence generation** (turning the plan into sentences) and **revision** (evaluating what has been written and revising it).

**Chenoweth and Hayes (2003)** expanded this to **four** processes:

| Process | What it does | Equivalent in Hayes & Flower (1986) |
|---|---|---|
| **Proposer** | Proposes ideas; higher-level planning | Planning |
| **Translator** | Turns the proposed message into **word strings** | Sentence generation |
| **Transcriber** | Turns word strings into **written or typed text** | (none) |
| **Evaluator/reviser** | Monitors, evaluates and revises | Revision |

The addition is the **transcriber**. Hayes and Flower assumed that transcription (writing down an already-composed sentence) needs almost **no resources** and so cannot interfere with other processes. That is wrong.

> **Key study, Hayes and Chenoweth (2006).** Participants **copied** text from one computer window to another. While also performing a trivially simple task (saying *tap* repeatedly), they transcribed **more slowly** and with **more errors**, so transcription does consume resources.

**Hayes's (2012)** latest model (Figure 11.10) places the four processes within **three levels** plus a task environment: a **control level** (**motivation**, **goal setting**), a **writing process level** (proposer, translator, transcriber, evaluator) and a **resource level** (including **working memory**, **attention** and **long-term memory**). Including **motivation** matters because writing well is so demanding that prolonged evaluation and revision only happen if the writer is highly motivated.

The "natural" order is propose, translate, transcribe, evaluate, but writers depart from it constantly, switching processes every few seconds.

> **Trap.** **Hayes and Flower (1986)**: **three** processes. **Chenoweth and Hayes (2003)**: **four**, adding the **transcriber**. **Hayes (2012)**: the four inside **three levels**, with **motivation** at the control level. Transcription is **not** resource-free.

### In the real world: effects of Alzheimer's disease on novel writing

If mild cognitive impairment affects speech (11.1), it should also affect writing, which is more complex still. The key case is **Iris Murdoch**, the renowned Irish novelist later diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease.

> **Key study, Garrard et al. (2005).** Comparing Murdoch's **first published** work, a novel from **her prime** and her **final** novel, her **vocabulary became progressively less sophisticated** (smaller, more common words), while syntactic changes were **less clear-cut**.

**Pakhomov et al. (2011)** found some evidence that her **syntactic complexity** also declined over time. Her writing was affected **several years before diagnosis**, when she probably had MCI.

> **Key study, Le et al. (2011).** A **longitudinal** analysis of **Iris Murdoch**, **Agatha Christie** (suspected of Alzheimer's disease late in life) and **P.D. James** (no signs of impairment; the comparison) confirmed early signs in Murdoch and indicated that Christie's **last novels** probably reflected the **onset** of Alzheimer's disease. In both, impairment involved **vocabulary much more than syntax**: a **sharp fall in vocabulary size**, more **repeated phrases** and more **irrelevant fillers**.

As with speech (Ahmed et al., 2013), cognitive impairment affects **what is written** more than its **grammar**, even at onset, and such changes may give an **early indication** of Alzheimer's disease.

> **Trap.** In both Alzheimer's boxes the early casualty is **vocabulary and content**; **syntax** is affected **later and less**. **P.D. James** = unimpaired comparison; **Christie** = suspected case; **Murdoch** = diagnosed case.

### Findings: how writers spend their time

**Directed retrospection** interrupts writers at intervals and has them categorise what they were just doing, giving a time budget without continuous commentary.

> **Key study, Kellogg (1994).** Across directed-retrospection studies, writers spent about **30%** of their time **planning**, **50%** on **sentence generation** and **20%** on **revision**.

**Kellogg (1988)** found that writers who first made an **outline** spent **more** time on sentence generation and **less** on planning and on reviewing or revising than writers without one.

> **Key study, Kaufer et al. (1986).** In think-aloud protocols, **expert** and **average** writers both accepted about **75%** of the sentence parts they verbalised, but experts' parts averaged **11.2 words** against **7.3** for average writers: good writers use **larger building blocks**.

> **Key study, Levy and Ransdell (1995).** Using verbal reports and **video** of essay writing on computers, they found very short episodes before switching: about **7.5 seconds** for **text generation** and **2.5 seconds** for **planning, reviewing and revising**. Writers were only partly aware of their time use, **underestimating** text generation and **overestimating** reviewing and revising (**estimated 30%, actual 5%**).

> **Key study, Beauvais et al. (2011).** Writers produced a **narrative** (a story about **six coloured pictures**) and an **argumentative** text (on a law **banning smoking in public places**), switching processes about **eight** times a minute for narrative and **six** for argumentative text. Translating episodes lasted **16 s** (narrative) and **17 s** (argumentative), planning **8 s** and **12 s**, and revision **4 s** in both. As in Levy and Ransdell, planning and revising episodes were **shorter** than translating episodes.

**Who decides when to switch?** **Hayes and Flower (1980)** proposed a **monitor** controlling the writing processes, closely resembling the **central executive**, whose functions include **switching attention** and **inhibiting unwanted responses**.

> **Key study, Quinlan et al. (2012).** If the monitor needs working memory, it should switch **less** under high demands. Offered the choice of fixing an error at once or **finishing the sentence first**, most participants finished the sentence, and **more so when total processing demands were high**.

#### Evaluation

Planning, sentence generation and revision are all essential but **cannot be neatly separated**, because writers move among them rapidly (Levy & Ransdell, 1995; Beauvais et al., 2011), probably under a **monitor** that shifts more readily when demands are **low** (Quinlan et al., 2012). Limitations: little is known about **what triggers** shifts; most research **neglects the social side** of writing, although the intended readership matters at every stage; and most models **do not specify processes or their interactions precisely** (e.g., **Hayes, 2012**, does not say how its **four resources** relate to the writing processes).

> **Trap.** Two different "30%" figures: **Kellogg (1994)** = the real share of time spent **planning**; **Levy and Ransdell (1995)** = writers' **mistaken estimate** of time spent reviewing and revising (actually **5%**).

### Writing expertise

As with any complex skill, **extensive deliberate practice** is essential. Writing loads limited-capacity working memory, and practice reduces that load, leaving experienced writers **spare capacity** for quality. Expert writers also have more **reading experience** and higher **reading comprehension** (**Daane, 1991**), since reading teaches structure and style and builds vocabulary and knowledge; but the evidence is mostly **correlational**, so causation is unclear.

#### Knowledge-telling, knowledge-transforming, knowledge-crafting

**Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987)** described two strategies:

- **Knowledge-telling**: writing down everything one knows about the topic with minimal planning, the text so far serving as the **retrieval cue** for what comes next.
- **Knowledge-transforming**: working in two **problem spaces**. The **rhetorical** space concerns achieving the task's goals (*Can I strengthen the argument?*); the **content** space concerns the specific information (*The case of Smith vs. Jones strengthens the argument*). Information should flow **both ways** between them.

Expert writers use **knowledge-transforming** more. **Kellogg (2008)** and **Kellogg and Whiteford (2012)** turned this into a **three-stage theory of the development of writing skill** (Figure 11.11), adding **knowledge-crafting**, in which the writer shapes content and expression **with the potential reader fully in mind**, anticipating how a reader might interpret the text and revising to allow for it.

| Stage | Focus | What drives the text |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge-telling** | The writer's knowledge | Association: written text cues the next idea |
| **Knowledge-transforming** | Content and rhetorical goals | Two-way traffic between the two problem spaces |
| **Knowledge-crafting** | The **reader's** interpretation | Anticipating readings and revising for them |

Knowledge-crafting counters the **knowledge effect**, the tendency to assume others know what we know: **Hayes and Bajzek (2008)** found that people familiar with technical terms **greatly overestimated** others' knowledge of them.

> **Trap.** **Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987)** gave **two** strategies (telling, transforming); **knowledge-crafting** is **Kellogg's (2008)** third stage. The **knowledge effect** (classic background: elsewhere called the "curse of knowledge") is a bias, not the knowledge-telling strategy.

#### Findings on expertise

Knowledge-transforming works because it involves **extensive interaction among planning, generation and reviewing**, and produces more **high-level main points** capturing key themes (**Bereiter et al., 1988**), giving texts **structure and coherence**.

> **Key study, Chuy et al. (2012).** Because knowledge-transforming uses a **content** space, more knowledge should help. Students given a **Knowledge Forum** (to post ideas, comment on others' and raise challenging questions) wrote essays that were **more coherent** and **easier to read and understand**.

**Kellogg and Whiteford (2012)** argue that "there is no such thing as an expert writer" in the sense of a generalist expert in every domain, because expertise depends on **relevant knowledge** and on the **type of text** (an advanced textbook versus a children's story). **Beauvais et al.'s (2011)** moderately expert writers used **more knowledge-telling** for **narrative** and **more knowledge-transforming** for **argumentative** texts.

Experts also **revise more and better**: writers of the **best** essays spent **40% more** of their time reviewing and revising than writers of the **worst** (**Levy & Ransdell, 1995**), and experts **detected 60% more problems** in a text (**Hayes et al., 1985**). Reader-responsiveness can be trained: telling students to **"read as the reader"** improved their revisions (**Holliway & McCutcheon, 2004**), and giving writers **actual readers' feedback** about their comprehension problems worked **better** (**Sato & Matsushima, 2006**).

### Working memory and writing

Writing combines attention, thinking and memory, and many theorists argue it relies heavily on **working memory** (reviews by **Kellogg & Whiteford, 2012**, and **Olive, 2012**): the writer must hold what has just been written while planning what comes next. The components are the **central executive** (attention-like coordination; the key component), the **visuo-spatial sketchpad** (visual and spatial processing) and the **phonological loop** (verbal rehearsal), all of **limited capacity**.

**Kellogg (2001)** linked the components to **five** writing processes. These overlap with Chenoweth and Hayes's: **planning** = proposing; **translating** = translating; **programming** (motor programming of the output) is part of transcribing; **reading** (going back over the text) and **editing** together form evaluating/revising.

> **Table 11.1: Working memory components in writing processes (as printed in the 7th edition, based on Kellogg, 2001)**
>
> | Process | Visuo-spatial sketchpad | Central executive | Phonological loop |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Planning | yes | yes | no |
> | Translating | no | yes | yes |
> | Programming | no | yes | no |
> | Reading | no | yes | yes |
> | Editing | yes | no | no |

The central executive is marked for four of the five processes, making it the dominant component.

> **Trap.** Answer from the table as printed: **programming** = central executive only; **translating** and **reading** = central executive plus phonological loop; **planning** = central executive plus sketchpad; **editing** is marked under the **sketchpad**, matching the edition's statement that Kellogg linked the sketchpad to **planning and editing**. **Kellogg et al. (2013)** later added the **phonological loop** to editing, and some other secondary sources place editing under the central executive, so read each option's exact claim.

#### Findings: the central executive

In the **probe reaction-time** method, participants respond to **auditory probes** alone (control) or while writing; the more executive capacity writing takes, the slower the responses. **Kellogg (2001)** found probe RTs much slower during **planning, translating, programming and reviewing**, especially the **reading** part of reviewing. **Kellogg et al. (2013)** later concluded that the **phonological loop** is involved in **editing**, citing Hayes and Chenoweth's (2006) finding that error correction while copying slowed when the loop was occupied; research since 2001 has firmly confirmed the **central executive's** key role.

> **Key study, Vanderberg and Swanson (2007).** Students' stories were scored for **general** skills (planning, translating, revision) and **specific** skills (grammar, punctuation). The best **central executive** functioning went with the best writing at **both** levels; individual differences in the **sketchpad** and **loop** had **no significant** effect.

**Connelly et al. (2012)** found that children's **essay quality** was predicted by **working memory capacity**, an approximate index of central executive function.

Clinically, frontal-lobe damage can impair the central executive (**dysexecutive syndrome**). **Ardila and Surloff (2006)** argued that many such patients have trouble **planning and organising ideas** in writing and **sustaining attention**, and named the condition **dysexecutive agraphia**.

> **Key study, Sitek et al. (2014).** All four members of **two sibling pairs** with **fronto-temporal dementia** had dysexecutive agraphia, and their writing deteriorated in step with their **general cognitive** decline.

#### Findings: the phonological loop

- **Chenoweth and Hayes (2003):** typing sentences about cartoons while **continuously repeating a syllable** (**articulatory suppression**, which occupies the loop) produced **shorter word sequences**, as if the writer's **"inner voice"** had been silenced.
- **Hayes and Chenoweth (2006):** articulatory suppression **slowed** transcription, including editing for errors.
- **Colombo et al. (2009):** writing down **three- and four-syllable** words heard or seen, participants under articulatory suppression were worse at producing the word's parts **in the correct order**.

The loop is often **used**, but writing may not **depend** on it: some patients with a **severely impaired phonological loop** have **essentially intact written language** (**Gathercole & Baddeley, 1993**).

#### Findings: the visuo-spatial sketchpad

Reviewed by **Olive and Passerault (2012)**:

- **Kellogg et al. (2007):** writing descriptions of **concrete** nouns (*house*, *pencil*) slowed detection of visual stimuli, but describing **abstract** nouns (*freedom*, *duty*) did not, so the sketchpad is engaged mainly by **concrete** content.
- **Olive et al. (2008):** both **visual** and **spatial** secondary tasks interfered with writing, so both sketchpad subcomponents are involved.
- **Galbraith et al. (2005):** testing Kellogg's claim about planning, they found a **spatial** task **reduced the number of ideas** and impaired their **structuring** more than a **visual** task did.

#### Evaluation

Writing makes heavy demands on working memory; **high working memory capacity** goes with good general and specific skills; the **central executive** is involved in most processes; and both the **sketchpad** (planning and editing) and **loop** (various processes) contribute. Limitations: it is not clear **why** planning, generation and revision are so demanding; little is known about how working memory governs **switching** between processes (Quinlan et al., 2012, is preliminary); and how the **components interact** during writing needs study.

### Word processing

Word processing has become common and **encourages revision** (**Lee & Stankov, 2012**). **Goldberg et al.'s (2003)** meta-analyses found that students who learned to write with computers were **more engaged** and produced **longer, higher-quality** work. The cost: **Kellogg and Mueller (1993)** found word processing makes **planning and revision** (but **not** sentence generation) more effortful than longhand, possibly because word-processor users were much **less likely to make notes** (**12% vs 69%**), leaving more to be held in mind.

## 11.8 Spelling

### Brain areas involved in handwriting

**Planton et al. (2013)** used a **meta-analysis** to find areas consistently active during **handwriting**, separating areas driven by the task's linguistic input from those driven by its motor output (Figure 11.12). Three stood out:

| Area | Hemisphere | Probable role |
|---|---|---|
| **Intraparietal sulcus** and **superior parietal lobule** | **Left** | Selecting and/or representing **letter shapes** |
| **Superior frontal sulcus** | **Left** | **Interface** between abstract letter combinations and **motor commands** |
| **Posterior cerebellum** | **Right** | Mainly **motor** activity |

These are probably parts of larger handwriting networks. Note the **right** cerebellum alongside **left** cortical areas; classic background: each cerebellar hemisphere works with the opposite cerebral hemisphere.

### The two-route model of spelling (Rapp & Dufor, 2011)

**Rapp and Dufor (2011)** sketched the processes involved in **spelling a heard word** (Figure 11.13). Two routes lead from hearing a word to spelling it:

1. **The lexical route.** It links a word's **phonological**, **semantic** and **orthographic** representations and retrieves detailed knowledge of **all** its features. Stored spellings are held in **orthographic long-term memory**, the **orthographic lexicon**. It is the main route for **familiar** words, whether the phoneme-grapheme relationship is **regular** (*cat*) or **irregular** (*yacht*), since a stored whole-word spelling makes irregularity irrelevant.
2. **The non-lexical route.** It bypasses knowledge of the word and applies **stored rules converting phonemes into graphemes** (**phonology-to-orthography conversion**). It is used for **unfamiliar words** and **non-words**, gives correct spellings for **regular** mappings (*cat*) and **systematic errors** for **irregular** ones (*yacht*, *comb*).

**Both routes converge** on **orthographic working memory**, the **graphemic buffer**, which briefly holds **abstract letters or letter groups** just before they are written or typed.

1. Heard word → **phonological lexicon** → **semantic system** → **orthographic lexicon** → **graphemic buffer** → writing or typing *(lexical route)*.
2. Heard word → **phonology-to-orthography conversion** → **graphemic buffer** → writing or typing *(non-lexical route)*.

The model predicts a **double dissociation** between two kinds of **dysgraphia** (impaired writing, including spelling): lose the non-lexical route and non-words fail while familiar words survive; lose the lexical route and irregular words fail while regular words and non-words survive.

### Phonological dysgraphia: only the lexical route is working

If the **non-lexical** route is barely usable but the **lexical** route is intact, familiar words will be spelled accurately from the orthographic lexicon, but **unfamiliar words and non-words**, which have no stored entry, will fail. This is **phonological dysgraphia**.

> **Key study, Shelton and Weinrich (1997): patient EA.** EA could not write **any** of **55 non-words** to dictation, yet wrote **50% of regular** and **45% of irregular** words correctly. Real words were impaired but far above zero, and regularity barely mattered, as expected if words come from stored knowledge.

**Cholewa et al. (2010)** found that **children** with phonological dysgraphia were much worse than controls at writing **non-words** and **irregular words**, especially non-words.

A plausible hypothesis is a **general phonological processing deficit**, not just a broken sound-to-letter converter, in which case these patients should fail phonological tasks that involve no spelling. **Cholewa et al. (2010)** confirmed this: on tasks such as judging whether two **spoken non-words** sound the same, or **repeating a non-word minus one consonant**, children with phonological dysgraphia were **considerably worse than controls on all** tests.

### Surface dysgraphia: only the non-lexical route is working

If the **lexical** route is damaged and the patient relies on **phoneme-grapheme conversion**, three predictions follow: misspellings that **sound like** the target, **some success with non-words**, and **regular** (consistent) words spelled better than **irregular** (inconsistent) ones. This is **surface dysgraphia**.

> **Key study, Macoir and Bernier (2002): patient MK.** MK spelled **92%** of **regular** but only **52%** of **irregular** words correctly.

**Cholewa et al. (2010)** found that children with surface dysgraphia spelled **56%** of irregular or inconsistent words **wrongly** but only **19%** of non-words wrongly, the reverse of the phonological dysgraphic children, for whom non-words were worst.

Two further predictions were confirmed:

- Surface dysgraphics should spell better the words for which they can reach **some semantic information**; **Macoir and Bernier (2002)** found exactly this with MK. **Bormann et al. (2009)** described **MO**, a male surface dysgraphic with very poor access to word information, who wrote the two German words *lass das* ("leave it") as the **single meaningless string** *lasdas*: sound-to-letter conversion with no lexical knowledge of word boundaries.
- Surface dysgraphics should have **no special phonological problem**. In Cholewa et al. (2010) they did **much better** than phonological dysgraphics on phonological tasks, though **still worse than controls**.

| | **Phonological dysgraphia** | **Surface dysgraphia** |
|---|---|---|
| Damaged route | **Non-lexical** (phoneme-grapheme conversion) | **Lexical** (orthographic lexicon) |
| Familiar irregular words | Reasonably good (EA: 45%) | **Poor** (MK: 52%) |
| Non-words | **Very poor** (EA: 0 of 55) | **Some success** (Cholewa: 19% wrong) |
| Typical errors | Failure on non-words | **Phonologically plausible** misspellings |
| General phonological tasks | **Impaired** | Better, but below controls |
| Cases | EA (Shelton & Weinrich, 1997) | MK (Macoir & Bernier, 2002); MO (Bormann et al., 2009) |

> **Trap.** The most important trap in the chapter, because the 7th edition contradicts itself. The body is clear: **phonological dysgraphia = non-lexical route lost, lexical route intact; surface dysgraphia = lexical route damaged, reliance on the non-lexical route.** But the subsection headings name the route that is **still working** ("Lexical route: phonological dysgraphia"), and the **chapter summary states the reverse**, attributing phonological dysgraphia to lexical-route damage and surface dysgraphia to non-lexical damage. Answer from the body: **phonological = cannot spell non-words; surface = cannot spell irregular words**. The same logic applies to phonological and surface **dyslexia** (classic background).

> **Trap.** Some texts reserve **agraphia** for acquired and **dysgraphia** for developmental writing disorders; this chapter uses **dysgraphia** for both (brain-damaged patients such as EA and MK, and Cholewa et al.'s children) and keeps **agraphia** for **dysexecutive agraphia** (11.7). The **graphemic buffer** is **orthographic working memory**, shared by both routes, not a third route.

### Are the two routes independent?

There is **increasing evidence that they interact**.

> **Key study, Rapp et al. (2002): patient LAT.** LAT, who had **Alzheimer's disease**, made many spelling errors but spelled **non-words** well, so his non-lexical route worked. Some errors blended the routes: *bouquet* as **BOUKET**, *knowledge* as **KNOLIGE**. The sound-based middles show the non-lexical route, but the silent final *t* of *bouquet* and initial *k* of *knowledge* could only come from the **orthographic lexicon**.

> **Key study, Martin and Barry (2012).** Asked to write the non-word **/vi:m/**, most people wrote **VEAM** just after hearing *dream* and **VEME** just after hearing *theme*: a recently activated **word** steers non-word spelling, so the lexical route influences the non-lexical one.

> **Key study, Delattre et al. (2006).** **Irregular** words make the two routes give **conflicting** outputs; regular words do not. If the routes interact, conflict should cost time, and irregular words did take **longer to write**.

### Overall evaluation of the two-route account

Spelling is surprisingly complex. The dysgraphias give reasonable support for a **lexical** and a **non-lexical** route, and the routes are now known to **interact**. Limitations: phonological dysgraphia may reflect a **general phonological** problem rather than a specific sound-to-letter one; little is known about **how** the routes interact; the precise **phoneme-grapheme rules** are unclear; and how the **three components of the lexical route** (phonological lexicon, semantic system, orthographic lexicon) combine is not known in detail.

### One or two orthographic lexicons?

Is one **orthographic lexicon** used for both reading and spelling (the simplest and most plausible view), or is there an **input lexicon** for reading and a separate **output lexicon** for spelling?

#### Evidence that seems to favour two

> **Key study, Tainturier et al. (2006): patient CWS.** CWS, a **58-year-old man** who had had a **stroke**, had severely impaired **spelling** but almost intact **reading**; for instance, he was very good at choosing the correct spelling from a sound-alike pair (*obey* vs *obay*).

Other patients read much better than they spell, and some show the opposite. But this falls well short of proof (**Rapp & Lipka, 2011**): patients whose **reading** is worse usually have damage to **visual** areas (e.g., **BA17/18**), and patients whose **spelling** is worse usually have **premotor** damage (e.g., **BA6**). The dissociation lies in the perceptual and motor machinery around the lexicon and says little about how many lexicons there are.

#### Evidence for a single lexicon

**Patients.** Most brain-damaged patients with **dyslexia** (impaired reading not due to low intelligence) also have **dysgraphia**, typically with damage to the **fusiform temporal gyrus (BA37)** and **inferior frontal gyrus (BA44/45)**, areas linked to the orthographic lexicon; and some patients have trouble with the **same specific words** in reading and writing (Rapp & Lipka, 2011).

> **Key study, Holmes and Carruthers (1998).** Healthy participants shown **five versions** of words they could not spell, including the **correct** spelling and **their own misspelling**, could **not** pick the correct one over their own. A separate, intact reading lexicon should have allowed recognition; the result suggests one representation serves both.

> **Key study, Rapp and Lipka (2011).** In healthy participants, the **mid-fusiform gyrus** and **inferior frontal gyrus** were strongly activated by **both** spelling and reading (Figure 11.14), with **activation peaks very close together**. **Rapp and Dufor (2011)** obtained very similar results with other spelling tasks.

> **Key study, Tsapkini and Rapp (2010): patient DPT.** Damage to the **left fusiform gyrus** left DPT with **severely impaired spelling and reading** but **intact spoken-language processing**.

Parts of the **left fusiform gyrus** appear central to the orthographic lexicon; the **inferior frontal gyrus** is used in spelling and writing, but its exact role is unclear.

#### Evaluation

The issue is **unresolved**, but converging patient, behavioural and imaging evidence favours a **single orthographic lexicon**, most closely tied to the **fusiform temporal gyrus (BA37)**. Limitations: the question is so complex that **convincing evidence is hard to get**; shared brain activity does **not necessarily** mean a shared lexicon; and since reading and spelling are usually **taught as separate skills**, two lexicons should not be dismissed outright.

> **Trap.** **CWS** (58, stroke): spelling impaired, reading spared, the case **for two** lexicons. **DPT** (left fusiform damage): both impaired, speech spared, evidence **for one**. Reading-worse = **BA17/18**; spelling-worse = **BA6**; single-lexicon site = **BA37**. The verdict is "**probably one**, unresolved".

## How the chapter fits together

1. **The interaction question.** Every theoretical section asks how far the levels of production overlap and feed back. Dell says a lot, Levelt almost none, Goldrick (2006) "limited interaction", and Mädebach et al. (2011) show the amount depends on processing demands. The same question returns in anomia (Soni et al., 2009, 2011: misleading sound cues harm naming) and in spelling (BOUKET; VEAM/VEME: the routes interact).
2. **Errors are informative because they are systematic.** Exchanges reveal planning span, semantic substitutions reveal frame-before-word, morpheme exchanges reveal separate placement of stems and inflections, mixed errors reveal interaction, neologisms reveal phonological breakdown, and dysgraphic misspellings reveal which spelling route is running.
3. **Production is resource-limited.** Preformulation and underspecification save effort; planning shrinks under time pressure and load; agreement errors rise under memory load; interaction collapses under high demand; perspective-taking needs inhibitory control; writing leans on the central executive; time pressure alone can mimic aphasic naming errors (Hodgson & Lambon Ralph, 2008).
4. **Monitoring is a component.** Comprehension-based (Levelt, 1983) and conflict-based (Nozari et al., 2011) detection; the editor behind lexical bias (Corley et al., 2011); the failed monitor of jargon aphasia; the evaluator and monitor in writing models.
5. **Speaker-centred versus listener-centred.** Speakers design for the audience when needs are clear and cheap to track, otherwise they say what is easy; gesture serves both parties; in writing the same axis runs from knowledge-telling to knowledge-crafting, with the knowledge effect as the obstacle.
6. **Dissociations are evidence, not proof of modules.** Broca/Wernicke is oversimplified; agrammatism is not a syndrome; the dysgraphias dissociate yet interact; reading-spelling dissociations reflect input and output machinery, not two lexicons.
7. **In decline, content goes before form.** Early Alzheimer's speech is fluent but empty (Ahmed et al., 2013), and novelists lose vocabulary before syntax (Garrard et al., 2005; Le et al., 2011).

## Worked example

*Mr K., a 64-year-old retired schoolteacher, has had a left-hemisphere stroke. His speech is fluent and sounds sentence-like but is full of non-existent and wrong words, and he becomes irritated when his family cannot follow him. Picture naming is poor; a correct first-sound cue sometimes helps, but a wrong one makes him worse. He is better on some days than others. To dictation he spells* yacht *and* colour *correctly but fails almost every simple non-word (*blick*). His wife adds that her 78-year-old mother has lately been "talking a lot but saying nothing", although she speaks smoothly and makes no obvious errors. Do they have "the same thing"?*

**Label or description?** "Wernicke's aphasia" is a reasonable first label, but the 7th edition calls such labels oversimplified (heterogeneous symptoms, overlapping lesions, networked pathways). Describe the specific impairments instead.

**The speech disorder.** Fluent speech, largely intact grammar, gross word-finding failure, neologisms and unawareness fit **jargon aphasia**, the mirror image of agrammatism. Checking whether his neologisms carry the right affixes for their slots (Butterworth, 1985) confirms that syntax is engaged.

**Where the neologisms come from.** Neologisms sharing sounds with the target point to access followed by **phonological** distortion; neologisms recycling recently used sounds point to residual activation (Goldman et al., 2001); if related and unrelated neologisms respond alike to frequency, imageability and length, a single deficit is likely (Olson et al., 2007).

**Why he is unaware.** Deficient **self-monitoring** is central: less monitoring goes with more jargon, and masking one's own voice increases it (Sampson & Faroqi-Shah, 2011); TK's recovery tracked improved monitoring (Eaton et al., 2011). His irritation is a symptom, and the family should be told so.

**Locus of the anomia.** A semantic (lemma) failure would give meaning-related errors and poor rejection of near neighbours (like JCU); a phonological failure would look like a magnified TOT state (like EST). ERPs would separate them: early (100 to 250 ms) versus late (300 to 450 ms) abnormality (Laganaro et al., 2008). With neologisms, a phonological locus is more likely.

**The cueing pattern.** Harm from a wrong first sound matches Soni et al. (2009): phonology feeds back into word selection, supporting **interactive** over strictly serial models. Clinically, cues must be accurate, because misleading cues do active harm.

**Day-to-day variation.** This fits McNeil et al. (2010): aphasic patients also have general **attention and memory** problems. Assess those too, and test on more than one occasion.

**The spelling disorder.** Correct irregular words with failed non-words means the **lexical route works** and the **non-lexical route does not**: **phonological dysgraphia**, like EA (0 of 55 non-words). Do not be misled by the chapter summary's reversed wording. Cholewa et al. (2010) predict that he will also fail non-spelling phonological tasks, consistent with the phonological impairment Harley (2013) places at the heart of jargon aphasia, and both point to **phonologically focused therapy** like that which helped FF (Bose, 2013).

**Reading.** If he misreads and misspells the **same** words, a single orthographic lexicon in the left fusiform region is implicated (as in DPT). If reading is spared, remember Rapp and Lipka's (2011) caution that such dissociations usually reflect perceptual or motor machinery.

**The mother-in-law.** Not the same thing. **Fluent, error-free, empty** speech is the profile of **MCI** and early **Alzheimer's disease** (Ahmed et al., 2013): lexical and semantic content fall first, fluency holds, syntax declines later. Semantic content is the measure to track; her letters might show shrinking vocabulary, repeated phrases and fillers (Le et al., 2011). She needs a cognitive and memory assessment, not a focal-aphasia work-up.

## MCQ traps

1. **Dell's four levels:** semantic, syntactic, morphological, phonological; **morpheme = meaning**, **phoneme = sound**.
2. **2 to 3 words a second (about 150 a minute)**; about **70%** of speech is reused combinations (**Altenberg, 1990**).
3. **Preformulation** = stock phrases (Kuiper, 1996: auctioneers, commentators); **underspecification** = vague expressions (De Cock, 2004).
4. **Gould (1978):** dictation at most **35%** faster though speech is **5 to 6 times** faster; **Gould (1980):** planning = **two-thirds** of the time.
5. **Hollien et al. (2001):** intoxication **tripled** dysfluencies, slowed speech, **raised** pitch; **Reichel and Kisler (2012):** **162** speakers, only **men** declined.
6. **Ahmed et al. (2013):** MCI speech **fluent but empty**; lexical and semantic content first, syntax later.
7. **Butterworth (1984)** = Reagan's speeches, **ten years** before diagnosis; **Butterworth (1985)** = jargon neologisms carry correct affixes.
8. **One error per 500 sentences** (Vigliocco & Hartsuiker, 2002).
9. **Wagner et al. (2010):** **more** planning by **slow** speakers, before **simple** sentences, under **low** load.
10. **Word exchanges** span phrases within a **clause**; **sound exchanges** span **shorter** distances.
11. **Semantic substitutions** keep the part of speech **99%** of the time ("syntactic traffic cop", Dell et al., 2008).
12. **Morpheme exchange** (*trunked two packs*): inflections **stay**, stems **move**.
13. **Mirković and MacDonald (2013):** **Serbian**; **several** draws more plural verbs than **many**.
14. **Perceptual loop = Levelt (1983)**, comprehension-based; **conflict-based = Nozari et al. (2011)**, production-based.
15. **Nozari et al. (2011):** **29** aphasics; comprehension **r = -0.01**; semantic **-0.59**; phonological **-0.43**.
16. **Blackmer and Mitton (1991):** **19%** of radio callers' corrections immediate; **Huettig and Hartsuiker (2010):** **overt** speech monitored.
17. **Dell = parallel, interactive, error data; WEAVER++ = serial, discrete, feedforward, RT data**; "limited interaction" = **Goldrick (2006)**.
18. **Mixed-error effect** = meaning **and** sound (*priest* to *none*; Ferreira & Griffin, 2003).
19. **Lexical bias vanished** when half the targets were non-words (**Corley et al., 2011**).
20. **Anticipatory proportion:** **0.37 to 0.59** with practice; **0.75** (low error rate) vs **0.40** (high); lower with fast speech and cognitive impairment.
21. **WEAVER** = Word-form Encoding by Activation and VERification; **lemma** = syntax plus semantics, **no** phonology.
22. **van Turennout et al. (1998):** **Dutch**, gender **40 ms** before first phoneme.
23. **Indefrey (2011):** **200 / 75 ms**; **20 ms per phoneme**, **50 to 55 ms per syllable**; articulation about **600 ms**.
24. **TOT:** **Italian 85%** gender (Vigliocco et al., 1997); **German** result against WEAVER++ (Biedermann et al., 2008).
25. **Meyer and Damian (2007):** related distractor pictures **speeded** naming, against WEAVER++.
26. **Mädebach et al. (2011):** **high** demand = **serial**; **low** demand = **interactive**.
27. **Broca = BA44/45 (IFG); Wernicke = posterior BA22 (STG)**; English hides deficits because it is **less inflected** (Dick et al., 2001).
28. **Hodgson and Lambon Ralph (2008):** a **500 ms** deadline made healthy people err like semantic aphasics.
29. **JCU** = semantic anomia (**86%** rejection); **EST** = phonological anomia (magnified TOT); ERPs **100 to 250** vs **300 to 450 ms**.
30. **Soni et al. (2009, 2011):** misleading cues **impaired** naming, supporting interactive models.
31. **Agrammatism is not a syndrome**; Broca's area computes **dependency relations**; caption "BA33/45" = **BA44/45**.
32. **Burkhardt et al. (2008):** slow but successful syntax = **processing** limit; **Christianson et al. (2010):** sequence-learning deficit.
33. **Griffiths et al. (2013):** pathway damage raised **syntactic**, not semantic, errors; **two pathways**.
34. **Jargon aphasia:** grammar spared, words lost, **unaware**; masking **increased** jargon; VS **84**, FF **77**, TK **21 months**.
35. **Grice:** relevance, quantity, quality, manner; overinformative **30%** (Engelhardt et al., 2006) vs **8%** (Davies & Katsos, 2013).
36. **Horton and Keysar (1996):** egocentric plan, then adjust; **Wardlow (2013):** **r = +0.42** with inhibitory control.
37. **Syntactic priming:** no awareness needed; strongest after **unexpected** structure (Jaeger & Snider, 2013).
38. **Gerwing and Allison (2011):** same **number** of gestures; **74%** vs **27%** of information gestural; gesture blocked = **21%** fewer words.
39. **Kraljic and Brennan (2005):** prosody produced **regardless** of listener need.
40. **Bolden (2006):** ***oh* 98.5%** speaker-related; ***so* 96%** listener-related.
41. **Hayes and Flower (1986):** 3 processes; **Chenoweth and Hayes (2003):** + **transcriber**; **Hayes (2012):** + **motivation**.
42. **Kellogg (1994):** **30/50/20**; **Levy and Ransdell (1995):** revising estimated **30%**, actual **5%**; best essays **40%** more revising.
43. **Kaufer et al. (1986):** **11.2** vs **7.3** words per sentence part; **Hayes et al. (1985):** experts find **60%** more problems.
44. **Telling and transforming** = Bereiter and Scardamalia (1987); **crafting** = Kellogg (2008).
45. **Vanderberg and Swanson (2007):** only the **central executive** predicted writing; **Gathercole and Baddeley (1993):** impaired loop, intact writing.
46. **Kellogg and Mueller (1993):** notes by **12%** of word-processor users vs **69%** of longhand writers.
47. **Phonological dysgraphia = non-lexical route damaged; surface = lexical route damaged**; the chapter summary **reverses** this.
48. **EA:** **0/55** non-words, **50%** regular, **45%** irregular; **MK:** **92%** vs **52%**; **Cholewa:** **56%** vs **19%** wrong.
49. **Route interaction:** **BOUKET** (Rapp et al., 2002); **VEAM/VEME** (Martin & Barry, 2012); irregular words slower (Delattre et al., 2006).
50. **Single orthographic lexicon** probably, at **BA37** (fusiform); reading-worse **BA17/18**, spelling-worse **BA6**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Preformulation** | Reusing often-produced phrases to cut the cost of speaking |
| **Underspecification** | Using simplified expressions that leave full meaning implicit, to save effort |
| **Morphemes** | Smallest units of meaning; a word has one or more |
| **Phonemes** | Smallest units of sound that distinguish words |
| **Alzheimer's disease** | Progressive loss of mental abilities from general brain degeneration |
| **Mild cognitive impairment** | Minor memory and thinking problems that often precede Alzheimer's disease |
| **Clause** | Part of a sentence with a subject and a verb |
| **Phrase** | Part of a sentence expressing a single idea |
| **Spoonerism** | Slip in which the initial sounds of two nearby words swap, forming two other words |
| **Freudian slip** | Slip thought to reveal the speaker's (often unconscious) sexual desires |
| **Semantic substitution / morpheme-exchange / number-agreement error** | Similar-meaning word replaces target / stems swap while inflections stay / verb mismatches subject in number |
| **Perceptual loop theory** | Levelt's view that errors are caught by hearing one's own speech via comprehension |
| **Conflict-based monitoring** | Nozari et al.'s view that errors are detected from competition inside production |
| **Spreading activation** | Activation of one node passing some activation to related nodes |
| **Insertion rules** | Choose the most active item of the right category, then reset it to zero |
| **Mixed-error effect** | Errors related to the target in both meaning and sound |
| **Lexical bias effect** | Tendency for errors to be real words rather than non-words |
| **Anticipatory / perseveratory error** | A sound or word produced too early / carried over too late |
| **Anticipatory proportion** | Anticipations divided by anticipations plus perseverations |
| **WEAVER++** | Levelt et al.'s serial, discrete, feedforward model of single-word production |
| **Lemmas** | Abstract words with syntactic and semantic features but no phonological ones |
| **Lexicalisation** | Turning a word's meaning into its sound form during speaking |
| **Tip-of-the-tongue state** | Having the concept but being unable to retrieve the word's sound |
| **Picture-word interference task** | Naming pictures while ignoring simultaneous distractor words |
| **Aphasia** | Brain-damage-induced problems in understanding and/or producing language |
| **Broca's aphasia** | Non-fluent speech with grammatical errors; BA44/45 |
| **Wernicke's aphasia** | Fluent speech missing many content words, with poor comprehension; posterior BA22 |
| **Inflected language** | Language marking grammar by changing the words themselves |
| **Anomia** | Brain-damage-induced difficulty naming objects |
| **Agrammatism** | Speech lacking grammatical structure, omitting function words and endings |
| **Jargon aphasia** | Fairly grammatical speech with severe word-finding failure |
| **Neologisms** | Made-up words produced in jargon aphasia |
| **Cooperative Principle / Grice's maxims** | Speakers and listeners should cooperate / relevance, quantity, quality, manner |
| **Audience design** | Tailoring speech to the needs of a particular audience |
| **Common ground** | Information shared by speaker and listener (co-present, mentioned, or cultural) |
| **Monitoring and adjustment model** | Plan egocentrically, then correct for common ground (Horton & Keysar, 1996) |
| **Syntactic priming** | Reusing the sentence structure one has just heard |
| **Prosodic cues** | Stress, intonation, rhythm and duration signalling structure and meaning |
| **Discourse markers** | Words or phrases (*oh*, *so*, *you know*) with functions but no content |
| **Proposer / translator / transcriber / evaluator** | Chenoweth and Hayes's four writing processes |
| **Directed retrospection** | Interrupting writers to classify what they were just doing |
| **Knowledge-telling / -transforming / -crafting** | Writing what one knows / interplay of rhetorical and content spaces / writing for the reader's interpretation |
| **Knowledge effect** | Assuming others know what we know |
| **Articulatory suppression** | Repeating a sound to occupy the phonological loop |
| **Dysexecutive agraphia** | Writing impaired by frontal damage to executive (planning, organising) functions |
| **Lexical / non-lexical route** | Spelling from stored word knowledge / by phoneme-grapheme rules |
| **Orthographic lexicon** | Long-term store of word spellings |
| **Graphemic buffer** | Orthographic working memory holding letters just before output |
| **Phonological dysgraphia** | Familiar words spelled reasonably well, non-words very poorly |
| **Surface dysgraphia** | Irregular words spelled poorly, regular words well, some non-words correct |
| **Dyslexia** | Impaired reading not explained by low intelligence |
| **Dysgraphia** | Impaired writing, including spelling |

## Likely exam points

1. **Speaking vs writing:** shared planning and knowledge (Gould, 1978, 1980; Hartley et al., 2003), four differences, dissociating patients (EB).
2. **Effort-saving strategies:** preformulation (70%; Kuiper, 1996), underspecification; alcohol studies (Hollien et al., 2001; Reichel & Kisler, 2012).
3. **Dell's four levels** and the claim that later levels can run alongside earlier ones.
4. **Alzheimer's and speech:** Butterworth (1984); Ahmed et al. (2013) fluent but empty speech.
5. **Planning scope and flexibility:** Lee et al. (2013); clause (Holmes, 1988) vs phrase (Martin et al., 2004); Ferreira and Swets (2002); Wagner et al. (2010).

7. **Error detection:** perceptual loop vs conflict-based; Nozari et al. (2011) correlations; Blackmer and Mitton (1991); limitations.
