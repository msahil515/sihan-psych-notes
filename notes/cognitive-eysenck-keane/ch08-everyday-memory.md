# Chapter 8: Everyday Memory

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapters 6 and 7 study memory the way a laboratory does: present a list, impose a delay, count what comes back. This chapter follows memory into the jobs it does in real life. Three dominate. **Autobiographical memory**, the store of your own life story, turns out to be organised around the self and its goals rather than around accuracy. **Eyewitness testimony** makes memory into courtroom evidence, where its fragility can send an innocent person to prison. **Prospective memory**, remembering to do things in the future, can kill when pilots or air traffic controllers get it wrong.

One idea runs underneath all three: everyday memory serves purposes (keeping friendships, building a sense of self, impressing an audience, getting through the day's plans), and accuracy is only one goal among several. Seeing memory as purposeful action explains why we remember a flattering version of our past, why depressed patients cannot retrieve specific memories, why retelling a story changes what you later recall, and why misleading questions can rewrite what a witness saw.

The chapter is also unusually clinical for a cognitive text: over-general memory in depression, checking in OCD, flashbacks in PTSD, the amnesic patient KC, and Jill Price's exceptional memory with obsessional features.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 8.1 contrasts traditional and everyday memory research and defines ecological validity. Sections 8.2 to 8.4 form one block on autobiographical memory: how it differs from episodic memory, flashbulb memories, childhood amnesia and the reminiscence bump, then Conway's self-memory system, its brain basis, and depression. Sections 8.5 and 8.6 cover eyewitness testimony and how to improve it (line-ups, the cognitive interview); they carry most of the chapter's percentages. Sections 8.7 and 8.8 cover prospective memory: its stages and types, its real-world failures, and the argument between PAM theory and multi-process theory.

## 8.1 Introduction

### What "everyday memory" means

Research on everyday memory, the study of how people use memory in their daily lives, has grown rapidly over roughly the last 35 years. The book's key observation, drawing on **Cohen (2008)**, is that much of everyday memory is bound up with goals and motives. The clearest case is **prospective memory**, remembering to carry out an intended action, because an intention exists to serve some current goal: Eysenck's example is meaning to track down an article in order to finish a textbook chapter.

### Traditional versus everyday memory research: four differences

The chapter sets out four ways in which everyday memory differs from memory as traditionally studied in the laboratory.

| | Traditional laboratory research | Everyday memory research |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Age of the memory** | Material presented minutes or hours earlier | Events from long ago that have been thought about and retold many times since |
| **2. Type of learning** | Intentional: the instructions determine what is learned | Incidental: people pick up what is relevant to their goals and interests |
| **3. Social factors** | Usually absent | Often important |
| **4. Motivation** | Participants try to be as accurate as possible | Remembering serves personal goals; accuracy is often not the main aim |

The first difference has a memorable consequence. Because real-life memories have been rehearsed and retold so often, Cohen (2008) points out that what we recall in daily life is very often a memory **of a previous act of remembering** ("memories of memories"), not of the original event.

The fourth difference is described by the book as the crucial one, and it comes from **Neisser (1996)**, who argued that "Remembering is a form of purposeful action". That slogan carries three assumptions about everyday memory:

1. It is **purposeful**, that is, motivated.
2. It has a **personal quality**: it is shaped by the rememberer's personality and other individual characteristics.
3. It is shaped by **situational demands**, such as wanting to impress the people you are talking to.

Put compactly, Neisser's position is that in daily life our personal goals decide what we remember, while in the laboratory the experimenter's demand for accuracy decides it. Sometimes real life does demand maximum accuracy (an examination is the book's example), but usually accuracy is not our main aim.

### Findings: deliberate distortion and the saying-is-believing effect

If everyday remembering serves goals, people should sometimes knowingly bend what they report. **Marsh and Tversky (2004)** tested this by asking students to keep a record of every occasion on which they retold a personal memory over the course of a month. The students themselves admitted that **42%** of their retellings were inaccurate: knowing distortions, not hidden memory errors.

Does distorting what you *say* change what you later *remember*? Very often it does. **Dudokovic et al. (2004)** had participants read a story and then retell it three times, either **accurately** (the laboratory goal) or **entertainingly** (a typical real-world goal). The entertaining versions were, unsurprisingly, more emotional and less detailed. The critical phase came when everyone later tried to recall the story accurately: the entertaining tellers now recalled fewer events and details and were less accurate than the accurate tellers. The way of telling had changed the memory itself.

This is the **saying-is-believing effect**: shaping your account of an event to suit a particular audience produces later inaccuracies in your own memory of that event.

**Hellmann et al. (2011)** extended the effect to something closer to testimony. Participants watched a video of a fight between two men in a pub. They then described the fight to a student who, they had been told, believed that one of the men (person A) was, or alternatively was not, to blame. The participants' descriptions drifted towards the listener's bias. On a later free-recall test they had not been warned about, their memory of the crime itself had shifted in the direction of their retelling, and those whose retellings had been most biased showed the most distorted recall. A witness who has retold the story to sympathetic listeners may no longer have access to what they originally saw.

> **Trap.** The saying-is-believing effect is about the **teller's own memory** being altered by having tailored a message to an audience. It is not the misinformation effect (8.5), where the distorting information comes **from someone else** after the event, and it is not confirmation bias, where the distortion comes from the witness's **prior expectations**. Three different sources of error: your own retelling, someone else's post-event information, your own prior beliefs.

### What should be done? Ecological validity

The natural conclusion is that memory research should have **ecological validity**, meaning applicability to real life. Eysenck and Keane split ecological validity into two components:

1. **Representativeness**: how natural the experimental situation, stimuli and task are.
2. **Generalisability**: how far the study's findings apply to the real world.

Of the two, **generalisability is the more important**: a study with artificial materials can still produce findings that hold in real life.

The book then corrects two common assumptions. First, everyday memory research does not automatically have more ecological validity than laboratory research; well-controlled naturalistic experiments whose tasks and conditions resemble real life can achieve it in the laboratory. Second, the claim that the two traditions are mutually antagonistic is wrong on two counts: the boundary between them is blurred, and there is much cross-fertilisation between them.

> **Trap.** If an option says ecological validity consists of "internal and external validity", or that "representativeness is more important than generalisability", it is wrong for this book. The two components are **representativeness** and **generalisability**, and **generalisability matters more**. "Everyday memory research is always more ecologically valid" is presented as a mistaken assumption.

## 8.2 Autobiographical Memory: Introduction

### What autobiographical memory is

Memories of our own experiences, especially those that matter to us, have a special status among everything we remember. Together they make up **autobiographical memory**: long-term memory for the events of your own life.

The obvious question is how this differs from **episodic memory** (memory for events that happened at a particular time and place, introduced in Chapter 7). There is one important similarity: both concern **personally experienced events**. But the book lists **four differences**:

1. **Personal significance.** Autobiographical memory concerns events that matter to the person. Episodic memory, sometimes called "laboratory memory", is often about trivial events, such as whether the word *chair* was in the first list.
2. **Complexity and scope.** Autobiographical memories are typically complex and are selected from an enormous collection of personal experiences. Episodic memory as studied in the laboratory is much narrower in scope.
3. **Time span.** Autobiographical memory reaches back over years or decades; laboratory episodic memory often reaches back only minutes or hours.
4. **Semantic content.** Some aspects of autobiographical memory are really **semantic memory** (general knowledge) rather than episodic memory (**Prebble et al., 2013**). Some brain-damaged patients with little or no episodic memory can still report facts about themselves, such as what kind of personality they have (**Klein & Lax, 2010**).

> **Trap.** Autobiographical memory is **not** simply a synonym for episodic memory, as is sometimes assumed. They overlap (both concern personally experienced events) but differ in significance, complexity, time span, and the **semantic** self-knowledge autobiographical memory contains. A question asking for the one *similarity* wants "both concern personally experienced events".

### Do they differ in the brain?

> **Key study, Gilboa (2004).** A review of meta-analytic neuroimaging evidence comparing episodic and autobiographical retrieval. Episodic memory produced much more activation in the **right mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**; autobiographical memory produced much more activation in the **left ventromedial prefrontal cortex**.

Gilboa's interpretation links each region to a different kind of checking. Laboratory episodic tasks demand **conscious monitoring to keep errors down** (was that word really on the list?), which fits dorsolateral prefrontal involvement. Autobiographical retrieval involves checking whether a retrieved memory **fits what you know about yourself**, which fits ventromedial involvement. (Figure 8.1: dorsolateral is towards the top and side of the frontal lobe, ventromedial towards the bottom and middle.)

> **Key study, Burianova et al. (2010).** The same photographs were used in every condition, and only the retrieval demand changed. Shown a picture of a tent, a participant might be asked to recall a camping trip of their own (autobiographical), the colour of the tent (episodic), or how many campsites there are in some area (semantic). All three kinds of retrieval activated a **common network of frontal, temporal and parietal areas**.

Because the stimulus was held constant, any difference had to come from the retrieval demand, and what Burianova found was mostly shared activity: important similarities that balance Gilboa's differences.

### Why do we recall our past at all?

**Bluck and Alea (2009)** identified three key reasons why we spend so much time recalling our past:

1. **Maintaining social bonds**, for instance through shared memories.
2. **Directing future behaviour**, using the past as a guide.
3. **Creating a sense of self-continuity** over time.

Autobiographical memories of traumatic childhood experiences (the recovered-memory debate) are dealt with in Chapter 7, not here.

### Flashbulb memories: the original claim

Most people believe they have very clear, lasting memories of where they were when they heard about some dramatic public event, such as the terrorist attacks on the United States on 11 September 2001. **Brown and Kulik (1977)** called these **flashbulb memories**: vivid, detailed memories of dramatic events.

Their theory was that an event which is **surprising** and which has **real consequences for the individual** triggers a special neural mechanism that, in effect, "prints" the details of the event permanently into memory, like a camera flash fixing a scene.

> **Classic background:** The "printing" idea came from a neurophysiological proposal by Livingston (1967), usually called the "Now Print!" mechanism, which Brown and Kulik borrowed. Many textbooks still use the phrase.

According to Brown and Kulik (1977), a flashbulb memory typically contains six kinds of information:

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Informant** | Who told you the news |
| **Place** | Where you were when you heard |
| **Ongoing event** | What you were doing at the time |
| **Own affect** | How you felt |
| **Affect in others** | How the people around you felt |
| **Aftermath** | What the event meant for you, its consequences |

The 7th edition makes a historical correction. Brown and Kulik are often portrayed as claiming that flashbulb memories are a different kind of memory, marked out by longevity, accuracy and a dedicated neural mechanism, but their actual position was more moderate (**Curci & Conway, 2013**). They themselves wrote that a flashbulb memory is "only somewhat indiscriminate and is very far from complete" (Brown & Kulik, 1977, p. 75): never a complete photographic record.

> **Trap.** Two versions of the flashbulb story circulate. The popular, older version says Brown and Kulik proposed that flashbulb memories are **accurate, permanent and special**, produced by a "Now Print!" mechanism. The 7th edition says this exaggerates their claim, and that the evidence shows flashbulb memories are **vivid and confidently held but not especially accurate or consistent**. For what the *evidence* shows, choose the modern answer; for what Brown and Kulik *proposed*, the special-mechanism answer is correct.

### In the real world: exceptional autobiographical memory

Most people wish they could remember more of their past; a few have the opposite problem. **Parker et al. (2006)** described **Jill Price**, a woman born in 1965 who can recall detailed information about almost every day of her life across several decades. They coined the term **hyperthymestic syndrome** (from Greek roots meaning "remembering" and "more than usual") for this exceptional ability to remember the events of one's own life.

Two features are counter-intuitive. First, she experiences her memory as a **burden rather than a gift**, because her whole life runs through her head every day. Second, her **memory in general is ordinary**: her word-list recall is average, and she struggles to remember which of the five keys on her key-ring opens which lock.

The book gives three reasons for her remarkable autobiographical memory:

1. **Obsessional tendencies.** She spends most of her time thinking about herself and her past, and has herself described the problem as OCD of her memories.
2. **Poor inhibition.** She finds it very hard to switch her personal memories off.
3. **Spatial representation of time.** She makes time concrete by laying it out in space; for example, she pictures the year as a circle with January at the 11 o'clock position, moving anticlockwise. Linking time to space in this way is often associated with very good memory (**Simner et al., 2009**).

**LePort et al. (2012)** studied **11** people with hyperthymestic syndrome in detail. When given random dates, they were **eight times better** than controls at recalling autobiographical experiences associated with those dates. About **80%** showed obsessional characteristics similar to Jill Price's. Yet, like her, they were only **average on several standard laboratory memory tasks**. **Patihis et al. (2013)** added that such people are **as prone to false memories** as ordinary controls. Theoretically, LePort et al.'s most important finding was structural: these individuals differed from controls in regions of an autobiographical memory network, such as the **parahippocampal gyrus** and **anterior insula**.

A further case is **HK**, a blind man tested at **20** years of age (**Ally et al., 2013**). Emotional material is remembered better when the amygdala is activated, and HK's **right amygdala was about 20% larger** than in most people. He also showed **enhanced connectivity between the amygdala and the hippocampus**.

The evaluation point concerns direction of causation: it is not yet established whether these brain differences **partly cause** the exceptional memory or are a **consequence** of it.

> **Trap.** Hyperthymestic individuals are **not** generally superior memorisers. They are average on standard laboratory tasks (word lists, Jill Price's keys) and **just as susceptible to false memories** as anyone else, so "immune to false memories" or "superior memory across all tasks" is wrong. Classic background: the same condition is also called **hyperthymesia** or **highly superior autobiographical memory (HSAM)**, LePort and colleagues' own term.

### Flashbacks: an intense relative of flashbulb memories

Some people exposed to traumatic events such as war or terrorist attacks develop **post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**, in which the trauma is re-experienced as intrusive memories. Of special interest are **flashbacks**: intense, sensory-perceptual, emotional memories of a traumatic event that push into consciousness involuntarily (**Bourne et al., 2013**). The book suggests that flashbacks may be an **intense form of flashbulb memory**.

> **Trap.** **Flashbulb memory** = a vivid memory of the circumstances in which you heard about a dramatic (often public) event, deliberately recalled. **Flashback** = an involuntary, intrusive, sensory re-experiencing of a personally experienced trauma, characteristic of PTSD. The book links them but does not equate them.

### Findings: how accurate and consistent are flashbulb memories?

Flashbulb memories are often assumed to be highly accurate. Often they are not.

> **Key study, Pezdek (2003).** American students were asked whether, on 11 September 2001, they had seen the videotape of the **first** plane hitting the **first** tower on television. On that day, only footage of the **second** tower being hit was available. Even so, **73%** said yes.

Accuracy is often impossible to check (nobody recorded how you felt when you first heard the news), so researchers use **consistency over time**: a permanently printed memory should not change between tests.

> **Key study, Talarico and Rubin (2003).** Students' memories of 9/11 were compared with their memories of an ordinary everyday event over **32 weeks**. Throughout that period, students rated their flashbulb memories as **much more vivid** than the everyday memories. But the flashbulb memories were **no more consistent** than the everyday ones; both changed to a similar degree.

This is the key flashbulb finding: what sets flashbulb memories apart is **confidence and vividness**, not accuracy. People believed their memories were far stronger than they proved to be.

Other studies found more consistency. **Kvavilashvili et al. (2009)** reported that most people's 9/11 memories were **reasonably consistent for up to three years**. The very few who showed major distortions had heard the news from several sources and confused them, a source problem of the kind that reappears in eyewitness testimony.

> **Key study, Rimmele et al. (2012), part 1.** 9/11 memories were compared at **one week** and **three years** after the event. Consistency depended on which feature was being remembered: **location** where the news was heard was highly consistent (**83%**), whereas **informant** (70%), **ongoing activity** (62%) and especially **own immediate reaction** (**34%**) were less consistent.

> **Key study, Rimmele et al. (2012), part 2.** Participants saw emotionally negative or neutral scenes with coloured dots added in the centre. For the **negative** scenes, memory for **time and place** was good, but memory for the **coloured dots** was **poorer** than with neutral scenes.

Together the two parts explain why flashbulb memories feel so complete. Strong negative emotion **selectively enhances** some features (where and when) while leaving others no better or worse, and vivid memory for those few features creates an **illusion** that the whole scene was stored clearly.

**Sharot et al. (2007)** argued that a genuine flashbulb memory might require an intense emotional experience.

> **Key study, Sharot et al. (2007).** Three years after 9/11, people who had been close to the World Trade Center on the day (about **2 miles**) were compared with people somewhat further away (about **4½ miles**). The flashbulb memories of those closer to the event were **more vivid**, **more detailed**, and associated with **more amygdala activation**.

The intensity of the emotional reaction to the event matters.

The final findings concern **flashbacks**. **Berntsen (2001)** studied students who all met criteria for PTSD, some of whom had experienced their trauma **more than five years earlier** and some **within the past year**. Both groups had **equally frequent and vivid flashbacks**, showing that trauma memories are remarkably persistent and suggesting that flashbacks resemble flashbulb memories in some respects. **Bourne et al. (2013)** had healthy participants watch a traumatic film. Scenes that later produced flashbacks had been processed with **greater activation in the amygdala** (emotional processing) and the **ventral occipital cortex** (higher-level visual processing and imagery). Flashbacks are more likely when the trauma is processed with intense emotion at the time.

> **Classic background:** The classic study usually cited here is Neisser and Harsch (1992) on the 1986 *Challenger* shuttle explosion. Students wrote down how they had heard the news the day after and were questioned again about two and a half years later; many later accounts differed substantially from the originals, yet confidence was often high. Same lesson as Talarico and Rubin (2003).

### Conclusions on flashbulb memories

The evidence on whether flashbulb memories are special is **mixed**:

- **Against specialness:** in many studies, memories labelled "flashbulb" are retained no better than ordinary ones, and their consistency across retests is only **moderate** (Talarico & Rubin, 2003). Their distinctive feature is subjective vividness and confidence.
- **For selective specialness:** some details, especially **where** the news was heard, are remembered very consistently (Rimmele et al., 2012). So there may be **selective enhancement** of some kinds of information.
- **Other reasons for good retention:** flashbulb memories are **distinctive** and usually **retrieved many times**, both of which help any memory (Bob Logie, personal communication); no special mechanism is needed.
- **Emotional intensity:** flashbulb memories are more detailed and longer-lasting when an **intense emotional experience** accompanied their formation (Sharot et al., 2007).
- **Link to trauma:** there may be important similarities between flashbulb memories and **flashbacks** of personally experienced traumatic events.

The chapter summary's bottom line: although perceived as more vivid, flashbulb memories are as susceptible to interference and forgetting as other memories.

## 8.3 Memories Across the Lifetime

### The lifespan retrieval curve

Imagine asking 70-year-olds to produce personal memories in response to cue words (nouns for everyday objects, for instance) and then dating each memory. Which periods of their lives would the memories come from? **Rubin et al. (1986)** answered this by pooling the results of several such studies. Apart from the expected pile-up of recent memories, two features stood out:

1. **Childhood amnesia** (also called **infantile amnesia**): an almost complete absence of memories from the **first three years** of life.
2. **The reminiscence bump**: a surprisingly large number of memories from between the ages of **10 and 30**, and especially **15 to 25**.

> **Classic background:** The cue-word method goes back to Galton (1879) and was revived for modern research by Crovitz and Schiffman (1974), which is why it is often called the Galton-Crovitz technique. A participant is given a word such as *car* and asked to report the first specific personal memory it brings to mind, which is then dated.

Figure 8.2 (data from **Rubin & Schulkind, 1997**, for participants aged **20, 35 and 70**) shows the childhood amnesia part of the curve: very few memories from before age **3**, and a rise that **levels off between the ages of 7 and 10**. Adults also show only limited recall for events between **3 and 6**.

> **Trap.** Keep the three age bands distinct. **Childhood amnesia**: almost no memories from the **first 3 years**, limited recall from **3 to 6**. **Reminiscence bump**: **10 to 30**, peaking **15 to 25** (Rubin et al., 1986). **Life-script events**: typically **15 to 30** (Rubin & Berntsen, 2003). Examiners swap these freely.

### Childhood amnesia

Why can adults recall almost nothing from their first few years? The book reviews five accounts and then evaluates them together.

#### Freud: repression

The most famous (the book says "notorious") explanation is **Freud's (1915/1957)**. He attributed childhood amnesia to **repression**: threatening thoughts and experiences from early childhood are pushed into the unconscious. The fatal objection is that adults cannot remember **positive and neutral** early events either, and a theory of forgetting threatening material cannot explain forgetting that is indiscriminate.

#### The cognitive self (Howe & Courage, 1997)

**Howe and Courage (1997)** argued that a child can form autobiographical memories only once it has a sense that some events are significant *to me*. That requires a **cognitive self**, which emerges late in the **second year** of life and is indexed by **visual self-recognition in a mirror**. The cognitive self supplies a framework for organising memories, so its arrival marks the start of autobiographical memory and the end of infantile amnesia.

**Howe et al. (2003)** supported the idea: young children performed well on a test of memory for personal events only **after** they had achieved self-recognition. This places the onset of autobiographical memory around, or shortly after, the second birthday. The book's caution is that other factors may contribute as well.

#### Social-cultural developmental theory (Fivush & Nelson, 2004)

The **social-cultural developmental theory** (**Fivush & Nelson, 2004; Fivush, 2010**) puts **language and culture** at the centre. Language matters partly because we communicate memories through it, and experiences laid down before a child has language are hard to put into words later.

The theory predicts that how adults talk with children about the past should shape the children's memories. Mothers differ greatly in **reminiscing style**, some being highly elaborate and others not. **Jack et al. (2009)** found that 12-year-olds whose mothers had used a highly elaborate reminiscing style when the children were preschoolers reported an **earlier first memory**.

Culture shapes the content as well. **Ross and Wang (2010)** found that people from **individualistic** cultures, which stress personal achievement, recall personal experiences and feelings such as successes and fears, whereas people from **collectivistic** cultures, which stress group cohesion, recall memories involving **interpersonal relations**.

#### Two-stage theory (Jack & Hayne, 2010)

**Jack and Hayne (2010)** challenged the common assumption that childhood amnesia fades gradually across the preschool years. In their study the earliest memory dated from **23 months**, but memories for the first **four to six years** were sparse. They proposed that childhood amnesia has **two stages**:

| Stage | Period | What ends it |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute amnesia** | The first **two years** of life (no recall at all) | The onset of the **cognitive self** |
| **Relative amnesia** | The remaining **preschool** years (sparse recall) | The development of **language** |

Supporting the language part, the amount recalled about a childhood event rose steadily with the child's age at the time of the event, plausibly mirroring the rapid growth of language in the early years.

The theory **combines** the two previous accounts: the cognitive self explains the first boundary, language the second.

#### The neurogenic hypothesis (Josselyn & Frankland, 2012)

**Josselyn and Frankland (2012)** pointed out that childhood amnesia is also found in **several non-human species**, so human concepts such as the cognitive self and language cannot be the whole explanation.

Their alternative centres on the **hippocampus**, which is crucial for declarative (including autobiographical) memory and which develops slowly after birth. In early life the hippocampus, especially the **dentate gyrus**, undergoes high levels of **neurogenesis**, the generation of new neurons. The **neurogenic hypothesis** states that high levels of neurogenesis interfere with the formation of enduring memories, most likely because newly added neurons **replace synaptic connections** within existing hippocampal memory circuits. 

The evidence is **indirect**:

- Mice with high dentate gyrus neurogenesis **forget especially fast**.
- Conversely, in several species the ability to form long-lasting memories **increases substantially when neurogenesis declines**.

The verdict: there is as yet little or no **definitive** evidence for the neurogenic hypothesis. However, because the hippocampus is central to forming long-term declarative memories, it offers a very **plausible** account of the almost complete lack of memories from the first three years.

> **Trap.** The neurogenic hypothesis says that **high** neurogenesis (in the **dentate gyrus**) **impairs** lasting memory. "Childhood amnesia occurs because of insufficient neurogenesis in infancy" is the reverse of the theory.

#### Overall evaluation of childhood amnesia research

The book's synthesis assigns each theory a role:

1. **Absolute amnesia** (first two years) is probably best explained by the **neurogenic hypothesis**.
2. The **onset** of autobiographical memory probably depends on **declining neurogenesis plus the emergence of the cognitive self**.
3. Its later **expression** depends heavily on **social and cultural factors** and **language development**.
4. Other factors may contribute, such as the growth of **semantic memory**: dramatic early changes in children's understanding of the world may limit access to early memories.

Two limitations of the research:

- **It is mostly correlational.** That the cognitive self emerges at about the same time as childhood amnesia ends does not prove that one causes the other.
- **Most studies test adults.** **Tustin and Hayne (2010)** asked children and adolescents for their earliest memories. Children aged **5 to 9** reported earliest memories from about **1½ years** on average, adolescents from about **2½ years**, and adults from **just over 3 years**. The boundary therefore moves later with age, so part of childhood amnesia is simply **forgetting over the years**.

> **Trap.** Do not confuse Tustin and Hayne (2010), showing the boundary of childhood amnesia **shifts later** the older you are when tested, with Jack and Hayne (2010), the **two-stage** theory with an earliest memory at **23 months**.

### The reminiscence bump

Is the bump universal? **Conway et al. (2005)** asked older adults from **America, China, Japan, England and Bangladesh** for autobiographical memories and found a bump in **all five cultures**. Culture changed the content instead: Chinese participants recalled more socially or group-oriented events (a **collectivistic** culture), Americans more events about themselves as individuals (an **individualistic** culture).

#### Life-script theory (Rubin & Berntsen, 2003)

The most influential explanation is **Rubin and Berntsen's (2003)** account based on the **life script**: the cultural expectations about what the major events of a typical life are and the order they come in. Falling in love, marriage and having children are examples. Most such events are **emotionally positive** and expected between about **15 and 30**. The theory proposes that the life script **guides and organises retrieval**, directing memory search to the period where scripted events belong. It predicts a strong bump for **positive script** events and little or none for **negative** events, which are mostly unscripted.

#### Findings

> **Key study, Bohn and Berntsen (2011).** Children aged **10 to 14** already possess a life script: when they wrote their future life stories, **79%** of the events they listed were life-script events. There was a stronger bump for life-script than for non-script events. In a second study, when young people described future events in a way that did **not** activate the life script, the bump **disappeared**.

The second study is the strongest: if the bump vanishes without the script, the script is doing causal work.

> **Key study, Berntsen et al. (2011).** Older adults identified the most positive and the most negative events of their lives. **Negative events showed no reminiscence bump.** **Positive life-script events** showed a strong bump, but **positive non-life-script events did not** (Figure 8.3). **68%** of the positive events related to the life script: having children (**34%**), marriage (**22%**), college (**6%**) and falling in love (**1%**). Few negative events (for example a death, or a life-threatening illness) did. Positive events were also rated as much more central to people's life story and identity.

A simple "positive events are better remembered" account cannot explain why positive **non-script** events showed no bump; only the script separates the two kinds of positive event.

**Scherman (2013)** found that in four countries (**Denmark, the USA, Turkey and the Netherlands**), the age distribution of life-script events resembled the shape of the reminiscence bump.

The awkward finding came from **Dickson et al. (2011)**. Older people showed a bump for highly expected personal events, as life-script theory predicts, but very **unexpected** events **also** showed a strong bump, which a script cannot easily explain. The alternative draws on **Conway (2005)**: our strongest memories are those most relevant to **self-identity**, which develops substantially in adolescence and early adulthood, scripted or not.

#### Evaluation

The finding that the bump is much stronger for **positive** than for **negative** memories supports life-script theory. But the late teens and twenties differ from the periods before and after them in several ways at once, including the **establishment of self-identity** and the large number of **novel or first-time experiences**. Several factors therefore probably contribute to the bump, with the life script being one of them.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition's main account of the bump is **life-script theory** (Rubin & Berntsen, 2003), supplemented by self-identity (Conway, 2005). Novelty and first-time experiences are contributing factors in the book's evaluation, not the main theory. Decisive data: **negative events show no bump**.

## 8.4 Theoretical Approaches to Autobiographical Memory

The book concentrates on **Conway and Pleydell-Pearce's (2000) self-memory system model** and its revision by **Conway (2005)**, then turns to cognitive neuroscience and to depression.

### The self-memory system model (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000)

The model proposes a **self-memory system** with two major components.

**1. The autobiographical memory knowledge base.** This holds personal information at **three levels of specificity**, from broad to narrow:

| Level | What it contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lifetime periods** | Long stretches of time defined by a major ongoing situation | The years you lived with a particular person |
| **General events** | Repeated events and single extended events, linked to each other and to lifetime periods | Regular visits to a sports club; a holiday in Australia |
| **Event-specific knowledge** | Images, feelings and other details of a general event, spanning seconds to hours, usually stored in the correct temporal order | The sight of the beach on the first morning of that holiday |

**2. The working self.** This is concerned with the self, with what the self may become in the future, and with the person's **current goals**. The working self's goals influence both which memories get stored in the knowledge base and which ones are recalled. Conway and Pleydell-Pearce summed this up by saying that autobiographical memories are mainly records of success or failure in achieving goals.

The model's most testable proposal is that there are two routes into autobiographical memory:

- **Generative retrieval** is deliberate and effortful. The working self constructs a memory by combining its own resources (goals, self-knowledge) with information in the knowledge base. Memories produced this way often relate to the person's goals.
- **Direct retrieval** bypasses the working self. A specific cue triggers a memory spontaneously; hearing the word "Paris" on the radio may suddenly bring back a holiday there. It is less effortful and involves less active participation by the rememberer.

> **Trap.** **Generative** = deliberate, effortful, goal-driven, constructed via the working self, slower. **Direct** = involuntary, cue-triggered, bypasses the working self, faster. A cue that "pops" a memory into mind is direct retrieval, even if the memory is rich and detailed.

### Conway's (2005) revision

**Conway (2005)** developed the model further (Figure 8.4). The knowledge structures are now divided into the **conceptual self** and **episodic memories**, the latter being the new name for what had been called event-specific knowledge. At the top of the hierarchy he added two new levels: the **life story** (very general factual and evaluative knowledge about ourselves) and **themes** (major life domains such as work and relationships). The resulting hierarchy, from most general to most specific, can be laid out like this:

1. **Life story**
2. **Themes** (for example work, relationships)
3. **Lifetime periods**
4. **General events**
5. **Episodic memories** (formerly event-specific knowledge)

Conway (2005) also introduced a tension between two things we want from our memories. **Coherence** means consistency with our current goals and beliefs. **Correspondence** means accuracy, matching what really happened. We want both, but over time **coherence tends to win** over correspondence.

> **Trap.** "Event-specific knowledge" (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000) and "episodic memories" (Conway, 2005) are the **same level** under two names. And the coherence/correspondence pair runs the opposite way to what many students guess: memory drifts towards **coherence** (fitting the self), at the expense of **correspondence** (accuracy).

### Findings supporting the model

**Different levels of knowledge dissociate in amnesia.** Patients with **retrograde amnesia** (widespread forgetting of events from before a brain injury) often struggle badly to recall episodic memories while being much less impaired at recalling general events and lifetime periods (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000). **Rosenbaum et al. (2005)** studied the amnesic patient **KC**, who had **no episodic memories** at all yet could access some general autobiographical knowledge about his own life. Patients with almost no episodic memory can still describe their own personality, even when **semantic** memory is also severely impaired (**Klein & Lax, 2010**): self-knowledge of personality is remarkably resilient.

**Memory reflects personality.** **Woike et al. (1999)** had **agentic** people (independence, achievement, power) and **communal** people (interdependence, similarity to others) write about a personal experience; each recalled memories matching their type:

| | Agentic participants recalling agentic memories | Communal participants recalling communal memories |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive experience** | 65% (e.g. success) | 90% (e.g. love, friendship) |
| **Negative experience** | 47% (e.g. failure) | 90% (e.g. betrayal of trust) |

**Generative and direct retrieval really differ.**

- **Uzer et al. (2012)** had participants recall memories to emotion words (such as *frustrated* or *amused*) while thinking aloud. Direct retrieval was **three times faster** than generative retrieval, as an automatic process should be, and was accompanied by much **less vocalisation**.
- **Johannessen and Berntsen (2010)** found that directly retrieved memories were **more specific** but **less significant** and less relevant to personal identity than generatively retrieved memories. That fits the claim that the working self and its goals are more involved in generative retrieval.
- **Addis et al. (2012)** found that generative retrieval produced more activation in parts of the **prefrontal cortex** associated with strategic search, whereas some areas, notably the **left hippocampus**, were **more active during direct** retrieval.

**Memories bend to fit the self.** Conway (2005) argued that autobiographical memories are often inaccurate because we want them to agree with the working self's goals. People often claim that aspects of their lives (their self, their marriage) are better now than they used to be, but the "improvement" usually comes from remembering their **past selves more negatively** than they were (reviewed by **Newman & Lindsay, 2009**). Such distortions can boost self-image and smooth social relationships.

### Evaluation of the self-memory system model

**Strengths.** The approach of Conway and Pleydell-Pearce (2000) and Conway (2005) gives a reasonably comprehensive account of autobiographical memory. Several of its central assumptions are well supported: the **hierarchical structure** of autobiographical knowledge, the **close relationship between autobiographical memory and the self**, the importance of **goals**, and the distinction between **generative and direct retrieval**.

**Limitations.**

1. Retrieval involves **many brain areas and processes**, as the next section shows, and the model does not capture this complexity.
2. We need to know more about **how the working self interacts with the knowledge base** to produce a specific memory.
3. Autobiographical memories vary in how much **episodic** information (contextual detail) and **semantic** information (world knowledge) they contain, and the model does not fully address this.

> **Trap.** For "which is NOT a limitation of the self-memory system model?", pick a strength: "no evidence for a hierarchical structure" or "no support for the generative/direct distinction" are **not** limitations, since both are well supported. The real limitations are neural complexity, the unexplained working-self/knowledge-base interaction, and the episodic/semantic mix.

### Cognitive neuroscience of autobiographical memory

**Prefrontal and temporal cortex.** **Svoboda et al. (2006)**, in a meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies, found that the **medial and ventromedial prefrontal cortex** were almost always active during autobiographical retrieval, as were the **medial and lateral temporal cortex**. **Summerfield et al. (2009)** looked more closely by having participants produce **real** and **imagined** autobiographical events. Only real events activated the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex** and the **posterior cingulate cortex**.

**The amygdala.** Autobiographical memories are often emotional, so the **amygdala**, deep in the temporal lobe and strongly tied to emotion, should be involved. **Buchanan et al. (2006)** confirmed this in patients with medial temporal lobe lesions: when the lesion extended into the amygdala, patients had **more difficulty retrieving emotional autobiographical memories** than patients whose amygdala was spared. **Markowitsch and Staniloiu (2011)** reviewed imaging work showing amygdala activation when people retrieved genuine autobiographical memories, with **no** such activation when they recalled fictitious material.

**Four interacting networks.** **St Jacques et al. (2011)** examined brain activity when people generatively retrieved autobiographical memories to emotionally arousing words, and identified four networks (Figure 8.5):

| Network | Role in construction / elaboration | Associated function |
|---|---|---|
| **Fronto-parietal** | Construction only | Adaptive controlled processes; probably verbal retrieval |
| **Cingulo-opercular** | Construction only | Goal maintenance |
| **Medial prefrontal cortex** | Construction **and** elaboration | Self-referential processing |
| **Medial temporal lobe** | Construction **and** elaboration | Declarative memory (conscious recollection) |

The networks are joined by **strong bidirectional connections**, so none works in isolation, and all four contribute to **construction** while only the **medial prefrontal** and **medial temporal lobe** networks support later **elaboration**.

### Depression and autobiographical memory

If autobiographical memory reflects personality and the sense of self, as the self-memory system model assumes, then how it differs in depressed people should illuminate depression itself. Research has studied patients with **major depressive disorder** and healthy people high in depression.

#### Over-general memory

A widely used task asks for a specific memory of an event lasting **less than one day** in response to each cue word. Depressed people show **reduced specificity**, giving many more **over-general memories**. Given the cue *angry*, a depressed person might answer "arguing with other people", which names a category of repeated events rather than one occasion (**Liu et al., 2013**, meta-analysis of studies on depressed patients).

> **Classic background:** This task is the **Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT)**, and over-general memory was first reported by Williams and Broadbent (1986) in patients who had attempted suicide.

Most of this evidence is **correlational**, showing only that the two go together. Two kinds of stronger evidence exist:

- **Prospective risk.** **Stange et al. (2013)** studied Caucasian adolescents. The degree of over-general memory predicted **increases in depressive symptoms eight months later**, but only in those exposed to high levels of **familial emotional abuse**.
- **Prognosis.** In a longitudinal study of depressed patients, **Brittlebank et al. (1993)** found that **greater memory specificity predicted recovery** from major depressive disorder.

What produces over-general memory? According to the **affect regulation hypothesis**, it reflects a **cognitive avoidance strategy**: depressed people may avoid retrieving specific negative memories because such memories carry intense negative emotion. **Debeer et al. (2011)** gave participants **threat instructions** (warning that retrieving autobiographical memories might have negative side effects). These instructions increased over-general memories **only among people high in avoidant coping**. The book notes that not all depressed individuals use avoidant coping, so avoidance is only part of the story.

> **Trap.** Direction matters: **more specific** memory predicts **recovery** (Brittlebank et al., 1993); **more over-general** memory predicts **later symptoms** under abuse (Stange et al., 2013). Debeer et al.'s (2011) threat effect appeared **only in high avoidant copers**.

#### The structure of autobiographical knowledge in depression

**Dalgleish et al. (2011)** compared three groups: patients with **current** major depressive disorder, patients **in remission**, and **healthy controls**. Each participant listed the most important lifetime periods ("life chapters") of their life; all three groups produced **nine to ten** on average. They then chose which positive and negative words or phrases applied to each period. Four measures were derived:

| Measure | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Proportion negative** | The share of selected items that were negative |
| **Compartmentalisation** | How much the proportion of negative items varied across lifetime periods |
| **Positive redundancy** | How far the same positive terms were reused across periods |
| **Negative redundancy** | How far the same negative terms were reused across periods |

The results (Figure 8.6):

- **Currently depressed** patients selected a much greater proportion of **negative** terms than controls.
- They showed greater **compartmentalisation**, that is, a less integrated sense of self.
- This compartmentalisation arose partly from **low positive redundancy**: they used positive terms inconsistently across their life chapters.
- Patients **in remission** were generally **intermediate** between current patients and controls.

Three interpretations follow. First, autobiographical knowledge in current depression reveals a **working self** (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000) that is predominantly negative and poorly integrated. Second, remitted patients' autobiographical knowledge is more integrated and less negative than that of current patients, which suggests that **part of recovery involves a healthier perspective on one's life history**. Third, remitted patients still had a more negative and less integrated view than healthy controls, which suggests they remain **at risk of relapse**.

#### Conclusions on depression

Depressed individuals produce over-general autobiographical memories partly because of **avoidant coping**. Their autobiographical memories reveal a **negative and poorly integrated sense of self**. People with over-general memories are **at risk of developing depression**, and increased specificity in depressed patients **predicts recovery**.

## 8.5 Eyewitness Testimony

### Is it safe to rely on an eyewitness?

Suppose you are the only witness to a murder, you pick someone out of a line-up, and that person is convicted with no other strong evidence. Is that safe? Many people think so. **Simons and Chabris (2011)** found that **37%** of Americans think one confident eyewitness should be enough to convict. **DNA testing** offers a check on whether the convicted person was the culprit (though it usually shows only presence at the scene). In the United States, **200** convicted people have been shown to be innocent by DNA tests, **mostly because of mistaken eyewitness identification**. In 2011, DNA evidence freed **Cornelius Dupree**, then 51, who had served **30 years** for the alleged rape of a 26-year-old Dallas woman, convicted because the victim identified him.

Those who decide cases tend to underestimate the problem. **Benton et al. (2006)** found that **judges disagreed with eyewitness experts on 60%** of eyewitness issues, and **jurors disagreed on 87%**.

> **Classic background:** Wells (1978) divided eyewitness factors into **estimator variables**, which the justice system cannot control (stress, weapons, witness age), and **system variables**, which it can (line-up and interview procedures, the subject of 8.6).

### Expectations: confirmation bias and schemas

**Confirmation bias** in eyewitness memory means that memory for an event is distorted by the witness's prior **expectations**.

> **Key study, Lindholm and Christianson (1998).** Swedish and immigrant students watched a simulated robbery in which the robber seriously wounded a cashier. They were then shown colour photographs of **eight** men, **four Swedes and four immigrants**. Participants were **twice as likely** to pick an innocent immigrant as an innocent Swede.

Immigrants are over-represented in Swedish crime statistics, and the resulting expectation about a criminal's likely ethnicity leaked into identification.

A broader version of the idea comes from **Bartlett (1932)**: stored **schemas**, packets of general knowledge, create expectations that lead us to reconstruct details on the basis of what "must have been true" (Chapter 10). Most people's **bank-robbery schema** says that robbers are usually male, wear disguises and dark clothes, demand money, and have a getaway car with a driver (**Tuckey & Brewer, 2003a**).

- **Tuckey and Brewer (2003a)** showed witnesses a video of a simulated bank robbery. As Bartlett's theory predicts, information **relevant** to the robbery schema was recalled **better** than schema-**irrelevant** information such as the colour of the getaway car.
- **Tuckey and Brewer (2003b)** examined **ambiguous** information. For some witnesses the robber's head was covered by a balaclava, so the robber's gender could not be seen. Witnesses mostly resolved such ambiguities in the **schema-consistent** direction (Figure 8.7 plots correct responses and schema-consistent intrusions in ambiguous and unambiguous conditions), so their recall contained schema content they had never actually seen.

### Post-event and pre-event information: the misinformation effect

The obvious explanation for inaccurate testimony is that witnesses fail to attend properly to a crime that happens suddenly and without warning. **Loftus and Palmer (1974)** made a more unsettling argument: eyewitness memories are **fragile**, and can be distorted remarkably easily by **misleading information presented afterwards**. This is the **misinformation effect**.

> **Key study, Loftus and Palmer (1974).** Participants watched a film of a multiple-car accident and then answered questions about it. Some were asked roughly how fast the cars were going when they **"smashed into"** each other; for others the verb was **"hit"**. Estimated speed averaged **41 mph** with "smashed" and **34 mph** with "hit". **One week later**, everyone was asked whether they had seen any **broken glass**. There had been none. **32%** of the "smashed" group said yes, compared with **14%** of the "hit" group and **12%** of controls who had not been asked about speed.

The speed estimates alone could reflect a reporting bias. The broken-glass result is what makes the study important: a week later, the "smashed" group reported a crash-consistent detail that had never been shown, so one changed word had altered the memory, not just the answer.

Two qualifications follow from later work.

- **Warnings help only partly.** **Ecker et al. (2010)** found that the misinformation effect survived even when witnesses were explicitly warned **beforehand** not to be influenced by misleading information, although the warning **reduced** its size.
- **Peripheral details are most vulnerable.** Most research has tested distortions of minor details such as broken glass. Memory distortion is **more common** after misinformation about **peripheral** features than about **central** ones (**Dalton & Daneman, 2006**), so the practical damage may be smaller than the laboratory figures suggest.

Misleading information can also come **before** the event. **Lindsay et al. (2004)** showed witnesses a video of a **museum burglary**. The previous day, they had heard a narrative that was either **thematically similar** (a **palace burglary**) or **thematically dissimilar** (a school field trip to a palace). Errors in recalling the video were much more frequent after the similar narrative. This matters because real witnesses bring relevant past experiences to a crime, and those experiences can contaminate what they remember.

> **Trap.** The misinformation effect classically involves **post-event** information (Loftus & Palmer, 1974) and is a form of **retroactive** interference: later information distorts memory for an earlier event. Lindsay et al. (2004) show that **pre-event** information also distorts eyewitness memory, which is the **proactive** direction. If an option claims misinformation works only when it follows the event, Lindsay et al. is the counter-example.

### Theoretical explanations of the misinformation effect

The book identifies two main processes and two supplementary ones.

**1. Source misattribution** (**Johnson et al., 1993; Lindsay, 2008**). A question activates memory traces that overlap with it in content, and the witness infers each trace's **source** from what it contains, so errors are most likely when memories from two sources **resemble** each other. Lindsay et al. (2004) fit this: narrative details intruded into memory of the video far more when the two events were similar. **Prull and Yockelson (2013)** found a robust misinformation effect when a slide sequence was followed by a misleading narrative, but the effect became **much smaller** when participants took a **source-recognition test** that pushed them to retrieve where each detail came from. Directing attention to source shrank the effect, so source confusion was producing much of it.

**2. Reconsolidation.** A consolidated memory that is **reactivated** becomes fragile again and must be restabilised through **reconsolidation** (Chapter 6), during which the trace can be **altered and updated**. **Chan and LaPaglia (2013)** had some participants recall details of a film about a terrorist attack **before** receiving misleading information, while others did not. The misinformation effect was strong in those who had **recalled first** and absent in those who had not. Recall reactivated the traces, and during reconsolidation the misleading information was written in, perhaps overwriting the original; without prior recall there was no reconsolidation and no effect.

> **Key study, Edelson et al. (2011).** Participants watched a crime scene in groups of **five**, but every memory test was individual. Tested **three days** later, their memory was accurate. **Four days** after that, they were falsely told that their fellow participants remembered several events differently, and were immediately tested again (Test 2) while brain activity was recorded. **Seven days** later still, they were told the co-observers' answers had been **randomly generated**, and were tested a third time (Test 3).

On Test 2, a participant might go along with the group by **pretending to agree** or because their **memory had really changed**; Test 3 decides, since an error that survives the exposure of the group's answers as random reflects altered memory. During Test 2, **enhanced connectivity between the amygdala and the hippocampus** was associated with memories that were genuinely changed but not with those that were not. Edelson and colleagues concluded that a lasting misinformation effect occurred only when reconsolidation had "modified the neural representation of memory" (Edelson et al., 2011, p. 108).

**3 and 4. Further factors** (**Wright & Loftus, 2008**):

- The **vacant slot** explanation: misinformation is more likely to be accepted when the related information from the original event was **never stored** in the first place. There is nothing to compete with it.
- The **blend** explanation: misinformation and original information are **integrated** into a single memory.

> **Trap.** Because retrieval practice usually strengthens memory (the testing effect), you might expect recalling the film first to protect against misinformation. In Chan and LaPaglia (2013) **prior recall made the misinformation effect stronger**, because reactivation opened the memory to **reconsolidation**. The book's two key explanations are **source misattribution** and **reconsolidation**; vacant slot and blend are supplementary.

### Anxiety and violence

#### Weapon focus

**Weapon focus** is the finding that eyewitnesses attend so heavily to a crucial feature of the scene, typically a weapon, that they fail to take in other details.

- **Loftus et al. (1987)** had participants watch a person either pointing a **gun** at a cashier or handing over a **cheque**, and then receiving cash. Participants looked more at the gun than at the cheque, and their memory for details unrelated to the gun or cheque was worse in the weapon condition.
- **Biggs et al. (2013)** found that weapons were fixated more often than neutral objects, at the expense of fewer fixations on **faces**. But note the qualification: **on average, faces were still fixated more often than weapons**.
- **Pickel (2009)** pointed out that people attend to whatever is **unexpected** in a situation (inconsistent with their schema for it), and this impairs memory for everything else. She predicted that weapon focus would be strongest when a weapon is especially unexpected, and found a strong effect when the criminal carrying a folding knife was **female**, since a woman with a knife violates expectation.
- **Fawcett et al. (2013)**, in a meta-analysis, found a **moderate** weapon focus effect, and importantly its size was **similar in the laboratory and in the real world**.

The chapter summary combines the two accounts: weapons attract attention because they are **threatening and/or unexpected**.

> **Trap.** Weapon focus is not only about threat. Pickel (2009) shows that **unexpectedness** drives it too (a woman with a knife). And Biggs et al. (2013) found that weapons reduce fixations on faces **without** making weapons more fixated than faces overall. An option claiming "witnesses look at the weapon more than at the face" overstates the finding.

#### Stress and anxiety

**Deffenbacher et al. (2004)** ran a meta-analysis of stress and eyewitness memory:

| Measure | Low stress / anxiety | High stress / anxiety |
|---|---|---|
| Culprit's face correctly identified | **54%** | **42%** |
| Details correctly recalled | **64%** | **52%** |

So stress and anxiety generally **impair** eyewitness memory. **Valentine and Mesout (2009)** confirmed this in a real-world setting: visitors encountered a person in the **Horror Labyrinth at the London Dungeon**, and those who felt most anxious there later remembered that person worst.

Why does stress impair memory? **Easterbrook's (1959) hypothesis** says that stress **narrows attention** onto central or important stimuli, which reduces the ability to remember **peripheral** details (Chapter 15 develops this). **Yegiyan and Lang (2010)** presented distressing pictures of increasing stressfulness. Recognition of **central** details **improved** progressively as stressfulness rose, whereas memory for **peripheral** details was much **worse** with highly stressful pictures than with moderately stressful ones, exactly the pattern Easterbrook predicts.

> **Trap.** Easterbrook's hypothesis does **not** say stress impairs all memory. It says stress **narrows** attention: **central** detail is preserved or even **improved**, and **peripheral** detail suffers (Yegiyan & Lang, 2010). The Deffenbacher figures are for overall identification and detail recall.

### Ageing and eyewitness memory

Older witnesses are generally **less accurate** than younger adults and much more susceptible to misinformation. **Jacoby et al. (2005)** presented misleading information to younger and older adults: on a later recall test, older adults had a **43%** chance of producing false memories, against **4%** for younger adults.

**Wright and Stroud (2002)** found an **own-age bias**: younger and older witnesses alike identified culprits more accurately when the culprit was of a **similar age** to themselves.

What causes own-age bias? **Harrison and Hole (2009)** traced it to **exposure**: most people spend more time with people of their own age. **Teachers**, who see children's faces for hours a day, showed **no** own-age bias, and neither did **young geriatric nurses**, whose experience made them much better than young controls at recognising **old** faces (**Wiese et al., 2013**). Using **event-related potentials (ERPs)**, Wiese et al. showed that this experience affected **later stages** of face processing but **not early perceptual** processing.

### Eyewitness identification: face recognition

Police commonly ask witnesses to pick out the culprit from people physically present or from photographs. Line-up identification is often unreliable. **Valentine et al. (2003)** analysed **640** witnesses attempting identifications in **314** real line-ups: about **20%** picked a **non-suspect** (a known innocent filler), **40%** picked the **suspect**, and **40%** made **no identification**.

Confidence carries some information: highly confident identifiers tend to be more accurate (**Brewer & Wells, 2011**). **Odinot et al. (2009)**, studying witnesses to a real **supermarket robbery in the Netherlands**, found a **moderate correlation of +0.38** between confidence and accuracy, which still leaves many confident witnesses wrong.

### Face recognition and identification errors

Identification depends heavily on face recognition (covered in Chapter 3). Three phenomena matter particularly for witnesses.

**Unconscious transference.** A face can be remembered without the circumstances of seeing it. **Ross et al. (1994)** had witnesses observe an event in which both a **bystander** and the **culprit** appeared. From a line-up that **excluded** the culprit, witnesses were **three times more likely** to choose the bystander than someone never seen before. The face was correctly judged familiar but wrongly attributed to the crime.

> **Trap.** **Unconscious transference** (a familiar but **innocent** face picked as the culprit) is a face-specific source error; do not confuse it with the **misinformation effect** (misleading post-event information) or **confirmation bias** (expectations).

**The other-race effect.** Recognition memory is generally more accurate for **same-race** than for **other-race** faces. One explanation is social: we process the faces of people from groups we identify with (an **ingroup**) more deeply than those from **outgroups**. **Shriver et al. (2008)** found that the usual other-race effect largely **disappeared** when white American students viewed photographs of **white** men shown in **impoverished** contexts (such as ramshackle housing), because those white faces were no longer treated as belonging to the students' ingroup. 

But memory is not the whole story. **Megreya et al. (2011)** showed that **perception** is involved too. **British and Egyptian** participants judged whether a target face appeared in an array of **ten** faces, and if so which one, with all photographs in view so that memory demands were minimal. The effect still appeared:

| | Same-race faces | Other-race faces |
|---|---|---|
| Correct identification when target present | **70%** | **64%** |
| Mistaken identification when target absent | **34%** | **47%** |

(The book's text calls the 34% condition "same-sex faces", which from the context is evidently a slip for same-race.)

> **Trap.** The chapter summary calls this the **cross-race effect**; the main text calls it the **other-race effect**. Other texts say **own-race bias**; all three mean the same thing. Megreya et al. (2011) shows it is partly **perceptual**.

**Unfamiliar faces are harder than research suggests.** Studies often underestimate this difficulty because the photographs to be matched were taken on the **same day**. **Megreya et al. (2013)** found that matching unfamiliar faces was much worse when the photographs were taken **months apart**.

> **Key study, Jenkins et al. (2011).** British participants were given **40** photographs, **20** each of **two Dutch celebrities** unknown in the UK, and asked to sort them into piles, one per person. On average they decided that **7.5** different people were shown.

Photographs of one face vary so much that identifying someone from a **single photograph**, as police usually require, is hard. **Jenkins and Burton (2011)** proposed combining multiple photographs of the same person into an **average face**. With familiar faces, averaged images were recognised **significantly faster** than single photographs, and a computer face-recognition system tested by Jenkins et al. (2011) recognised **100%** of averaged faces against only **54%** with individual photographs. Detectives should therefore use averaged faces where possible.

### From laboratory to courtroom

Can laboratory findings be applied to real crimes? Real witnesses are much more often **victims**, watching violence on video is far **less stressful** than experiencing it, and a laboratory mistake is trivial whereas in an American court it can be a matter of **life or death**. Yet the similarities are important:

- **Ihlebaek et al. (2003)** staged a robbery by two robbers with handguns. In the **live** condition witnesses were repeatedly ordered to "Stay down!"; in the **video** condition other witnesses watched a recording of the live event. Both groups **overestimated the duration** of the event and showed **similar patterns** of what they remembered, although the **video** group recalled **more** information.
- **Pozzulo et al. (2008)** had witnesses observe a staged theft live or on video. **Correct identification of the culprit was comparable**, although live witnesses reported more stress and arousal.
- **Tollestrup et al. (1994)** analysed police records of eyewitness identifications for fraud and robbery. Factors that matter in the laboratory, such as **weapon focus** and **retention interval**, also mattered in real crimes.

The conclusion is that artificial laboratory conditions distort findings only **modestly**, and if anything laboratory studies **underestimate** the memory errors of real witnesses. The best strategy is to use diverse methods and combine their findings (**Chae, 2010**). Overall, laboratory research is relevant to the legal system.

## 8.6 Enhancing Eyewitness Memory

This section turns to what the justice system can control: how line-ups are run and how witnesses are interviewed.

### Line-ups

A line-up can be **simultaneous** (the witness sees all the members at once) or **sequential** (the witness sees one person at a time and decides about each before seeing the next). Which is better?

> **Key study, Steblay et al. (2011), meta-analysis.**
>
> | | Simultaneous | Sequential |
> |---|---|---|
> | Culprit **present**: culprit correctly chosen | **52%** | **44%** |
> | Culprit **absent**: someone (innocent) mistakenly chosen | **54%** | **32%** |

Witnesses therefore adopt a **stricter criterion** with sequential line-ups.

The book concludes that the **sequential line-up is probably preferable**: the small drop in correct identifications (**8%**, from 52 to 44) is outweighed by the large drop in misidentifications (**22%**, from 54 to 32). The trade makes sense because police never know whether the culprit is in the line-up, and a false identification is the error that produces wrongful convictions like Cornelius Dupree's.

Two further refinements reduce false identifications:

- **A "not sure" option.** Explicitly offering witnesses a not-sure response in sequential line-ups cut misidentifications from **22% to only 12%** (**Steblay & Phillips, 2011**).
- **Unbiased instructions.** Warning witnesses that the culprit **may not be in the line-up** reduced mistaken identifications in culprit-absent line-ups by **42%**, while reducing correct identifications in culprit-present line-ups by only **2%** (**Steblay, 1997**, meta-analysis).

> **Trap.** Sequential line-ups do **not** produce more correct identifications: slightly **fewer** hits (44% vs 52%) but far **fewer false alarms** (32% vs 54%), and the chapter summary says culprits are **slightly more likely to be identified from simultaneous** line-ups. Keep the two Steblay numbers apart: **42% vs 2%** is the warning effect (Steblay, 1997); **22% to 12%** is the not-sure option (Steblay & Phillips, 2011).

### The cognitive interview

To maximise what police obtain from witnesses, psychologists developed the **cognitive interview** (for example **Geiselman & Fisher, 1997**), built on **four retrieval rules**:

1. **Mental reinstatement** of the environment and of any personal contact experienced during the crime.
2. **Report everything**, including details that seem minor.
3. **Recall in different orders**, for example backwards in time.
4. **Change perspective**: report the incident from different viewpoints, including those of other witnesses. **Anderson and Pichert (1978)** had found this kind of perspective shift useful in recall (Chapter 10).

Its effectiveness comes from being grounded in memory research, with two theoretical sources:

| Rules | Theoretical basis |
|---|---|
| **1 and 2** (reinstate context, report everything) | The **encoding specificity principle** (Tulving, 1979; Chapter 7): recall depends on the **overlap** between the context at encoding and the context at retrieval |
| **3 and 4** (change order, change perspective) | Memory traces are **complex** and contain several kinds of information, so the same event can be reached by **different retrieval routes** |

> **Trap.** **Rules 1 and 2** (not context reinstatement alone) come from **encoding specificity**; rules 3 and 4 come from the **multiple-retrieval-routes** idea.

Two developments followed (**Memon et al., 2010**):

- **The enhanced cognitive interview** (for example **Fisher & Geiselman, 1992**) adds an emphasis on **rapport** between interviewer and witness. **Roy (1991)** listed ways to achieve it, including minimising distractions, slowing the witness's speech, pausing before the next question, adapting language to the witness, reducing anxiety, and avoiding judgemental or personal comments.
- **A shortened police version.** Because the full interview is very time-consuming, police typically use only the **first two rules** (reinstatement and report everything) and drop the third and fourth.

#### Findings

> **Key study, Memon et al. (2010), meta-analysis.** Compared with the standard police interview, the cognitive interview (basic or enhanced) produced a **large increase** in the number of **correct** details recalled, and the gain was similar whether the incident had been seen **live or on video**.

The same meta-analysis found that the benefit was **reduced**, but still present, when the situation was **highly arousing** and when there was a **long retention interval** before the interview. The interview had only **one negative effect**: a **fairly small but significant increase in incorrect details**.

Further findings refine the picture:

- **Eye closure.** Advocates often recommend that witnesses recall with their **eyes closed**. **Vredeveldt et al. (2011)** found that this does enhance recall, because it **reduces cognitive load and distraction**.
- **Misinformation.** **Memon et al. (2009)** found that the cognitive interview did **not** protect against misleading information presented **before** the interview, but it **did reduce** the impact of misleading information presented **after** it. A well-conducted interview seems to fix the witness's account before later contamination can occur.
- **Which components matter?** Not all are needed. **Colomb and Ginet (2012)** found that **mental reinstatement** and **reporting all details** both enhanced recall, whereas **changing perspective** and **changing order** were **ineffective**. **Dando et al. (2011)** went further: requiring recall in **backward** temporal order **reduced** correct details and **increased** errors, because it disrupted the temporal organisation of the witness's memory.

> **Trap.** The chapter summary states that **mental reinstatement** and **reporting all details** are the components crucial to the interview's success. **Reverse-order recall** can actively **harm** performance (Dando et al., 2011). An option claiming that all four components contribute equally is wrong for this edition.

#### Evaluation of the cognitive interview

**Strengths.** It has a well-established theoretical and empirical basis. There is compelling evidence that it extracts as much accurate information as possible from witnesses in most circumstances. Progress has been made in identifying which components do most of the work.

**Limitations.**

1. The **small increase in incorrect recall** could lead detectives to misread the evidence.
2. **Context reinstatement**, a key ingredient, can **harm recognition memory** by making **non-target faces** seem more familiar (**Wong & Read, 2011**). The technique that helps recall of events can hurt identification of faces.
3. The interview is **less effective** when the event was **stressful** and when there is a **long delay** before the interview.

## 8.7 Prospective Memory

### Retrospective and prospective memory

Most memory research studies **retrospective memory**, memory for past events and previously acquired knowledge. **Prospective memory** looks forward: **Graf (2012)** describes it as the function by which we form plans and promises, retain them, and recall them later at the right time or on the right cue, such as remembering to meet a friend at a coffee shop. The key-term definition stresses that it operates **without an explicit reminder**.

Its importance is shown by a tragic case described by **Einstein and McDaniel (2005)**. After a change to his usual routine, a devoted father who was meant to turn off towards the day-care centre drove his habitual route to the university instead, and his infant son, asleep in the back seat, died in the car hours later. What failed was not knowledge or care but the retrieval of an intention at the moment it was needed, overridden by a well-practised routine.

### Stages in prospective memory

Because prospective memory involves several separate processes, it can fail in several different ways. **Zogg et al. (2012)** summarised several theorists' views as a sequence of stages (Figure 8.9):

1. **Intention formation.** The person encodes an intention linked to a particular cue ("I will talk to my friend when I see him").
2. **Retention interval.** A delay of anything from minutes to weeks separates forming and executing the intention. During it there is usually some **monitoring** of the environment for relevant cues (catching sight of the friend).
3. **Cue detection and intention retrieval.** The person detects and recognises the cue, and this is followed by **self-initiated** retrieval of the intention.
4. **Intention recall.** The person retrieves the content of the intention from **retrospective** memory. This can go wrong if the intention is complex, is related to other stored intentions, or competes with other intentions.
5. **Intention execution.** Usually fairly **automatic** and undemanding.

> **Trap.** Notice that **stage 4 is a retrospective-memory stage**: knowing *what* you intended to do is retrieved from retrospective memory. Prospective memory tasks therefore always contain a retrospective component. Also note that the chapter summary lists the stages slightly differently (intention formation, monitoring, cue detection, intention retrieval, execution); the content is the same.

### How different are prospective and retrospective memory?

People **interpret** the two kinds of failure differently. A broken promise (prospective failure) is read as poor **motivation and reliability**; a forgotten fact or event (retrospective failure) is blamed on a **poor memory**. In Graf's (2012) terms, the first suggests a "flaky person", the second a "faulty brain".

Further differences:

1. **Informational content.** Retrospective memory usually concerns *what* we know and can be high in content (**Baddeley et al., 2015**). Prospective memory concerns *when* to do something and is low in content. The low content helps ensure that a prospective failure is not really a retrospective one.
2. **Relevance to plans.** Prospective memory is more tied to the plans and goals of daily life.
3. **External cues.** More external cues are usually available for retrospective memory than for prospective memory.
4. **What is being studied.** **Moscovitch (2008)** observed that prospective memory research is almost the only major area of memory research whose problem is not memory itself but the **uses to which memory is put**.

In real life the two work together: buying groceries for your flatmates requires remembering to go to the supermarket (**prospective**) and then what you agreed to buy (**retrospective**). **Crawford et al. (2003)** found separate **prospective** and **retrospective** factors in a memory questionnaire, together with a **general memory factor** drawing on both.

### Event-based versus time-based prospective memory

An important distinction:

- **Time-based** prospective memory: remembering to perform an action at a particular **time** (phone a friend at 8 pm).
- **Event-based** prospective memory: remembering to perform an action when the right **circumstances** arise (pass on a message when you see someone).

There is far more research on **event-based** tasks, because researchers can control what the cue is and when it appears. Laboratory set-ups mimic real life in one respect: participants usually perform an **unrelated ongoing task** alongside the prospective task, just as real intentions must be remembered while we are busy with something else.

> **Key study, Sellen et al. (1997).** In a real work environment, participants wore badges with buttons. They were told to press the button either at a pre-arranged **time** (time-based) or when in a pre-specified **place** (event-based). Performance was better on the **event-based** task (**52%**) than on the **time-based** task (**33%**).

Sellen et al. argued that event-based tasks are easier because **external cues** are more likely to trigger the intended action. **Kim and Mayhorn (2008)** supported this, finding event-based superiority in both laboratory and naturalistic conditions.

**Hicks et al. (2005)** confirmed that event-based tasks are **less demanding** than time-based ones, but added an important qualification. Both kinds of task were more demanding when the task was **ill-specified** (detect animal words) than when it was **well-specified** (detect the words *nice* and *hit*). In fact, a **well-specified time-based** task was **no more demanding** than an **ill-specified event-based** task. Specification matters as much as type.

**Monitoring strategies differ.** Because the moment of a time-based cue is usually **predictable**, people monitor only **sporadically**, increasing monitoring as the target time approaches (**Tarantino et al., submitted**). On event-based tasks, where the cue's arrival is unpredictable, there is much more evidence of **continuous** monitoring.

> **Key study, Cona et al. (2012).** On the ongoing task, five letters were shown and participants judged whether the **second and fourth** letters were the same. The prospective task was either **event-based** (respond whenever a **B** turned up in one of those two critical positions) or **time-based** (make a response at **five-minute** intervals), and ERPs were recorded. In the event-based condition, ERP amplitudes were greater at **130 to 180 ms** after stimulus onset, probably reflecting **greater use of attentional resources**, and at **400 to 600 ms**, probably reflecting **more frequent target checking** (Figure 8.10). Overall there was more processing activity in the event-based condition.

> **Trap.** Event-based tasks are **performed better** (Sellen et al., 1997) and are generally **less demanding** (Hicks et al., 2005), yet involve **more continuous monitoring** and **more processing activity** per trial (Cona et al., 2012): the external cue makes success easier, its unpredictability keeps you watching. Time-based monitoring is **sporadic** and **rises as the deadline nears**.

### Prospective memory in real life

#### In the real world: plane crashes, pilots and air traffic controllers

**Dismukes and Nowinski (2006)** examined pilot errors involving memory: of **75** incidents or accidents, **74** involved failures of **prospective** memory and only **one** a failure of retrospective memory. Pilots know the operations of flying extremely well; what fails is doing them at the right moment.

A concrete case: on **31 August 1988**, a **Boeing 727 (Flight 1141)** was waiting in a long queue for take-off at **Dallas-Fort Worth**. Air traffic control unexpectedly told the crew to move past the other aircraft to the runway. In the disruption the crew forgot to set the **wing flaps and leading-edge slats to 15 degrees**, a prospective memory failure, and the plane crashed beyond the end of the runway with several deaths.

Dismukes and Nowinski found failures most likely when pilots were **interrupted while carrying out a plan**. Commercial pilots interrupted while flying a simulator made **53% more errors** than uninterrupted pilots (**Latorella, 1998**).

> **Key study, Dodhia and Dismukes (2009).** Participants had to return to an interrupted task after completing a different one. With a **ten-second pause after** the interruption, **88%** returned to the interrupted task; with a **four-second pause just before** it, **65%**; with **no pause** before or after, only **48%**.

People benefit from a few seconds to form a new plan when an interruption changes the situation, and a few more at its end to retrieve the intention to resume; pilots often get neither. **Trafton and Monk (2007)** described how interrupted people often use **prospective rehearsal** ("What was I about to do?"), which keeps the goal active and improves performance afterwards, but is hard to use when time is very short.

**Air traffic control** demands remembering to perform intended actions while monitoring a display (**Loft et al., 2011**), and **38%** of the memory errors reported by controllers involve failing to complete intentions (**Shorrock, 2005**). In a fatal case at **Los Angeles International Airport** in **1991**, a tower controller had one aircraft waiting in position on **runway 24L**; amid communication delays, haze and glare, the controller forgot to clear it for take-off but cleared another aircraft to land on 24L, which struck it, killing **34** people.

**Loft and Remington (2010)** used a simulated air traffic control task and found that prospective memory errors were more common when participants had to deviate from **strong** (well-practised) routines than from **weak** ones. This is **habit capture**: well-practised routines run off automatically and override the intended deviation. Pilots and controllers spend much of their time on habitual tasks, unlike laboratory participants (**Dismukes, 2012**), which makes them vulnerable when something unexpected requires a change. Finally, **Loft et al. (2013)** found that **flashing visual aids** accompanying target planes improved prospective performance, but sometimes **impaired** handling of non-target planes, probably by drawing attention away from them.

> **Trap.** **Habit capture** (Loft & Remington, 2010) causes prospective failures; **prospective rehearsal** (Trafton & Monk, 2007) protects against them.

#### Obsessive-compulsive disorder and checking

Most patients with **obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)** have **checking compulsions**: they repeatedly check the door or the gas yet remain unsure whether they did.

**Hypothesis 1: poor retrospective memory.** Most findings say no: compulsive checkers **do not differ** from controls in retrospective memory (**Cuttler & Graf, 2009a**). Their **meta-memory** (knowledge and beliefs about their own memory) for retrospective memory is also generally comparable to that of controls.

**Hypothesis 2: poor prospective memory.** Checkers **are** impaired on both **event-based and time-based** prospective memory tasks (**Cuttler & Graf, 2009b**). One contributor is meta-memory in the form of **confidence** in one's prospective memory. **Cuttler et al. (2013)** gave participants **fake feedback** that their prospective memory was poor. This lowered their confidence in their prospective memory and **increased their urge to check**.

**The causal direction could run the other way.** Someone who checks the front door several times a day will certainly remember checking it hundreds of times, but may be unsure about **today**, because the many similar memories compete.

- **Van den Hout and Kindt (2004)** had some participants check a **virtual gas stove** repeatedly. Those who checked repeatedly had **less vivid and less detailed** memories of what happened on the final trial.
- **Linkovski et al. (2013)** ran a similar study and also measured **inhibitory control**, because obsessional patients have deficient inhibitory control, which may produce intrusive thoughts and memory problems. Repeated checking did **not** impair prospective memory performance, but it **did** reduce memory vividness and detail and lowered **confidence**, and all these effects were much stronger in people with **poor inhibitory control** (Figure 8.11).

In sum, obsessionals check compulsively because of **poor prospective memory**, **low confidence** in their memory and **deficient inhibitory control**, and checking **further undermines their confidence**.

> **Trap.** Compulsive checkers have **normal retrospective memory** (Cuttler & Graf, 2009a) but **impaired prospective memory** (Cuttler & Graf, 2009b). Repeated checking reduces **vividness, detail and confidence**, not prospective performance itself (Linkovski et al., 2013), and these effects are greatest with **poor inhibitory control**. "A retrospective memory deficit" is the trap answer.

## 8.8 Theoretical Perspectives on Prospective Memory

Does successful prospective memory **always** require capacity-consuming monitoring? **Smith and Bayen (2005)** say always; **Einstein and McDaniel (2005)** say sometimes.

### PAM theory (Smith & Bayen, 2005)

The **preparatory attentional and memory processes (PAM) theory** holds that prospective memory requires two processes:

1. A **capacity-consuming monitoring process** that begins when the intention is formed and continues until the action is performed.
2. **Retrospective-memory processes** that make sure we remember **what** action is to be performed.

It is implausible that preparatory attention runs for every intention over days or weeks, and **Smith et al. (2007)** accepted this, but they kept the core claim: retrieving an intention on a prospective task **always incurs a cost** and is **never automatic**.

### Multi-process theory (Einstein & McDaniel, 2005)

**Einstein and McDaniel's (2005) multi-process theory** allows that various processes, including attentional ones, can support prospective memory. But cue detection will usually be **automatic**, needing no attention, when four criteria are met (the first being the most important):

1. The **ongoing task** encourages processing of the target event of the prospective task.
2. The **cue and the action** are highly associated.
3. The cue is **conspicuous or salient**.
4. The intended **action is simple**.

The first criterion gives the theory its key distinction. A **focal** ongoing task encourages processing of the prospective target (for example, if the ongoing task involves word meaning and the cue is a particular word). A **non-focal** ongoing task does not (for example, if the ongoing task is about word meaning but the cue is a syllable). According to **McDaniel and Einstein (2011)**, **frontal** systems for planning and monitoring matter much more with **non-focal** tasks.

This yields a prediction about **ageing**. Ageing particularly impairs frontal functioning, so it should damage prospective memory mainly on non-focal tasks, with **little** age-related decline on **focal** tasks. **Uttl (2011)**, in a meta-analysis, found the opposite: there was a **substantial age-related decline even with focal tasks**. The most likely reason is that even focal tasks usually require some cognitive resources, for example to retrieve the intention. Uttl's findings give very little support to this prediction.

> **Trap.** **PAM theory** (Smith & Bayen, 2005) = prospective memory **always** requires capacity-consuming monitoring. **Multi-process theory** (Einstein & McDaniel, 2005) = monitoring **or** automatic, spontaneous retrieval, depending on the task. Do not swap the names. And Uttl (2011) is evidence **against** the multi-process prediction about ageing (decline was found on **focal** tasks too), not for it.

### The dynamic multi-process framework (Scullin et al., 2013)

**Scullin et al. (2013)** developed Einstein and McDaniel's theory into the **dynamic multi-process framework**, which names two processes:

1. **Monitoring**: **top-down** attentional control, searching for cues that the action should now be performed.
2. **Spontaneous retrieval**: **bottom-up** processing triggered by encountering a cue.

Scullin et al. argued that **monitoring is used mainly when prospective-memory cues are expected**, and spontaneous retrieval can switch it on when the context makes acting possible. Their examples (Figure 8.12):

- **Posting a package after work (Examples 1 and 2).** At work, a letter on a colleague's desk may spontaneously bring the intention to mind, but this does **not** start monitoring, because the package cannot be posted from work. Only **after work** does spontaneous retrieval lead to monitoring.
- **Passing a message to a colleague (Example 3).** Where the colleague is not expected (the coffee shop) there is no monitoring; walking past his cubicle spontaneously retrieves the intention, which triggers monitoring and a search for him.

Monitoring is thus neither always on (contrary to strong PAM) nor absent; context switches it on and off.

### Findings

**Monitoring has costs.** Monitoring is agreed to be demanding, so it should slow an ongoing task. **Smith (2003)** found that ongoing-task performance was **45% slower** in participants who also had a prospective task than in those doing the ongoing task alone. On trials where monitoring lapses, ongoing performance should improve, as **Gilbert et al. (2013)** found.

**A test of automaticity.** Smith et al. (2007) set out an explicit falsification criterion: PAM theory would be disproved by successful prospective performance accompanied by convincing evidence that a sufficiently sensitive and demanding background task was **not** disrupted. They used a very simple prospective task, pressing the **P** key when a **pink** stimulus appeared. Even so, it **slowed** the ongoing task, which seems to support PAM theory's claim that prospective memory always takes some capacity.

**Evidence for spontaneous noticing.** **Knight et al. (2011)** used **lexical decision** as the ongoing task and a prospective task of detecting **animal names beginning with "C"**. Participants were told the prospective task would only start **later**, so there was no reason to monitor yet, and indeed lexical decision speed was **unaffected** (**no active monitoring**). Yet future targets such as *cougar* were **spontaneously noticed**, as a later recognition test showed. Prospective cues can therefore be noticed **without conscious monitoring**, as multi-process theory predicts.

> **Key study, McDaniel et al. (2013).** The ongoing task required semantic processing. In the **non-focal** condition the prospective task was to respond to a **syllable** (*tor*, as in *tornado* or *history*); its demands differed from those of the ongoing task, so it was expected to need **top-down monitoring**. In the **focal** condition the prospective task was to respond to a particular **word** (*table*); its demands matched the ongoing task, so it was expected to rely on **bottom-up spontaneous retrieval**. Imaging showed **sustained** activity in the **anterior prefrontal cortex** (an attentional control region) in the **non-focal** condition, but only **transient** activation in the **focal** condition (Figure 8.13, left anterior prefrontal cortex).

**Monitoring follows expectation.** In Scullin et al.'s (2013) test, participants responded whenever *table* or *horse* appeared in any of **three** ongoing tasks, and, as predicted, monitored more on a task if they had **detected its first cue** (and so expected more) than if they had missed it.

### Evaluation

There is **good support for multi-process theory**, especially for its claim that prospective memory can involve **either spontaneous retrieval or monitoring**. Spontaneous retrieval occurs when the prospective task is easy and is sometimes relatively automatic (Knight et al., 2011), and the dynamic framework adds that monitoring occurs much more when cues are **expected**. PAM theory keeps some support (costs even with very simple tasks; Smith et al., 2007), but the evidence favours the multi-process approach.

### Cognitive neuroscience: prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex is involved in prospective memory, especially when monitoring is needed.

- **Burgess et al. (2000)** studied **65** brain-damaged patients with prospective memory problems and found damage to various frontal regions. In particular, **BA10** (Brodmann area 10, at the very front of the brain, also called **rostral prefrontal cortex**) was involved in **maintaining intentions**. The **retrospective** component (remembering **which** action to perform) depended instead on the **anterior and posterior cingulate**.
- **Gilbert et al. (2006)**, in a meta-analysis, mapped three processes onto different parts of BA10: **episodic memory retrieval** onto **lateral** BA10, **coordinating two processing demands** onto **anterior** BA10, and **self-reflection** onto **medial** BA10.
- **Burgess et al. (2011)** confirmed the importance of BA10 and argued that it would be "exceptionally inefficient" for prospective memory to use unique processes shared with no other cognitive activity. For example, **anterior BA10**, consistently active in prospective tasks, is also typically active in studies of **future thinking** or **mental time travel**.

> **Trap.** **BA10** maintains intentions, but the **retrospective** component (knowing what to do) was linked to the **cingulate**, not BA10 (Burgess et al., 2000). Within BA10: **lateral** = episodic retrieval, **anterior** = coordinating two demands, **medial** = self-reflection.

### Improving prospective memory

**Practical measures.** When an interruption strikes, deliberately telling yourself that you will go back to the abandoned task at the first opportunity (an **explicit resumption intention**) cuts down the resulting failures (**Dodhia & Dismukes, 2009**). **Dismukes (2012)** recommended creating **distinctive reminder cues** and placing them where they will be seen at the right moment, for example leaving a book you must take to college next to your keys the night before.

**Implementation intentions**, one of the most widely used techniques, "specify the when, where, and how of responses leading to goal attainment" (Gollwitzer, 1999, p. 494): conscious action plans that tie a goal to a specific situation, such as resolving to take your medicine immediately after brushing your teeth at night.

- **Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006)**, in a meta-analysis, found that implementation intentions **greatly increased** the probability of carrying out intentions.
- **Gollwitzer (1999)** argued that forming an implementation intention is like creating an **"instant habit"**, so it should **reduce the processing cost** of retrieving the intention.
- **McFarland and Glisky (2012)** tested this. The main task was answering trivia questions; the prospective task was to detect occasional uses of the word **"state"** in them; and participants also listened to digits and responded to **two consecutive odd digits**. With implementation intentions, prospective detection was **54%** versus **31%** for controls, and detection of odd-digit pairs was **59%** versus **35%**. Because the secondary task also improved, the implementation intentions apparently made target detection relatively **automatic**, freeing resources for the digits.
- **Rummel et al. (2012)** found better prospective performance (detecting *peach* or *tennis* within an ongoing task) after implementation-intention instructions, and tested the automaticity explanation directly. On some trials participants were told **not** to respond to the prospective targets. For participants who had formed implementation intentions, those targets **disrupted the ongoing task more**, because they retrieved the intention spontaneously even when it was not wanted.

> **Trap.** Rummel et al. (2012) is a case where **worse** performance is the evidence for the theory: the extra **disruption** on no-response trials shows that implementation intentions make retrieval **automatic**, as Gollwitzer's "instant habit" idea predicts.

### Overall evaluation of prospective memory research

**Strengths (five).**

1. The **number and nature of the processes** involved have been identified with increasing clarity.
2. Reasons for prospective failures in particular groups (pilots, air traffic controllers, obsessional individuals) have been identified.
3. **Theoretical progress**: most research fits multi-process theory, including spontaneous cue detection, and the dynamic framework shows that monitoring occurs mainly where cues are expected.
4. **Cognitive neuroscience** is increasingly identifying the brain areas associated with different prospective processes.
5. A new field of **"prospection"** or future thinking, which includes prospective memory, is developing.

**Limitations (three).**

1. **Weak incentives in the laboratory.** Participants have little reason to carry out the experimenter's intended actions, whereas real-life incentives include keeping friendships and even saving lives.
2. **Exaggerated differences** between prospective and retrospective memory. They share features (responding on the basis of what has been learned), and many prospective tasks include a retrospective component.
3. **Laboratory versus real-life differences.** Real intentions often have to be held for much **longer**, and with long retention intervals attention and monitoring matter less and **long-term memory** matters more (**Kvavilashvili & Fisher, 2007**). Also, laboratory participants usually know the **task context** in which cues will appear, whereas in real life we often do not know where we will need to act on an intention.

> **Trap.** For "which is NOT a limitation of prospective memory research?", the genuine limitations are weak incentives, exaggerated prospective/retrospective differences, and short intervals and known cue contexts in the laboratory. "Spontaneous retrieval has never been demonstrated" is not one (Knight et al., 2011).

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Memory is purposeful action.** Neisser's (1996) slogan explains the saying-is-believing effect, the working self's control over retrieval, the drift from correspondence to coherence, and our self-flattering memories of the past. In everyday life accuracy competes with social and self-related goals.
2. **Vividness and confidence are not accuracy.** Flashbulb memories (Talarico & Rubin, 2003), hyperthymestic individuals' ordinary susceptibility to false memories (Patihis et al., 2013) and the moderate confidence-accuracy link in identification (Odinot et al., 2009) make the same point from three directions.
3. **Source errors and retrieval both reshape memory.** Source confusion produces the misinformation effect and unconscious transference; retelling, reconsolidation and repeated checking show that remembering is never read-only.
4. **The self organises autobiographical memory.** The cognitive self ends absolute childhood amnesia, the life script and self-identity build the reminiscence bump, the working self steers generative retrieval, and in depression a negative, compartmentalised self appears as over-general memory.
5. **Controlled versus automatic retrieval.** Generative versus direct retrieval and monitoring versus spontaneous retrieval are the same contrast in two domains; implementation intentions shift prospective retrieval towards the automatic mode, and habit capture is the automatic mode working against you.
6. **Frontal control, medial temporal content.** Ventromedial PFC (autobiographical retrieval) and BA10 (intentions) handle search, monitoring and self-reference; hippocampus and amygdala supply recollection and emotional enhancement.

## Worked example

*Ms R., a 41-year-old schoolteacher, has recurrent major depressive disorder, now in partial remission, and checking compulsions (door, gas, taps). She has been missing doses of her antidepressant. On a cue-word task she answers "happy" with "holidays with my family" and "lonely" with "evenings after work". She says her whole life "has been one long failure", although her history includes a good marriage and a successful career. Last week she saw a chain-snatching outside her school, and the police want to interview her and show her a line-up.*

**What do her cue-word answers mean?** They are **over-general memories**: categories of repeated events rather than single episodes lasting under a day, the reduced specificity typical of depression (Liu et al., 2013). Prognostically, greater specificity predicts **recovery** (Brittlebank et al., 1993). The affect regulation hypothesis suggests she may be **avoiding** specific negative memories, which threat would worsen if she copes avoidantly (Debeer et al., 2011).

**Why does she call her whole life a failure?** In the self-memory system model her **working self** governs generative retrieval, and a negative working self pulls negative material from every lifetime period. Dalgleish et al. (2011) would predict negative, **compartmentalised** life chapters with **low positive redundancy**, and as a partly remitted patient she probably sits **between** current patients and controls, a marker of **relapse risk**.

**Is her checking caused by a bad memory?** Probably not bad **retrospective** memory, which is usually normal in checkers (Cuttler & Graf, 2009a). The better account is weak **prospective memory** (Cuttler & Graf, 2009b), **low confidence** (Cuttler et al., 2013) and **poor inhibitory control**. Each check makes her memory of the latest one **less vivid and detailed** and lowers her **confidence** (van den Hout & Kindt, 2004; Linkovski et al., 2013), so the checking maintains itself.

**What would help her take her medication?** A missed dose is a **time-based** failure, the harder type (Sellen et al., 1997). Convert it into an **event-based** task with an **implementation intention** ("straight after brushing my teeth at night, I take my tablet"; Gollwitzer, 1999), which makes retrieval relatively automatic (McFarland & Glisky, 2012), and put a **distinctive reminder cue** at the point of action (Dismukes, 2012).

**What should the police be advised?** She was frightened, so expect poorer face identification and peripheral detail (Deffenbacher et al., 2004; Easterbrook, 1959). Use a **cognitive interview** stressing **context reinstatement** and **reporting everything** (Colomb & Ginet, 2012), with rapport and perhaps eyes closed (Vredeveldt et al., 2011) but no backward-order recall (Dando et al., 2011). Avoid leading questions (Loftus & Palmer, 1974); discussing the incident with colleagues risks co-witness contamination (Edelson et al., 2011). For identification, use a **sequential** line-up with a warning that the culprit **may not be present** (Steblay, 1997) and a **not-sure** option (Steblay & Phillips, 2011), and consider **unconscious transference** (Ross et al., 1994) if a familiar face from the school gate is picked.

## MCQ traps

1. **Ecological validity** = **representativeness** + **generalisability**; generalisability matters **more**.
2. **Saying-is-believing** (Dudokovic et al., 2004; Hellmann et al., 2011): your **own** tailored retelling distorts your memory; Marsh and Tversky (2004): **42%** of retellings admitted inaccurate.
3. **Gilboa (2004)**: episodic = **right mid-dorsolateral** PFC; autobiographical = **left ventromedial** PFC.
4. **Brown and Kulik (1977)**: surprise plus consequentiality; six features; their claim was **less extreme** than usually portrayed (Curci & Conway, 2013).
5. **Talarico and Rubin (2003)**: over **32 weeks**, flashbulb memories more **vivid**, **not more consistent**; Pezdek (2003): **73%** "saw" non-existent first-tower footage.
6. **Rimmele et al. (2012)**: location **83%**, informant **70%**, activity **62%**, own reaction **34%**.
7. **Sharot et al. (2007)**: about **2** vs **4½ miles** from the WTC; closer = more vivid, more detail, more amygdala.
8. **Hyperthymestic syndrome**: LePort et al. (2012): **11** people, **8 times** better on dates, about **80%** obsessional, **average** on laboratory tasks, normal false-memory rates (Patihis et al., 2013); HK's right amygdala about **20%** larger (Ally et al., 2013).
9. **Flashbulb memory vs flashback**: flashbacks are **involuntary** traumatic intrusions in PTSD; Berntsen (2001): as vivid **over five years** on as **within one year**.
10. **Jack and Hayne (2010)**: **absolute** amnesia (first **2 years**, ended by the **cognitive self**, late in the second year, indexed by mirror self-recognition) then **relative** amnesia (preschool, ended by **language**); earliest memory **23 months**.
11. **Neurogenic hypothesis** (Josselyn & Frankland, 2012): **high** dentate gyrus neurogenesis **impairs** lasting memory; prompted by childhood amnesia in **animals**.
12. **Tustin and Hayne (2010)**: earliest memories about **1½** (age 5 to 9), **2½** (adolescents), **just over 3** years (adults).
13. **Reminiscence bump** (Rubin et al., 1986): **10 to 30**, especially **15 to 25**; present in **all five** cultures (Conway et al., 2005).
14. **Life-script theory** (Rubin & Berntsen, 2003): **negative** events show **no bump**, nor do positive **non-script** events (Berntsen et al., 2011); **68%** of positive events were script events, led by **having children (34%)**.
15. **Bohn and Berntsen (2011)**: children aged **10 to 14** have a life script (**79%**); unexpected events also show a bump (Dickson et al., 2011), pointing to **self-identity**.
16. **Conway (2005)** added **life story** and **themes**, renamed event-specific knowledge **episodic memories**, and argued **coherence beats correspondence**.
17. **Direct retrieval**: **3 times faster** (Uzer et al., 2012), more specific but less identity-relevant (Johannessen & Berntsen, 2010), more **left hippocampus** (Addis et al., 2012).
18. **St Jacques et al. (2011)**: all **four** networks construct; only **medial PFC** and **MTL** elaborate.
19. **Depression**: **specificity predicts recovery** (Brittlebank et al., 1993); threat raises over-generality only in **avoidant copers** (Debeer et al., 2011); remitted patients **intermediate** (Dalgleish et al., 2011).
20. **Loftus and Palmer (1974)**: **41** vs **34 mph**; broken glass **32% / 14% / 12%** (smashed / hit / control).
21. **Pre-event** information also distorts (Lindsay et al., 2004); **peripheral** details are most vulnerable (Dalton & Daneman, 2006); warnings reduce but do not abolish the effect (Ecker et al., 2010).
22. **Prior recall increases** the misinformation effect via **reconsolidation** (Chan & LaPaglia, 2013); genuine change tracks **amygdala-hippocampus** connectivity (Edelson et al., 2011).
23. **Weapon focus**: driven by **unexpectedness** too (Pickel, 2009); **moderate**, equal in lab and real world (Fawcett et al., 2013); faces still fixated more than weapons overall (Biggs et al., 2013).
24. **Deffenbacher et al. (2004)**: faces **54% vs 42%**, details **64% vs 52%**; **Easterbrook (1959)**: central memory improves, peripheral worsens.
25. **Older witnesses**: **43% vs 4%** false memories (Jacoby et al., 2005); **own-age bias** reflects **exposure** (teachers, geriatric nurses show none).
26. **Valentine et al. (2003)**: **640** witnesses, **314** line-ups, **20 / 40 / 40%** (filler / suspect / none); confidence-accuracy r = **+0.38** (Odinot et al., 2009).
27. **Unconscious transference**: bystander chosen **3 times** as often (Ross et al., 1994); **other-race = cross-race** effect, partly **perceptual** (Megreya et al., 2011: 70% vs 64%; 34% vs 47%).
28. **Jenkins et al. (2011)**: **40** photos of **2** people sorted into **7.5** identities; averaged faces **100%** vs **54%**.
29. **Line-ups** (Steblay et al., 2011): hits **52%** simultaneous vs **44%** sequential; false IDs **54% vs 32%**; sequential preferred. Warning: false IDs **down 42%**, hits **down 2%** (Steblay, 1997); not-sure option **22% to 12%**.
30. **Cognitive interview**: rules **1 and 2** from **encoding specificity**; enhanced version adds **rapport**; backward recall **harms** (Dando et al., 2011); it protects against misinformation **after**, not before, the interview (Memon et al., 2009).
31. **Prospective vs retrospective** (Graf, 2012): "flaky person" vs "faulty brain"; prospective memory is **low** in informational content.
32. **Event-based > time-based**: **52% vs 33%** (Sellen et al., 1997); a **well-specified time-based** task is no harder than an **ill-specified event-based** one (Hicks et al., 2005).
33. **Pilots**: **74 of 75** memory failures prospective (Dismukes & Nowinski, 2006); interruption pauses **88% / 65% / 48%** (Dodhia & Dismukes, 2009); habit capture vs prospective rehearsal.
34. **OCD checkers**: retrospective memory **normal**, prospective **impaired**; checking lowers **vividness and confidence**, not prospective performance (Linkovski et al., 2013).
35. **PAM** (Smith & Bayen, 2005) = monitoring **always**; **multi-process** (Einstein & McDaniel, 2005) = **sometimes**; Uttl (2011) found age decline on **focal** tasks, **against** the multi-process prediction.
36. **BA10** = **rostral PFC**, maintaining intentions (Burgess et al., 2000, **65** patients); implementation intentions = "instant habit" (Gollwitzer, 1999), **54% vs 31%** (McFarland & Glisky, 2012).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Ecological validity** | Real-life applicability: **representativeness** (naturalness of situation, stimuli, task) plus **generalisability** (real-world applicability of findings, the more important) |
| **Saying-is-believing effect** | Later memory distortion caused by having tailored your account of an event to an audience |
| **Autobiographical memory** | Long-term memory for the events of one's own life |
| **Flashbulb memories** | Vivid, confidently held memories of the circumstances of hearing about a dramatic event |
| **Hyperthymestic syndrome** | Exceptional recall of the events of one's own life alongside ordinary memory otherwise |
| **Flashbacks** | Involuntary, intense, sensory re-experiencing of a trauma in PTSD |
| **Childhood (infantile) amnesia** | Adults' inability to recall events from early childhood, especially before age 3 |
| **Reminiscence bump** | Older adults' excess of memories from adolescence and early adulthood |
| **Cognitive self** | Sense of oneself as having personal experiences, emerging late in the second year |
| **Social-cultural developmental theory** | Account placing language, parental reminiscing and culture at the centre of autobiographical memory development |
| **Absolute / relative amnesia** | No memories (first two years) / sparse memories (rest of preschool) |
| **Neurogenesis** | The production of new neurons in the brain |
| **Neurogenic hypothesis** | High infant hippocampal neurogenesis disrupts the formation of lasting memories |
| **Life script** | A culture's shared expectations about the major events of a typical life and their timing |
| **Self-memory system** | Model combining an autobiographical knowledge base with a working self |
| **Autobiographical knowledge base** | Hierarchy of lifetime periods, general events and event-specific knowledge |
| **Working self** | The current self, its possible future selves and goals, which control encoding and retrieval |
| **Coherence / correspondence** | Fit of memory with current goals and beliefs / accuracy to what happened |
| **Generative retrieval** | Deliberate, effortful construction of an autobiographical memory guided by current goals |
| **Direct retrieval** | Spontaneous, cue-triggered autobiographical recall without working-self involvement |
| **Over-general memory** | Recalling categories of repeated events instead of specific episodes; typical of depression |
| **Affect regulation hypothesis** | Over-general memory as avoidance of the emotion attached to specific negative memories |
| **Compartmentalisation** | Variation in negativity across lifetime periods, indicating a poorly integrated self |
| **Confirmation bias** | Distortion of eyewitness memory towards prior expectations |
| **Misinformation effect** | Distortion of eyewitness memory by misleading information met after the event |
| **Source misattribution** | Assigning a remembered detail to the wrong source |
| **Reconsolidation** | Restabilisation of a reactivated memory, during which it can be modified |
| **Vacant slot / blend explanations** | Misinformation fills a gap where nothing was stored / merges with the original information |
| **Weapon focus** | Attention captured by a weapon at the cost of other details |
| **Easterbrook's hypothesis** | Stress narrows attention onto central stimuli, impairing memory for peripheral ones |
| **Own-age bias** | Better identification of culprits of the witness's own age, due to exposure |
| **Unconscious transference** | Misidentifying a familiar but innocent face as the culprit |
| **Other-race (cross-race) effect** | Better recognition of same-race than other-race faces |
| **Simultaneous / sequential line-up** | All members shown at once / one at a time |
| **Cognitive interview** | Interview using four retrieval rules: reinstate context, report everything, vary order, vary perspective |
| **Enhanced cognitive interview** | Cognitive interview plus rapport-building and anxiety reduction |
| **Retrospective memory** | Memory for past events, people and knowledge |
| **Prospective memory** | Remembering to perform an intended action without an explicit reminder |
| **Time-based / event-based prospective memory** | Acting at a set time / acting when a particular event or circumstance occurs |
| **Habit capture** | A strong routine running automatically and displacing an intended deviation |
| **Prospective rehearsal** | Refreshing an interrupted intention ("What was I about to do?") |
| **Meta-memory** | Knowledge and beliefs about one's own memory, including confidence |
| **PAM theory** | Prospective memory always needs capacity-consuming preparatory monitoring (Smith & Bayen, 2005) |
| **Multi-process theory** | Prospective memory may use monitoring or automatic retrieval (Einstein & McDaniel, 2005) |
| **Focal / non-focal task** | Ongoing task that does / does not process the features defining the prospective cue |
| **Dynamic multi-process framework** | Monitoring is switched on mainly where cues are expected (Scullin et al., 2013) |
| **BA10 (rostral prefrontal cortex)** | Frontal pole region for maintaining intentions and future thinking |
| **Implementation intentions** | Conscious if-then plans specifying when, where and how a goal will be acted on |
