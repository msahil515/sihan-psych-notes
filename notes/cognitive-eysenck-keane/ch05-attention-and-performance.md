# Chapter 5: Attention and Performance

**What this chapter is really about.** Chapter 5 closes Part I ("Visual perception and attention") and is the chapter where perception meets limitation. Chapters 2 to 4 asked how we build a picture of the world; this one asks why we cannot process everything in that picture at once, and what decides which parts win. Two questions run through every section. The first is about **selection**: when several inputs arrive together (two voices, a crowded visual scene, a light and a sound), how do we pick one, and what happens to the rest? The second is about **sharing**: when we try to do two things together (drive and talk, read and take dictation), why does performance usually suffer, and can practice make the cost disappear? The first question is the study of **focused (selective) attention**; the second is the study of **divided attention**, or multitasking.

The chapter's verdict on both questions is the same, and it is worth holding from the start: **attention is far more flexible than the classic theories claimed.** Broadbent put a rigid filter early in processing; the evidence says the bottleneck can sit at various stages. The spotlight metaphor implied one fixed beam; the evidence says the beam can widen, narrow and even split. Shiffrin and Schneider drew a clean line between automatic and controlled processes; the evidence says automaticity comes in degrees. Older exam keys tend to reward the classic, tidy answers, while the 7th edition keeps saying "it depends". You need both versions, and this chapter flags each place where they part company.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 5.1 gives three distinctions every later section uses. Section 5.2 is auditory selection (Cherry; Broadbent, Treisman, Deutsch and Deutsch). Section 5.3 is visual selection: spotlight, zoom lens and split attention; space versus object; Lavie's load theory; Posner's and Corbetta and Shulman's attention networks. Section 5.4 applies those networks to **neglect** and **extinction**, the most clinically important material here. Section 5.5 is visual search (feature integration theory and its successors), 5.6 cross-modal attention, 5.7 dual-task performance, and 5.8 automatic processing, ending with the psychological refractory period. Most theories end with an evaluation, and the limitations are examined as often as the claims.

## 5.1 Introduction

### What "attention" means

Attention earns its place in daily life every time we cross a road without being hit, find the keys we put down somewhere, or manage two jobs at once. The word has several meanings, but in cognitive psychology it nearly always refers to **selectivity of processing**: out of everything that could be processed at a given moment, only some of it is. The classic statement comes from **William James (1890)**, who described attention as the mind taking possession, clearly and vividly, of one out of several objects or trains of thought that seem to be available at the same time, with focusing and concentration of consciousness at its core.

James also drew a distinction that is still central to modern theory:

- **Active attention** is controlled **top-down**, by the person's own goals and expectations. You decide to look for your friend's red coat.
- **Passive attention** is controlled **bottom-up**, by external stimuli. A sudden loud bang grabs you whether you like it or not.

This distinction reappears, in neural form, in Corbetta and Shulman's (2002) goal-directed and stimulus-driven attention networks and in Corbetta et al. (2008) (section 5.3).

> **Trap.** "Active versus passive" (James, 1890), "endogenous versus exogenous" (Posner, 1980), "goal-directed (dorsal) versus stimulus-driven (ventral)" (Corbetta & Shulman, 2002) and "top-down versus bottom-up" are four labels for closely related contrasts from four different eras. An option that attributes the endogenous/exogenous distinction to James, or the dorsal/ventral attention networks to Posner, is a look-alike trap. Match the label to the author.

### Focused versus divided attention

The second distinction is between two experimental arrangements, both of which present at least two inputs at the same time:

| | **Focused (selective) attention** | **Divided attention** |
|---|---|---|
| Instruction | Respond to only one input; ignore the rest | Attend and respond to all inputs |
| Everyday example | A predator tracking one animal in a flock | Texting while watching television; multitasking |
| What it tells us | How well we select, how well we resist distraction, how selection works, and what happens to the unattended input | What our processing limits are and how much capacity attentional mechanisms have |

The chapter's first half (5.2 to 5.5) is mainly about focused attention; the second half (5.7 and 5.8) is mainly about divided attention.

### External versus internal attention

The third distinction comes from **Chun et al. (2011)**. **External attention** is the selection and modulation of incoming sensory information. **Internal attention** is the selection, modulation and maintenance of information the mind itself generates: task rules, responses, long-term memories and the contents of working memory. The link to **Baddeley's working memory model** (Chapter 6) matters here: its **central executive** controls attention and plays a major part in both kinds.

### Two limitations of attention research

The authors warn that most research shares two weaknesses. First, it has concentrated on **external** attention at the expense of internal attention. Second, in almost every laboratory study, what participants attend to is fixed by the **experimenter's instructions**, whereas in real life it is set mostly by our **current goals and emotional states**.

Two related topics are covered elsewhere: **change blindness** (which shows how tightly attention and perception are linked) is in Chapter 4, and the relationship between **attention and consciousness** is in Chapter 16.

> **Trap.** Change blindness (missing a *change* in a scene) and inattentional blindness (missing an unexpected object that is *present*, as in the gorilla studies) are Chapter 4 topics. Inattentional blindness reappears here only in the mobile-phone driving research (5.7).

## 5.2 Focused Auditory Attention

### The cocktail party problem

In 1953 the British scientist **Colin Cherry** became interested in what he called the **cocktail party problem**: how can a listener follow one conversation when several people are talking at once? There is no single answer, and the section builds one from several pieces.

**McDermott (2009)** pointed out that the listener actually faces two problems, one after the other:

1. **Sound segregation.** The ears receive one mixed pressure wave. The listener has to work out which bits of sound belong together (to one source) and which do not.
2. **Selection.** Once the sources are separated, the listener has to direct attention to the one that matters and ignore the others.

How hard the first problem is can be seen from machines. Automatic speech recognition programmes are close to perfect with one speaker in a quiet room but often fail badly when several sound sources are active together (**Shen et al., 2008**).

McDermott also explained why **auditory segregation is often harder than visual segregation** (deciding which visual features belong to which object). There are two reasons:

- **Overlap at the receptor.** Signals from different sound sources overlap heavily in the cochlea, whereas different visual objects usually fall on different parts of the retina.
- **Addition versus occlusion.** Every sound source adds its energy to the signal at the ear. In vision, a nearer object tends to block out the one behind it. Imagine trying to pick out one object if everything in the room were transparent and superimposed: that is roughly the listener's situation.

The other big question is how much of the **ignored** voice is processed; the competing answers form the early versus late selection debate below.

### Cherry's dichotic listening studies

Cherry (1953) used a **dichotic listening task**: a different auditory message goes to each ear, and the listener usually attends to one. Because it is hard to be sure a listener is really attending to the target ear, he added **shadowing**: the listener repeats the attended message aloud, word for word, as it arrives.

His main findings:

- Listeners solved the cocktail party problem by using **physical differences** between the messages: the speaker's **sex**, the **intensity** of the voice and the speaker's **location**.
- When Cherry removed those physical cues by playing two messages **in the same voice to both ears**, listeners found it very hard to separate them on the basis of **meaning** alone.
- Very little seemed to get through from the **unattended** ear. Listeners seldom noticed that the unattended message had switched to a **foreign language** or to **reversed (backwards) speech**.
- **Physical changes** in the unattended ear, such as a switch to a **pure tone**, were nearly always detected.

**Moray (1959)** supported the conclusion that unattended speech gets almost no processing: listeners had very little memory for words in the unattended message even when each was presented **35 times**.

> **Trap.** Moray (1959) is cited for two findings that point in opposite directions. Unattended words repeated **35 times** left almost no memory trace (evidence for little processing), yet listeners often detected their **own name** in the unattended ear (evidence that something meaningful gets through). A question may quote either finding; know both. *Classic background:* the figure usually quoted for Moray's own-name result is that only about a third of listeners noticed their name, which is why "often" does not mean "usually".

### Where is the bottleneck? Early versus late selection

Many psychologists explained the difficulty of handling two auditory inputs with a **processing bottleneck**. A narrow stretch of road lets only a little traffic through at a time; a narrow stage in the processing system would likewise limit how much of two simultaneous inputs could be processed. A bottleneck is not only a limitation, though: for the cocktail party problem it could be useful, because it might let the listener process only the wanted voice. The question that divided the theorists was **where** the bottleneck sits.

**Broadbent (1958): early selection.** Broadbent placed a **filter** early in processing. Both inputs are first held briefly in a **sensory buffer**. The filter then lets one input through on the basis of its **physical characteristics** (which ear, which voice, how loud). The other input waits in the buffer and is **rejected** unless attention turns to it very quickly. Meaning is analysed only after the filter, so the unattended message should receive essentially no semantic processing.

**Treisman (1964): attenuation (flexible selection).** Treisman argued that the bottleneck's position is **more flexible** than Broadbent allowed. Analysis proceeds through a hierarchy of tests: first **physical cues**, then **syllable pattern** and **specific words**, and finally **grammatical structure** and **meaning**. If there is not enough processing capacity to analyse every input fully, the **later** stages of the hierarchy are dropped for the unattended input. So the unattended message is not blocked; it is turned down. Treisman also stressed **top-down** processes such as expectation. Shadowers sometimes say aloud a word that was actually in the unattended ear, and such **breakthroughs** happen mostly when that word is **highly probable** given the context of the attended message.

**Deutsch and Deutsch (1963): late selection.** At the opposite extreme from Broadbent, Deutsch and Deutsch argued that **all stimuli are fully analysed**, including for meaning, and that the bottleneck lies close to the **response** end of the system: the most **important or relevant** stimulus is the one that determines the response.

Figure 5.1 in the book sets the three models one above the other. In words:

| | **Broadbent (1958)** | **Treisman (1964)** | **Deutsch & Deutsch (1963)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Label | Early selection (filter) | Attenuation; flexible bottleneck | Late selection |
| Where selection happens | Before meaning, on physical features | Varies with available capacity; later analyses dropped for unattended input | After full analysis, near the response |
| Processing of unattended message | Little or none | Reduced and flexible; some can break through | Reasonably thorough |
| Key role of meaning | Only after the filter | Context and expectation can let probable words through | Importance decides which input controls the response |

*Classic background (not in the 7th edition):* Broadbent's **split-span** studies (1954) found that listeners given simultaneous digit pairs to the two ears preferred to report ear by ear, fitting a filter that selects by channel. **Gray and Wedderburn (1960)** showed that listeners report by meaning when a message makes sense across ears ("Dear Aunt Jane"), and **Treisman (1960)** found shadowers sometimes followed a story into the unattended ear. Meaning influencing selection is impossible for a strict early filter, and these results motivated attenuation theory, usually described with a **dictionary** of word units whose thresholds differ: important words (your own name, "fire") have permanently low thresholds and so break through.

> **Trap.** The older key is still examined: Broadbent = **early selection** (all-or-none filter on physical features); Treisman = **attenuation** (weakened, not blocked); Deutsch and Deutsch = **late selection** (full analysis, selection at response). The 7th edition adds the verdict: unattended messages get **some semantic processing, but less than attended ones**, and the bottleneck can sit at **various stages**, which is closest to Treisman. "No semantic processing at all" (strict Broadbent) and "processed as fully as attended speech" (strict Deutsch and Deutsch) are both wrong by the 7th edition.

### Findings on the unattended input

The three theories make different predictions. Broadbent predicts little or no processing of the unattended message; Treisman predicts a flexible, reduced amount; Deutsch and Deutsch predict fairly thorough processing. The evidence comes down in the middle: **some processing, but less than for attended input.**

- **Treisman and Riley (1969)** had listeners shadow one of two messages and stop shadowing and **tap** whenever they heard a target word in **either** message. Many more targets were detected in the **shadowed** message. If both messages were fully analysed (Deutsch and Deutsch), detection should have been similar for both.
- **Bentin et al. (1995)** presented different words to each ear and told listeners to attend to one ear only. The **unattended** words showed evidence of **some semantic processing**, but **less** than the attended words. This is the cleanest single result for the "some but less" conclusion.
- **Li et al. (2011)** showed that personal significance increases processing of unattended words. Listeners shadowed one message while distractor words played in the other ear. **Women dissatisfied with their weight** made **more shadowing errors** than other women when the unattended ear carried weight-related words (for example *fat*, *chunky*, *slim*). The extra errors show that the weight words were being processed more by the women for whom they mattered. Note the clinical relevance: concerns about body shape bias what gets through the "filter".
- **Own name.** Listeners often detect their own name in the unattended message (**Moray, 1959**). **Conway et al. (2001)** showed that this depends on **working memory capacity** (Chapter 6). Low-capacity participants detected their own name far more often than high-capacity participants (**65% versus 20%**), because they had **less attentional control** over what reached awareness.

> **Trap.** The direction of the Conway et al. (2001) result catches students. It is the **low** working memory capacity group that noticed their name more (**65%**), not the high group (**20%**). Detecting your name in the ignored ear is a *failure* of focused attention, and people with better attentional control fail less often.

The brain evidence points the same way:

- **Coch et al. (2005)** recorded **event-related potentials (ERPs)** while listeners attended to one of two inputs and detected targets on either. ERPs about **100 ms** after a target were **larger** when it arrived on the **attended** message, indicating more processing there. Similar results had been reported earlier (see **Talsma and Kok, 2002**).
- Greater activity for attended sounds is ambiguous: it could mean the attended input is **enhanced**, or that the unattended input is **suppressed**, or both. **Horton et al. (2013)** tested this with separate speech messages to each ear. Activity was greater for the attended message in several areas, especially at about **90 ms** after stimulus onset, and the difference came from **both** enhancement of the attended message **and** suppression of the unattended one.

### Findings on the cocktail party problem: how humans segregate voices

Humans manage something machines still struggle with (Shen et al., 2008). The answer combines **bottom-up** and **top-down** factors.

**Bottom-up factors.** Cherry's physical cues (speaker sex, intensity) work very well with two voices, but with many voices the task becomes much harder. **Shamma et al. (2011)** identified **temporal coherence**: all the sound features belonging to one source tend to switch on together when that source is active and off together when it is silent. If the listener can identify even one distinctive feature of the target voice, the voice's other features can be gathered up because they rise and fall in step with it.

**Top-down factors.** Bottom-up cues are often not enough, and then knowledge and expectation take over. The anatomy suggests this: there are extensive **descending pathways** from auditory cortex back to areas of early auditory processing (**Robinson & McAlpine, 2009**). McDermott (2009) reports two behavioural examples:

- A target message is easier to follow when its words form **sentences** rather than random word strings.
- Listeners are much more accurate at picking out one speaker among several if they have **already heard that speaker's voice alone**. Familiarity helps.

**Marozeau et al. (2010)** applied the same logic to music. Listeners tried to follow a melody among irrelevant notes. **Musicians** did better than non-musicians, showing that expertise and knowledge help segregation. The researchers also varied how much the target melody overlapped in sound with the other notes over time. Following the melody was **easier when overlap increased** across the trial, because the melody could be identified at the start, while it was still distinct, and then tracked.

**Mesgarani and Chang (2012)** recorded directly from **auditory cortex** in patients with implanted **multi-electrode arrays**. Two messages, one in a male voice and one in a female voice, were played to the **same ear**, and participants attended to one. The cortical responses carried the spectral and temporal features of the **attended** speaker, much as if that speaker had been heard alone. Selection was therefore already shaping the representation in auditory cortex.

**Golumbic et al. (2013)** pointed out that at a real party we can also see the speaker. With two simultaneous messages (male and female voices) and an instruction about which to attend to, processing of the attended message was **enhanced** when participants could watch a **movie of that speaker talking**. The visual input probably made the target message easier to attend to (a cross-modal effect; see 5.6).

In sum, listeners generally do select one message from several, using a mixture of bottom-up and top-down processes. Much of what happens in the auditory system looks like a **"winner-takes-all"** competition in which processing of the winning input suppresses brain activity for all the losing inputs (**Kurt et al., 2008**).

*Classic background:* older textbooks present Lavie's load theory (5.3) as a **resolution** of the early versus late debate (selection looks early when the task fills perceptual capacity, late when capacity is spare). The 7th edition treats it under vision and notes the perceptual-load prediction **fails for hearing** (Murphy et al., 2013).

## 5.3 Focused Visual Attention

Visual attention has been studied far more than auditory attention, mainly because vision is our dominant sense with more cortex than any other (and because visual timing used to be easier to control, an advantage modern equipment has removed).

The section asks four questions: what focused visual attention is like, what it selects, what happens to unattended stimuli, and which brain systems are involved.

### Spotlight, zoom lens or multiple spotlights?

**The spotlight.** A spotlight lights up a fairly small region, leaves little visible outside its beam, and can be swung to any object. **Posner (1980)** and others argued that visual attention works in the same way: one beam of fixed size, moved from place to place.

**The zoom lens.** **Eriksen and St James (1986)** argued that attention is more flexible than that. Like a zoom lens, the area of focal attention can be deliberately **widened or narrowed**. Driving shows why this is useful: most of the time a driver benefits from attending to as much of the visual field as possible, to anticipate danger, but the moment a hazard is spotted the beam narrows onto it.

**Multiple spotlights.** The most flexible view is the **multiple spotlights** theory (for example **Awh & Pashler, 2000**), which allows **split attention**: attention directed at **two or more regions that are not next to each other**, with the space in between left unattended. Split attention could save resources, since nothing is wasted on the irrelevant space between two relevant regions.

Split attention is controversial. **Jans et al. (2010)** argued that attention is tied to **action**, so attending to two separated objects should interfere with acting on either, although there is no strong evidence that it does. **Cave et al. (2010)** replied that the flexibility of split attention is often an **advantage**.

#### Findings for the zoom lens

- **LaBerge (1983)** presented **five-letter words**, each followed immediately by a **probe** that required a fast response and could appear at the position of any of the five letters. In the **narrow-beam** condition, participants categorised only the **middle letter**; in the **broad-beam** condition, they categorised the **whole word**. The logic was that probes falling inside the beam should be answered faster. The probe reaction times (Figure 5.2) showed the beam focused tightly on the **third (middle) letter** in the narrow condition, with no such central concentration in the broad condition. The same stimuli, a different task, and a differently sized beam: exactly what a zoom lens predicts.
- **Müller et al. (2003)** showed four squares arranged in a semicircle and cued observers to attend to **one, two or all four**. Four objects then appeared, one per square, and observers decided whether a target (for example a white circle) was among them. Activation in **early visual areas** was **most widespread** when the attended region was largest (all four squares) and most restricted when it was smallest (one square). The attended area changed size, and the brain's visual map changed with it.

#### Findings for split attention

- **Awh and Pashler (2000)** cued two locations a little apart, at which two digits would probably appear (Figure 5.3). On some trials a digit appeared in the space **between** the two cued locations. A zoom lens stretched to cover both cued locations should include that middle space, so detection there should have been very good. In fact it was **poor**. Attention had been split across two separate regions, as the multiple spotlights view predicts.
- **Morawetz et al. (2007)** presented letters and digits at **five locations** at once, one in each quadrant and one at the centre. When participants attended to the **upper left** and **bottom right** stimuli and ignored the others, brain activation showed **two peaks**, corresponding to the attended areas, with **less activation for the region between them**.
- **Niebergall et al. (2011)** recorded single neurons in **monkeys** attending to two moving random-dot patterns while ignoring a distractor. In the key condition the distractor lay **between and close to** the two attended patterns, and neuronal responses to it **decreased**.

These results are best explained by split attention plus a mechanism that **actively suppresses** distractors between attended stimuli. **Verdict:** depending on the situation and the observer's goals, visual attention can behave like a spotlight, a zoom lens or multiple spotlights.

> **Trap.** Signature studies: **spotlight** = Posner (1980); **zoom lens** = Eriksen and St James (1986), tested by LaBerge (1983) and imaged by Müller et al. (2003); **split attention** = Awh and Pashler (2000), whose key result is *poor* detection in the gap between two cued locations, exactly what a zoom lens cannot explain. Crediting Awh and Pashler with support for the zoom lens reverses the finding.

### What is selected? Space, objects and features

Spotlight and zoom-lens metaphors imply that we select a **region of space** (**space-based attention**); the alternative is that we select **objects** (**object-based attention**). Object-based selection is plausible because perception is mainly about objects that matter to us (Chapters 2 and 3), and **Henderson and Hollingworth (1999)** reviewed evidence that eye fixations on natural scenes land **almost exclusively on objects**. A preference for objects does not rule out attending to space; the system is probably flexible enough to do either.

#### Evidence for object-based attention

- **O'Craven et al. (1999)** superimposed a **face** and a **house** transparently at the **same location** and asked participants to attend to one. Face-processing areas were more active when the face was attended, and house-processing areas were more active when the house was attended. Because the two objects occupied the same space, a purely space-based mechanism could not have separated them. Attention was therefore at least partly **object-based**.
- **Hou and Liu (2012)** also used two superimposed objects and found that several areas are involved in top-down selection of one object, with **dorsal fronto-parietal** areas being particularly important.

#### The Egly et al. (1994) two-rectangle task

**Egly et al. (1994)** devised what became the standard method for comparing space-based and object-based attention (Figure 5.4). Two rectangles are on the screen. A cue flashes at one end of one rectangle, indicating where a target will probably appear, and the participant detects the target as fast as possible. The target can appear at:

1. **V (valid):** the cued location.
2. **IS (invalid, same object):** the uncued end of the **cued** rectangle.
3. **ID (invalid, different object):** an uncued end of the **other** rectangle, at the **same distance** from the cue as IS.

Detection was slower on invalid than valid trials, as expected. The key result was that invalid targets were detected **faster at IS than at ID**, even though the two were equally far from the cue. If attention were purely spatial, the two invalid locations should not differ. The advantage for the cued object shows that attention **spreads through the cued object**: it is at least partially object-based.

Three follow-up questions refine this.

**Is object-based attention automatic or strategic?** If it were automatic, it should always appear; if strategic, only sometimes. The evidence favours **strategic** processes (**Shomstein, 2012**). **Drummond and Shomstein (2010)** found **no** object-based effect when the cue predicted the target's location with **100% certainty**. If spreading attention through the object would only hurt performance, people stop doing it.

**Does recent experience matter?** **Lee et al. (2012)** used the Egly task and found the usual object-based advantage overall, **except** when the target had appeared in the **uncued object on the previous trial**. Attention, they concluded, tracks the short-term structure of the environment and adjusts automatically to make the most of it.

**Is space-based attention also present?** Finding an object-based effect does not mean the space-based effect is absent. **Hollingworth et al. (2012)** used a version of the task with three kinds of within-object cue at different distances from the target (Figure 5.5). They found **both**:

- **Object-based:** when the target was far from the cue, accuracy was worse if the cue had been in a **different** object.
- **Space-based:** when target and cue were in the **same** object, accuracy **declined with distance** between them.

So object-based and space-based attention are **not mutually exclusive**. **Pilz et al. (2012)** went further. Across a range of standard tasks (including the Egly task) they found **much more evidence for space-based than object-based** attention, and strong **individual differences**: only a **small fraction** of participants showed object-based attention at all.

#### Inhibition of return

Searching a scene would be inefficient if attention kept going back to places already checked. In fact we show **inhibition of return**: a reduced likelihood of attention returning to a region that was recently its focus. The theoretical question is whether the inhibition is attached to **locations** or to **objects**.

The evidence is mixed. Several studies report object-based inhibition of return (reviewed by **Chen, 2012**). However, **List and Robertson (2007)**, using the Egly et al. task, found that **object-based** effects were slow to appear, small and easily disrupted by minor changes of procedure, whereas **location-based** inhibition of return appeared **rapidly**, was **much larger** and was found **consistently**. The most likely conclusion is that **both** kinds exist.

*Classic background:* inhibition of return was first described by **Posner and Cohen (1984)** in the cueing paradigm. A non-predictive peripheral cue speeds detection at the cued location if the target follows quickly, but when the interval between cue and target is longer (roughly beyond 300 ms) responses at the cued location become **slower** than at an uncued location. Early facilitation followed by later inhibition is the classic pattern.

#### Feature-based attention

We can also attend to **features**. If you are looking for a friend in a crowd and she nearly always wears red, you may attend to **colour** rather than to any particular object or place. **Kravitz and Behrmann (2011)** found that space-based, object-based and feature-based attention **interact** to enhance the processing of objects.

#### Evaluation

**What the research has established.**

1. Object-based attention is much more common than space-based attention in **everyday life**, but there are many exceptions.
2. Visual attention can combine **space-based, object-based and feature-based** processes.
3. The balance between object-based and space-based attention is **flexible**, shaped by **strategic processes** (Drummond & Shomstein), **recent experience** (Lee et al.) and **individual differences** (Pilz et al.).

**Limitations.**

1. The forms of attention clearly **interact** (Kravitz & Behrmann, 2011), but such interactions have rarely been studied.
2. It is surprisingly hard to define what counts as an **"object"**. The Gestalt laws of perceptual organisation (Chapter 3) show how complex it is to predict which elements will be grouped and attended as one object.
3. We do not know **why** object-based attention dominates in everyday life but not in the laboratory. One suggestion is that real life gives us an **incentive** to attend to objects that laboratory tasks often lack.

> **Trap.** The factors shown to shift the object/space balance are **strategic processes**, **recent experience** and **individual differences** (Drummond & Shomstein; Lee et al.; Pilz et al.). Note the paradox the book states: object-based attention dominates in **everyday life**, but laboratory tasks (Pilz et al., 2012) find **more space-based** attention.

### What happens to unattended visual stimuli?

As you would expect, ignored visual stimuli are processed **less** than attended ones; the interesting question is from what point. **Martinez et al. (1999)** recorded ERPs to attended and unattended visual stimuli. The two were **similar for the first 50 to 55 ms** after stimulus onset; after that, ERPs to **attended** stimuli were **larger**. Selective attention therefore affects **all but the very earliest** stages of visual processing.

Irrelevant stimuli are often impossible to ignore; load theory asks what decides whether they hurt performance.

### Load theory (Lavie)

**Lavie's (2005, 2010) load theory** is one of the most influential accounts of distraction. It distinguishes two kinds of load that work in **opposite directions**.

**Perceptual load.** How much a task demands of **perceptual capacity** decides how much is left over for distractors. A **high perceptual load** task uses nearly all of our perceptual capacity, so there is little left for irrelevant stimuli and they are processed less. A **low perceptual load** task leaves **spare** capacity, which "spills over" automatically onto whatever else is present, so irrelevant stimuli **are** processed and can distract.

**Cognitive load.** Lavie (2005) argued that **cognitive load**, such as demands on **working memory**, also matters, but in the reverse direction. **High cognitive load increases distraction**, because it reduces our ability to use **cognitive control** to keep targets and distractors apart.

Perceptual load effects are relatively **automatic**; cognitive load effects involve **controlled** processes; and the theory assumes the two act **independently**.

| | **High perceptual load** | **High cognitive load** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on distraction | **Less** distraction | **More** distraction |
| Why | No spare perceptual capacity to spill onto distractors | Working memory is busy, so cognitive control over targets versus distractors is weakened |
| Process type | Relatively automatic | Controlled |
| Key study | Forster and Lavie (2008) | de Fockert et al. (2001) |

> **Trap.** The two loads push distraction in **opposite** directions, and examiners exploit this constantly. High **perceptual** load **reduces** distractor interference; high **cognitive (working memory)** load **increases** it. An option stating "any increase in task load reduces distraction" or "high working memory load protects against distractors" is wrong.

#### Findings

- **Forster and Lavie (2008)** presented **six letters in a circle**; participants decided which target (**X or N**) was present. In the **high-load** condition, the five non-target letters were **more similar in shape** to the targets. On some trials a cartoon character (for example **SpongeBob SquarePants**) appeared as a distractor. The distractor interfered **only under low load**.
- **Schwartz et al. (2005)** measured brain responses to **flickering draughtboard** distractors during a low or high perceptual-load task. Under **high** perceptual load, the distractors produced **less activation** in several visual areas: distractor processing really was reduced, not just ignored at the response stage.
- **Murphy et al. (2013)** tested the perceptual-load prediction in **hearing**. The result was quite different: distractor processing was **unrelated** to perceptual load. They suggested that the auditory system does not allow capacity to be focused as tightly as the visual system does.
- Distraction can also be **internal**. **Forster and Lavie (2009)** found significantly **fewer task-irrelevant thoughts** (mind-wandering) during a high perceptual-load task than a low one.
- **de Fockert et al. (2001)** tested the cognitive-load prediction. Participants classified **famous written names** as **pop stars or politicians** while holding either a short or a long **digit string** in memory (low or high working memory load). **Famous faces** served as distractors. The faces impaired performance **more under high working memory load**, and there was **more face-related activity in visual cortex** under high load.
- If cognitive load works by reducing attentional control, people whose attentional control is poor should resist distraction badly. **Eysenck et al. (2007)** proposed **attentional control theory**, which holds that **anxious** individuals have poor attentional control. As both that theory and load theory predict, **Moser et al. (2012a)** found anxious individuals **much more susceptible to distraction** than non-anxious ones. This is one of the chapter's direct bridges to clinical psychology (Chapter 15 develops anxiety and attention further).

Two findings show the theory is **oversimplified**:

- **Hains and Baillargeon (2011)** used **human faces** as distractors and found they **could not be ignored even under high perceptual load**, probably because attending to faces is so important in everyday life.
- **Linnell and Caparos (2011)** tested the independence assumption and found that perceptual and cognitive processes **interact**. Perceptual load worked as load theory predicts **only when cognitive load was low**. So perceptual-load effects are not automatic after all; they depend on cognitive resources being available.

#### Evaluation of load theory

**Strengths.** Load theory has become a major theory of attention. The distinction between **perceptual and cognitive load** has proved very useful. There is strong support for the prediction that high perceptual load reduces distraction from both **external** and **internal** stimuli, provided the tasks are **visual**, and for the prediction that high cognitive load **increases** susceptibility to distraction.

**Limitations.**

1. High perceptual load does **not** reduce distraction in the **auditory** modality (Murphy et al., 2013).
2. Some distractors, notably **faces**, distract even under high perceptual load (Hains & Baillargeon, 2011).
3. Perceptual and cognitive load do **not** have entirely separate effects (Linnell & Caparos, 2011).
4. The theory ignores other factors known to influence selective visual attention, such as the **salience** of distractors and the **spatial distance** between distractors and task stimuli (**Khetrapal, 2010**).

### Major attention networks

Several theorists (for example **Posner, 1980**; **Corbetta & Shulman, 2002**) have argued for **two major attention networks**: one **goal-directed** or **endogenous**, the other **stimulus-driven** or **exogenous**. This is James's active versus passive distinction (5.1) given an experimental method and, later, an anatomy.

#### Posner's cueing studies: endogenous and exogenous systems

Posner (1980) studied **covert attention**: a shift of attention to a location **without any eye movement** towards it. Participants kept their eyes still and pressed a key as fast as possible when a light appeared to the left or right. Before the light came a cue of one of two kinds:

- a **central cue**: an **arrow** at fixation pointing left or right;
- a **peripheral cue**: a brief brightening of the outline of a **box** at one of the possible locations.

Most cues were **valid** (they correctly indicated where the light would come), some were **invalid** (they pointed to the wrong side), and a **neutral** cue (a central **cross**) gave no information.

The results:

1. Responses were **fastest after valid cues**, **intermediate after neutral cues** and **slowest after invalid cues**. Attention had moved to the cued side without the eyes moving, producing a benefit when the cue was right and a cost when it was wrong.
2. The pattern was similar for **central** and **peripheral** cues.
3. The decisive manipulation: when cues were valid on only a **small fraction** of trials (so they were useless), participants **ignored central cues**, but **peripheral cues still influenced performance**.

The third result is what separates the two systems. An arrow has to be interpreted and acted on voluntarily, so when it is uninformative people simply stop using it. A flash at a location captures attention whether or not it helps. Posner therefore distinguished:

| | **Endogenous system** | **Exogenous system** |
|---|---|---|
| Control | The person's **intentions** | **Automatic** shifts of attention |
| Typical trigger | **Central** cues (arrows) that must be interpreted | **Peripheral** cues, even when **uninformative** |
| What attracts it | Whatever the goal specifies | Stimuli that are **salient** or **different** from their surroundings (for example in colour) |
| Ignored when uninformative? | Yes | No |

> **Trap.** **Covert** attention moves **without an eye movement** (overt attention moves the eyes); Posner's task measures covert orienting because fixation is held. **Central arrow = endogenous (voluntary); peripheral flash = exogenous (automatic).** The decisive evidence is that an **uninformative** peripheral cue still captures attention while an uninformative central cue is ignored; an option saying the reverse has the systems swapped.

*Classic background (not in the 7th edition):* **Posner and Petersen (1990)** proposed **three** networks: **alerting** (reaching and keeping a ready state), **orienting** (selecting a location, as in the cueing task) and **executive control** (resolving response conflict, as in the Stroop task), all measured by the Attention Network Test (Fan and colleagues). Indian papers often ask for these three. Their **orienting** network roughly covers what Corbetta and Shulman split into dorsal and ventral networks, and **alerting** returns in the treatment of neglect (5.4).

#### Corbetta and Shulman's two networks

**Corbetta and Shulman (2002)** proposed two attention systems that map onto Posner's two.

**1. The dorsal attention network (goal-directed, top-down).** This resembles Posner's endogenous system. It is a **fronto-parietal** network that includes the **intraparietal sulcus**. It is shaped by **expectations, knowledge and current goals**, and it is used whenever an observer has a cue predicting the location (or some other feature) of a stimulus that is about to appear.

**2. The ventral attention network (stimulus-driven, bottom-up).** This resembles Posner's exogenous system. It is a **right-hemisphere ventral fronto-parietal** network that comes into play when an **unexpected but potentially important** stimulus appears (the book's example is flames appearing under a door). Its job is **"circuit-breaking"**: it interrupts the current focus and redirects attention.

What actually triggers the circuit-breaker? You might expect the most salient or distinctive stimuli. **Corbetta et al. (2008)** argued instead that distractors **closely resembling the task stimuli** are more likely than merely salient ones to engage the ventral network.

The networks **interact**, with the intraparietal sulcus heavily involved. Corbetta et al. (2008) described two interactions:

1. The **top-down** system sends signals that **suppress** distractor information in the stimulus-driven system, so that goal-directed processing can carry on.
2. When the **stimulus-driven** system detects something irrelevant to the current goal (but potentially important), it sends signals that **disrupt** processing in the goal-directed system.

Why should there be two systems? A purely goal-directed system would let us concentrate on what is relevant to our plans, but it would be dangerously rigid: we would never notice the fierce animal approaching while we read. The stimulus-driven system provides the necessary interruption.

> **Trap.** The **dorsal and ventral attention networks** are **not** the dorsal and ventral **visual streams** of Chapter 2. The streams (Milner and Goodale's ventral "what/perception" and dorsal "how/action"; older keys say "what versus where") concern **recognition versus visually guided action**. The attention networks concern **goal-directed versus stimulus-driven attention**: dorsal = **IPS, SPL, FEF**; ventral = **right-lateralised TPJ, IFG, SMG, STG, insula**. "The ventral attention network performs object recognition" merges the two.

#### Findings on the two networks

**Meta-analytic origins.** Corbetta and Shulman (2002) built their model from **meta-analyses of brain-imaging studies**. Areas most often active when participants are **expecting** a stimulus that has not yet appeared formed the **dorsal** network. Areas most often active when participants **detect infrequent targets** formed the **ventral** network.

**The areas.** Later work (**Corbetta & Shulman, 2011**; Figure 5.6) specified the key regions:

| **Dorsal (goal-directed) network** | **Ventral (stimulus-driven) network** |
|---|---|
| Superior parietal lobule (SPL) | Inferior frontal junction (IFJ) |
| Intraparietal sulcus (IPS) | Inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) |
| Inferior frontal junction (IFJ) | Supramarginal gyrus (SMG) |
| Frontal eye field (FEF) | Superior temporal gyrus (STG) |
| Middle temporal area (MT) | Insula (Ins) |
| V3A | (plus the temporo-parietal junction, TPJ: Shomstein et al., 2010) |

Note that the **inferior frontal junction** appears in **both** lists, an early hint that the networks are not completely separate. There is also much evidence that the **temporo-parietal junction** takes part in bottom-up attention (**Shomstein et al., 2010**).

**Separation.** **Hahn et al. (2006)** compared activation when top-down and bottom-up processes were required and found **practically no overlap**, with the areas matching Corbetta and Shulman's fairly well. **Talsma et al. (2010)** confirmed that the two kinds of processing involve different areas, but also found that both use a **common network of parietal areas**.

**Causal evidence from TMS.** Imaging shows that an area is active, not that it is necessary. **Chica et al. (2011)** used **transcranial magnetic stimulation** to create a temporary "lesion". TMS over the **right temporo-parietal junction** impaired the **stimulus-driven** system but **not** the top-down system. TMS over the **right intraparietal sulcus** impaired **both** systems, consistent with the IPS being where the two interact.

**Causal evidence from patients.** **Shomstein et al. (2010)** gave brain-damaged patients one task requiring stimulus-driven attention and one requiring top-down attention. Patients with more trouble on the **top-down** task typically had damage to the **superior parietal lobule** (dorsal network). Patients with more trouble on the **stimulus-driven** task typically had damage to the **temporo-parietal junction** (ventral network). This is a double dissociation across patients.

**Fifteen differences.** **Chica et al. (2013)** reviewed the research and listed **15 differences** between the two systems. For example, stimulus-driven attention is **faster** than top-down attention, more **object-based**, and, once activated, more **resistant to interference** from other peripheral cues. So many differences strengthen the case that the systems are separate.

**What drives the ventral network.** **Indovina and Macaluso (2007)** tested Corbetta et al.'s (2008) claim that task-relevant distractors matter more than salient ones. Participants reported the orientation of a coloured letter **T** while a distractor appeared: either a T in a **different colour** (task-relevant) or a **flickering draughtboard** (salient). As predicted, the bottom-up system responded **more to the task-relevant distractor** than to the salient one.

**Interactions.** **Wen et al. (2012)** measured activity while participants responded to targets in one visual field and ignored everything in the other. Two findings:

1. **Stronger causal influence of the top-down system on the stimulus-driven system** went with **better** performance. When an object appeared at the attended location, the top-down system appears to have suppressed the stimulus-driven system.
2. **Stronger causal influence of the stimulus-driven system on the top-down system** went with **worse** performance. Stimuli outside the focus of attention activated the stimulus-driven system, which then broke the attentional set the top-down system was maintaining.

#### Evaluation of the two-network approach

**Successes.**

1. There appear to be **somewhat separate** stimulus-driven and top-down attention systems.
2. Each system has its **own brain network**.
3. **TMS** studies show that major areas in each network play a **causal** role.
4. Some of the ways the networks **interact** have been identified.
5. Research on brain-damaged patients (5.4) gives good support for dorsal and ventral networks.

**Limitations.**

1. The **precise areas** belonging to each network have been hard to pin down, probably because they depend on the detailed demands of the task (Talsma et al., 2010).
2. The model is **oversimplified**: attention contributes to countless tasks, and it is unlikely that all those processes can be assigned neatly to one network or the other.
3. There is **more overlap** between the networks (especially in the **parietal lobe**) than Corbetta and Shulman (2002) assumed.
4. Much remains unknown about **how the networks interact**.

## 5.4 Disorders of Visual Attention

Brain-damaged patients show what attention does by showing what happens when it fails. The chapter covers two disorders, **neglect** and **extinction**, and gives more space to neglect because it has attracted more research.

### Neglect: what it looks like

**Neglect** (spatial neglect, often called unilateral or hemispatial neglect in clinical texts) is a lack of awareness of stimuli on the side of space **opposite** the brain damage, the **contralesional** side. The damage is usually in the **right hemisphere**, so it is usually the **left** side of space that is neglected. Visual information from the left visual field is processed by the right hemisphere, which is why damage there affects awareness of the left.

There are two forms:

- **Subject-centred (egocentric) neglect.** Little awareness of stimuli on the **left side of the visual field**, defined relative to the patient's own body or gaze.
- **Object-centred (allocentric) neglect.** Lack of awareness of the **left side of each object**, wherever that object is. Figure 5.7 shows the classic signs: when copying figures, a patient distorts or omits their left halves; when given a clock face and told to fill in the numbers, the patient crowds them into the right side. Object-centred neglect is often **more important** than subject-centred neglect. **Gainotti and Ciaraffa (2013)** reviewed several right-hemisphere patients who, when copying a scene of several objects, drew the **right side of every figure** and left out the left side of most of them, **regardless of whether each figure lay in the left or the right visual field**.

Two bedside tests appear in every exam:

| Test | What the patient does | Typical result in left neglect |
|---|---|---|
| **Cancellation task** | Crosses out every target in an array spread across the page | Crosses out **more targets on the right**, missing many on the left |
| **Line bisection task** | Puts a mark through the centre of a horizontal line | Places the mark **to the right of** the true centre |

Are egocentric and allocentric neglect different disorders or two faces of one? **Rorden et al. (2012)** found strong evidence that they are similar: across **33 patients** the correlation between the severity of the two forms was **+0.80**, and the brain regions associated with each overlapped heavily.

> **Trap.** Directions: in **left** neglect after **right**-hemisphere damage, the bisection mark goes to the **right** and cancellation misses the **left**. And the book calls **object-centred (allocentric)** neglect often the more important form: Gainotti and Ciaraffa's patients omitted the left half of figures even in the **right** visual field.

### Extinction

**Extinction** is often found in neglect patients. A patient with extinction can detect a single stimulus on the contralesional (left) side, but **fails to detect it when a second stimulus appears at the same time on the ipsilesional (right) side**. The left stimulus is "extinguished" by competition from the right one. This is a serious problem in daily life, where several stimuli are almost always present together.

Extinction and neglect are closely related, but most of the evidence suggests they are **separate deficits** (**de Haan et al., 2012**).

> **Trap.** **Neglect**: unaware of the left of space or of objects even with **one** stimulus present. **Extinction**: a single left stimulus **is** detected but is missed when a **simultaneous** right stimulus competes (the glossary calls neglect the **more severe** of the two). **Blindsight** (Chapter 2): V1 damage, a real field defect, denial of seeing, yet above-chance forced-choice guessing. *Classic background:* **hemianopia** is a field loss from visual-pathway damage; the patient knows the field is missing and scans into it, whereas a neglect patient typically does not.

### Which brain areas are damaged?

**Neglect** is a **heterogeneous** condition, and the damaged areas vary a great deal from patient to patient. **Molenberghs et al. (2012b)**, in a meta-analysis, found **nine** brain areas that are sometimes damaged. The main areas, typically in the **right hemisphere**, are (Corbetta & Shulman, 2011):

- **superior temporal gyrus**
- **inferior frontal gyrus**
- **insula**
- **supramarginal gyrus**
- **angular gyrus**

Nearly all of these lie within the **ventral (stimulus-driven) attention network** (Figure 5.6). That is why the attentional problems of neglect are best described as depending on **damaged networks** rather than on any single area (Corbetta & Shulman, 2011; **Bartolomeo et al., 2012**).

**Extinction** can also follow damage to several areas, generally centred on the **right hemisphere**. The **temporo-parietal junction** and the **intraparietal sulcus** are especially likely to be involved (de Haan et al., 2012), and applying **TMS** to those areas in healthy people to create a temporary "lesion" produces **extinction-like** behaviour.

### Processing without awareness

Neglect patients typically say they are not aware of stimuli in the left field, but that does not mean those stimuli go unprocessed.

- **McGlinchey-Berroth et al. (1993)** gave neglect patients a **lexical decision** task (is this letter string a word?). "Yes" decisions were faster when the letter string was preceded by a **semantically related object**, and this **priming** effect was **the same size whether the related object appeared in the left or the right field**. The "unseen" left-field object had been processed to the level of meaning.
- **Viggiano et al. (2012)** showed pictures of **animals** and **artefacts** (for example an alarm clock, a camera) in the left field. The patients showed evidence of processing the **artefacts but not the animals**, perhaps because artefacts automatically activate information about how to **use** them.
- **Di Russo et al. (2008)** used **ERPs**. Processing over roughly the **first 130 ms** after a left-field stimulus was **comparable to healthy controls**; only **later** processing was disrupted. Neglect is therefore not a failure of early sensory registration.

Extinction patients show the same pattern:

- **Vuilleumier et al. (2002)** presented two pictures together, one in each field. The patients remembered very little of the **left-field** pictures. Later they had to identify **degraded** pictures, and there was a **facilitation effect for the left-field pictures**, showing they had been processed despite not being reported.
- **Sarri et al. (2010)** also presented two stimuli at once. Extinction patients had **no awareness** of the left-field stimuli, yet those stimuli produced activation in **early visual processing areas**.

### Theoretical accounts

#### Corbetta and Shulman (2011): neglect as a network imbalance

Corbetta and Shulman placed neglect inside their two-network model. In most neglect patients the **ventral (bottom-up) network is damaged**. The **dorsal (goal-directed) network is not damaged**, but it **works poorly** because the two networks interact. Molenberghs et al.'s (2012b) meta-analysis supports both halves of that claim: damage is concentrated in ventral-network regions, not dorsal ones.

How does damage to one network hurt the other? According to Corbetta and Shulman (2011), damage to the ventral network does three things: it impairs **non-spatial** functions that operate across the whole visual field (such as alertness), it reduces activation in (**hypoactivates**) the **right hemisphere**, and it **unbalances** activity in the dorsal attention network, biasing it towards the right side of space.

#### de Haan et al. (2012): extinction as biased competition plus reduced capacity

De Haan et al. built their theory of extinction on two assumptions:

1. Extinction results from **biased competition for attention** between the ipsilesional (right-field) and contralesional (left-field) stimuli, a competition the right-field stimulus usually wins.
2. Extinction patients have **much reduced attentional capacity**, so often only **one** target (the right-field one) can be detected.

> **Trap.** The book's end-of-chapter summary says neglect involves reduced activation and alertness in the **left** hemisphere, but the body text and the Corbetta and Shulman (2011) account it reports say the damaged ventral network **hypoactivates the right hemisphere**. Go with the body text: right-hemisphere damage, ventral network damaged, dorsal network intact but underactive, alertness reduced.

### Findings that test the theories

**Neglect: reduced alertness.** If the intact dorsal network is underperforming because of reduced alertness and resources, raising alertness should help. **Robertson et al. (1998)** found that neglect patients became aware of left-field stimuli **half a second later** than right-field stimuli, but this lag **disappeared** when **warning sounds** were used to increase alertness.

**Neglect: reduced attentional resources.** Neglect patients have **limited attentional resources** (**Bonato, 2012**). In **Bonato et al. (2010)**, patients detected left-field targets either alone or while doing another attention-demanding task. Healthy participants were almost perfect under both conditions; neglect patients were **markedly worse** when the second task was added.

**Neglect: the dorsal network can still be used.** If the dorsal network is intact, patients should be able to use it when the task encourages them to. **Duncan et al. (1999)** briefly flashed arrays of letters and asked neglect patients either to report **all** the letters or only those in a **pre-specified colour**. Only the colour condition allows top-down (dorsal) selection. When all letters had to be reported, left-side recall was **much worse** than right-side recall. When letters were defined by **colour**, the patients, like healthy controls, recalled letters **equally well from both sides**.

**Neglect: the networks work together.** **Bays et al. (2010)** studied neglect patients with damage to the **posterior parietal cortex**, using eye movements during visual search to measure top-down and stimulus-driven attention. Both were **equally impaired**, and the two deficits correlated at a remarkable **+0.98**. This fits Corbetta and Shulman's claim that the networks operate together, and suggests they may interact even more than is usually assumed.

**Extinction: biased competition.** If extinction comes from competition between stimuli, stimuli that can be **integrated** should compete less. **Riddoch et al. (2006)** presented pairs of objects that are **often used together** (a wine bottle and a wine glass) or **never used together** (a wine bottle and a ball). Extinction patients identified **both** objects more often for the related pairs (**65% versus 40%**).

Another test: impair the intact **left** hemisphere with **TMS**, which should reduce its competitive advantage and so reduce extinction. The findings are **mixed**, but some support the prediction (**Oliveri & Caltagirone, 2006**).

**Extinction: reduced capacity.** Bonato et al. (2010) also studied extinction with and without an added attention-demanding task. As de Haan et al.'s capacity assumption predicts, the extinction rate rose substantially, **from 18% to over 80%**, when the extra task was added.

### Reducing neglect

**Alertness training.** Since part of neglect is reduced alertness (Corbetta & Shulman, 2011), training alertness should help. **Thimm et al. (2009)** found that at the end of an **alertness training** course, neglect patients showed **improved alertness** and **reduced neglect**.

**Prism adaptation.** When neglect patients in the dark try to point straight ahead, they typically point **several degrees to the right**. **Rossetti et al. (1998)** had patients wear **prisms that shifted the visual field 10 degrees to the right**. After adapting to the prisms, the patients pointing in the dark aimed **almost straight ahead**, and their bias was reduced on several other tasks. **Newport and Schenk (2012)** reviewed prism adaptation research and concluded it has **generally, but not always**, been effective.

Why does it work? **Nijboer et al. (2008)** found that prism adaptation made it easier for patients to use **goal-directed** processes to shift attention **leftwards voluntarily**, compensating for their habitual rightward bias. It had **no effect** on the **stimulus-driven** system.

> **Trap.** The prisms used by Rossetti et al. (1998) shift the visual field **to the right** (by **10 degrees**), the same direction as the patient's existing bias. Adapting to them produces a compensatory **leftward** after-effect in pointing, which is what corrects the bias. And the mechanism, according to Nijboer et al. (2008), is improved **voluntary (goal-directed, dorsal)** leftward shifting, **not** repair of the stimulus-driven system.

### Overall evaluation

**What research on neglect and extinction has shown.**

1. Patients can process unattended visual stimuli **without conscious awareness** of them.
2. Most findings suggest neglect involves **damage to the ventral network** that impairs the functioning of the **undamaged dorsal network**.
3. Extinction seems to arise from **biased competition** for attention plus **reduced attentional capacity**.
4. Training programmes designed to reduce neglect have shed light on its underlying processes.

**Limitations.**

1. Symptoms and lesion sites **vary considerably** across patients, making it hard to build an account that fits all neglect or extinction patients.
2. The **relationship between neglect and extinction** remains unclear, although they are generally regarded as separate conditions with similar underlying processes.
3. The dorsal and ventral networks in neglect may **interact more** than is usually assumed (Bays et al., 2010).

## 5.5 Visual Search

Much of daily life is spent looking for things: a friend in a crowd, the car keys, a word on a page. Laboratory research studies this as **visual search**: detecting a specified **target** as quickly as possible within a display of **distractors**. The standard measure is how reaction time changes as the number of items (the **set size** or **display size**) increases. A flat function means set size does not matter; a steep function means each extra item adds time.

The section opens with a real-world case where search can be a matter of life and death, then covers Treisman's feature integration theory, and then two newer accounts (the texture tiling model and the dual-path model).

### In the real world: airport security checks

Security screening became more thorough after 9/11. A screener sits beside the X-ray machine searching each bag image for illegal or dangerous items (Figure 5.8, from **McCarley et al., 2004**, challenges the reader to spot a bladed weapon in a cluttered bag image). Training makes this search effective, but mistakes still happen: on **17 November 2002**, a man got through security at **Ben Gurion airport in Tel Aviv** carrying a **pocketknife** and then tried to storm the cockpit of **El Al flight 581** to Istanbul.

Screeners face two special problems.

**1. Many different kinds of target.** Screeners must look for knives, guns, improvised explosive devices and more. **Menneer et al. (2009)** had observers search for two categories (**metal threats** and **improvised explosive devices**). Observers looking for **both** categories on every trial (**dual-target search**) detected targets **worse** than observers looking for just **one** (**single-target search**). The two categories in that study shared no obvious feature. When the categories **did** share a feature (**colour**), dual-target detection was **as good as** single-target detection. The explanation is that observers search with a **search template** built from their representations of the targets (**Bravo & Farid, 2012**); a shared colour makes a two-target template simpler.

**2. Targets are rare.** Dangerous items are present in only a minute fraction of bags. **Wolfe et al. (2007)** showed observers X-ray images of packed bags in which the targets were knives or guns. When a target was present on **50% of trials**, **80%** of targets were detected. When targets were present on only **2% of trials**, just **54%** were detected. The main reason was **excessive caution** about saying "target present" when targets were so unexpected; this is a shift in decision criterion rather than a failure to see. Two remedies work:

- **Schwark et al. (2012)** gave observers **false feedback** telling them they had missed rare targets. This made them less cautious and improved detection.
- Screeners are routinely shown **fictional threat items** inserted into real bag images, artificially raising target frequency; this improves detection (**von Bastian et al., 2010**).

**3. Individual differences.** **Rusconi et al. (2012)** found that people who rated themselves **high in attention to detail** detected threats in security X-ray images better than those who rated themselves low.

> **Trap.** The low-prevalence effect is a classic data-recall question: Wolfe et al. (2007), **50% prevalence gives 80% detection, 2% prevalence gives 54%**. The cause the book gives is **excessive caution in reporting** a target, which is why the fixes (false miss feedback, inserted fictional threats) work by changing willingness to respond, not by sharpening vision.

### Feature integration theory (Treisman and Gelade)

**Feature integration theory** (FIT) was proposed by **Treisman and Gelade (1980)** and later updated (for example **Treisman, 1998**). It rests on a distinction between an object's **features** (colour, size, line orientation) and the **object** itself, and it proposes **two stages**:

1. **Preattentive feature stage.** Basic visual features are registered **rapidly** and **in parallel** across the whole scene, without attention.
2. **Focused attention stage.** A **slower, serial** process, in which focused attention acts as the **"glue"** that binds the features at one location into an object. Elongated shape plus yellow colour at the same place becomes a banana.

If focused attention is not available, features from different objects can be combined at random, producing an **illusory conjunction**: perceiving an object that is not actually there (for example a red X when the display contained a red O and a blue X).

The predictions follow directly:

| | **Feature search** | **Conjunction search** |
|---|---|---|
| Target defined by | A **single** feature (a blue letter; an S) | A **combination** of features (a green T among green Xs and brown Ts, say) |
| Stage needed | Preattentive only | Focused attention to bind |
| Processing | **Parallel** across the display | **Serial**, item by item |
| Effect of display size | Little or none | **Large**: more items, longer search |

#### Findings

**Treisman and Gelade (1980)** tested these predictions with targets defined by a single feature or by a conjunction, using displays of **1 to 30** items with the target present or absent (Figure 5.9). The results were as predicted. For **single-feature** targets, responses were **fast** and display size had **very little effect**, suggesting **parallel** processing. For **conjunction** targets, responses were **slower** and display size had a **large effect**, suggesting **serial** processing.

The theory also predicts illusory conjunctions when focused attention fails. **Friedman-Hill et al. (1995)** studied a brain-damaged patient who had difficulty **locating** visual stimuli. He produced **many illusory conjunctions**, combining the **shape** of one stimulus with the **colour** of another, exactly as FIT predicts if spatial attention is what binds features. *Classic background:* this patient is usually identified as **RM**, who had **Balint's syndrome** after bilateral parietal damage; and **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)** had earlier shown illusory conjunctions in **healthy** observers given brief displays while their attention was occupied.

> **Trap.** "Pop-out" (the target seems to leap out, and search time is flat across display sizes) is the signature of **feature** search, not conjunction search. In FIT, **attention is needed for binding**, not for detecting a single feature. An option saying "feature search requires focused attention" or "conjunction search is preattentive" has the stages reversed.

#### Limitations of feature integration theory

**Similarity matters, not just feature versus conjunction.** **Duncan and Humphreys (1989, 1992)** identified two further factors that govern search speed:

1. **Distractor-distractor similarity.** Search is faster when the distractors are **very similar to each other**, because they are easier to reject as a group.
2. **Target-distractor similarity.** When targets resemble distractors, the **number of distractors has a large effect** even on **single-feature** targets (Duncan & Humphreys, 1989), which FIT does not predict.

They also showed that conjunction search is usually confined to distractors that **share at least one feature** with the target. Looking for a **blue circle** among blue triangles, red circles and red triangles, you can ignore the **red triangles** altogether, since they share nothing with the target.

**Serial versus parallel is a continuum.** In the original theory, feature processing is necessarily parallel and attention-based processing necessarily serial. That is an oversimplification. **Wolfe (1998)** pointed out that the slopes of reaction time against set size in real experiments range **continuously from flat to steep**, which makes it implausible to sort search tasks neatly into "serial" and "parallel".

**A better test: multiple targets.** Slopes alone cannot settle whether search is serial or parallel, so **Thornton and Gilden (2007)** used the **multiple-targets** logic. Suppose **every** item in the display is a target. With **serial** processing, the first item inspected is always a target, so detection time should **not change** as the number of targets increases. With **parallel** processing, information is gathered from all the targets at once, so more targets means more evidence and detection should get **faster** as target number rises.

Thornton and Gilden compared single-target and multiple-target trials across **29** different visual tasks:

- The **parallel** pattern appeared when targets and distractors differed along **a single feature dimension** (for example colour or size), consistent with FIT.
- The **serial** pattern appeared with **complex** tasks, such as detecting a particular **direction of rotation** (for example pinwheels rotating clockwise).
- Overall, **72%** of tasks appeared to involve **parallel** processing and only **28%** serial processing.

So both kinds of search exist, but **parallel processing is much more common** than Treisman and Gelade assumed, and serial processing is limited to the most complex tasks with the longest detection times.

*Classic background: guided search (not described in the 7th edition).* **Wolfe's guided search model** (Wolfe, Cave & Franzel, 1989; Wolfe, 1994) is the successor to FIT that many exam keys expect. It keeps a parallel feature stage, but that stage builds an **activation (priority) map** combining **bottom-up** salience (how different an item is from its neighbours) with **top-down** guidance (how well it matches the target's features). Attention then visits items **in order of activation**, not at random, so some conjunction searches become efficient and search slopes form the continuum Wolfe (1998) described. The dual-path model below comes from the same group.

> **Trap.** FIT versus guided search in one line each. **FIT (Treisman & Gelade, 1980):** parallel features, then **serial** attention as the glue; conjunction search is serial and slow. **Guided search (Wolfe):** parallel feature information **guides** a limited-capacity stage towards likely candidates, so conjunction search can be **efficient** and search slopes are **continuous**. If an option credits Treisman with "top-down guidance by target features producing efficient conjunction search", it has merged the two.

### The texture tiling model

FIT treats **attention** as the central problem in visual search. **Rosenholtz et al. (2012a)** argued that this gives attention too much weight. Attention may well be needed to find a rotated **T** among rotated **L**s, but in the real world the main limit on search lies, they argued, in **perception**, specifically in **peripheral vision**. When we fixate a scene, the central region is seen sharply, but information is increasingly lost towards the periphery. Rosenholtz and colleagues propose that the peripheral information is represented as **textures** (summary statistics over patches) that become coarser with distance from fixation: hence the **texture tiling model**.

How much does one fixation actually capture? **Freeman and Simoncelli (2011)** briefly showed observers two different but related images while they fixated a central point, followed by a third image identical to one of the first two; observers had to say which one it matched. The key finding (Figure 5.10, a photograph of the Brunnen der Lebensfreude fountain in Rostock, Germany, and two distorted versions) was that observers **could not tell original photographs from versions with gross distortions in the periphery**. Why, then, does the world not look blurred? Because **top-down** processes construct a clear-looking image.

**Applying this to search.** **Rosenholtz et al. (2012b)** argued that search difficulty depends mainly on what information the peripheral representation contains. Search is **easy** when peripheral vision carries enough information to steer attention to the target, and **hard** when it does not. They tested this with **five** search tasks. Besides measuring search speed, they showed participants **blurred** versions of target-plus-distractor and distractor-only displays, degraded to contain only the information available in peripheral vision, and asked them to tell the two apart. How well participants could discriminate these displays predicted search speed **almost perfectly**.

#### Evaluation of the texture tiling model

**Strengths.** The model shows that **peripheral vision** matters much more in visual search than had been assumed, and that selective attention probably matters **less**. This fits Thornton and Gilden's (2007) finding that most search tasks are **parallel**: several kinds of information are processed in peripheral vision at once.

**Limitations.**

1. More needs to be discovered about **how far** observers can use peripheral information to find targets.
2. Rosenholtz et al. (2012b) accepted that selective attention often plays a part, but did not say **when**.
3. Peripheral vision may have complexities the model does not capture. **Young and Hulleman (2013)** found that observers' **effective visual field was smaller** in hard search tasks than in easy ones.

### The dual-path model

In most laboratory studies a target is equally likely to appear anywhere, so search is essentially random. Real search is not like that. Looking for your cat in the garden, you would ignore the sky and check the ground and perhaps the trees: your search is guided **top-down** by knowledge of where cats tend to be.

**Ehinger et al. (2009)** recorded the eye fixations of observers searching for a **person** in many real outdoor scenes (Figure 5.11). Observers mostly fixated the regions where a person was likely to be (**pavements**) and ignored implausible regions (**sky**, **trees**). They also fixated locations that differed sharply from their surroundings and areas containing features typical of a human figure. Fixations agreed more across observers in scenes that offered fewer plausible regions.

To account for findings like these, **Wolfe et al. (2011b)** proposed a **dual-path model** (Figure 5.12):

| Pathway | What it does | Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective pathway** | Selects objects one at a time, **binds** their features and **recognises** them; the focus of most traditional research | **Limited** (a bottleneck) |
| **Non-selective pathway** | Extracts the **gist** of the whole scene (for example the typical layout of a kitchen) and uses it to **guide** the selective pathway | Not bottlenecked; works in parallel with the selective pathway |

The non-selective pathway is far more useful in the real world than in typical laboratory displays, because it lets us exploit stored knowledge of environments.

#### Findings

- **Wolfe et al. (2011a)** compared search for objects placed within a **scene** with search for objects scattered randomly on a **white background**. Search was much faster in the scene: about **10 ms per item** versus **40 ms per item**. They explained this with **functional set size**: in a scene, most regions can be ruled out because they are very unlikely to hold the object, so the effective number of items to search is much smaller than the actual number.
- **Võ and Wolfe (2012)** strongly supported functional set size. Observers searching scenes for objects (for example a **jam jar in a kitchen**) rarely fixated **80%** of each scene; they did not look at the sink or the cupboard doors.
- **Hollingworth (2012)** asked whether **specific** knowledge of a scene, not just general knowledge of scene types, also helps. Some participants had previously viewed the scenes while deciding which object was least likely to belong in that type of scene. Their later search was **significantly faster** for scenes they had seen before.
- **Chukoskie et al. (2013)** showed how quickly such knowledge is learned. An **invisible** target was placed at random positions within a fairly small region of a blank screen, and observers heard a reward tone whenever they fixated it. At first fixations were spread over the whole screen; they rapidly became concentrated on the region where the target could be (Figure 5.13).

#### Evaluation of the dual-path model

**Strengths.** Knowledge of where objects are likely and unlikely to be in a scene is the **most important determinant** of real-world visual search. The dual-path model is an advance on most earlier theories because it gives scene knowledge its full weight, and **functional set size** is an important, well-supported idea.

**Limitations.**

1. It remains unclear how **gist** is used so quickly to shrink the search area.
2. The model says too little about the **learning** processes that often transform search. **Experts** in several domains find targets faster and more accurately than non-experts (**Gegenfurtner et al., 2011**; Chapter 12 covers radiologists' expertise), and the model would need extending to explain this.

## 5.6 Cross-Modal Effects

Almost all the research so far studied one sense at a time. That was justified by the assumption that attention in each modality runs **independently** of attention in the others. The assumption is **wrong**. In the real world we regularly coordinate information from two or more senses at once, which is **cross-modal attention**. Lip reading is the everyday example: seeing a speaker's lip movements helps us understand what they are saying (Chapter 9).

### Cross-modal links in spatial attention: Eimer and Schröger (1998)

**Eimer and Schröger (1998)** presented two streams of **lights**, one on the left and one on the right, and at the same time two streams of **sounds**, one on each side. In one condition participants detected **deviant visual** events (for example lights lasting longer than usual) on **one side only**; in the other condition they detected **deviant auditory** events in only one stream. **ERPs** tracked where attention was allocated.

Within the **relevant** modality, ERPs to deviants were larger on the to-be-attended side than on the to-be-ignored side: participants were doing as instructed. The interesting result concerned the **irrelevant** modality. When participants were attending to **visual** targets on the left, ERPs to deviant **sounds** were also larger on the **left**. Likewise, attending to **auditory** targets on one side enlarged ERPs to **visual** deviants on that side. Voluntary (**endogenous**) attention to a location in one sense **carried over** to the same location in the other sense. Spatial attention is at least partly shared across modalities.

### The ventriloquism effect

What happens when vision and hearing **conflict**? The best-known case is the **ventriloquism effect**: a sound is mislocated towards its **apparent visual source**. The ventriloquist speaks without moving his lips while moving the dummy's mouth, and the voice seems to come from the dummy. The cinema shows the same thing every day: the voices come from loudspeakers beside the screen, but we hear them coming from the actors' moving mouths.

Three conditions favour the illusion (**Recanzone & Sutter, 2008**):

1. The visual and auditory events must be **close in time**.
2. The sound must **fit expectations** created by the visual stimulus (for example a high-pitched sound apparently coming from a small object).
3. The visual and auditory sources should be **close in space**.

The ventriloquism effect is an instance of **visual dominance**. Another is the **Colavita effect** (**Colavita, 1974**). Participants press one key for visual stimuli and another for auditory stimuli presented in a random sequence. On occasional trials a light and a sound arrive **together**, and participants should press **both** keys. They nearly always respond to the **visual** stimulus but sometimes **fail to respond to the auditory** one (**Spence et al., 2011**).

**Is the ventriloquism effect automatic?** It has often been assumed so, partly because it persists even when people **know** the sound and sight are in different places. However, **Maiworm et al. (2012)** found the effect was **smaller** after participants had heard syllables spoken in a **fearful voice**. When the auditory channel becomes more important, its weight increases, so the effect is not entirely automatic.

**Why does vision capture sound?** Vision normally gives **more precise** information about **location**. When visual stimuli are badly **blurred** and poorly localised, the effect **reverses** and **sound captures vision** (**Alais & Burr, 2004**). So the brain is not simply favouring vision; it is weighting each sense by **how informative** it is in the situation.

### Temporal ventriloquism

That explanation develops the **modality appropriateness and precision hypothesis** (**Welch & Warren, 1980**): when two or more modalities give conflicting information, the modality with the **best acuity for the judgement in question** tends to dominate. The hypothesis predicts a second illusion. Hearing is usually **more precise than vision for timing**, so judgements of **when** a visual stimulus began should be pulled towards a slightly asynchronous sound. **Chen and Vroomen (2013)** called this **temporal ventriloquism** and reviewed several studies supporting it. In the simplest example (Figure 5.14), the perceived onset of a **flash** is shifted towards an abrupt **sound** presented slightly before or after it. Other studies show that the perceived **duration** of visual stimuli can be distorted by asynchronous sounds.

> **Trap.** The two ventriloquism effects run in **opposite** directions: **spatial** = vision dominates for **where**; **temporal** = hearing dominates for **when**. Both follow from **Welch and Warren's (1980)** modality appropriateness and precision hypothesis (the more precise sense for the judgement wins), and blurred vision reverses even spatial capture (Alais & Burr, 2004). "Vision always dominates" is the trap.

### In the real world: using warning signals to promote safe driving

A quarter (**25%**) of road accidents are **front-to-rear-end collisions**, and the commonest cause of these is a driver who was not paying attention (**Spence, 2012**). Warning signals (a car horn, say) could alert drivers, and they might work best if **informative** about the danger, provided they do not demand slow cognitive processing.

**Ho and Spence (2005)** played a **car horn** from the **front** or **rear** of drivers who had to **brake** to avoid a car in front or **accelerate** to avoid a speeding car behind. The horn's direction matched the direction of the danger on **80%** of trials in one experiment (predictive) but on only **50%** in another (non-predictive). Two results:

1. Reactions were **faster** in **both** experiments when sound and visual event came from the same direction (valid cues).
2. The benefit was **larger** in the **80%** (predictive) experiment.

The interpretation uses the same distinction as Posner's cueing studies, now across senses:

- Sounds influence **visual** attention.
- The benefit even from **non-predictive** sounds reflects **exogenous spatial attention**, the "automatic" capture of attention by a location.
- The extra benefit when sounds were **predictive** reflects additional **endogenous spatial attention**, attention directed by the driver's intentions.

So the **informativeness** of a warning matters.

**Gray (2011)** studied subtler aspects of informativeness. Drivers braked to avoid hitting the car in front, and auditory warnings **grew louder** as **time to collision** shrank, with the rate of increase varying across conditions; another condition used a car horn. **All** warnings speeded braking compared with a no-warning control, but the most effective was the one with the **fastest rate of increase**, because that implied the **shortest time to collision**.

**Gray et al. (2014)** used **vibrotactile** signals (vibrations felt through the skin) at **three sites on the abdomen**, arranged vertically. The best condition presented successive vibrations moving **towards the driver's head** at an increasing rate that tracked how fast the driver was closing on the car ahead. Braking was **250 ms faster** than with no warning, because the signal was so informative.

Cross-modal research therefore shows real promise for reducing accidents. Two limitations: warnings occur **far more often** in the laboratory than on real roads, and it is sometimes unclear **why** one signal beats another. Gray et al. (2014), for instance, found **upward**-moving vibrotactile stimulation more effective than the same stimulation moving **downward**, with no clear explanation.

### Overall evaluation of cross-modal research

1. **Theory lags behind data**, as the warning-signal research shows: findings have accumulated faster than explanations.
2. Much research uses **complex, artificial** tasks; cross-modal effects need studying in more **naturalistic** conditions.
3. **Individual differences** have mostly been ignored, although there is growing evidence that they (for example a preference for auditory or for visual stimuli) influence cross-modal effects (reviewed by **van Atteveldt et al., 2014**).

## 5.7 Divided Attention: Dual-Task Performance

### Media multitasking: does practice at multitasking help?

Doing two things at once (texting while watching television, or while walking along) is now routine. **Ophir et al. (2009)** sorted people into heavy and light media multitaskers with a questionnaire they called the **Media Multitasking Index**. **Heavy multitaskers** were **more susceptible to distraction**. Ophir et al. concluded that people who habitually attend to several media at once develop **"breadth-based cognitive control"**: they are not selective about where their attention goes. Light multitaskers are more likely to exercise **top-down** control. **Cain and Mitroff (2011)** supported this: only **light** multitaskers made good use of top-down instructions to reduce distraction and improve performance.

That is somewhat surprising, since one might expect years of multitasking to improve attention, and in some respects it seems to:

- **Yap and Lim (2013)** found heavy multitaskers **better** at **splitting attention** between two non-adjacent locations (split attention, section 5.3).
- **Alzahabi and Becker (2013)** studied **task switching**. Each trial showed a digit and a letter, and participants classified one of them (digit odd or even? letter vowel or consonant?). On **50%** of trials the stimulus to be classified **switched** from the previous trial; on the rest it **repeated**. Heavy multitaskers switched **more efficiently**, with smaller **switch costs** (switch reaction time minus repeat reaction time; Figure 5.15).

> **Trap.** The multitasking findings are **correlational**. Heavy multitasking is *associated* with more distractibility (Ophir et al., 2009) and with better task switching (Alzahabi & Becker, 2013), but we do not know whether multitasking changes attention or whether people with certain attentional styles choose to multitask. An option stating that "media multitasking causes poor attentional control" overstates the evidence.

### What makes two tasks hard to combine?

**Similarity** between tasks is one major factor.

- **Stimulus modality.** **Treisman and Davies (1973)** found that two **monitoring** tasks interfered with each other much more when both used the **same** modality (both visual or both auditory).
- **Response modality.** **McLeod (1977)** combined a continuous **manual tracking** task with a **tone-identification** task. Some participants answered the tones **vocally**; others used the **hand not doing the tracking**. Tracking was **worse** when both tasks needed **manual** responses (high response similarity).

**Practice** is probably the **most important** factor. **Spelke et al. (1976)** trained two students, **Diane and John**, for **five hours a week over four months**. Their first task was to read short stories for comprehension while writing down words read out to them, which they found very hard at first. After **six weeks** they could read **as fast and with as much comprehension** while taking dictation as when only reading. With more training, they could write down the **category** each dictated word belonged to while still reading at normal speed with normal comprehension.

The study looks like proof that practice can remove dual-task interference, but the book identifies two problems:

1. It relied on **accuracy** measures, which are less sensitive to dual-task interference than **speed** measures.
2. Reading is self-paced, so Diane and John could choose **when** to attend to the text, and may have **alternated** attention between the tasks rather than doing both at once.

Better-controlled studies of practice appear in section 5.8 (the psychological refractory period).

### In the real world: can we think and drive?

Driving is the riskiest activity in which tens of millions of adults engage, and **more than 40 countries** restrict drivers' use of handheld mobile phones. Is that justified? **Strayer et al. (2011)** reviewed the evidence:

- A driver using a mobile phone is **four times** more likely to have an accident, whether the phone is **handheld or hands-free**.
- About **28%** of car crashes in the United States are caused by drivers using phones.

**Caird et al. (2008)** reviewed **33** simulated-driving studies. Reaction times to events such as the brake lights of the car in front coming on were **250 ms slower** with a phone than without. The slowing was similar for **handheld and hands-free** phones and **greater when talking than when listening**. Drivers had **very little awareness** of the impairment: they did not slow down or leave a bigger gap. A quarter of a second sounds trivial, but for a driver at **50 mph (80 kph)** it means travelling an extra **18 feet (5.5 m)** before stopping, which could decide whether a child in the road survives.

Laboratory results do generalise. In a naturalistic study discussed by Strayer et al. (2011), drivers were observed at a junction where the law required a full stop: **21%** of drivers **not** using a phone failed to stop completely, compared with **75%** of drivers **using** one.

**Why does phoning impair driving?** One possibility is **specific** shared processes. **Bergen et al. (2013)** asked drivers in a simulator to judge statements as true or false. Statements with **motor** content (about using scissors) or **visual** content (about a camel's fur) disrupted driving, measured by distance from the vehicle in front; **abstract** statements did not. Language that draws on visual or motor processes shared with driving interferes.

Most theorists, however, emphasise **general** attentional processes. Strayer et al. (2011) identified two:

1. **Inattentional blindness** (Chapter 4): failing to perceive an unexpected object. In **Strayer and Drews (2007)**, **30** objects (pedestrians, advertising hoardings and so on) were clearly visible during simulated driving, followed by a surprise recognition test. Drivers who had been using a phone recognised **under 25%** of the objects they had **looked at**, against **50%** for drivers not using a phone. In a second experiment, drivers responded to the brake lights of the car ahead while ERPs were recorded: the **P300**, a positive wave associated with attention, was **reduced by 50%** in phone users.
2. **Reduced attentional flexibility.** In an unpublished eye-movement study, drivers using **hands-free** phones were more likely to look **almost exclusively at the road straight ahead**, and so less likely to see objects in the periphery, such as a small child at the roadside.

> **Trap.** Two numbers from this box are often swapped. **Caird et al. (2008):** **33 studies**, **250 ms** slowing, about **18 feet (5.5 m)** at **50 mph**. **Strayer and Drews (2007):** recognition of fixated objects **under 25% versus 50%**, and **P300 reduced by 50%**. And the hands-free point: the impairment is similar for **handheld and hands-free** phones, so laws banning only handheld phones target the wrong mechanism, since the problem is **attentional**, not manual.

### Serial or parallel processing?

When two tasks share a time period, people might process them **serially** (switching attention back and forth, attending to only one task at any moment) or in **parallel** (attending to and processing both at once). The debate has often ignored how **flexible** processing is.

- **Lehle et al. (2009)** trained people to use either serial or parallel processing on two combined tasks. **Serial** processing gave **better performance** but felt **more effortful**, because it required **inhibiting** one task while performing the other.
- **Lehle and Hübner (2009)** found that people followed instructions to process serially or in parallel; those told to work in **parallel** performed **much worse**. Yet most participants **given no instructions** tended to **favour parallel** processing. Their tasks were simple (both involved odd/even judgements on digits).
- **Han and Marois (2013)** used a much harder task (pressing a different key for each of **eight** different sounds). Even when parallel processing was more efficient and was encouraged with **financial rewards**, participants processed **serially**. Parallel processing is probably hard to sustain with difficult tasks.

### Central capacity versus multiple resources

*Classic background: central capacity (not in the 7th edition).* Older keys frame dual-task research as **central capacity** versus **multiple resources**. The best-known central capacity theory is **Kahneman's (1973)** *Attention and Effort*: one general pool of capacity (mental effort), varying with **arousal**, shared out among tasks by an **allocation policy**. Two tasks interfere when their combined demands exceed the pool, whatever they are. Baddeley's **central executive** (Chapter 6) is a related modality-free controller. A single pool cannot explain why interference depends so much on task **similarity** (Treisman & Davies, 1973; McLeod, 1977); the underadditivity findings below are the 7th edition's nearest evidence for a **general** resource.

### Multiple-resource theory (Wickens)

**Wickens** (for example **1984, 2008**) proposed that the processing system contains several **independent** processing mechanisms, or **resources**. His model has **four dimensions** (Figure 5.16):

1. **Processing stages:** successive stages of **perception**, **cognition** (for example working memory) and **responding**.
2. **Processing codes:** perception, cognition and responding can all use **spatial** and/or **verbal** codes.
3. **Modalities:** perception can use **visual** and/or **auditory** resources.
4. **Response type:** responses can be **manual** or **vocal**.

The figure also distinguishes, within vision, **focal** vision (used for **object recognition**) from **ambient** vision (used for perceiving **orientation and movement**).

The theory's central prediction is that time-sharing (dual-task performance) improves to the extent that the two tasks use **different levels** along the dimensions. Tasks that need different resources can be combined more successfully than tasks that need the same ones.

The approach resembles **Baddeley's (1986, 2001) working memory model** (Chapter 6), whose components include the **phonological loop** (speech-based information) and the **visuo-spatial sketchpad** (visual and spatial information). That model also predicts that two tasks can be combined successfully if they use **different components**.

**Support.** Much evidence fits the general approach (Wickens, 2008), including the stimulus-similarity and response-similarity effects already described (Treisman & Davies, 1973; McLeod, 1977). **Lu et al. (2013)** ran meta-analyses. When someone doing an ongoing **visual-motor** task (such as driving) was interrupted by a task in the visual, auditory or **tactile** modality, the **non-visual** interrupting tasks, **especially tactile** ones, were handled better than visual ones, as predicted. But the **ongoing visual task** was **not** performed better with an auditory than a visual interruption. Lu et al. suggested that the advantage of separate resources was cancelled out because auditory stimuli are more **conspicuous and disruptive** than visual ones.

**Limitations.**

1. Successful dual-tasking often depends on **higher-level processes** that coordinate and organise the two tasks' demands, which the theory **de-emphasises**.
2. The theory says little about the many forms of cognitive processing **between perception and responding**.
3. People often use **complex strategies** in dual-task situations, and the theory contributes little to understanding them.

> **Trap.** Wickens's model has **four** dimensions (stages, codes, modalities, responses), but the prediction Wickens states refers to tasks differing along **three** dimensions; the book prints both. The four-dimension list is the safer exam answer for "dimensions of the model". The response dimension is **manual versus vocal** (McLeod, 1977, is its classic illustration), and the codes dimension is **spatial versus verbal**. Do not confuse "focal versus ambient vision" (a distinction within the visual channel) with the **modalities** dimension (visual versus auditory).

### Threaded cognition (Salvucci and Taatgen)

**Salvucci and Taatgen (2008, 2011)** proposed **threaded cognition**: streams of thought can be represented as **threads** of processing, so doing two tasks may involve two threads. The core assumptions (Salvucci & Taatgen, 2011) are that several threads or goals can be **active at the same time**; that there is **no interference** as long as they do not need the same cognitive resource; and that when two threads need the **same resource at the same moment**, one must **wait**, and its performance suffers. Every resource has **limited capacity**.

**Taatgen (2011)** identified the resources over which tasks can compete (Figure 5.17): **vision**, **long-term memory** (**declarative** and **procedural**), **manual control** and **working memory**, which the theory calls the **"problem state"**. Each resource can work in parallel with the others but can serve only **one** task at a time.

Unlike Baddeley's model, threaded cognition has **no central executive** allocating resources. Instead, each thread controls resources in a **"greedy, polite"** way: it **grabs** a resource as soon as it needs it and **releases** it as soon as it is finished. This is what allows one of the theory's most original claims, that **several goals can be active at once**, each with its own thread.

Threaded cognition resembles Wickens's theory (several independent resources, no overarching executive) but has one big advantage: it has been implemented as a **computational model**, which makes specific, testable predictions in a way Wickens's theory cannot.

#### Findings

- **Nijboer et al. (2013)** used **multicolumn subtraction** (answers typed on a keypad) as the primary task. Problems were **easy** (no borrowing between columns) or **hard** (borrowing required). The theory says only hard problems need **working memory** (the problem state). The secondary task was either **tracking** (visual and manual resources) or **tone counting** (working memory). As predicted, **easy** subtraction suffered more with **tracking** (competition for visual and manual resources), and **hard** subtraction suffered more with **tone counting** (competition for working memory, which is especially disruptive).
- **Borst et al. (2013)** likewise found that a working-memory secondary task hurt subtraction with borrowing more than easy subtraction. The theory also predicts that **environmental support** can help. Adding an explicit **visual indicator** that borrowing was needed **considerably improved** performance when both tasks needed working memory.

The theory also claims people manage task combination **better than is often assumed**. Laboratory **task switching**, where the experimenter dictates the switch, produces substantial time costs (Chapter 6). Real-life switching may be easier for two reasons: people usually **choose when** to switch, and the environment often provides **support**.

- **Janssen and Brumby (2010)** tested the theory's claim that people switch **flexibly** to maximise performance. Participants drove a simulated car while manually dialling a well-practised **11-digit** phone number, prioritising either the driving or the dialling. Each task was done **better when prioritised**. With driving prioritised, they returned attention to the road **earlier**. They had learned the number as **two chunks** (the first **five** digits, then the remaining **six**), yet when driving was the priority they switched back after only **three** digits.

People do not always adapt well, though. In Nijboer et al. (2013), participants chose before each trial which secondary task to pair with subtraction. **One-third** showed **no** adaptation or learning across trials, and the rest took **many trials** before consistently choosing optimally. Finding the best strategy in dual-task situations is often hard.

#### Evaluation of threaded cognition

**Successes.**

1. Progress in identifying the most important **cognitive resources**.
2. **Computational modelling** allows detailed testing of predictions.
3. It avoids positing a vaguely defined **central executive**.
4. There is evidence that people approach dual tasks **flexibly**.
5. Related to that, people often find doing two things at once **less problematic** than is generally assumed.

**Limitations.**

1. The theory predicts that practising two tasks **together** produces the same performance as practising them **separately** (Salvucci & Taatgen, 2008). That plays down processes that **coordinate and manage** two tasks (see 5.8).
2. Leaving out anything like Baddeley's **central executive** may prove unwise.
3. It has mostly been tested with pairs of **simple** tasks; it is unclear whether it applies to **complex** ones.
4. It cannot yet explain why some people find it much **easier** than others to choose task combinations that minimise interference.

> **Trap.** Both Wickens's multiple-resource theory and Salvucci and Taatgen's threaded cognition assume **several limited-capacity resources and no central executive**. Baddeley's working memory model has a central executive. The distinctive features of threaded cognition are the **greedy-polite** resource rule, **"problem state"** as its name for working memory, and a working **computational model**. An option attributing a central executive to threaded cognition is wrong.

### Cognitive neuroscience of dual-task performance

Suppose the resource demands of two tasks done together simply **add up** to the demands of each done alone. Then brain activation during the dual task should equal the **sum** of the activations for each single task: the **additivity assumption**. It is rarely confirmed.

#### Findings

**Underadditivity.** Several imaging studies find **underadditivity**: activation in the dual-task condition is **less** than the sum of the single-task activations.

- **Just et al. (2001)** chose two tasks that seem to need very different resources: **auditory sentence comprehension** and **mental rotation of 3-D figures**. Performance on **both** tasks was worse when combined. Activation in **language** regions **fell by 53%** in the dual-task condition, and activation in **mental-rotation** regions **fell by 29%**. Such strong underadditivity, for such different tasks, suggests that only **limited general resources** were available when both had to be done together.
- **Schweizer et al. (2013)** had participants perform a **straight driving** task alone or while answering **auditorily presented general-knowledge questions**. Driving performance was **unaffected**, but brain activity differed markedly. With distraction, activation **fell** in **posterior** areas involved in spatial and visual processing (underadditivity) and **rose** in **prefrontal cortex** (Figure 5.18 shows increases in ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and auditory cortex, and decreases in occipital visual areas), probably because driving while distracted needs extra attentional processing.

**Extra executive processes.** Dual-tasking plausibly needs **executive processes** (coordinating demands, attentional control, overall task management) that single tasks need less, and these should involve **prefrontal cortex**.

- **Johnson and Zatorre (2006)** had participants do visual and auditory tasks singly or together. The **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** (DLPFC) was active **only in the dual-task condition**.
- Activation does not prove necessity, so **Johnson et al. (2007)** used **TMS** to disrupt the DLPFC during the same tasks. As predicted, this **impaired** the ability to divide attention between them. They suggested the DLPFC is needed to **manipulate information in working memory** when doing two tasks.
- **T. Wu et al. (2013)** used simple **tapping** and **visual counting** tasks (counting occurrences of a specified letter). The dual-task condition produced **greater activation in the cerebellum** (a structure at the back of the brain involved in coordinating movement and balance), and this increase allowed the separate networks for the two tasks to be **integrated**.

#### Evaluation

Dual-task brain activity differs in several ways from the sum of its single-task parts. It often shows **underadditivity**; it sometimes **recruits additional regions** (DLPFC, cerebellum); and it can involve **integrated** functioning of separate networks. These findings matter theoretically: dual-tasking can involve **executive and other processes** hardly needed for single tasks, and it can **reconfigure** brain processing in ways most theories ignore.

**Limitations.**

1. It is still unclear **why** two very different tasks produce underadditivity.
2. It is often unclear whether observed activation reflects **task processing** or **non-task** processes.
3. Findings on **extra prefrontal involvement** are very **mixed**; several studies find none, and no theory explains which task features determine whether it appears.

> **Trap.** **Underadditivity** means dual-task activation is **less than the sum** of the single-task activations (Just et al., 2001: **53%** fall in language areas, **29%** in mental rotation areas). It is **not** "less than either single task alone". And the two neuroscience themes point in different directions: underadditivity suggests a **shared general resource** being divided, while new DLPFC and cerebellar activation suggests **extra coordination processes** being added.

## 5.8 Automatic Processing

One of the most striking findings in divided attention research is how dramatically **practice** improves dual-task performance. The usual explanation is that, with prolonged practice, some processes become **automatic**. This section covers the classic account (Shiffrin and Schneider), its critics (Moors and De Houwer), the memory-based accounts that explain automaticity (Logan; Ashby and Crossley), and finally the **psychological refractory period**, the most sensitive test of whether practice can truly abolish interference.

### The traditional approach: Shiffrin and Schneider (1977)

**Shiffrin and Schneider (1977)** and **Schneider and Shiffrin (1977)** distinguished two kinds of process:

| | **Controlled processes** | **Automatic processes** |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | **Limited** | **No** capacity limitations |
| Attention | **Required** | **Not** required |
| Flexibility | Used **flexibly** as circumstances change | **Very hard to modify** once learned |
| Speed and mode | Relatively **slow**, **serial** | **Fast**, **parallel** |

#### The search paradigm

Participants first memorised a **memory set** of up to **four** letters. Then a **visual display** of up to **four** characters appeared, and they decided as quickly as possible whether **any** item in the display matched **any** item in the memory set. The crucial manipulation was the **mapping**:

- **Consistent mapping.** Memory-set items always came from one category and distractors from the other: for example, **consonants** as memory items and **numbers** as distractors (or vice versa). A participant with consonants to remember knew that **any consonant** in the display had to be a target.
- **Varied mapping.** **Consonants and numbers were mixed**, serving both as memory items and as distractors from trial to trial, so category membership was no guide.

The effect of mapping was dramatic (Figure 5.19). The number of items in the memory set and in the display had a **large effect on decision speed only in the varied mapping** condition. Schneider and Shiffrin's interpretation:

- **Varied mapping** used a **controlled** process: **serial** comparison of each memory item with each display item until a match was found or every comparison had been made. More items, more comparisons, slower decisions.
- **Consistent mapping** used **automatic** processes operating **independently and in parallel**. These had developed over **years of practice** at telling letters from numbers.

#### Creating automaticity, and the cost of inflexibility

To show that automaticity grows with practice, Shiffrin and Schneider (1977) used consistent mapping with two **arbitrary** letter sets: consonants **B to L** formed one set and **Q to Z** the other. Memory items always came from one set and distractors from the other. Performance **improved greatly over 2,100 trials**, reflecting the development of automatic processing.

The biggest weakness of automatic processes is **inflexibility**, which hurts when conditions change. In the second part of the study, the first **2,100** trials were followed by **another 2,100** trials with the mapping **reversed** (former targets became distractors and vice versa). After the reversal it took **nearly 1,000 trials** for performance to get back to the level it had been at the **very start** of the experiment. The automatic process kept pulling attention to items that were now irrelevant.

In sum: **automatic** processes are **fast and parallel but inflexible**; **controlled** processes are **flexible and versatile but slow and serial**.

> **Trap.** **Consistent mapping produces automatic processing; varied mapping requires controlled processing.** An option with these reversed is a common distractor. Remember the numbers too: **2,100** training trials, then **2,100** reversed trials, with **nearly 1,000** trials needed just to return to the starting level. That reversal cost is the evidence for *inflexibility*, the defining weakness of automatic processes.

#### Evaluation of Shiffrin and Schneider

**Strengths.** The approach has had an enormous impact. Its **criteria for automaticity** are still influential (**Ashby & Crossley, 2012**), and the findings revealed very large differences between automatic and controlled processing.

**Limitations.**

1. The neat distinction between automatic and controlled processes is **oversimplified** (see below).
2. If automatic processes are truly parallel and make **no demands on capacity**, the function relating decision time to memory-set or display size should be **flat** in consistent mapping. It was not: decisions were **slower** when both the memory set and the display contained several items (Figure 5.19).
3. The theory is **descriptive rather than explanatory**. It does not say **how** serial controlled processing turns into parallel automatic processing.

### Classic background: the Stroop effect

The 7th edition does not discuss the Stroop task, but it is the standard illustration of automaticity in Indian papers. In **Stroop's (1935)** task, people name the **ink colour** of colour words; when the word names a **different** colour (RED printed in blue), colour naming is **slower** and more error-prone than for a neutral stimulus such as a row of coloured Xs. For a skilled reader, **word reading is automatic** (fast, unintended, impossible to switch off), so it produces a competing response that the slower, controlled colour-naming process must overcome. The reverse effect is small: ink colour barely slows **reading** the word.

Two links to the 7th edition. First, Stroop reading is clearly **fast** and **goal-unrelated** (it happens against the instruction), but that alone does not show it is capacity-free or unconscious, which is exactly Moors and De Houwer's point below. Second, the **emotional Stroop** is a clinical staple: anxious individuals tend to be slower to name the ink colour of **threat** words, usually read as an attentional bias towards threat (compare attentional control theory, Eysenck et al., 2007, and Chapter 15). In Posner and Petersen's scheme, resolving Stroop conflict is the job of the **executive** network.

> **Trap.** In the Stroop effect the **slowed** response is **naming the ink colour**, not reading the word. The automatic process (reading) does the interfering; the controlled process (colour naming) suffers the interference. A question that says "subjects are slower to read colour words printed in incongruent ink" has the direction wrong.

### Definitions of automaticity: Moors and De Houwer (2006)

**Moors and De Houwer (2006)** rejected the idea of a clear-cut division between automatic and controlled processes. They identified **four features** associated with automaticity:

1. **Unconscious:** the process occurs without conscious awareness.
2. **Efficient:** it uses very little attentional capacity.
3. **Fast.**
4. **Goal-unrelated:** it is not influenced by the person's current goals.

Their two key arguments:

- The four features **do not always occur together**, so it is risky to infer one feature from the presence or absence of another.
- Each feature is **continuous**, not all-or-none: a process can be fairly fast, or partly conscious. So there is **no firm dividing line** between automatic and non-automatic processing, and most processes are a **blend**.

The weakness of this approach is imprecision, since few processes are wholly automatic or wholly non-automatic. Its strength is that we can still make **relative** statements: process x is more (or less) automatic than process y.

### Instance theory (Logan)

Why should the four features *often* appear together, even if not always? **Logan's instance theory** (**Logan, 1988**; **Logan et al., 1999**) gives an influential answer. Each time a task is performed, a record of that episode (an **instance**) is stored in **long-term memory**, and these stored instances speed up later performance. Logan's central claim is that automaticity **is** memory retrieval: performance becomes automatic when it rests on **single-step, direct-access retrieval** of past solutions instead of working the answer out.

That one idea explains three features:

- **Fast**, because only retrieval of a past solution is needed.
- **Efficient** (few attentional demands), because retrieving heavily over-learned information is relatively effortless.
- **Unconscious**, because no significant processing intervenes between stimulus and retrieved response, so there is nothing to be aware of.

**Limitation.** Instance theory does not distinguish between **types of long-term memory**, although there is strong evidence for a distinction between **declarative** memory (with conscious recollection) and **procedural** memory (without it; Chapter 7). **Ashby and Crossley (2012)** argued for **three** memory types: **declarative**, **procedural** and **automatic**. Declarative and procedural memories differ markedly **early** in practice, but with prolonged practice very **similar automatic memories** form regardless of which kind of learning the task originally encouraged.

### Findings on automaticity

**Support for the classic features.** Shiffrin and Schneider's (1977) results show that supposedly automatic processes tend to be **fast**, need **little attention**, and are **goal-unrelated** (shown by their inflexibility when the goal changed). **Jansma et al. (2001)** added evidence for reduced **conscious awareness**: as automaticity increased in a consistent-mapping task (measured by performance), activation **decreased** in areas associated with conscious awareness, such as **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** and **superior frontal cortex**.

**The features dissociate.** A growing number of studies support Moors and De Houwer's claim that the features often come apart. Strikingly, and against Shiffrin and Schneider's approach, **unconscious** processes can influence **cognitive control**, the very thing that was supposed to require controlled processing (**van Gaal et al., 2012**):

- **van Gaal et al. (2010)** studied **inhibitory control** (stopping a planned response). A **subliminal** stimulus, below the threshold of awareness, produced inhibitory control involving higher-level **prefrontal** processes (also discussed in Chapter 16).
- **Capa et al. (2013)** varied the reward for success on a task requiring executive processes (a task-switching task; Figure 5.20) between **50 cents** and **1 cent**. Information about the reward was shown either **supraliminally** (**300 ms**, consciously visible) or **subliminally** (**27 ms**). Performance was **better with the higher reward even in the subliminal condition**, and **ERPs** showed participants made **greater preparatory effort** when the unseen reward was higher.

**Convergence of memory systems with practice.** Ashby and Crossley (2012) studied two kinds of **category learning** (Figure 5.21):

| | **Rule-based learning** | **Information-integration learning** |
|---|---|---|
| Categories | Separable by a simple **verbal rule** (for example respond "A" if the bars are narrow and "B" if they are wide) | Boundary is **hard or impossible to describe** in words |
| Memory system early in learning | **Declarative** | **Procedural** |
| Brain areas early on | **Prefrontal cortex** and **hippocampus** | **Putamen** (part of the **striatum**) |

Neuroimaging research (reviewed by Ashby & Crossley, 2012; see also **Soto et al., 2013**) shows this split **early** in learning. After **extensive** training (up to about **12,000 trials**), **none** of those areas correlates with performance any more. Instead, performance on **both** tasks correlates only with certain aspects of **cortical** activation, for example in **premotor cortex**.

**Hélie et al. (2010)** found the same convergence behaviourally. After **thousands** of trials, rule-based (declarative) and information-integration (procedural) tasks behaved alike in two ways:

1. **Switching the locations of the response keys** caused **severe interference** (the hallmark inflexibility of automatic processes).
2. A **second task** performed at the same time did **not** interfere with categorisation (the hallmark efficiency of automatic processes).

### Conclusions on automaticity

A clear-cut split between automatic and controlled processing is **oversimplified**; it is better to think in terms of **degrees of automaticity**. "Pure" automaticity is found so rarely that the concept itself has limited applicability, although degrees of automaticity can be identified. Even so, **memory-based** accounts (Logan; Ashby and Crossley) give useful explanations of how automaticity develops, and the idea that different kinds of learning **converge** on similar automatic memories after prolonged practice is valuable.

> **Trap.** Moors and De Houwer's four features are **unconscious, efficient, fast, goal-unrelated**; "serial", "flexible" or "capacity-demanding" are distractors (they describe controlled processing). Older keys treat automatic versus controlled as a **dichotomy** (Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977); the 7th edition treats automaticity as a **matter of degree** with features that can **dissociate** (unconscious processes can even drive cognitive control: van Gaal et al., 2010; Capa et al., 2013).

### Cognitive bottleneck

Spelke et al. (1976) seemed to show that two complex tasks can be done together with almost no disruption. But their participants had a lot of freedom over **when and how** they processed each task, so interference may have gone unnoticed through weak control or insensitive measures. A far more sensitive method is needed.

#### The psychological refractory period

The most sensitive dual-task method uses **two stimuli** (for example two lights) and **two responses** (for example two button presses), one response for each stimulus, with participants responding to each as fast as possible. When both stimuli arrive together, performance on both tasks is usually worse than when each is done alone.

The key manipulation is the interval between the two stimuli (in the wider literature, the **stimulus onset asynchrony**, or SOA). When the second stimulus follows the first **very closely**, the response to the **second** stimulus is markedly **slowed**. This is the **psychological refractory period (PRP) effect**. As the interval **increases**, the slowing becomes **much smaller**.

The PRP effect is **robust**. **Ruthruff et al. (2009)** still found a large PRP effect even when participants had **strong incentives** to eliminate it. It also matters in the real world: **Hibberd et al. (2013)** studied braking while drivers did a simple **in-vehicle** task when the car ahead braked and slowed. They found a classic PRP pattern: braking was slowest when the in-vehicle task came **just before** the lead vehicle braked.

#### The central bottleneck theory

Many theorists divide performance of a task into three successive stages:

1. **Perceptual** (identifying the stimulus),
2. **Central** (for example **deciding which response to make**: response selection),
3. **Motor** (preparing and executing the response).

According to the **central bottleneck theory**, originally proposed by **Welford (1952)**, a bottleneck prevents **more than one central decision process** from operating at any one time (**Pashler et al., 2008**). Perceptual and motor stages of two tasks can overlap, but the central stage of task 2 must **wait** until the central stage of task 1 is finished. That waiting time is the PRP. The longer the gap between stimuli, the more of task 1's central stage is already complete when stimulus 2 arrives, so the less task 2 has to wait.

The central bottleneck remains the **most influential** explanation of the PRP effect and of many dual-task costs. Two alternatives are possible:

1. Participants in most studies get **too little practice** to eliminate the effect.
2. People **choose** serial processing because they judge it will give better performance, not because parallel processing is impossible.

> **Trap.** The PRP is slowing of the **second** response, **largest at short** intervals. The bottleneck is **central (response selection)**, not perception or motor execution, and the theory is **Welford's (1952)**. Wrong option to watch for: "stimulus 2 cannot be perceived until response 1 is made"; in the bottleneck account perception of stimulus 2 proceeds, and only the **decision** waits.

#### Findings

**ERP evidence for a central locus.** **Hesselmann et al. (2011)** recorded ERPs during a PRP task, focusing on the **P300**, which probably reflects **central decision** processes. The slowing of second-task responses was closely matched by a delay in the **onset of the P300**, while **earlier** ERP components reflecting **perceptual** processing were **not** delayed. The PRP effect therefore comes from slowed central decision processes, not slowed perception, just as the central bottleneck theory predicts.

**Can practice abolish the bottleneck?**

- **Schumacher et al. (2001)** seemed to say yes. Their two tasks were (1) saying **"one", "two" or "three"** to **low-, medium- and high-pitched tones** respectively, and (2) pressing keys corresponding to the **position of a disc** on screen. After **more than 2,000** dual-task trials, **some** participants performed the tasks **as well together as singly**. In general, those who did each task especially well **alone** showed the **smallest** PRP effect.
- **Strobach et al. (2013)** ran a very similar study with **more than 5,000** trials and found that dual-task costs were **not** eliminated (Figure 5.22). With practice, costs for the **auditory** task fell from **185 to 60 ms** and for the **visual** task from **83 to 20 ms**: big reductions, but not zero.

Why the difference? Both studies rewarded fast responding on single and dual trials, but Schumacher et al.'s reward scheme may have led participants to try **harder on dual-task trials** than on single-task trials, which would shrink the apparent cost. Strobach et al. avoided that bias, which may be why they found clear residual costs.

**What does practice change?** Strobach et al. (2013) found that practice mainly **sped up the central response-selection stage** of **both** tasks. It also sped the **perceptual** stage of the **auditory** task. It had **no effect at all** on the **motor** stage of either task. The bottleneck stage is where practice does its work.

**The brain basis of response selection.** Much evidence points to the **left posterior lateral prefrontal cortex** as important for response selection (**Filmer et al., 2013**), but mostly correlational evidence. Filmer et al. applied **cathodal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)**, which **decreases** the excitability of the region. Surprisingly, dual-task costs (slower dual-task than single-task responding) were **significantly reduced**. Why is unclear, but the study shows that this region is probably **causally** involved in response selection.

**Practice reduces, but rarely eliminates, the PRP.** **Pashler (1993)** still found a PRP effect after **more than 10,000** practice trials.

**Is serial processing a choice?** **Miller et al. (2009)** argued that serial processing in PRP tasks usually gives **better** performance than parallel processing, so people adopt it. Their analysis also showed that parallel processing should win when the two stimuli are **mostly presented close together in time**, and, as predicted, people shifted from predominantly serial towards **parallel** processing under those conditions. But Miller et al. used very **simple** tasks, and parallel processing is more likely with simple tasks. With a harder task, Han and Marois (2013) found serial processing even when parallel processing was financially rewarded (section 5.7).

> **Trap.** Counter-intuitive directions: cathodal tDCS, which *decreases* excitability of left posterior lateral prefrontal cortex, *reduced* dual-task costs (Filmer et al., 2013); practice sped the **central** stage (and auditory perception) but not the **motor** stage (Strobach et al., 2013). Can practice eliminate the PRP? Schumacher et al. (2001, over 2,000 trials) said sometimes, possibly a reward artefact; Strobach et al. (2013, over 5,000) said no; Pashler (1993) still found it after over 10,000. Safe answer: **reduced, rarely eliminated**.

#### Summary and conclusions on the bottleneck

Most behavioural and neuroscience findings on the PRP effect and dual-task interference fit the **cognitive (central) bottleneck** theory. Practice usually produces a **substantial reduction** in both, but they are **rarely eliminated**, which suggests central decisions are usually made **serially**. There is some evidence for **parallel** processing when **both tasks are easy**. Serial processing may predominate partly because it generally yields **better** performance (Miller et al., 2009).

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Selection is flexible, not fixed.** The bottleneck can sit early or late (Treisman's attenuation fits better than Broadbent or Deutsch and Deutsch); the beam can be a spotlight, a zoom lens or split; selection can be by space, object or feature; load decides how much leaks through. Each classic rigid model gave way to "it depends on the task, the goal and the person".
2. **Unattended does not mean unprocessed.** Bentin et al.'s semantic effects in the ignored ear, Li et al.'s weight words, own-name detection, McGlinchey-Berroth's priming in neglect, Vuilleumier's facilitation in extinction: in every case, processing without awareness. What attention changes is **how much** processing and **whether it reaches awareness**.
3. **Two control systems everywhere.** James's active and passive attention, Posner's endogenous and exogenous systems, Corbetta and Shulman's dorsal and ventral networks, Ho and Spence's predictive and non-predictive horns: the same split between goal-driven and stimulus-driven control keeps returning, and neglect is what happens when the stimulus-driven half is damaged and drags the goal-driven half down with it.
4. **Capacity limits explain interference.** Lavie's perceptual load, extinction's reduced capacity, Bonato's dual-task neglect results, multiple resources, threaded cognition's one-task-per-resource rule, underadditivity, the central bottleneck: interference appears whenever demands exceed a limited resource, and the argument is about whether that resource is general or specific.
5. **Practice changes processing, but rarely removes limits.** Spelke's Diane and John, Shiffrin and Schneider's 2,100 trials, Ashby and Crossley's 12,000 trials, the PRP after 10,000 trials: practice makes processes faster, more efficient and more automatic, yet the central bottleneck shrinks rather than vanishes.
6. **Laboratories underestimate the real world in both directions.** Scene knowledge makes real search far easier than random displays (functional set size), and people choose when to switch tasks; but rare targets (airport security), phone conversations and real roads make attention fail in ways tidy experiments miss.

## Worked example

*A 67-year-old retired schoolteacher is seen two weeks after a right-hemisphere stroke involving the temporo-parietal junction and superior temporal gyrus. He eats only from the right of his plate. He marks a horizontal line well right of centre, crosses out only right-hand targets on a cancellation sheet, and crowds all twelve numbers into the right half of a clock face. A finger wiggled in either field alone is detected, but when both move at once he reports only the right. His daughter asks whether he is "blind on the left", whether he can drive, and what can be done.*

**What is the main disorder, and is there a second one?** The plate, the rightward bisection error, right-only cancellation and the crowded clock are signs of **left spatial neglect** after right-hemisphere damage; the clock shows an **object-centred** component too, which is expected given how strongly the two forms correlate (+0.80 across 33 patients; Rorden et al., 2012). The finger test shows **extinction** as well: single left stimuli are detected but lost when a right one competes. De Haan et al. (2012) treat extinction as a related but **separate** deficit.

**Is he blind on the left?** Probably not. Neglect is a disorder of **attention and awareness**, not sensory registration: early ERPs are normal for about 130 ms (Di Russo et al., 2008), left-field objects prime decisions (McGlinchey-Berroth et al., 1993) and extinguished pictures still facilitate later identification (Vuilleumier et al., 2002). A hemianopic patient would usually know the field was missing and scan into it; this man does not (classic background).

**Why these areas?** The TPJ and superior temporal gyrus belong to the right-lateralised **ventral attention network** (Corbetta & Shulman, 2011; Molenberghs et al., 2012b). Its damage lowers right-hemisphere activation and alertness and unbalances the **intact but underactive dorsal network**, biasing attention rightwards.

**What should the family expect?** Symptoms will be **worse under dual-task load** or fatigue: a demanding second task made neglect patients much worse at left-field detection and raised extinction from 18% to over 80% (Bonato et al., 2010). A busy ward or conversation while walking will make him miss more on the left.

**Driving?** Not while neglect and extinction persist. Driving depends on the stimulus-driven "circuit-breaker" for unexpected hazards, which is the damaged system, and even healthy drivers on a phone slow by about 250 ms and miss peripheral objects (Caird et al., 2008; Strayer & Drews, 2007).

**What can be done?** Three levers follow from the theory:

1. **Raise alertness:** warning sounds abolished the half-second left-field lag (Robertson et al., 1998); alertness training reduced neglect (Thimm et al., 2009).
2. **Prism adaptation:** prisms shifting the field 10 degrees rightwards reduce the rightward pointing bias (Rossetti et al., 1998), generally but not always (Newport & Schenk, 2012), by easing **voluntary leftward** shifts (Nijboer et al., 2008).
3. **Use the intact dorsal network:** goal-defined cues help; when letters were selected by **colour**, neglect patients recalled both sides equally (Duncan et al., 1999), so "find every red item" may beat "look left". For extinction, items that **belong together** compete less (Riddoch et al., 2006: 65% versus 40%).

**What would you not conclude?** That nothing on the left is processed (it is, implicitly), or that the dorsal network is destroyed (it is intact but poorly driven).

## MCQ traps

1. **Cherry (1953)**: cocktail party problem; dichotic listening with **shadowing**; selection by **physical cues** (sex, intensity, location); foreign language or reversed speech in the unattended ear went unnoticed, a **pure tone** was noticed.
2. **Moray (1959)**: almost no memory for unattended words repeated **35 times**, yet **own name** often detected.
3. **Broadbent (1958)** = early filter on physical features with a sensory buffer; **Treisman (1964)** = attenuation, flexible bottleneck, hierarchy from physical cues to meaning; **Deutsch and Deutsch (1963)** = late selection near the response.
4. 7th-edition verdict on unattended speech: **some semantic processing, but less** than attended (Bentin et al., 1995); the bottleneck can occur at **various stages**.
5. **Conway et al. (2001)**: **low** working memory capacity **65%** versus high **20%** detected own name.
6. **Coch et al. (2005)**: larger ERPs to attended targets at **100 ms**; **Horton et al. (2013)**: about **90 ms**, attended **enhanced and** unattended **suppressed**; **Mesgarani and Chang (2012)**: auditory cortex represented the attended speaker as if alone.
7. **Zoom lens** = Eriksen and St James (1986), LaBerge (1983) (five-letter words, middle letter versus whole word); **split attention** = Awh and Pashler (2000), poor detection **between** cued locations.
8. **Egly et al. (1994)**: invalid-same-object targets detected **faster** than equidistant invalid-different-object targets = object-based attention.
9. **Drummond and Shomstein (2010)**: no object-based effect with **100%** valid cues, so object-based attention is **strategic**.
10. **Pilz et al. (2012)**: **more space-based** than object-based attention; only a small fraction showed object-based.
11. **Inhibition of return**: location-based is fast, large, consistent; object-based is slow, small, fragile (List & Robertson, 2007).
12. **Load theory**: high **perceptual** load **reduces** distraction (Forster & Lavie, 2008, SpongeBob); high **cognitive** load **increases** it (de Fockert et al., 2001, famous faces).
13. Load theory failures: no perceptual-load effect in **hearing** (Murphy et al., 2013); **faces** distract even under high load (Hains & Baillargeon, 2011); the two loads **interact** (Linnell & Caparos, 2011).
14. **Posner (1980)**: valid fastest, neutral intermediate, invalid slowest; **uninformative peripheral** cues still work (exogenous), **uninformative central** cues ignored (endogenous).
15. **Dorsal attention network**: SPL, IPS, FEF, IFJ, MT, V3A (goal-directed). **Ventral attention network**: right-lateralised TPJ, IFJ, IFG, SMG, STG, insula (stimulus-driven "circuit-breaker").
16. **Chica et al. (2011)** TMS: right **TPJ** impairs stimulus-driven only; right **IPS** impairs **both**. **Chica et al. (2013)**: **15** differences. The ventral network responds more to **task-relevant** than merely **salient** distractors (Corbetta et al., 2008; Indovina & Macaluso, 2007).
17. **Neglect** = typically right-hemisphere damage, left side unattended; line bisection mark to the **right**; cancellation misses the **left**.
18. **Rorden et al. (2012)**: egocentric and allocentric neglect correlate **+0.80** across **33** patients.
19. Neglect lesions: right STG, IFG, insula, SMG, angular gyrus (mostly **ventral** network; Molenberghs et al., 2012b, **nine** areas). Extinction: right **TPJ and IPS**.
20. **Corbetta and Shulman (2011)**: ventral network damaged, dorsal network **intact but underactive**; right hemisphere hypoactivated (the book's summary saying "left" is the error).
21. **Bays et al. (2010)**: top-down and stimulus-driven deficits correlated **+0.98**. **Riddoch et al. (2006)**: related pairs **65% versus 40%**. **Bonato et al. (2010)**: extinction **18% to over 80%** with a second task.
22. **Rossetti et al. (1998)**: prisms shift the field **10 degrees to the right**; mechanism = easier **voluntary** leftward shifts (Nijboer et al., 2008).
23. **Wolfe et al. (2007)**: target prevalence **50% gives 80%** detection, **2% gives 54%**; cause = excessive caution in reporting.
24. **FIT (Treisman & Gelade, 1980)**: display sizes **1 to 30**; feature search flat (parallel), conjunction search steep (serial); attention is the **glue**; failures give **illusory conjunctions** (Friedman-Hill et al., 1995).
25. **Thornton and Gilden (2007)**: **29** tasks; **72%** parallel, **28%** serial.
26. **Wolfe et al. (2011a)**: search in scenes **10 ms per item** versus **40 ms** on a blank background (**functional set size**); **Võ and Wolfe (2012)**: **80%** of a scene rarely fixated.
27. **Spatial ventriloquism** = vision dominates for location; **temporal ventriloquism** = audition dominates for timing; both from **Welch and Warren's (1980)** modality appropriateness and precision hypothesis; blurred vision reverses capture (Alais & Burr, 2004); **Colavita (1974)**: the visual response wins on bimodal trials.
28. **Ho and Spence (2005)**: valid horns speed responses at both **80%** and **50%** validity (exogenous), more at 80% (endogenous added). **Gray et al. (2014)**: vibrotactile cue moving **towards the head**, **250 ms** faster braking.
29. **Caird et al. (2008)**: **33** studies, **250 ms** slowing, handheld = hands-free, talking worse than listening; **Strayer and Drews (2007)**: recognition **under 25% versus 50%**, P300 **halved**; junction stops: **21%** versus **75%** failed to stop.
30. **Spelke et al. (1976)**: Diane and John, **five hours a week for four months**; flaw = **accuracy** measures and self-paced reading allowing alternation.
31. **Wickens**: **four** dimensions (stages, codes, modalities, responses); **threaded cognition** (Salvucci & Taatgen): greedy-polite threads, **no central executive**, "problem state" = working memory.
32. **Just et al. (2001)**: underadditivity, language areas **down 53%**, rotation areas **down 29%**. **Johnson and Zatorre (2006)**: DLPFC active only in dual task; **Johnson et al. (2007)**: TMS to DLPFC impairs dividing attention; **T. Wu et al. (2013)**: cerebellum.
33. **Shiffrin and Schneider (1977)**: **consistent** mapping = automatic, **varied** mapping = controlled; **2,100** trials then reversal needing **nearly 1,000** trials to recover.
34. **Moors and De Houwer (2006)**: unconscious, efficient, fast, goal-unrelated; features **dissociate** and are **graded**. **Logan (1988)**: automaticity = single-step direct-access **memory retrieval**.
35. **PRP**: second response slowed most at **short** intervals; **central bottleneck (Welford, 1952)** at **response selection**; P300 onset delayed, early components not (Hesselmann et al., 2011).
36. Practice and the PRP: Schumacher et al. (2001) **over 2,000** trials, some eliminated it; Strobach et al. (2013) **over 5,000** trials, auditory **185 to 60 ms**, visual **83 to 20 ms**; Pashler (1993) still present after **over 10,000**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Focused (selective) attention** | Trying to process one source of information while ignoring others presented at the same time |
| **Divided attention** | Performing two tasks at once; multitasking |
| **Cocktail party problem** | The difficulty of following one voice when several people speak at once (Cherry, 1953) |
| **Dichotic listening task** | Different messages delivered to the two ears, with attention directed to one |
| **Shadowing** | Repeating aloud, word for word, the attended message in a dichotic listening task |
| **Early / attenuation / late selection** | Broadbent's filter on physical features before meaning; Treisman's weakening (not blocking) of unattended input; Deutsch and Deutsch's full analysis with selection near the response |
| **Spotlight / zoom lens** | Metaphors for visual attention as a fixed movable beam (Posner) or a beam whose width can be adjusted (Eriksen & St James) |
| **Split attention** | Attention allocated to two or more separate regions of space, without the space between them |
| **Space-based / object-based / feature-based attention** | Selecting a location, a whole object, or a property such as colour |
| **Inhibition of return** | A reduced tendency for attention to go back to a location or object it has just left |
| **Perceptual load / cognitive load** | Demands of a task on perceptual capacity (high load cuts distraction) versus on working memory and control (high load increases distraction) (Lavie) |
| **Covert attention** | Shifting attention to a location or object without moving the eyes towards it |
| **Endogenous / exogenous system** | Posner's voluntary, intention-driven orienting (central cues) versus automatic, stimulus-driven orienting (peripheral cues) |
| **Dorsal attention network** | Goal-directed fronto-parietal network (SPL, IPS, FEF and others) used when we expect a stimulus (Corbetta & Shulman) |
| **Ventral attention network** | Right-lateralised stimulus-driven network (TPJ, IFG, SMG, STG, insula) that interrupts the current focus when something unexpected and relevant appears |
| **Neglect** | A disorder, usually after right-hemisphere damage, in which stimuli on the left side of space or of objects go unnoticed; more severe than extinction |
| **Egocentric / allocentric neglect** | Neglect of the left of the visual field relative to the body, versus neglect of the left side of each object |
| **Extinction** | Failure to detect a contralesional stimulus only when another stimulus appears at the same time on the ipsilesional side |
| **Biased competition** | De Haan et al.'s account of extinction: stimuli compete for limited attention and the ipsilesional one is favoured |
| **Prism adaptation** | A neglect treatment in which patients adapt to prisms that shift the visual field rightwards, producing a corrective leftward shift |
| **Visual search** | Detecting a specified target among distractors as quickly as possible |
| **Feature integration theory** | Treisman's theory that features are registered in parallel and then bound into objects by serial focused attention |
| **Illusory conjunction** | Wrongly combining features from different stimuli into an object that was not present |
| **Texture tiling model** | Rosenholtz's account that search difficulty depends mainly on how much information peripheral vision, represented as textures, provides |
| **Dual-path model** | Wolfe's model of a limited-capacity selective pathway guided by a non-selective pathway that extracts scene gist |
| **Functional set size** | The effective number of locations searched once implausible regions of a scene are excluded |
| **Cross-modal attention** | Coordinating attention across two or more senses |
| **Ventriloquism effect** | Hearing a sound as coming from its apparent visual source |
| **Temporal ventriloquism** | The perceived timing of a visual event being pulled towards a nearby sound |
| **Colavita effect** | Visual dominance in which people respond to the visual part of a simultaneous light and sound and miss the auditory part |
| **Exogenous spatial attention** | Attention to a location driven by "automatic" processes |
| **Endogenous spatial attention** | Attention to a stimulus directed by intentions or goals |
| **Multiple-resource theory** | Wickens's view that tasks draw on separate resources (stages, codes, modalities, responses) and interfere when they share them |
| **Threaded cognition** | Salvucci and Taatgen's theory that tasks run as threads that claim resources greedily and release them politely, with no central executive |
| **Underadditivity** | Brain activation for two tasks performed together being less than the sum of their activations when performed separately |
| **Controlled versus automatic processes** | Slow, serial, capacity-limited, flexible processes versus fast, parallel, capacity-free, inflexible ones (Shiffrin & Schneider, 1977) |
| **Consistent versus varied mapping** | Targets and distractors always from separate categories (allows automaticity) versus mixed across trials (requires control) |
| **Instance theory** | Logan's view that automaticity is direct retrieval of stored past solutions from long-term memory |
| **Stroop effect** | Slower naming of ink colour when the printed word names a different colour (classic background) |
| **Psychological refractory period (PRP) effect** | Slowing of the response to the second of two stimuli presented close together in time |
| **Central bottleneck theory** | Welford's (1952) view that only one central decision (response selection) can be carried out at a time |
