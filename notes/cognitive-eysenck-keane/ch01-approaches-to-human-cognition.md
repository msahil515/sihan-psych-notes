# Chapter 1: Approaches to Human Cognition

**What this chapter is really about.** Every later chapter reports four kinds of evidence: reaction times and errors from healthy people in the laboratory, the patterns of loss and sparing in brain-damaged patients, images and waveforms from scanners and electrodes, and the behaviour of programs built to reproduce human performance. Chapter 1 is the manual for all four. It names the approaches (**experimental cognitive psychology**, **cognitive neuropsychology**, **cognitive neuroscience** and **computational cognitive science**), states the assumptions each relies on, and lists what each does well and where it misleads. No approach wins. Each is blind in a different place, so the strongest conclusions come from **converging operations**: several methods with different weaknesses pointing to the same answer.

For a clinical psychology entrant this is the book's research-methods chapter. Dissociation logic is the logic of neuropsychological assessment; "what can a scan show, and what can it not?" lies behind every fMRI claim you will be asked to judge; and the contrast between correlational imaging and the causal leverage of lesions and TMS is an interview favourite. The chapter also notes that the cognitive approach is now central to clinical psychology, because cognitive processes (especially **cognitive biases**) help explain how disorders develop and how they are successfully treated.

**How to read this chapter.** 1.1 sets out the four approaches. 1.2 covers experimental cognitive psychology: 1956, the information-processing model and its two corrections, task impurity, and five limitations. 1.3 covers cognitive neuropsychology: Coltheart's four assumptions, dissociation logic, single cases versus case series, strengths and limitations. 1.4, the longest module, covers brain vocabulary, network organisation, every technique with its spatial and temporal resolution, TMS, and the strengths and eight general limitations of cognitive neuroscience. 1.5 covers modelling versus AI, connectionism, production systems, ACT-R and the evaluation. 1.6 compares the approaches (Table 1.1 rebuilt) and 1.7 outlines the book. Most modules end with strengths then limitations, and "which is NOT a limitation of..." items are built straight from those lists.

## 1.1 Introduction

### Why cognition matters now

Interest in the brain and mind has never been higher: research in cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience has surged, and the media are full of colour-coded brain images. Two neighbouring fields have also become more cognitive. In **clinical psychology**, it is now accepted that cognitive processes, particularly cognitive biases, play a part in why disorders develop and in how they are treated (Chapter 15 develops this). In **social psychology**, cognitive processes are increasingly used to explain social communication.

### Defining the terms: cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience

**Cognitive psychology** studies the internal processes we use to make sense of the environment and to decide what to do: attention, perception, learning, memory, language, problem solving, reasoning and thinking. In the **narrow** sense, it tries to understand these processes by observing the **behaviour** of people doing cognitive tasks. In a **broader** sense, the term also takes in evidence about brain activity and brain structure. Eysenck and Keane use the broad sense in the title of the book.

**Cognitive neuroscience** shares the same aims but insists that the brain must be studied alongside behaviour. The argument is simple: cognition happens in the brain, so brain evidence is relevant. Cognitive neuroscience therefore uses information about **both behaviour and the brain** to understand human cognition. Notice what follows: the line between cognitive neuroscience and cognitive psychology *in the broad sense* is blurred, while the line between cognitive neuroscience and cognitive psychology *in the narrow sense* is clear (brain data or not).

Cognitive neuroscientists work in three main ways:

1. **Brain-imaging techniques**, of which **functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)** is the best known.
2. **Electrophysiological techniques**, which record the electrical signals the brain generates.
3. **The study of brain damage**, on the assumption that the pattern of impairments a patient shows tells us both how normal cognition works and which brain areas are responsible for which processes.

### The four main approaches

The book organises the field into four approaches, while stressing that researchers increasingly combine two or more of them.

| Approach | What it uses to understand cognition | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cognitive psychology** (experimental) | Behavioural evidence from healthy people | Because the other three approaches also rely on behavioural data, its influence is enormous |
| **2. Cognitive neuropsychology** | The performance of brain-damaged patients, used to understand *normal* cognition | Originally tied closely to cognitive psychology; recently also linked to cognitive neuroscience |
| **3. Cognitive neuroscience** | Evidence from behaviour *and* the brain | Imaging, electrophysiology, brain stimulation |
| **4. Computational cognitive science** | Computational models of cognition | Models increasingly take account of behavioural and brain evidence |

> **Trap.** The 7th edition's four approaches are experimental cognitive psychology, cognitive neuropsychology, cognitive neuroscience and computational cognitive science. "Artificial intelligence" is not one of the four (1.5 explains that AI does not try to mimic human processes), and neither is "behaviourism" or "neuropsychological assessment". Also note that cognitive neuropsychology studies patients to learn about **normal** cognition; it is not the clinical study of brain damage for its own sake.

## 1.2 Cognitive Psychology

### Origins: why 1956

Asking exactly when cognitive psychology began has no precise answer, but **1956** is the year the book singles out. At a meeting held at the **Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)** that year:

- **Noam Chomsky** set out his theory of language;
- **George Miller** discussed the "magic number seven" in short-term memory (**Miller, 1956**);
- **Newell and Simon** discussed their highly influential **General Problem Solver** model (written up as **Newell et al., 1958**).

The same year, **Bruner et al. (1956)** published the first systematic cognitive study of **concept formation**.

Classic background: the MIT meeting is usually identified as the Symposium on Information Theory, and Miller's figure is usually quoted as "seven plus or minus two". The 7th edition revises it in Chapter 6 to about three to four chunks, so check whether a question wants Miller's figure or the modern one.

> **Trap.** Four names, one year. If an option pairs 1956 with "Chomsky: language", "Miller: magic number seven", "Newell and Simon: General Problem Solver" or "Bruner: concept formation", all four are correct pairings. The General Problem Solver discussed in 1956 is cited as Newell et al. (1958); the later book that established **production-system** models (1.5) is **Newell and Simon (1972)**. Do not merge the two dates.

### The information-processing approach

For a long time most cognitive psychologists worked within the **information-processing approach**, which rests loosely on an analogy between the mind and a computer. The chapter summary puts the central idea compactly: the mind was viewed as a **general-purpose, symbol-processing system of limited capacity**.

The book's Figure 1.1 shows a version popular in the **1970s**, and its logic is a straight line:

1. A **stimulus** (an environmental event, such as a problem or task) is presented.
2. It triggers a sequence of **internal cognitive processes**.
3. Those processes eventually produce the **response** (the answer).

Two assumptions were built into this picture. First, processing was driven by the stimulus, which is **bottom-up processing**: processing directly influenced by environmental input. Second, only one process was thought to occur at a time, which is **serial processing**: each process finishes before the next begins.

The book calls this picture drastically oversimplified, and it corrects both assumptions.

### Correction 1: top-down processing

Real task performance also involves **top-down processing**: processing shaped by what the person already knows and expects, not only by the stimulus. The book's Figure 1.2 is the classic demonstration. A short phrase is printed inside a triangle, and most readers report "Paris in the spring". Look again and the word "the" appears twice. Your expectation that this was a familiar phrase (top-down) overrode what was actually on the page (bottom-up), so you did not notice the repetition.

> **Trap.** Bottom-up = driven by the stimulus (data-driven). Top-down = driven by knowledge, expectation and past experience (conceptually driven). In the "Paris in the the spring" demonstration, missing the repeated word is evidence of **top-down** processing dominating, not a failure of top-down processing. Examiners like to reverse this.

### Correction 2: parallel processing

The traditional view also assumed processing was usually serial. In fact, more than one process typically runs at the same time, which is **parallel processing**. Parallel processing is much more likely on a **highly practised** task than on a new one (Chapter 5 returns to this under automaticity). The book's example is driving. A learner in the first lesson finds it nearly impossible to change gear, steer accurately and watch other road users simultaneously; an experienced driver does all three without strain.

### The laboratory tradition

For many years almost all research on cognition consisted of tightly controlled, "scientific" laboratory experiments on healthy people. Researchers devised ingenious tasks to expose the processes behind attention, perception, learning, memory and reasoning, and this work has heavily shaped the studies run by cognitive neuroscientists. The book states that nearly all the research it discusses owes a great deal to this approach.

### Task processes: the task impurity problem

A central difficulty for cognitive psychologists is the **task impurity problem**: most cognitive tasks draw on a complex mixture of processes, so it is hard to say which process a given result reflects.

One solution is to extract what several tasks have in common. **Miyake et al. (2000)** were interested in the process of deliberately **inhibiting a dominant response**, and studied three tasks that all seem to demand it:

| Task | What participants do | The response to be inhibited |
|---|---|---|
| **Stroop task** | Name the ink colour of a colour word (e.g., RED printed in green) | Reading the word aloud, which is hard to suppress (see Macleod, 2015, for a discussion of the task) |
| **Anti-saccade task** | A visual cue appears; look in the **opposite** direction | The automatic eye movement towards the cue |
| **Stop-signal task** | Categorise words as fast as possible, but withhold the response when a tone sounds | The already-prepared categorisation response |

Miyake et al. found the three tasks shared similar processes. Using complex statistical techniques (the chapter summary calls this **latent-variable analysis**), they extracted the variance common to all three and treated it as a relatively **pure measure of inhibition**. The logic is worth stating: each task is impure in its own way, but the impurities differ from task to task, so what survives across all three is more likely to be the process of interest. (Chapter 6 develops Miyake's unity and diversity of executive functions.)

### Task processes: showing that a process is used

A second strategy is to interfere with a suspected process and see whether the effect it supposedly causes disappears. The book's example is the **word-length effect**. When people see five words and must repeat them back, they do worse with long words than with short ones (**Baddeley et al., 1975**; Chapter 6). The likely reason is **verbal rehearsal**: people say the words to themselves while they are being shown, and long words take longer to rehearse. (Chapter 6 also gives an alternative account.)

To test whether rehearsal is really involved, Baddeley et al. (1975) used **articulatory suppression**: while the list was presented, participants repeated the digits **1 to 8** in order, which occupies the speech machinery and blocks rehearsal. Two results followed. Overall performance fell, as predicted. More tellingly, **the word-length effect disappeared**. If removing rehearsal removes the effect, the effect must have depended on rehearsal.

> **Trap.** The diagnostic finding is not that suppression lowers recall (almost any distraction would do that); it is that suppression **abolishes the word-length effect**. Note also the suppression material in this study: the digits 1 to 8, not the word "the" used in some later studies.

### Strengths of cognitive psychology

1. **It was the engine room.** For many years cognitive psychology drove progress, and every other approach has benefited from it. Cognitive neuropsychology became important about **25 years after** cognitive psychology, and the timing was not accidental: until there were reasonable accounts of *healthy* cognition, nobody could tell which patterns of impairment in patients mattered theoretically.
2. **It supplies the theories.** Computational models are typically built on pre-computational psychological theories, and most theories that drive cognitive neuroscience began in cognitive psychology. **Coltheart (2011)** went further and claimed (controversially) that cognitive neuroscience has so far made **no difference** to cognitive theorising.
3. **It supplies the tasks.** Cognitive psychology has dominated the development of cognitive tasks and of **task analysis** (working out how a task is actually done).

The book adds a caution: none of this should downplay the substantial contributions of the other three approaches.

### Limitations of cognitive psychology

Learn these five as a numbered list; they reappear in Table 1.1 and in the computational and neuroscience evaluations.

1. **Ecological validity.** Behaviour in the laboratory may differ from behaviour in everyday life. **Ecological validity** is the extent to which laboratory findings apply to real life. In most experiments the sequence of stimuli is fixed in advance by the experimenter and does not change in response to what the participant does; **Wachtel (1973)** called this the **implacable experimenter**. In daily life, by contrast, much of our cognition is about changing the situation to suit ourselves.
2. **Indirect measures.** Speed and accuracy are only indirect evidence about internal processes. From reaction times and errors alone it is hard, for example, to tell whether the processes on a complex task run serially or in parallel.
3. **Vague verbal theories.** Theories stated only in words tend to be vague, so it is unclear what they predict (this is becoming less common). Computational modelling can largely fix this, because a program forces every assumption to be spelled out (1.5).
4. **Paradigm specificity.** Findings from one experimental task sometimes fail to appear even on apparently very similar tasks, so some findings are narrow in scope (**Meiser, 2011**). Meiser's sharper point is that once a paradigm is introduced it can turn from a research tool into the object of research itself. The remedy is to build theories that account for performance across several similar tasks.
5. **No overarching framework.** Cognitive psychology has lacked a comprehensive theoretical **architecture** that clarifies how the parts of the cognitive system relate to each other. There has been some progress, and **ACT-R** (J.R. Anderson et al., 2004; see 1.5) is the book's example of a cognitive architecture.

> **Trap.** Ecological validity and paradigm specificity are different complaints. Ecological validity asks whether a lab finding generalises to **everyday life**; paradigm specificity asks whether it generalises to **other lab tasks**. Wachtel's "implacable experimenter" belongs to ecological validity (the fixed stimulus sequence is unlike the interactive real world). Both limitations are later said to apply to cognitive neuroscience too, and ecological validity probably more so (1.4).

## 1.3 Cognitive Neuropsychology

### What the approach studies

**Cognitive neuropsychology** studies the patterns of cognitive performance, both what is intact and what is impaired, in patients who have suffered **lesions**: structural damage to the brain caused by injury or disease. Its distinctive claim is that such patients can tell us a great deal about **normal** cognition.

That claim sounds unpromising until you see it pay off. In the **1960s** nearly every memory researcher believed that information could only get into long-term memory by first passing through short-term memory (Chapter 6 covers the multi-store model). Then **Shallice and Warrington (1970)** described **KF**, a brain-damaged man whose **short-term memory was severely impaired** while his **long-term memory was intact**. If long-term storage required prior short-term processing, KF's pattern should have been impossible. His case was a major reason theories of normal memory changed.

> **Trap.** KF is the patient with **impaired short-term** and **intact long-term** memory (Shallice & Warrington, 1970). HM is the reverse: **impaired long-term**, **intact short-term**. Swapping the two is the commonest error in this chapter, and the pair together form the textbook double dissociation (below).

### Mind, not brain: Coltheart's position

You might expect people who study brain-damaged patients to be mainly interested in the brain. Many are not. **Max Coltheart**, a leading cognitive neuropsychologist, has argued that the field's principal aim is to understand the **mind**, specifically the **functional architecture of cognition**, and that learning about the brain is not its main goal (**Coltheart, 2010**). On this view, where a lesion is matters less than which cognitive component it has knocked out.

Other cognitive neuropsychologists disagree with Coltheart (2010). A growing number do take the brain into account, using techniques such as **magnetic resonance imaging** to locate each patient's damage, and there is increasing willingness to consider neuroimaging findings.

> **Trap.** Three Coltheart citations do three different jobs in this chapter. **Coltheart (2001)**: the clearest statement of the **four assumptions** of cognitive neuropsychology. **Coltheart (2010)**: the field's aim is to learn about the **mind** (functional architecture), not the brain. **Coltheart (2011)**: the controversial claim that cognitive neuroscience has made **no difference** to cognitive theorising. Match year to claim.

### Theoretical assumptions (Coltheart, 2001)

The assumptions of cognitive neuropsychology have been debated for years (e.g., **Davies, 2010**). The book follows **Coltheart's (2001)** account, which has four assumptions. For each one, ask what would go wrong if it were false; that is how the book justifies them, and it is how exam questions are framed.

#### Assumption 1: modularity

**Modularity** is the assumption that the cognitive system consists of many **modules** or processors that operate fairly independently of each other, each specialised for one type of processing. Modules are assumed to show **domain specificity**: each responds only to one class of stimuli. The book's example is a possible **face-recognition module** that responds only when a face is presented.

Is the assumption correct? It is very controversial. The majority position, according to the book, is that the human cognitive system shows **some** modularity, but cognitive neuropsychologists often **exaggerate** how much.

#### Assumption 2: anatomical modularity

**Anatomical modularity** is the assumption that each module is located in a **specific brain area**. Why does this matter? Cognitive neuropsychologists make most progress when a patient's damage is confined to a single module. If modules were not anatomically localised, and each one were spread over large areas of brain, almost every patient would have damage to most modules at once, and it would be impossible to work out how many modules exist or what each one does.

The evidence is mixed. There is some anatomical modularity in the **visual processing system** (Chapter 2). For most **complex tasks**, support is much weaker:

- **Duncan and Owen (2000)** found that the **same frontal-lobe areas** were activated by very different complex tasks.
- **Yarkoni et al. (2011)** examined more than **3,000 studies** and found that areas such as the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** and **anterior cingulate cortex** were activated in **20%** of them, despite the enormous variety of tasks involved. (This meta-analysis returns in 1.4, where the exact figure of 3,489 studies is given.)

#### Assumption 3: uniformity of functional architecture across people

Coltheart's phrase is **uniformity of functional architecture across people**: everyone's cognitive system is organised in the same way. Consider the alternative. If functional architecture differed from person to person, findings from one patient would say nothing about anyone else, and single-case research would be pointless.

The same idea is common in cognitive neuroscience. For example, it is often claimed that face processing in virtually everyone relies heavily on the **fusiform face area** (**Weiner & Grill-Spector, 2012**). If there were large individual differences in architecture and in the brain areas involved in a process, understanding cognition would become very much harder.

#### Assumption 4: subtractivity

**Subtractivity** is the assumption that brain damage **removes or impairs** one or more modules but does **not change or add** anything. Again consider the alternative: if patients built new modules to compensate for what they had lost, their performance would no longer be "normal system minus one part", and it would tell us little about the intact system.

This assumption is **sometimes incorrect**. There is often **partial recovery** of impaired processes (**Cus et al., 2011**), and recovery can involve either restored function **within** the damaged area or the **recruitment of different brain regions**.

| Assumption | What it claims | Why it matters | Status in the 7th edition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Modularity** | Many fairly independent, domain-specific modules | Patients can lose one module and keep the rest | Some modularity, but exaggerated |
| **Anatomical modularity** | Each module sits in a specific brain area | Otherwise every lesion would hit most modules | Some support in vision; weak for complex tasks (Duncan & Owen, 2000; Yarkoni et al., 2011) |
| **Uniformity of functional architecture** | Everyone's system is organised the same way | Otherwise one patient tells us nothing about others | Little or no convincing evidence for it (see Limitations) |
| **Subtractivity** | Damage subtracts; it adds or changes nothing | Otherwise compensation masks the normal system | Sometimes wrong: recovery and recruitment (Cus et al., 2011) |

> **Trap.** The four assumptions are modularity, **anatomical** modularity, uniformity of functional architecture and subtractivity. Distractors include "domain specificity" (a property of modules, folded into the first assumption, not a separate fifth one), "functional specialisation" (a cognitive neuroscience term, 1.4) and "double dissociation" (a method, not an assumption).

### The modularity assumption examined

Modular systems usually work by **mostly serial processing**: one module finishes before the next begins, so modules interact very little. There is some support from the **evolutionary** approach, since species with larger brains tend to have more specialised brain regions that could support modular processing.

Against it, heavy modularity is hard to square with neuroimaging and other brain-activity evidence. The human brain has a **moderately high level of connectivity** (**Bullmore & Sporns, 2012**; 1.4), which implies **more parallel processing** than most cognitive neuropsychologists assume.

Classic background: the modern debate about modules is usually traced to **Fodor's (1983)** *The Modularity of Mind*, which proposed strictly encapsulated input modules. Older textbooks and exam keys sometimes present strong modularity as the settled foundation of cognitive neuropsychology. The 7th edition's position is softer: some modularity exists, but the system is more interactive and flexible than strict modularity allows.

### Research in cognitive neuropsychology: dissociations

The central research tool is the search for **dissociations**. A dissociation occurs when a patient performs **normally on one task** (task X) but is **impaired on another** (task Y). Amnesia is the standard example: these patients do close to normally on short-term memory tests yet badly on many long-term memory tests (Chapter 6).

It is tempting to conclude that the two tasks use different modules and that the long-term memory module has been damaged. The book calls this tempting but dangerous. The patient might do well on task X and badly on task Y simply because **task Y is harder**. A single dissociation can therefore reflect a difference in **task difficulty or complexity** rather than separate modules.

### Double dissociations

The standard remedy is the **double dissociation**: one patient is normal on task X and impaired on task Y, while **another patient shows the opposite pattern** (impaired on X, normal on Y). Now the difficulty explanation fails, because no single difficulty ordering can make X harder for one patient and Y harder for the other.

The memory example completes itself. Amnesic patients have impaired long-term but intact short-term memory; KF (Shallice & Warrington, 1970) had impaired short-term but intact long-term memory. Together they form a double dissociation that strongly suggests short-term and long-term memory are genuinely distinct and depend on different brain regions.

| Pattern | Patient A | Patient B | What it can show |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Single dissociation** | X normal, Y impaired | (none) | Weak: Y may just be harder |
| **Double dissociation** | X normal, Y impaired | X impaired, Y normal | Stronger evidence for separate processes or modules for X and Y |
| **Association** | X impaired, Y impaired | (often found together) | Weakest: may reflect anatomical neighbours, not a shared mechanism |

The double dissociation approach has **limitations**, which the book lists:

1. It **assumes that separate modules exist**, which may itself be mistaken.
2. Double dissociations can usually be explained in **several ways**, so they provide only **indirect** evidence for separate modules underlying the two tasks (**Davies, 2010**).
3. With so many double dissociations reported, it is hard to decide **which ones are theoretically important**.

> **Trap.** A double dissociation is "reasonable but not definitive" evidence (the chapter summary's wording). An option claiming that a double dissociation **proves** two modules exist, or that it is free of theoretical assumptions, is wrong on both counts: it presupposes modularity, and other explanations are usually possible.

### Associations and syndromes

An **association** occurs when a patient is impaired on **both** tasks X and Y. Associations are sometimes taken as evidence for a **syndrome**, a set of symptoms that often occur together and are assumed to share a common origin.

The syndrome-based approach has a serious flaw. Two impairments may co-occur not because they depend on the **same** mechanism but because the different mechanisms they depend on are **anatomically adjacent**, so one lesion tends to damage both. The book's example is **Gerstmann's syndrome**, defined by four very different symptoms:

1. problems of **finger identification**;
2. problems in **calculation**;
3. impaired **spelling**;
4. **left-right disorientation**.

It is unlikely that one mechanism underlies all four. More plausibly, four separate mechanisms happen to sit next to each other in the brain.

> **Trap.** Gerstmann's syndrome: finger identification, calculation, **spelling** and left-right disorientation. Many clinical textbooks list "agraphia" (writing) for the third symptom; the 7th edition says impaired spelling. Either way, the chapter uses it to make one point: an association can reflect **anatomical adjacency** rather than a shared cognitive mechanism.

### Single-case studies versus case series

For much of its history, cognitive neuropsychology emphasised **single-case studies**. There were two reasons:

1. Researchers often have access to only **one patient** with a given pattern of impairment.
2. Every patient is assumed to be **unique**, because no two have exactly the same brain damage, so averaging several patients who supposedly share a disorder was thought to be misleading.

This can be surprising, because most psychological research recommends reasonably large samples. The field is now moving towards the **case-series study**: several patients thought to have similar impairments are tested, then their **individual** data are compared and the **variation across patients** is assessed. This is not the same as a traditional group study, because the individual profiles are kept rather than being averaged away.

Why is the case-series approach generally preferable (**Lambon Ralph et al., 2011**)?

1. **Richer data.** It lets you actually **measure** variation among patients rather than just worry about it, as the single-case approach must.
2. **Representativeness.** Theories can be built on the **majority** of a series, with "outlier" patients de-emphasised. With one patient you cannot know whether that patient is typical of the condition or an outlier.

> **Trap.** A case series is not a group-averaged study. It tests several similar patients but keeps and compares their **individual** results. Also note a misprint in the book's own chapter summary, which says the "case-study approach" is generally more informative than the single-case approach; the text makes clear it means the **case-series** approach (Lambon Ralph et al., 2011).

### Strengths of cognitive neuropsychology

1. **Theories of language, especially reading.** One might expect damage to language areas to impair reading of everything. It does not (Chapter 9). Patients read three kinds of item aloud: **regular words** (pronunciation predictable from the spelling), **irregular words** (not predictable) and **non-words**. Some patients read regular words and non-words reasonably well but irregular words poorly; others read regular words but struggle with unfamiliar words and non-words. These selective patterns have transformed theories of reading (**Coltheart, 2015**). Classic background, covered fully in Chapter 9: the first pattern is **surface dyslexia** and the second is **phonological dyslexia**, and together they form a double dissociation that supports dual-route models of reading.
2. **Theories of memory.** The clearest example is **HM**, now known to be **Henry Molaison**. His long-term memory was severely impaired, **except his ability to learn motor skills**, while his short-term memory was intact (Chapter 7). His case supported three hypotheses at once:
   - there is a major distinction between **short-term and long-term memory**;
   - long-term memory is divided into **at least two different systems** (his preserved motor-skill learning shows one system can survive when another fails);
   - because HM had severe damage to the **hippocampus**, that structure must be crucial for long-term memory.
   **Eichenbaum (2015)** discusses HM's enormous impact.

### Limitations of cognitive neuropsychology

1. **Modularity is too strong.** The assumption that cognition is fundamentally modular is reasonable but seems too strong. Modular systems tend to be **inflexible** and **serial**, whereas human cognition is notable for its **flexibility** and **extensive interactions** across the brain. If modularity is wrong, the whole enterprise is affected (**Patterson & Plaut, 2009**).
2. **The other assumptions are too extreme.** Neuroimaging offers only **modest** support for **anatomical modularity**, and there is **little or no convincing evidence** for **uniformity of functional architecture**.
3. **Compensation disguises the damage.** **Compensatory strategies** learned during recovery can hide what the damage did. Patients with **pure alexia** (severe reading problems) get through words by identifying **one letter at a time**, which healthy readers never do. Brain function also reorganises during recovery (**Cus et al., 2011**), reflecting considerable **neural plasticity** (**Overgaard & Mogensen, 2011**).
4. **Too little interest in the brain.** Paradoxically for a field defined by brain-damaged patients, it has historically paid little attention to brain function and cognitive neuroscience. This is changing, as in combined work on recognition memory (1.4).
5. **Too much reliance on single cases.** Some single cases (HM above all) have deservedly been hugely influential, but single-case studies have real limitations and case series give richer data.

The chapter summary adds one more limitation not spelled out in the main text: brain damage is often **so extensive** that the findings are hard to interpret.

> **Trap.** Compensatory strategies are a **limitation** of cognitive neuropsychology (they break the subtractivity assumption), and in Table 1.1 they are its first-listed limitation. "Causal links can be shown between brain damage and cognitive performance" is listed as a **strength**. A "which is NOT a limitation" question will often hide one of the strengths (double dissociations, causal links, revealing unexpected complexity in language, transforming memory research, straddling cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience) among the limitations.

## 1.4 Cognitive Neuroscience: The Brain in Action

Cognitive neuroscience studies the brain intensively alongside behaviour. The difficulty is that the brain is extremely complicated: about **100 billion neurons**, connected in very complex ways. To follow imaging research you first need the vocabulary for naming brain areas. The book gives **three** ways of describing a brain area: by **lobes and landmarks**, by **Brodmann area**, and by **function**.

### Way 1: lobes, sulci, gyri and directional terms

The cerebral cortex in each hemisphere has **four lobes**: **frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital** (Figure 1.3 shows them in the left hemisphere). The boundaries are marked by grooves:

| Boundary | Separates |
|---|---|
| **Central sulcus** | Frontal lobe (in front) from parietal lobe (behind) |
| **Lateral fissure** | Temporal lobe (below) from the frontal and parietal lobes (above) |
| **Parieto-occipital sulcus** and **pre-occipital notch** | Occipital lobe from the parietal and temporal lobes |

A **sulcus** is a groove or furrow in the brain's surface; a **gyrus** (plural **gyri**) is a ridge or elevated area between grooves.

To say more precisely where activation lies, researchers use six directional terms. Learn them as three pairs plus their synonyms:

| Term | Synonym | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal** | Superior | Towards the top |
| **Ventral** | Inferior | Towards the bottom |
| **Rostral** | Anterior | Towards the front |
| **Posterior** | (the book gives no synonym; classic background: caudal) | Towards the back |
| **Lateral** | | At the side |
| **Medial** | | In the middle |

> **Trap.** Rostral means **anterior** (towards the front), not "towards the top"; dorsal means **superior** (towards the top). Dorsal/ventral is the top/bottom pair, rostral/posterior the front/back pair, lateral/medial the side/middle pair. So "dorsolateral prefrontal cortex" is the upper, outer part of the front of the frontal lobe, and "ventrolateral" is the lower, outer part.

### Way 2: Brodmann areas

The German neurologist **Korbinian Brodmann (1868 to 1918)** mapped the cortex according to differences in how **cell types are distributed across the cortical layers** (classic background: this is called cytoarchitecture). He identified **52 areas** (Figure 1.4 shows them on the lateral and medial surfaces). Researchers refer to them by number: **BA17** simply means Brodmann Area 17.

### Way 3: function

Areas can also be named by what they do. **BA17** is usually called **primary visual cortex**, because it is strongly associated with the **early processing of visual stimuli**. The same patch of cortex can therefore be described three ways: by lobe (occipital), by Brodmann number (BA17) or by function (primary visual cortex).

### Brain network organisation

**Bullmore and Sporns (2012)** argued that two principles, which pull in opposite directions, might shape how the brain is wired (Figure 1.5):

1. **Cost control.** Energy and space costs would be lowest if the brain had only a **limited number of short-distance** connections.
2. **Efficiency.** The ability to **integrate information** across the brain would be greatest with **very many connections, many of them long-distance**.

You cannot have both high efficiency and low cost. Suppose the brain were built purely for efficiency, with every neuron connected to every other: the brain would have to be about **12½ miles wide** (**Ward, 2010**). In reality neurons mostly connect with **nearby** neurons, and **no neuron connects to more than about 10,000** others. The human brain therefore sits between the two extremes, achieving a **near-optimal trade-off**: **moderate efficiency at moderate cost**. Figure 1.5's left panel shows a cheap but inefficient network, its right panel a costly but efficient one, and its middle panel the actual human brain.

Within this network there are two kinds of structure worth naming:

- **Modules**: small areas of **tightly clustered** connections.
- **Hubs**: regions with **large numbers of connections** to other regions. Hubs include areas such as the **anterior cingulate**, which are linked to high-level cognition and to **consciousness** (Chapter 16).

Does network organisation explain individual differences in ability? **Van den Heuvel et al. (2009)** found that IQ was **not** correlated with the **total number** of connections in the brain network. It was strongly associated with the **global efficiency** of functional brain networks, with correlations of **+0.75** in **medial prefrontal cortex (BA9/10)** and **+0.72** in **inferior parietal regions (BA39/40)**. How the brain is wired, not how much wiring it has, matters for the efficiency of cognition.

### Techniques for studying the brain: introduction

Modern techniques allow us, at least in principle, to work out **where** and **when** particular cognitive processes occur in the brain. That in turn tells us the **order** in which areas become active during a task, and whether two tasks use the same areas in the same way.

The book introduces seven main techniques before examining each in detail: **single-unit recording** (a micro-electrode records single neurons), **event-related potentials** (averaged scalp electrical activity), **positron emission tomography** (detection of positrons from a radioactive tracer), **functional magnetic resonance imaging** (blood oxygenation imaged by an MRI scanner), **event-related fMRI** (the experiment is divided into discrete events in time so that the brain response to each kind of event can be analysed separately; Huettel, 2012), **magneto-encephalography** (the magnetic fields generated by electrical brain activity) and **transcranial magnetic stimulation** (brief magnetic pulses that generally, but not always, inhibit processing in the area beneath the coil). Event-related fMRI is very informative and has become markedly more popular. Delivering TMS pulses several times in rapid succession is **repetitive TMS (rTMS)**, which is very widely used.

TMS is often said to create a very brief "**lesion**" (a lesion being a structural alteration caused by brain damage), and it has been jokingly compared to hitting someone's brain with a hammer. The book immediately qualifies this: the effects are more complex. What actually happens is **interference**, because the targeted area is engaged both in the task and in the activity produced by the stimulation.

### Spatial and temporal resolution

Which technique is best? There is no single answer: each has strengths and limitations, so researchers match the technique to the question. The most basic way the techniques differ is in:

- **spatial resolution**: how precisely they locate the brain areas active during a task (**where**);
- **temporal resolution**: how precisely they track the time course of that activity (**when**).

Figure 1.6 (from **Ward, 2006**, adapted from **Churchland & Sejnowski, 1991**) plots the techniques on these two dimensions. High resolution on both is valuable when a detailed account of brain function is needed. The book adds a point students overlook: **low** temporal resolution can be **more useful** when you want a general overview of brain activity across a **whole task**.

The numbers the book gives, gathered in one place (each is explained in the detailed sections below):

| Technique | What it measures | Spatial resolution | Temporal resolution | Invasive? | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Single-unit recording** | Activity of single neurons | Finest of all (single neuron) | Very fine | **Yes**, so rarely used in humans | Samples a tiny fraction of neurons |
| **ERPs (from EEG)** | Averaged scalp electrical activity | **Poor** | **Excellent** (within a few ms) | No | Skull and tissue distort fields; simple tasks only |
| **PET** | Positrons from radioactive tracer (labelled water) | Reasonable: **5 to 10 mm** | **Very poor**: activity summed over **30 to 60 s** | Mildly (tracer injection; tiny radioactivity) | Largely replaced by fMRI |
| **fMRI** | **BOLD** signal (deoxyhaemoglobin) | Very good: about **1 mm** | About **2 to 3 s** | No | Indirect; noisy; movement-sensitive |
| **MEG** | Magnetic fields via **SQUID** | Often very good | **Excellent** (millisecond level) | No | Extremely expensive |
| **TMS / rTMS** | Disrupts an area (about **1 cubic cm**) with a pulse lasting **under 1 ms** | Area of about 1 cm³ | Can be timed precisely (e.g., 80 to 100 ms after a stimulus) | No | Only areas under the skull, not under muscle; rare seizures |

> **Trap.** The resolution pairings examiners test: **ERP and MEG** have **excellent temporal** resolution (milliseconds); **fMRI** has the best **spatial** resolution of the non-invasive imaging methods (about 1 mm) but only seconds-level timing; **PET** is worst on time (30 to 60 s) and only reasonable on space (5 to 10 mm); **ERP** is poor on space. "fMRI has better temporal resolution than ERP" is false; "fMRI has better spatial **and** temporal resolution than PET" is true. Other textbooks sometimes quote slightly different values; when the options match the book's numbers, use these.

### Single-unit recording in detail

Single-unit recording (also called single-cell recording) inserts a micro-electrode about **one 10,000th of a millimetre** in diameter into the brain to record from **individual neurons**. It is extremely sensitive, picking up electrical charges as small as **one-millionth of a volt**, and it is the **most fine-grained** technique available (Chapter 2 uses it extensively). It is **invasive**, so it is rarely used with humans. A notable exception is **Quiroga et al. (2005)**, who recorded from **epileptic patients** who already had electrodes implanted to locate the focus of their seizures (Chapter 3). One neuron in the **medial temporal lobe** responded strongly to pictures of the actress **Jennifer Aniston** but not to pictures of other famous people.

The finding must be interpreted carefully. Only a **tiny fraction** of medial temporal lobe neurons was sampled, so it is extremely unlikely that this was the **only** neuron responding to Jennifer Aniston. The result returns in 1.5 as evidence that some information may be stored **locally** rather than in a fully distributed way.

### Event-related potentials in detail

**Electroencephalography (EEG)** records the brain's electrical potentials through a series of **scalp electrodes**. Very small changes in electrical activity are picked up and displayed. The problem is that **spontaneous background activity** swamps the much smaller response to any one stimulus.

The solution is averaging. The same stimulus (or very similar stimuli) is presented **many times**. The stretch of EEG following each presentation is cut out and aligned to the moment of **stimulus onset**, and the segments are **averaged** into a single waveform. Background activity, which is not time-locked to the stimulus, tends to cancel out, while the stimulus-related response, which recurs at the same latency every time, survives. The result is the **event-related potential (ERP)**.

What ERPs offer:

- **Excellent temporal resolution**: they can often show when a process happened to **within a few milliseconds**.
- A waveform made of **positive (P)** and **negative (N)** peaks, each named for its latency after the stimulus. **N400** is a negative wave peaking at about **400 ms** (Chapter 10 uses it in sentence comprehension).
- A **continuous** record of brain activity over the trial, whereas a behavioural measure such as reaction time gives only **one** time value per trial.

What ERPs cannot do:

- Locate activity precisely, partly because the **skull and brain tissue distort** the brain's electrical fields.
- Deal well with complex stimuli: they are mainly useful with **simple stimuli** and tasks involving **basic processes triggered by the stimuli** (e.g., target detection).
- Study most **complex cognition**, such as **problem solving**.

> **Trap.** An ERP is not raw EEG. It is EEG **averaged across many time-locked trials** of the same stimulus. The "N" or "P" gives the polarity and the number gives the approximate latency in milliseconds, so N400 is a negative deflection around 400 ms, not the 400th component or a wave of 400 microvolts.

### Positron emission tomography in detail

PET detects **positrons**, atomic particles emitted by some radioactive substances. The procedure:

1. **Radioactively labelled water** (the **tracer**) is injected and quickly collects in the brain's blood vessels.
2. When part of the cortex becomes active, the labelled water moves there rapidly.
3. A scanner measures the positrons emitted from the radioactive water and builds a picture of activity levels across brain regions.

Only **tiny amounts** of radioactivity are involved. Spatial resolution is reasonable (any active area can be located to within **5 to 10 mm**), but temporal resolution is very poor: a PET scan reflects the **total activity in a region over 30 to 60 seconds**.

### Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI and fMRI) in detail

**Structural MRI.** An MRI scanner contains a very large magnet, weighing **up to 11 tons**. Its strong magnetic field **aligns protons** (subatomic particles) in the brain. A brief **radio-frequency pulse** then knocks the aligned protons into spinning; as they return to their original orientation they give off a small amount of energy, and the regions emitting most energy appear brightest on the scan. Images can be taken from many angles, but MRI shows only **structure**, not function.

**Functional MRI.** The same physics can be used to measure function:

1. When neurons consume oxygen, **oxyhaemoglobin** is converted to **deoxyhaemoglobin**.
2. Deoxyhaemoglobin **distorts the local magnetic field**.
3. fMRI detects this distortion, which gives a measure of the **concentration of deoxyhaemoglobin** in the blood.

Technically, what fMRI measures is the **BOLD** signal (**blood oxygen level-dependent contrast**). Because the BOLD change produced by increased neural activity takes time to develop, fMRI's temporal resolution is about **2 or 3 seconds**; its spatial resolution is very good, about **1 mm**. Because fMRI beats PET on both resolutions, it has **largely replaced PET** in neuroimaging research.

> **Trap.** fMRI measures neither neural firing nor glucose use directly. It measures the **BOLD signal**, which depends on the **concentration of deoxyhaemoglobin** distorting the local magnetic field. That is why the book repeatedly calls it an **indirect** measure of neural activity. Structural MRI shows anatomy only; "MRI shows which areas are active during a task" is false unless it says **functional** MRI.

### Event-related fMRI: the subsequent memory effect

Suppose we want to know why participants remember some items and forget others. **Event-related fMRI** lets us sort trials after the event and compare brain activity for each kind. **Wagner et al. (1998)** recorded fMRI while participants learned a list of words. Brain activity **at learning** was greater for words that were **later recognised** than for words later forgotten. The implication is that forgotten words had been processed **less thoroughly** at encoding. (The logic, sorting encoding trials by their later fate, is what makes this an efMRI design rather than a blocked design.)

### Limitations of fMRI

The book lists five:

1. It is an **indirect** measure of neural activity.
2. The BOLD signal is **distorted in some regions**, for example near the **sinuses** and near the **oral cavity**.
3. The scanner is **noisy**, which creates problems for studies with **auditory** stimuli.
4. Some people, especially those with **claustrophobia**, find being enclosed in the scanner uncomfortable. **Cooke et al. (2007)** found that **43%** of participants in an fMRI study were at least a bit upset by it, and **33%** reported side effects such as **headaches**.
5. There are **constraints on stimuli** that can be presented to someone lying in a scanner, and on the **responses** they can make, because even small movements distort the BOLD signal.

### Magneto-encephalography in detail

The detector in MEG is a **superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID)**, which picks up the tiny magnetic fields that accompany the brain's electrical activity. The engineering is hard because those fields are **minute** relative to the earth's own magnetic field. MEG nevertheless measures brain activity very accurately, partly because the **skull is virtually transparent to magnetic fields** (unlike the electrical fields that ERPs depend on, which the skull distorts).

MEG has **excellent temporal resolution** (millisecond level) and often **very good spatial resolution** too. Its drawbacks are that it is **extremely expensive** and that some people find it uncomfortable: **Cooke et al. (2007)** found **35%** of participants found MEG "a bit upsetting", and **35%** reported side effects such as **muscle aches or headaches**.

### In the real world: can cognitive neuroscientists read our brains or minds?

There is growing evidence that researchers can work out what someone is looking at from their brain activity alone (**Tong & Pratte, 2012**).

- **Haxby et al. (2001)** showed participants pictures from **eight categories** (such as cats, faces and houses) while recording fMRI. Computer analysis of the activity patterns predicted the category being viewed on **96%** of trials.
- **Kay et al. (2008)** pointed out two weaknesses of earlier "brain reading" studies: the stimuli were much simpler than everyday scenes, and prediction was made easier by matching test-trial activity to activity recorded when the **same** objects or categories had been shown before. They avoided both by showing **two participants 120 previously unseen natural images** of moderate complexity. fMRI data identified the image being viewed on **92%** of trials for one participant and **72%** for the other, against a **chance level of 0.8%**.

Such studies show that far more information can be extracted from brain activity than was once thought. But they are not literally **mind reading**. Some aspects of the brain's response to a visual stimulus are relevant to the person's **perceptual experience** and others probably are not (**Vilarroya, 2013**). **Hung et al. (2005)** supported this: computer analysis of brain activity in **macaques** correctly classified stimuli that the **macaques themselves could not tell apart**. The decoder was reading information in the brain that never reached the animal's perception.

### Transcranial magnetic stimulation in detail

In TMS a coil, often shaped like a **figure of eight**, is held close to the participant's head. A very brief (**under 1 ms**) but large pulse of current is passed through it, creating a short-lived magnetic field. That field induces **electrical stimulation** in the brain beneath, which generally (but not always) **inhibits processing** in the affected area, typically about **1 cubic centimetre** of tissue. In practice several pulses are given in a short period, which is **rTMS**. Most research uses rTMS, and the book uses "TMS" as the general term for both.

**Choosing the control condition.** What should TMS performance be compared with? The obvious comparison, task performance with versus without TMS, is flawed. TMS makes a **loud noise** and causes **muscle twitching** at the side of the forehead, and either could impair performance on its own. A better control is to apply TMS to a **non-critical** brain area that is irrelevant to the task. The prediction is then specific: performance should be worse when TMS hits the **critical** area than when it hits the non-critical one, even though the noise and twitching are the same in both.

> **Trap.** The preferred TMS control is **stimulation of a non-critical (task-irrelevant) area**, not "no stimulation", because the click and the muscle twitch are confounds that the no-TMS comparison fails to equate. Other sources also describe sham stimulation as a control; when the options include a non-critical site, that is the 7th edition's answer.

**Why TMS is useful: necessity.** Because TMS usually creates a "temporary lesion", it lets us test the **role** of a given area. If TMS to an area **impairs** performance, that area is **necessary** for the task. If TMS has **no effect**, the area is **not necessary**. This supports **stronger causal statements** than most other techniques allow.

**Why TMS is useful: timing.** TMS can also show **when** an area is most involved. **Cracco et al. (1999)** asked participants to detect letters. Performance was most impaired when TMS was applied to **occipital cortex 80 to 100 ms** after the letter appeared, rather than earlier or later. By varying the delay between stimulus and pulse, TMS maps out the window in which an area does its work.

#### Evaluation of TMS

**Strengths.**

- In principle, the greatest advantage of TMS over neuroimaging is that it increases confidence that an area is **necessary** for a task. TMS **manipulates** whether a region is available for the task; neuroimaging only establishes **associations or correlations** between activation and performance.
- Compared with research on patients with real lesions, TMS has two advantages. The **same person** can be tested with and without the "lesion", which is rarely possible with patients. And the **experimenter controls** which area is affected, which is impossible with naturally occurring damage.

**Limitations.** The book lists four:

1. **Its effects are complex and not fully understood.** **Allen et al. (2007)** applied rTMS to the early visual cortex of **cats** that were not doing any task: spontaneous brain activity **increased** for up to **one minute**. Yet activity produced by viewing **gratings** was **reduced by up to 60%**. TMS effects on performance are usually **negative** but sometimes **positive**. Why might TMS help? Suppose area x normally **inhibits** area y. TMS to x reduces that inhibition and so may **enhance** the functioning of y. More generally, brain function is so complex that variable effects are to be expected.
2. **It is hard to know exactly which areas are affected**, and some may be **distant** from the stimulation point. Combining TMS with neuroimaging can clarify what TMS actually does to brain activity (**Ziemann, 2011**).
3. **It reaches only areas lying beneath the skull**, not areas covered by **overlying muscle**, which limits its usefulness.
4. **Safety.** TMS has very occasionally caused **seizures**, despite strict safety rules.

> **Trap.** "TMS creates a virtual lesion" is the older textbook slogan, and it is still roughly how the 7th edition introduces the method. The 7th edition's more accurate statement is that TMS causes **interference**: the target area is engaged both in the task and in the activity the pulse creates. It also stresses that TMS can occasionally **improve** performance (release from inhibition) and can affect **distant** areas. An option saying TMS "always impairs" or "affects only the area under the coil" is wrong.

### Overall strengths of cognitive neuroscience

The book does not claim the brain techniques "answer all our prayers", but it credits cognitive neuroscience with four major strengths.

#### Strength 1: resolving theoretical controversies

Cognitive neuroscience has increasingly settled disputes that purely behavioural studies could not (**White & Poldrack, 2013**). Two examples.

**Speech perception (Chapter 9).** Degraded speech is much more intelligible when the words are predictable. The open question was **when** knowledge of what is being said exerts its influence. Either it acts **early**, directly changing basic auditory processing, or it acts **late**, only after basic auditory processing is finished; theorists disagree (**Mattys et al., 2012**). **Wild et al. (2012)** had listeners hear degraded speech accompanied by visual stimuli that either **matched** or did not match the speech. Activity in **primary auditory cortex**, which handles early auditory processing, was **greater** when the visual input matched. Knowledge of the content therefore reached down to **basic auditory processes**: the early-influence account wins.

**Visual imagery (Chapter 3).** Does imagery use the same processes as perception? Behavioural evidence has mostly been inconclusive. Brain imaging shows that about **two-thirds** of the areas activated during visual perception are also activated during visual imagery (**Kosslyn, 2005**). A **meta-analysis** (a statistical combination of the findings from many studies) by **Kosslyn and Thompson (2003)** found that even areas involved in the **early stages** of visual perception are often active during imagery.

So imagery and perception overlap heavily. But neuroimaging also revealed a difference. **R.J. Lee et al. (2012)** had participants view or imagine common objects and then tried to identify which object was involved from the pattern of activation. For **perceived** objects, identification was much better using **early** visual areas than later ones; for **imagined** objects, the reverse held. Low-level visual processes contribute **more to perception than to imagery**.

#### Strength 2: matching the complexity of the system

Understanding the cognitive system and the brain organisation behind it is a huge challenge. Bullmore and Sporns (2012), as described above, see the brain as many tight clusters (modules) linked by long-distance connections. Neuroimaging data are so rich that cognitive neuroscientists can, at least in principle, build models that capture this complexity. Cognitive neuropsychology, by contrast, seems **less flexible** and more committed to **modular** organisation.

#### Strength 3: self-correction, and the shift from specialisation to integration

Because neuroimaging data are so rich, if one way of analysing them proves limited, they can be **reanalysed** within another framework. Face processing is the book's example. It used to be assumed that most face processing happens in the **fusiform face area**, but this was a considerable oversimplification (**Weiner & Grill-Spector, 2012**; Chapter 3). An account in which face processing depends on a **network** of regions fits better (**Atkinson & Adolphs, 2011**). In this sense cognitive neuroscience can be **self-correcting**.

More broadly, cognitive neuroscientists now place **less weight** than they used to on **functional specialisation**, the idea that each brain region is specialised for a different function (such as colour or face processing). They accept that there is substantial **integration and coordination** across the brain. **Functional integration** can be studied by **correlating activity across regions**: if a network of areas supports a process, activity in all of them should rise and fall together (positively correlated) when that process occurs. There is strong evidence for such integration in **conscious perception**, which seems to depend on coordinated activity across several regions (Chapter 16).

The field has also identified a growing number of large-scale **brain networks**. **Corbetta and Shulman (2002**; Chapter 5) used neuroscience findings to identify **two attention networks**, one for **goal-directed** attention and one for **stimulus-driven** attention (Chapter 5 calls them the dorsal and ventral attention networks). Other networks are reviewed by **Anderson et al. (2013)**.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition describes a **shift away from functional specialisation towards functional integration**. Older keys, and older editions' framing, treat functional specialisation as the core assumption of cognitive neuroscience. If a question asks what modern cognitive neuroscience "attaches less importance to", the answer is functional specialisation; what it now emphasises is integration and networks, measured by correlated activity across regions.

#### Strength 4: power in combination with other approaches

Cognitive neuroscience is often most useful when combined with other approaches. The book's example is **recognition memory**, thought to rest on two processes (Chapter 6):

- **Recollection**, which involves **conscious retrieval of contextual information**;
- **Familiarity**, which does not.

The theory holds that recollection depends on the **hippocampal** system and familiarity on the nearby **perirhinal** system. Cognitive neuropsychology supplies a **double dissociation**: patients with **hippocampal** damage show impaired recollection with intact familiarity, while patients with **perirhinal** damage show impaired familiarity with intact recollection. Neuroimaging strengthens the case: recollection is linked to more activation in the **hippocampus** than the perirhinal cortex, and familiarity to the opposite (**Diana et al., 2007**). Two independent methods converge on one theory.

### General limitations of cognitive neuroscience

The book lists **eight** general limitations. They are the backbone of any question about how to interpret brain-imaging data.

#### Limitation 1: over-interpretation, blobology and reverse inference

Cognitive neuroscientists often over-interpret their data by assuming **one-to-one links** between cognitive processes and brain areas (**Brown, 2012**). A small patch of activation (a "blob") gets labelled the "love area" or the "religion area". This has been called, unflatteringly, **blobology**.

Blobology is in decline, but there is still too much reliance on **reverse inference**: arguing **backwards** from activation in a region to the presence of a particular cognitive process. The book's example is the **amygdala** (part of the limbic system). People exposed to threat-related information typically show amygdala activation (**Sander, 2009**), which led many researchers to conclude that the amygdala is the centre of a **fear system**. The problem: the processing of **most emotions** is associated with amygdala activation (**Lindquist et al., 2012**; Figure 1.7 shows the proportion of studies on the experience and perception of anger, disgust, fear, happiness and sadness that report amygdala activity). If the amygdala responds to nearly every emotion, its activation cannot tell you that a person is specifically afraid.

The general lesson is that **most brain regions take part in several different processes**, so activation in any one area is not very informative on its own (Brown, 2012). **Yarkoni et al. (2011)** showed this across **3,489 studies**: some areas, including the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, **anterior cingulate cortex** and **anterior insula**, were activated in **20%** of all studies.

> **Trap.** **Forward** reasoning goes from a process (a fear task) to the activation it produces. **Reverse inference** goes from an activation (the amygdala lit up) back to a process ("so the person felt fear"). Reverse inference is weak whenever the region is **non-selective**, which is most regions. The amygdala-and-fear example is the 7th edition's illustration; Lindquist et al. (2012) is the evidence that undermines it.

#### Limitation 2: the gap between psychological concepts and brain activity

It is very hard to bridge psychological concepts and patterns of brain activation. **Harley (2012)** argued we may never find brain patterns that correspond closely to processes such as "attention" or "planning", because, as he put it, "Our language and thought may not divide up in the way in which the brain implements these processes" (Harley, 2012, p. 1372).

#### Limitation 3: underpowered studies

Most neuroimaging studies are **underpowered**, typically testing **20 participants or fewer**. **Yarkoni et al. (2010)** warned that such studies detect only a fraction of the true effects, which creates a misleading impression that activation is "selective" (only a few areas seem to respond because the rest were missed). One remedy is to **combine findings across studies**. Yarkoni et al. (2011) pooled **3,489 studies** containing **100,953 activation points**. This greatly increased the chance of finding most true effects, reduced the percentage of false positives, and made it much easier to identify which processes were associated with activation in a given area.

#### Limitation 4: false positives

A **false positive** here means mistaking random activity in an area for task-related activation. False positives are common and may occur as often as **15%** of the time (**Yarkoni et al., 2010**). They arise because imaging studies generate **huge amounts of data** and some researchers do not **correct their statistical thresholds** (the p values needed for significance) to allow for the number of tests.

**Bennett et al. (2009)** made the point memorably. Their "participant" was shown pictures of people in social situations and asked to determine the emotions displayed. With uncorrected thresholds, there was significant "activation" (Figure 1.8). The participant was a **dead salmon**, so the finding was certainly a false positive.

> **Trap.** The dead salmon study (Bennett et al., 2009) illustrates **false positives from failing to correct for multiple comparisons**. It is not about underpowering, reverse inference or ecological validity. Keep the two Yarkoni papers apart too: **Yarkoni et al. (2010)** gives the **underpowering** warning and the **15%** false-positive figure; **Yarkoni et al. (2011)** is the **3,489-study, 100,953-point** meta-analysis.

#### Limitation 5: correlation, not causation

Most brain-imaging techniques reveal only **associations** between activation and behaviour. Reasoning tasks are associated with prefrontal activation, but that does not show the prefrontal cortex is **essential** for reasoning. The activation might instead reflect participants **monitoring their own performance** unnecessarily, or attending to **non-task stimuli**.

**TMS offers a partial solution**: if TMS to an area disrupts performance, that area is necessary. But because TMS has complex effects, even this needs cautious interpretation.

#### Limitation 6: intrinsic brain activity

Many cognitive neuroscientists assume that most brain activity is driven by the **environment or the task**. If so, most activity should be a response to task demands. It is not. The extra activity when someone performs a cognitive task typically adds **less than 5%** to **resting** brain activity, probably far less than the energy the brain spends on **intrinsic activity** in unstimulating environments.

Why is the resting brain so busy? Partly because people use cognitive resources to **predict future changes** in the environment (**Raichle, 2010**). But activity patterns are similar across very different states of consciousness, including **coma, anaesthesia and slow-wave sleep**, which suggests that most intrinsic activity reflects **basic brain functioning**. One consequence: because the baseline is already so active, performing a task is often associated with **decreased** activity in some regions rather than the expected increase.

> **Trap.** Task-related activity adds **less than 5%** to resting activity. The intrinsic-activity argument rests on similar activity patterns in **coma, anaesthesia and slow-wave sleep** (not REM sleep), and on Raichle (2010) for the prediction account. Task-induced **decreases** in activation are a real, expected consequence, not an artefact.

#### Limitation 7: ecological validity and paradigm specificity

Cognitive neuroscience shares cognitive psychology's problems of **ecological validity** and **paradigm specificity**, and the ecological validity problem may be **worse**. In fMRI, the most used technique, participants lie on their backs in cramped, noisy conditions and can barely move, which is not much like everyday life. **Gutchess and Park (2006)** compared long-term **recognition memory** in an MRI scanner with memory in an ordinary laboratory: memory was **significantly worse in the scanner**, presumably because it was more distracting or anxiety-provoking.

#### Limitation 8: the neuroimaging illusion

The **neuroimaging illusion** is the tendency to overvalue what pictures of brain activity add to our understanding (Chapter 14 returns to it). **Keehner et al. (2011)** gave people neuroscience articles accompanied by brain images: the more **three-dimensional** the images looked, the more **positively** the articles were judged. A concrete case: on **28 August 2007** *The Guardian* ran a headline claiming that brain scans had pinpointed how "chocoholics" are hooked. The study behind it (**Rolls & McCabe, 2007**) had found that the sight of chocolate produced more activation in brain **reward** centres in chocolate cravers than in non-cravers. As **Beck (2010)** pointed out, this tells us little more than that people who crave chocolate find chocolate rewarding.

| 2 | Hard to map psychological concepts onto brain activity | Harley (2012) |
| 3 | **Underpowered** studies (20 or fewer participants) | Yarkoni et al. (2010); remedy Yarkoni et al. (2011) |
| 4 | **False positives** (up to 15%) from uncorrected thresholds | Yarkoni et al. (2010); Bennett et al. (2009), dead salmon |
| 5 | **Correlational**, not causal; TMS a partial fix | (prefrontal cortex and reasoning example) |
| 6 | **Intrinsic activity**: tasks add under 5% | Raichle (2010) |
| 7 | **Ecological validity** and paradigm specificity, worse in scanners | Gutchess & Park (2006) |
| 8 | **Neuroimaging illusion** | Keehner et al. (2011); Rolls & McCabe (2007); Beck (2010) |

> **Trap.** The 7th edition gives no numbered list of "ways of interpreting imaging data"; it gives these **eight general limitations**, plus functional specialisation versus integration. For any older-pattern question on interpreting neuroimaging, reach for reverse inference, correlation versus causation (TMS as the fix), false positives and multiple comparisons, underpowering, intrinsic activity, and specialisation versus integration.

## 1.5 Computational Cognitive Science

### Computational modelling versus artificial intelligence

The book begins by separating two activities that are often confused.

- **Computational modelling** means programming computers to **simulate or mimic** human cognitive processes. The program is a theory of how humans do the task.
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** means building computer systems that produce **intelligent outcomes**, possibly by methods that bear little resemblance to human cognition.

The book's example of AI is **Deep Blue**, the chess program that beat the world champion **Garry Kasparov** on **11 May 1997**. Deep Blue examined up to **200 million positions per second**, vastly more than any human player (Chapter 12 describes how human chess experts instead rely on pattern recognition and very selective search). Deep Blue is intelligent in outcome but not a model of human chess thinking.

> **Trap.** Computational modelling aims to reproduce **how** humans do a task; AI aims only to get an intelligent **result**. Deep Blue (Kasparov, 11 May 1997, 200 million positions per second) is the book's AI example precisely because it does **not** work like a human. Computational cognitive science is the modelling enterprise, not AI.

### Why build computational models?

A good computational model shows how a theory can be **specified precisely** and lets us **predict behaviour in new situations**. Early **mathematical** models could predict but often lacked any **explanation**. The book's illustration: knowing that someone has had **three traffic violations** predicts well that they are a bad car-insurance risk, but says nothing about **why**. The computational models of cognitive science, by contrast, often provide both an explanatory and a predictive basis for a phenomenon (**Costello & Keane, 2000**).

Models also solve the vagueness problem from 1.2. Many experimental psychologists, past and present, state their theories in loose verbal form, which makes it hard to tell whether evidence fits. **Murphy (2011)** said verbal theories give theorists undesirable "wiggle room", whereas a computational model forces the researcher to be **explicit** about the theory. Turning a theory into a working program is a good check for **hidden assumptions** and **imprecise terms**, and it often reveals predictions the theorist had not realised the theory made.

### Relating program performance to human performance

There is a subtle issue in comparing a program with people (**Costello & Keane, 2000**). It is rarely meaningful to compare a program's **absolute speed** on a simulated task with human **reaction times**, because program speed depends on psychologically irrelevant factors: the same program runs faster on a more powerful computer. What should match is the **pattern**. Differences in the program's operation time **across different materials** should correlate closely with differences in participants' reaction times to the **same** materials.

> **Trap.** A model is not supported by running "as fast as humans". It is supported when the **relative** differences in its processing time across conditions or items track the **relative** differences in human reaction times.

### Types of models and cognitive architectures

Most computational models address **fairly specific** aspects of cognition. Some of the most successful model **reading words and non-words aloud** (**Plaut et al., 1996**; **Coltheart et al., 2001**; **Perry et al., 2007**; Chapter 9).

Others are far more ambitious. These are **cognitive architectures**: comprehensive frameworks for understanding cognition in the form of computer programs. **Sun (2007)** describes them as models that are domain-generic (covering many domains) and have a wide range of applicability. **Byrne (2012)** evaluated several major architectures, including **ACT-R** (J.R. Anderson et al., 2004), described below.

There are a great many computational models, but a large number can be grouped as **connectionist models**, which the book focuses on, and many others are **production systems**, discussed more briefly. The emphasis is on what these approaches achieve rather than the fine detail of how.

### Connectionism

**Connectionist models** are networks of simple, interconnected units that **learn**. In such networks, each item of knowledge is represented as a **pattern of activation spread across many units**, not stored in one place. Connectionist networks often, but not always, have these characteristics (Figure 1.9):

1. The network consists of **elementary, neuron-like units** (nodes), each linked to **many** other units.
2. Units influence each other by **excitation or inhibition**.
3. A unit usually takes the **weighted sum** of all its input links and sends a single output to another unit if that sum **exceeds a threshold**.
4. The network as a whole is characterised by **three** things: the properties of its units, the way they are connected, and the **rules used to change connection strengths**.
5. Networks can have **layers**: a layer of input units, intermediate layers of **hidden units**, and a layer of output units.
6. A concept can be represented in a **distributed** way, as an activation pattern across the network.
7. The **same network can store several patterns** without disruption, provided they are sufficiently distinct.
8. An important learning rule is **backward propagation of errors (BackProp)**.

**No explicit rules.** Connectionist networks model performance **without** explicit rules. They store patterns of activation that associate inputs with outputs. A typical network has an **input layer**, which encodes a stimulus as a pattern of activation, and an **output layer**, which produces a response as a pattern of activation. Once the network has learned to give a particular output for a particular input, its behaviour **looks rule-based**, even though no rule is written anywhere in it.

Figure 1.9 (from **Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986**) shows this architecture as three tiers: input units at the bottom, a layer of internal representation (hidden) units in the middle, and output units at the top, with connections running upwards so that a given input pattern generates the appropriate output pattern.

#### Back-propagation, step by step

**Back-propagation** is a learning mechanism that lets a network learn to associate an input pattern with an output pattern by **comparing its actual responses with the correct ones**.

1. The network starts with **random weights** on the links between units.
2. Early in learning, when an input pattern is presented, the output units usually produce the **wrong** pattern.
3. BackProp compares this imperfect output with the **known correct response** and notes the **errors**.
4. It then propagates this error information **backwards** through the network, **adjusting the weights** between units so that the output moves closer to the required pattern.
5. The cycle repeats until the network produces the required pattern.

The network therefore learns the right behaviour **without being explicitly programmed** with it. The catch: research in cognitive neuroscience has found **little or no evidence** that back-propagation happens in the human brain (**Mayor et al., 2014**).

> **Trap.** Back-propagation is a **learning rule** (supervised, error-driven: it needs the correct answer to compare against), not a processing stage, and it is the connectionist rule the book names. Its main weakness in the 7th edition is **biological implausibility**: little or no evidence for it in the brain (Mayor et al., 2014).

#### Distributed versus localist representation

Several connectionist models, such as the **parallel distributed processing (PDP)** approach of **Rumelhart et al. (1986)**, assume knowledge is stored in a **distributed** way across the brain. This raises problems:

- **The two-word problem.** Suppose two words are encoded at once. Many units become active, and it becomes hard or impossible to tell which units belong to which word (**Bowers, 2002**).
- **Evidence for local storage.** Much information seems to be stored in **particular locations** rather than distributed (**Bowers, 2009**, reviews this). The Jennifer Aniston neuron of **Quiroga et al. (2005)**, which responded strongly to her picture but not to other famous people, is the book's example.

Other connectionist models assume **local representation** of knowledge. **Localist connectionist models** include:

| Model | Domain | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Coltheart et al. (2001)** reading model | Reading aloud | 9 |
| **TRACE** (McClelland & Elman, 1986) | Spoken word recognition | 9 |
| **Dell (1986)** | Speech production | 11 |
| **Levelt et al. (1999)** | Speech production | 11 |

The book's conclusion is that **some knowledge is probably represented locally and some in a distributed way** (Chapter 7).

> **Trap.** "Connectionist" does not automatically mean "distributed". PDP models (Rumelhart et al., 1986) are distributed; TRACE, Dell (1986), Levelt et al. (1999) and Coltheart et al.'s (2001) reading model are **localist** connectionist models. And the Jennifer Aniston neuron is evidence **for local** storage and **against** fully distributed storage.

### Production systems

**Production systems** consist of very large numbers of "**If ... then**" **production rules** together with a **working memory**. A production rule is a condition-action rule: the action is carried out whenever the condition is present. Working memory is the system holding the information currently being processed.

The book's everyday illustration is a pedestrian-crossing rule: if the green walking figure is showing, then cross. When information that the signal is green reaches **working memory**, it matches the **IF-part** of the rule stored in **long-term memory**, and this triggers the **THEN-part** (cross the road).

The general characteristics of production systems:

1. **numerous "If ... then" rules**;
2. a **working memory** containing information;
3. a system that operates by **matching** the contents of working memory against the IF-parts of rules and **executing** the THEN-parts;
4. a **conflict-resolution strategy** that selects one rule when working memory matches the IF-parts of **two or more** rules.

Many kinds of cognition can be written as sets of such rules. Chess knowledge, for instance, could include a rule that moves the Queen to safety whenever she comes under attack. On this view a person's basic knowledge can be regarded as a large collection of productions.

It was **Newell and Simon (1972)** who first showed how useful production-system models could be, using their **General Problem Solver** to pin down the processes of problem solving (Chapter 12). The approach reaches well beyond problem solving, as ACT-R shows.

> **Trap.** The two big model families contrast sharply. **Connectionist** models: no explicit rules, distributed (or local) activation patterns, learning by adjusting weights (BackProp). **Production systems**: explicit "If ... then" rules, a working memory, matching, and conflict resolution when several rules apply. "Conflict resolution" belongs to production systems; "hidden units" and "weights" belong to connectionism.

### ACT-R (Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational)

**John Anderson** has produced several versions of ACT-R. The version described by **J.R. Anderson et al. (2004)** assumes the cognitive system consists of several **modules** (relatively independent subsystems). What makes it distinctive is that it combines computational cognitive science with cognitive neuroscience by assigning each module to a **brain region** (Figure 1.10, reprinted from Anderson et al., 2008). Four modules are especially important:

| Module | Function | Proposed brain location |
|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval** | Maintains the **retrieval cues** needed to access information | **Inferior ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** |
| **Imaginal** | **Transforms problem representations** to help problem solving | **Posterior parietal cortex** |
| **Goal** | Keeps track of the person's **intentions** and **controls** information processing | **Anterior cingulate cortex** |
| **Procedural** | Uses **production (If ... then) rules** to decide what action to take next | **Head of the caudate nucleus**, within the **basal ganglia** |

Each module has a **buffer** holding a small amount of important information. How is the information from the separate buffers brought together? A **central production system** detects patterns across the buffers and takes coordinated action (J.R. Anderson et al., 2004). If several productions could be triggered by what is in the buffers, one is chosen according to the **value or gain** of its outcome together with the **time or cost** of achieving that outcome.

**Evaluation of ACT-R.** It is an impressive attempt to provide a theoretical framework covering information processing across many tasks, and an ambitious attempt to integrate computational cognitive science with cognitive neuroscience. Its limitations:

1. A theory this wide-ranging is very **hard to test adequately**.
2. It arguably **de-emphasises** areas of prefrontal cortex, such as the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, that are generally thought crucial for cognition.
3. Cognitive neuroscience increasingly shows the importance of **brain networks** rather than specific regions, which sits uneasily with ACT-R's one-module-one-region mapping.

> **Trap.** ACT-R module locations are a classic matching question. **Retrieval: inferior ventrolateral PFC. Imaginal: posterior parietal. Goal: anterior cingulate. Procedural: head of the caudate (basal ganglia).** Note that the **dorsolateral** PFC is **not** one of the four, which is the second limitation. ACT-R is both a **cognitive architecture** and a **production system** (its procedural module and central production system run on If ... then rules).

### Links with other approaches

Until fairly recently, most computational models were built to predict and explain **behavioural** data from cognitive psychology experiments. There has since been a substantial increase in models directly relevant to **cognitive neuropsychology** and **cognitive neuroscience** (ACT-R is one).

How are models applied to brain-damaged patients? The usual sequence (**Dell & Caramazza, 2008**):

1. Build a model that accounts for **healthy** people's performance on a task.
2. Alter parts of the model to simulate "**lesions**", and assess the effect on task performance.
3. Compare the **lesioned model's** performance with that of **brain-damaged patients**.

### Overall evaluation of computational cognitive science

**Strengths.**

1. **An overarching framework.** Cognitive architectures promise a single framework for the whole cognitive system, valuable because much empirical work is narrow and paradigm-specific. Whether they have delivered is controversial.
2. **Growing scope.** Once mainly behavioural, modelling now also targets **functional neuroimaging** data, and lesioned models are compared with patients.
3. **Rigour.** A program must contain detailed information about every process involved in a task, so theorists are forced to think carefully. Verbal theories are often vague about what their assumptions predict.
4. **Nested incremental modelling.** Progress can be made by building each new model on the strengths of earlier related models, removing their weaknesses and accounting for extra data. **Perry et al. (2007)** proposed the **connectionist dual-process model (CDP+)** of reading aloud, which improved on the dual-process model it was derived from.

**Limitations.**

1. **Bonini's paradox.** As **Dutton and Starbuck (1971)** put it, "As a model of a complex system becomes more complete, it becomes less understandable." A model realistic enough to capture everything becomes as hard to understand as the real process it represents. The map analogy makes the point: a map the same size as its territory would be perfectly precise and completely useless. In practice, though, models have gained substantially in **power** as they grew more complex.
2. **Falsifiability.** Computational models are sometimes **hard to falsify**, although usually easier to test than purely verbal theories. Modellers are ingenious, so many models can accommodate numerous behavioural findings. A promising future direction is applying models more systematically to **neuroimaging** as well as behavioural findings.
3. **Inflating apparent success: overfitting.** One way modellers make a model look better is **overfitting** (**Ziegler et al., 2010**): the model fits one data set extremely well but fails to generalise to others, because it has captured the **noise** in the data as well as the genuine effects.
4. **Motivation and emotion are neglected.** Human cognition is shaped by several potentially conflicting motivational and emotional factors, which most models ignore. **ACT-R** (Anderson et al., 2008) is a partial exception, with a **motivational component in its goal module**. **Norman (1980)** distinguished the **Pure Cognitive System** from the biological **Regulatory System**; much of what the cognitive system does is set by the regulatory system's needs (survival, food, water). Computational cognitive science, like most of cognitive psychology, plays down the regulatory system's key role.
5. **Models are hard to assess.** **Addyman and French (2012)** listed the reasons: modellers each program in their own favourite language, source code is rarely made available, models are effectively inaccessible to researchers who do not program, and even other modellers face such a variety of languages and coding styles that checking, exploring, reusing or extending a model is nearly impossible.

> **Trap.** Bonini's paradox (Dutton & Starbuck, 1971) is about **understandability**: more complete means less understandable. It is not about overfitting (fitting noise, Ziegler et al., 2010) and not about falsifiability. Norman (1980): **Pure Cognitive System** versus **Regulatory System**; the neglected one is the **Regulatory** system. Nested incremental modelling (CDP+, Perry et al., 2007) is a **strength**.

## 1.6 Comparisons of Major Approaches

### Which approach is best? The wrong question

Having worked through the four approaches, it is natural to ask which is the most useful. The book argues this is the wrong question, for three reasons:

1. **Research increasingly combines** two or more approaches, so they are not really rivals.
2. **Each approach makes a distinctive contribution**, so all are needed. The book's analogy is golf: asking whether a driver or a putter is more useful to a golfer is pointless, because a round needs both.
3. **Each approach has its own limitations** alongside its strengths (Table 1.1, rebuilt below).

### Converging operations

Given that every method is weak somewhere, the best strategy is **converging operations**: using several methods with **different** strengths and limitations to address the same theoretical question, so that each method's strengths offset the others' weaknesses.

- If two or more methods give the **same answer**, the evidence is **stronger** than any single method could provide.
- If they give **different answers**, **more research** is needed to work out why.

The recognition memory example in 1.4 is the model case: a neuropsychological double dissociation (hippocampal versus perirhinal patients) and neuroimaging (Diana et al., 2007) converge on the same recollection-familiarity theory.

The book adds a note about its own coverage. Its aim for every topic is to present the research that best advances understanding, so a given approach is heavily represented in some chapters and barely present in others. That uneven representation reflects where each approach has been most informative, not a ranking.

> **Trap.** Converging operations means **different methods with different weaknesses** addressing **one** question. Replicating the same fMRI study three times is replication, not converging operations. And disagreement between methods is not a failure of the strategy; the book says it signals that further research is needed.

## 1.7 Outline of This Book

The authors close by explaining how the book is organised and why the organisation is partly artificial. Almost every process in the cognitive system depends on others. Their example is a **student reading a textbook to prepare for an examination**:

- the student is **learning**;
- **visual perception** takes in the printed words, and **attention** is directed to the content;
- benefiting from the book requires considerable **language** skill and extensive relevant knowledge in **long-term memory**;
- relating the book to conflicting information learned elsewhere involves some **problem solving**;
- deciding how much time to give each chapter involves **decision making**;
- what is learned depends partly on the student's **emotional state**;

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Every method is indirect, in its own way.** Reaction times are indirect evidence about processes; patients' performance is filtered through compensation and plasticity; BOLD is a delayed proxy for neural activity and ERPs are smeared by the skull; programs can reach human outputs by non-human routes. Hence converging operations (1.6).
2. **The causal ladder.** Imaging is **correlational**; lesions and **TMS** show **necessity**. TMS beats patient studies by allowing within-person comparison and experimenter control of the site. Most "which method shows area X is necessary?" questions turn on this.
3. **Modularity versus integration.** Coltheart's assumptions imply separable, serially linked parts. The 7th edition keeps pushing back: moderate connectivity and hubs (Bullmore & Sporns, 2012), the same frontal areas for different tasks (Duncan & Owen, 2000), the same regions active in 20% of studies (Yarkoni et al., 2011), face processing as a network, and the shift from functional specialisation to integration. Even ACT-R is faulted for one-module-one-region mapping.
4. **Inference from patterns.** Single dissociations can be difficulty artefacts, double dissociations presuppose modules, associations can reflect adjacency, and reverse inference fails for non-selective regions. In each case a pattern supports a conclusion only once alternatives are excluded.
5. **Sample size and statistics.** Single cases versus case series, underpowered scans and false positives, overfitted models: small or narrow data sets mislead in every approach.
6. **Generalisation.** Ecological validity, paradigm specificity, worse memory in the scanner (Gutchess & Park, 2006) and overfitting all ask whether a finding survives outside the conditions that produced it.

## Worked example

*A 62-year-old retired teacher is referred to a neuropsychology clinic six months after a stroke. She forgets conversations and appointments within an hour, yet in the clinic she repeats digit strings normally. A trainee proposes: (a) since her MRI shows medial temporal damage, report that "her medial temporal lobe stores long-term memories"; (b) compare her with a published patient showing the opposite pattern; (c) run an fMRI study in healthy people to show the area "lights up" during learning. Evaluate each proposal using Chapter 1.*

**Describe the pattern.** Normal short-term performance with impaired long-term memory is a **single dissociation**, HM's pattern. On its own it cannot show separate systems, because the long-term task may simply be **harder**.

**Proposal (b).** The opposite-pattern patient is **KF** (Shallice & Warrington, 1970). Together they make a **double dissociation**, which defeats the difficulty explanation: no single difficulty ordering makes the long-term task harder for her and the short-term task harder for KF. It is good evidence that STM and LTM are distinct, but it **assumes modularity** and can usually be explained in more than one way (Davies, 2010), so it is strong rather than conclusive.

**Proposal (a).** The lesion gives a **causal** link between damage and deficit, a recognised strength of the approach, but the localisation claim overreaches. Stroke damage is rarely confined to one structure, so neighbouring tissue may be responsible. Six months on, her performance may reflect **compensatory strategies** and **plasticity** (Cus et al., 2011), which breaks **subtractivity**. Generalising from her to everyone assumes **uniformity of functional architecture**, which the 7th edition says lacks convincing support. A **case series** (Lambon Ralph et al., 2011) would show whether she is typical or an outlier.

**Proposal (c).** fMRI suits a **where** question (about 1 mm), but it is **correlational**: activation does not show necessity. An **event-related** design sorting encoding trials by later memory (Wagner et al., 1998) would be far more informative than "it lights up". Calling the activation "storage" risks **reverse inference** if the region is active in many other tasks (check against Yarkoni et al., 2011). A sample of 20 or fewer would be **underpowered**, uncorrected thresholds invite dead-salmon **false positives** (Bennett et al., 2009), and memory is worse inside a scanner (Gutchess & Park, 2006).

**What would add causal evidence in healthy people?** **TMS** tests necessity, with a **non-critical site** as control, but it reaches only areas just beneath the skull, so the deep medial temporal lobe is out of range. If the question concerned **timing** (when recognition begins), **ERPs** or **MEG** would be the choice, not fMRI (2 to 3 s) or PET (30 to 60 s).

**Conclusion.** Each line of evidence has a different weakness. When the single dissociation, the double dissociation with KF, event-related fMRI and (where anatomy allows) TMS agree, the conclusion is far stronger than any one could support: **converging operations** applied to a real referral.

## MCQ traps

1. **1956 at MIT:** Chomsky (language), Miller (magic number seven), Newell and Simon (General Problem Solver, cited as Newell et al., 1958), plus Bruner et al. (1956) on concept formation. Production-system models came later (**Newell & Simon, 1972**).
2. **Information-processing approach:** a **general-purpose, symbol-processing, limited-capacity** system; the 1970s version was **serial and bottom-up**, which the book calls drastically oversimplified.
3. **"Paris in the the spring"**: missing the repeated "the" shows **top-down** processing dominating bottom-up input.
4. **Parallel processing** is more likely on **practised** tasks (experienced driver), not novel ones.
5. **Task impurity:** **Miyake et al. (2000)** used the **Stroop, anti-saccade and stop-signal** tasks with latent-variable analysis to isolate **inhibition**.
6. **Baddeley et al. (1975):** suppression with the digits **1 to 8** **abolished the word-length effect**; the abolition, not the overall drop, is the evidence for rehearsal.
7. **Three Coltheart dates:** 2001 = four assumptions; 2010 = aim is the **mind**, not the brain; 2011 = cognitive neuroscience has made **no difference** to theorising (controversial).
8. **Implacable experimenter (Wachtel, 1973)** is an **ecological validity** problem; **paradigm specificity (Meiser, 2011)** is failure to generalise to other **lab tasks**.
9. **KF (Shallice & Warrington, 1970):** impaired **STM**, intact LTM. **HM (Henry Molaison):** impaired LTM except **motor skills**, intact STM, **hippocampal** damage.
10. **Coltheart's (2001) four assumptions:** modularity, **anatomical** modularity, **uniformity of functional architecture**, **subtractivity**. Domain specificity is a property of modules, not a fifth assumption.
11. **Subtractivity** fails when recovery restores function or **recruits other regions** (Cus et al., 2011).
12. **Against anatomical modularity:** **Duncan and Owen (2000)**, same frontal areas for different complex tasks; Yarkoni et al. (2011), the same areas active in **20%** of studies.
13. **Single dissociation** may be a **difficulty** artefact; a **double dissociation** rules that out but **assumes modularity** and allows other explanations (Davies, 2010).
14. **Association** may reflect **anatomical adjacency**: **Gerstmann's syndrome** = finger identification, calculation, spelling, left-right disorientation.
15. **Case series** compares **individual** patients and measures variation (Lambon Ralph et al., 2011); the summary's "case-study approach" means case series.
16. **Pure alexia** (letter-by-letter reading) = a **compensatory strategy**, a limitation of cognitive neuropsychology.
17. **Rostral = anterior; dorsal = superior; ventral = inferior.** The **central sulcus** separates frontal from parietal; the **lateral fissure** separates temporal from frontal and parietal.
18. **Brodmann (1868 to 1918): 52 areas** from cell-type distribution across cortical layers; **BA17 = primary visual cortex**.
19. **Bullmore and Sporns (2012):** cost versus efficiency; the brain gets **moderate efficiency at moderate cost**; full interconnection would need a brain **12½ miles wide** (Ward, 2010); no neuron has more than about **10,000** connections; hubs include the **anterior cingulate**.
20. **Van den Heuvel et al. (2009):** IQ tracks network **global efficiency** (**+0.75** medial prefrontal BA9/10, **+0.72** inferior parietal BA39/40), **not** connection count.
21. **Single-unit:** electrode about **1/10,000 mm**, detects about **one-millionth of a volt**, invasive. **Quiroga et al. (2005)**: Jennifer Aniston neuron, **medial temporal lobe**, **epileptic** patients; evidence for **local** coding.
22. **ERP** = time-locked **averaged** EEG; timing within a few ms; poor spatial resolution; simple tasks only; **N400** = negative peak near **400 ms**.
23. **PET:** labelled **water** tracer; **5 to 10 mm**; **30 to 60 s**. **fMRI:** **BOLD** (deoxyhaemoglobin); about **1 mm**; **2 to 3 s**; replaced PET. **MRI** magnet up to **11 tons**, structure only.
24. **MEG:** **SQUID**; skull nearly **transparent** to magnetic fields; ms timing; **extremely expensive**. **Cooke et al. (2007):** fMRI **43%** upset, **33%** side effects; MEG **35%** and **35%**.
25. **Wagner et al. (1998):** event-related fMRI; more activity at learning for words **later recognised**.
26. **Brain reading:** **Haxby et al. (2001)** 8 categories, **96%**; **Kay et al. (2008)** 2 participants, 120 novel images, **92%** and **72%**, chance **0.8%**; **Hung et al. (2005)** macaque decoder separated stimuli the **monkeys could not**, so decoding is not mind reading.
27. **TMS:** pulse **under 1 ms**, about **1 cm³** affected; best control = **non-critical site** (noise and twitching confound "no TMS"); **Cracco et al. (1999)**, occipital TMS most disruptive at **80 to 100 ms**.
28. **Allen et al. (2007)**, cats: rTMS raised spontaneous activity for up to **a minute** yet cut grating responses by up to **60%**; TMS can **enhance** performance by releasing inhibition.
29. **TMS limitations:** complex effects, uncertain and **distant** areas affected, only areas **beneath the skull** (not under muscle), rare **seizures**.
30. **Resolving controversies:** **Wild et al. (2012)**, primary auditory cortex, knowledge acts **early**; **Kosslyn (2005)**, **two-thirds** overlap of perception and imagery areas; **Kosslyn & Thompson (2003)** meta-analysis, even early visual areas; **R.J. Lee et al. (2012)**, early areas favour **perceived** objects. Chapter 3 places this in the Kosslyn (depictive) versus Pylyshyn (propositional) debate.
31. **Recollection = hippocampus; familiarity = perirhinal cortex** (patient double dissociation plus Diana et al., 2007).
32. **Reverse inference:** amygdala and threat (Sander, 2009) undermined by amygdala activity for **most emotions** (Lindquist et al., 2012).
33. **Two Yarkoni papers:** **2010** = underpowering (20 or fewer participants) and up to **15%** false positives; **2011** = **3,489 studies, 100,953 activation points**, DLPFC, anterior cingulate and anterior insula active in **20%**.
34. **Dead salmon (Bennett et al., 2009)** = false positives from **uncorrected** thresholds, not underpowering or reverse inference.
35. **Intrinsic activity:** tasks add **under 5%**; similar patterns in **coma, anaesthesia and slow-wave sleep**; Raichle (2010) on prediction; task-related **decreases** follow.
36. **Gutchess and Park (2006):** recognition memory **worse in the scanner**. **Keehner et al. (2011):** more **3D** images, higher ratings. **Guardian, 28 August 2007**, chocoholics (Rolls & McCabe, 2007; Beck, 2010).
37. **Deep Blue** beat **Kasparov on 11 May 1997** at **200 million positions per second**: AI, not modelling.
38. **BackProp:** error-driven weight change from random starting weights; **little or no evidence in the brain** (Mayor et al., 2014). **Localist** connectionist models: Coltheart et al. (2001), **TRACE**, Dell (1986), Levelt et al. (1999); **distributed**: PDP (Rumelhart et al., 1986).
39. **ACT-R (J.R. Anderson et al., 2004):** retrieval = **inferior ventrolateral PFC**; imaginal = **posterior parietal**; goal = **anterior cingulate**; procedural = **head of caudate (basal ganglia)**. Criticised for **de-emphasising DLPFC**.
40. **Bonini's paradox (Dutton & Starbuck, 1971)** = completeness costs understandability; **overfitting (Ziegler et al., 2010)** = fitting noise; **Norman (1980)**: the neglected system is the **Regulatory System**; **CDP+ (Perry et al., 2007)** = nested incremental modelling, a strength.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive psychology** | Studying human cognition through behaviour; in the broader sense used in the book's title, also through brain activity and structure |
| **Cognitive neuroscience** | Studying human cognition by combining evidence from behaviour and from the brain |
| **Cognitive neuropsychology** | Studying the intact and impaired performance of brain-damaged patients to understand normal cognition |
| **Computational cognitive science** | Building computational models to understand human cognition, increasingly informed by behavioural and brain data |
| **Information-processing approach** | The traditional mind-as-computer view: a limited-capacity, general-purpose symbol processor passing from stimulus to response |
| **Bottom-up processing** | Processing driven directly by the stimulus |
| **Top-down processing** | Processing shaped by expectations, knowledge and past experience rather than by the stimulus alone |
| **Serial processing** | One process finishes before the next begins |
| **Parallel processing** | Two or more processes run at the same time |
| **Task impurity problem** | The difficulty that most tasks involve a mixture of processes, so results are hard to attribute to any one |
| **Articulatory suppression** | Repeating irrelevant speech (in Baddeley et al., 1975, the digits 1 to 8) to block verbal rehearsal |
| **Ecological validity** | How far laboratory findings apply to everyday settings |
| **Implacable experimenter** | Wachtel's (1973) term for the lab situation in which the experimenter's actions do not respond to the participant's behaviour |
| **Paradigm specificity** | When a finding from one task or paradigm does not appear even with apparently very similar tasks |
| **Cognitive architecture** | A comprehensive, domain-general framework of cognition implemented as a computer program (e.g., ACT-R) |
| **Lesion** | A structural alteration of the brain caused by injury or disease |
| **Modularity** | The assumption that cognition is made up of many fairly independent modules, each specialised for one type of processing |
| **Domain specificity** | A module's property of responding only to one class of stimuli (e.g., faces) |
| **Anatomical modularity** | The assumption that each module is located in a specific brain area |
| **Uniformity of functional architecture** | The assumption that the cognitive system is organised the same way in everyone (Coltheart, 2001) |
| **Subtractivity** | The assumption that brain damage only removes or impairs modules and does not add or alter anything |
| **Dissociation** | A patient performs normally on one task but is severely impaired on another |
| **Double dissociation** | One patient is intact on task X and impaired on task Y, while another shows the reverse pattern |
| **Association** | Particular impairments consistently occurring together across many patients |
| **Syndrome** | The idea that co-occurring symptoms share a common origin |
| **Case-series study** | Testing several patients with similar impairments and comparing their individual data, so variation across patients can be assessed |
| **Sulcus** | A groove or furrow in the brain's surface |
| **Gyri** | Raised ridges on the brain's surface (singular: gyrus) |
| **Dorsal** | Superior; towards the top of the brain |
| **Ventral** | Inferior; towards the bottom of the brain |
| **Rostral** | Anterior; towards the front of the brain |
| **Posterior** | Towards the back of the brain |
| **Lateral** | At the side of the brain |
| **Medial** | In the middle of the brain |
| **Brodmann areas** | Brodmann's 52 cortical regions, defined by the distribution of cell types across cortical layers (e.g., BA17, primary visual cortex) |
| **Spatial resolution** | How precisely a technique locates where brain activity occurs |
| **Temporal resolution** | How precisely a technique tracks when brain activity occurs |
| **Single-unit recording** | An invasive technique recording the activity of individual neurons with a micro-electrode |
| **Electroencephalography (EEG)** | Recording the brain's electrical potentials through scalp electrodes |
| **Event-related potentials (ERPs)** | EEG responses averaged over many presentations of the same or similar stimulus, time-locked to stimulus onset |
| **Positron emission tomography (PET)** | Brain scanning by detecting positrons from a radioactive tracer; reasonable spatial, poor temporal resolution |
| **Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)** | Imaging blood oxygenation with an MRI scanner to show where, and roughly when, brain activity occurs |
| **BOLD** | Blood oxygen level-dependent contrast, the signal fMRI measures, reflecting deoxyhaemoglobin concentration |
| **Event-related fMRI (efMRI)** | fMRI in which activity linked to specific kinds of event (e.g., later-remembered versus later-forgotten items) is analysed and compared separately |
| **Magneto-encephalography (MEG)** | Non-invasive recording of the magnetic fields produced by brain activity |
| **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** | Brief magnetic pulses that disrupt an area's functioning; a "temporary lesion", more accurately interference with task processing |
| **Repetitive TMS (rTMS)** | TMS pulses given several times in rapid succession |
| **Meta-analysis** | A statistical analysis combining the findings of many studies on the same issue |
| **Functional specialisation** | The assumption that each brain region is specialised for a specific function |
| **Functional integration** | Coordination of activity across brain regions, studied by correlating activity between areas |
| **Reverse inference** | Inferring that a cognitive process occurred from activation in a given brain region |
| **Blobology** | The disparaging label for treating one activated patch as the dedicated seat of a complex function |
| **False positive** | Wrongly treating random activity in an area as task-related activation |
| **Neuroimaging illusion** | Overvaluing what brain images add to our understanding of cognition |
| **Computational modelling** | Writing programs that simulate human cognitive processes |
| **Artificial intelligence** | Writing programs that produce intelligent outcomes, whether or not by human-like means |
| **Connectionist models** | Learning networks of simple interconnected units, with knowledge usually distributed across many units |
| **Back-propagation** | A connectionist learning rule that adjusts connection weights by comparing actual outputs with correct ones |
| **Localist representation** | Knowledge stored in specific units or locations rather than spread across many |
| **Production systems** | Systems made of large numbers of "If ... then" rules plus a working memory |
| **Production rules** | Condition-action ("If ... then") rules whose action is carried out whenever the condition holds |
| **Working memory** | A system holding information that is currently being processed |
| **Conflict resolution** | In a production system, the strategy that selects one rule when several match working memory |
| **ACT-R** | Anderson's Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational architecture, with retrieval, imaginal, goal and procedural modules mapped to brain regions |
| **Nested incremental modelling** | Building each new model on the strengths of earlier ones while fixing their weaknesses (e.g., CDP+) |
| **Bonini's paradox** | The more complete a model of a complex system becomes, the less understandable it is |
| **Overfitting** | A model fitting one data set very well, including its noise, but failing to generalise |
| **Converging operations** | Using several methods with different strengths and weaknesses to address the same question |
