# Chapter 2: Basic Processes in Visual Perception

**What this chapter is really about.** Seeing feels like the simplest thing you do. You open your eyes and the world is just there, in colour, in depth, with objects you can name and reach for. Eysenck and Keane open Part I of the book ("Visual perception and attention") by insisting that this ease is an illusion produced by an enormous amount of hidden work. Two observations make the point. First, more of the cortex is given over to vision than to any other sense, most obviously the occipital lobes at the back of the head. Second, when early artificial intelligence researchers tried to make computers "see", they found that even a toy world of plain white blocks, with a task as trivial as counting how many blocks were present, needed extremely complicated programs; no machine yet matches more than a fraction of what an ordinary adult does visually. The working definition the book adopts comes from **Sekuler and Blake (2002)**: perception is the acquisition and processing of sensory information so that we can see, hear, taste or feel objects in the world, and it also guides the organism's actions towards those objects. Hold on to the second half of that definition. Perception is not only for knowing what is out there; it is also for acting on it, and the most important theory in this chapter turns on exactly that distinction.

Part I runs across four chapters. **Chapter 2** (this one) covers the basic processes: the brain systems for vision, the claim that recognising an object and acting on it use different systems, and three classic topics (colour, depth, perception without awareness). **Chapter 3** covers object and face recognition and the imagery debate; **Chapter 4** covers perception in a moving world and whether attention is needed for conscious perception (change blindness, inattentional blindness); **Chapter 5** covers attention proper, visual search, disorders of attention and doing two things at once. The book presents the whole area as one of the fastest-moving in the field, driven largely by brain research.

For a clinical psychology student, this chapter is where neuropsychology starts to matter. Achromatopsia, akinetopsia, optic ataxia, visual form agnosia, extinction and blindsight are all named syndromes with named patients (LM, DF, VK, VE, DB, GY, GR), and each patient was studied because their damage separates two things that in a healthy brain always occur together. The chapter also sets up a theme that runs through the rest of the book: a great deal of sophisticated processing goes on without any conscious awareness at all.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 2.1 is a short introduction about how varied visual perception is. Section 2.2 is the anatomy and physiology: from the retina to the cortex, then Zeki's theory that different cortical areas handle different features (form, colour, motion), and the binding problem that theory creates. Section 2.3 is Milner and Goodale's two-visual-systems theory, tested against brain-damaged patients and against visual illusions; it is the most examined section, and its evaluation (what the theory got wrong) matters as much as the theory itself. Section 2.4 is colour: trichromacy, opponent processes, their combination, and then a long treatment of colour constancy. Section 2.5 is depth: the cues (monocular, oculomotor, binocular), how cues are combined, and size constancy. Section 2.6 is perception without awareness: blindsight in V1-damaged patients and subliminal perception in healthy people. There is one "In the real world" box, on the blindsight patient DB, placed in 2.6. The figures did not survive in the source, so where a figure carried exam-relevant content it is explained in prose.

## 2.1 Introduction

The chapter's first move is to warn against thinking of "visual perception" as one process. Much of the recent progress has come from cognitive neuroscientists, who have mapped the brain systems involved, and the chapter follows that order: first the brain areas and what each does, then theories about how those systems are organised, then specific achievements (colour, depth), and finally the question of whether perception can happen without awareness. Object and face recognition are left to Chapter 3.

Two qualifications apply to everything that follows. First, for clarity the book treats one aspect of vision at a time, but in a working visual system all of these processes interact. Second, which processes you use depends heavily on what you are looking at and what you are trying to achieve (**Hegdé, 2008**).

The contrast that makes the second point concrete:

- **Very fast gist.** **Thorpe et al. (1996)** showed observers photographs of natural scenes for only **20 ms**; some contained an animal and some did not. EEG recordings showed that the brain had registered whether an animal was present within about **150 ms**. The overall meaning of a scene can therefore be picked up almost instantly.
- **Slow, effortful analysis.** The book's Figure 2.1 (a scene from Hegdé, 2008) is a cluttered photograph in which the reader is asked to count the animals. That takes several seconds of deliberate searching.

The same visual system does both. When later sections describe a single "process", remember that it is one tool among many and that the task determines which tools are used.

> **Trap.** Thorpe et al.'s numbers are often reversed in options. The photographs were shown for **20 ms**; the EEG detection of an animal occurred at about **150 ms**. The 150 ms figure is a brain-response latency, not an exposure duration.

## 2.2 Vision and the Brain

A large part of the back half of the cortex is devoted to vision. **Felleman and Van Essen (1991)** counted roughly **25 distinct brain areas** that process visual information. The visual cortex includes the whole of the occipital lobe and extends a long way forward into the temporal and parietal lobes. Before the cortex can be understood, though, you need the route by which signals get there from the eye.

### From eye to cortex

#### Rods, cones and retinal ganglion cells

The retina has two kinds of receptor cell, **cones** and **rods**.

- **Cones** serve **colour vision** and **sharpness of vision** (fine detail). They are discussed again under colour vision in 2.4.
- **Rods** are specialised for **vision in dim light**. There are about **125 million** of them, concentrated in the outer (peripheral) regions of the retina.

The receptors do not send their signals straight to the brain. They feed into **retinal ganglion cells**, the cells that provide the retina's output signal. The key structural difference is the **convergence** onto those ganglion cells: a ganglion cell receives input from only a **few cones** but from **hundreds of rods**. Many of the differences between the two systems follow from this. Because hundreds of rods pool their weak signals into one ganglion cell, the rod system can produce a usable output in poor light, which is why in dim conditions it is essentially only the rods that drive much ganglion-cell activity. The price of pooling is that the ganglion cell cannot tell which of its hundreds of rods was stimulated, so rod vision is blurred; the cone system, with little convergence, preserves detail.

> **Trap.** The main text describes rods as specialised for **dim light**; the book's end-of-chapter summary loosely calls them specialised for motion detection (because the movement-sensitive M pathway gets most of its input from rods). If an item asks what rods are specialised for, the standard answer is **dim-light (scotopic) vision**; cones are for **colour and acuity**. The 125 million figure belongs to **rods**.

#### The retina-geniculate-striate pathway

The main route from eye to cortex is the **retina-geniculate-striate pathway**. Signals leave the retina, pass through the **lateral geniculate nuclei (LGN)** of the **thalamus**, and arrive at **V1** (primary visual cortex, also called striate cortex, hence "striate" in the name), from which they spread to **V2** and beyond.

The whole system preserves the spatial layout of the retina. Two points that are next to each other in the retinal image are also represented next to each other at the higher levels of the pathway. The book's term for this mapping of retinal receptors onto points on the surface of the visual cortex is **retinopy** (most other textbooks call it **retinotopy** or retinotopic organisation; they are the same idea).

#### The optic chiasma

Each eye sends its own **optic nerve** back into the head, and the two nerves meet at the **optic chiasma**. Here the fibres sort themselves:

1. Axons from the **outer half** of each retina (the half nearer the temple) continue to the hemisphere on the **same side**.
2. Axons from the **inner half** of each retina (the half nearer the nose) **cross over** to the opposite hemisphere.

The consequence is that each half of visual space ends up in the **opposite** hemisphere. Beyond the chiasma the signals run in two **optic tracts**: one carries information from the left half of each eye's retina and the other from the right half (Figure 2.2 in the book shows that the left visual cortex receives from the left sides of both retinas, and the right visual cortex from the right sides of both). Because the lens inverts the image, the left half of each retina receives light from the **right** visual field, which is why the left hemisphere "sees" the right side of space.

The optic tract runs to the **LGN** in the thalamus, and from there impulses reach **V1** in the occipital lobe before spreading to neighbouring areas such as V2.

> **Trap.** Only the **inner (nasal)** halves cross at the chiasma; the outer (temporal) halves stay on the same side. The result is that the **left visual field is processed by the right hemisphere** and vice versa, for both eyes, not that the left eye goes to the right hemisphere. This is the anatomy behind the split-brain work in Chapter 16 and behind the hemifield losses of the blindsight patients in 2.6.

#### Two (or three) channels: parvocellular and magnocellular

Within the retina-geniculate-striate system there are two **relatively independent** channels:

| Pathway | Most sensitive to | Main input from | Later associated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parvocellular (P)** | **Colour** and **fine detail** | **Cones** | Ventral ("what") stream |
| **Magnocellular (M)** | **Movement** | **Rods** | Dorsal ("how") stream |

"Relatively" is doing real work. There are many interconnections between the two, the visual system is turning out to be extremely complex (**Wade & Swanston, 2013**), and the two pathways are clearly intermingled by the time they reach V1 (**Nassi & Callaway, 2009; Leopold, 2012**). There is also a third, **koniocellular** pathway, whose functions are still poorly understood.

### Brain systems beyond V1

Neurons of both the P and M pathways project mainly to V1. What happens next is summarised in the book's Figure 2.3 (from Gazzaniga et al., 2008), which shows two routes leaving V1. To read it you need three working labels: **V3** is generally assumed to process **form**, **V4** **colour**, and **V5/MT** **motion**.

- The **P pathway** is associated with the **ventral** or **"what"** pathway, which runs down into the **inferotemporal cortex**.
- The **M pathway** is associated with the **dorsal** or **"how"** pathway (previously called the **"where"** pathway), which runs up into the **posterior parietal cortex**.

The book immediately says these pairings are only rough approximations of a complicated reality; for instance, some parvocellular neurons project into dorsal areas (**Parker, 2007**). Three points to keep in mind:

1. **Different emphases.** The ventral stream, ending in inferotemporal cortex, is mainly concerned with **form and colour** and with **object recognition** (Chapter 3). The dorsal stream, ending in parietal cortex, is more concerned with **movement**.
2. **No rigid division of information.** **Gilaie-Dotan et al. (2013b)** studied patients whose damage was confined to the ventral stream. Although motion is usually attributed to the dorsal stream, these patients had widespread impairments of **motion perception**. So a "dorsal" kind of information depended partly on the ventral stream.
3. **No total segregation.** The two streams are richly interconnected (**Felleman & Van Essen, 1991; Pisella et al., 2009**), and both project to the **primary motor cortex** (**Rossetti & Pisella, 2002**).

#### A more detailed map (Mather's figure)

The book's Figure 2.4 (from Mather, 2009) is a fuller diagram in which box size shows the size of each cortical area, arrows show the percentage of fibres in each projection, and vertical position shows how quickly cells in each area respond. Three exam-relevant points come out of it:

1. The interconnections among visual areas are **complicated**, not a simple chain.
2. The areas in the **ventral** stream are **more than twice as large** as those in the dorsal stream.
3. In timing, cells in the **LGN** respond first to a new stimulus, then cells in **V1**, but cells in several other areas (**V3/V3A, MT, MST**) become active very shortly afterwards. Activity does not trickle slowly up a long hierarchy; several areas are engaged almost at once.

#### The ventral pathway as recurrent networks (Kravitz et al., 2013)

The traditional view treated the ventral pathway as a **serial hierarchy** running from simple features to complex objects. **Kravitz et al. (2013)** proposed instead that it consists of **several overlapping recurrent networks**, with connections running **in both directions** between their components (the book's Figure 2.5, drawn on the macaque brain, includes V1, V2, V3, V4, the MT/MST complex, the superior temporal sulcus and inferior temporal cortex area TE). The important change is the two-way traffic: later areas send signals back to earlier ones, rather than merely receiving from them.

### V1 and V2

**V1** is primary visual cortex (also **Brodmann area 17, BA17**); **V2** is secondary visual cortex (**BA18**). Three general points are needed to understand what they do.

#### Point 1: receptive fields

The **receptive field** of a neuron is the region of the retina in which light affects that neuron's activity. Because the retina maps one-to-one onto visual space, the term can equally refer to the region of visual space that drives the neuron. A cell in V1 does not respond to light anywhere; it responds only to light in its own small patch.

#### Point 2: neurons influence one another (lateral inhibition)

Neurons are not independent detectors. In **lateral inhibition**, activity in one neuron reduces the activity of its neighbour. The useful effect is to **exaggerate contrast at edges**: where a bright region meets a dark one, cells on the bright side inhibit their dark-side neighbours more strongly than they are inhibited back, so the boundary is sharpened and the dividing line between objects becomes easier to find.

**Simultaneous contrast** is the demonstration (the book's Figure 2.6, from Lehar, 2008). Two physically identical grey squares are placed on different surrounds: the one on a **much darker surround looks lighter**, and the one on the lighter surround looks darker. Lateral inhibition from the surround produces the difference. The point to take away is that perceived lightness depends on context, not only on the light coming from the patch itself.

> **Trap.** The square on the **dark** surround looks **lighter**; the square on the **light** surround looks **darker**. Students often reverse this. The mechanism named for it is **lateral inhibition**, not adaptation.

#### Point 3: early processing is more extensive than once thought

V1 and V2 are both large areas (see Figure 2.4), and the processing they do is substantial. Recording in macaque V2, **Hegdé and Van Essen (2000)** found that about **one cell in three** was selective for **complex shapes**, and for **size and orientation** differences as well. "Early" does not mean "only simple features".

#### Feedforward sweep and recurrent processing

V1 and V2 are not only the first stages of a one-way process. **Lamme (2006)** distinguishes two phases:

1. An initial **feedforward sweep** that passes rapidly through the visual areas, starting with V1 and then V2.
2. A second phase of **recurrent processing**, in which signals travel in the **opposite** direction, from higher areas back to earlier ones. Some recurrent processing reaches V1 within **120 ms** of stimulus onset.

**Boehler et al. (2008)** found that observers had **greater visual awareness** of a stimulus when recurrent processing was strongly present. Lamme's theory that recurrent processing is the neural signature of consciousness is treated fully in Chapter 16; here the point is that V1 takes part in late, feedback-driven processing as well as early processing.

> **Trap.** Feedforward = **upward**, fast, first. Recurrent = **backward** (higher to lower areas), later, and linked to **conscious awareness** (Lamme, 2006; Boehler et al., 2008). Recurrent activity can reach V1 by about **120 ms**.

### Functional specialisation (Zeki)

#### The theory

**Zeki (1993, 2001)** proposed a **functional specialisation theory**: different areas of visual cortex are specialised for different visual functions. His analogy is a team of workers, each solving one part of a complex problem on their own, whose separate results are then combined to give the final solution, which here is coherent visual perception.

Why would the brain be built like this? **Zeki (2005)** gave two reasons.

1. **Attributes combine unpredictably.** A green object could be a car, a sheet of paper or a leaf; a car could be red, black or green. Knowing one attribute tells you little about the others, so each has to be worked out in its own right if the object is to be perceived accurately.
2. **Different attributes need different computations.** Motion processing has to integrate information across **two or more moments in time**. Form processing has to work out the **spatial relationships** among elements **at a single moment**. It is efficient to give such different jobs to different machinery.

Much of the evidence came from the **macaque monkey**, whose visual system closely resembles ours, which is why people speak of human V1, V2 and so on (the book's Figure 2.7 is Zeki's 1992 cross-section of macaque visual cortex). The functions Zeki (1993, 2005) ascribed to each area:

| Area | Zeki's proposed function |
|---|---|
| **V1 and V2** | Early processing; contain different groups of cells responsive to **colour** and to **form** |
| **V3 and V3A** | **Form**, especially the shapes of **objects in motion**; **not** colour |
| **V4** | Most cells respond to **colour**; many also respond to **line orientation** |
| **V5** | Specialised for **motion**. In macaques Zeki found **all** V5 cells responded to motion and **none** to colour. In humans the motion-specialised areas are called **MT** and **MST** |

Zeki's core assumption is that **colour, form and motion are processed in anatomically separate parts** of visual cortex. The next three subsections test that assumption for each attribute in turn, using human evidence.

#### Form processing

Several areas contribute to form processing in humans: **V1, V2, V3 and V4**, culminating in the **inferotemporal cortex** (**Kourtzi & Connor, 2011**). Some inferotemporal neurons respond to particular **semantic categories** such as animals or body parts (Chapter 3), and there is also strong evidence that inferotemporal cortex handles **shape** as such:

- **Yamane et al. (2008)** found inferotemporal neurons that responded to **three-dimensional object shape**.
- **Baldassi et al. (2013)** recorded from anterior inferotemporal cortex in **two monkeys** and found many neurons whose responses reflected **aspects of shape** (round, star-like, horizontal thin, pointy, vertical thin) **rather than the object's category**.

If form had its own separate region, some patients should have lost form perception while keeping colour and motion. That pattern does not seem to occur. **Zeki (1991)** explained why: any lesion big enough to destroy V3, V4 and inferotemporal cortex together would almost certainly destroy V1 as well, and the patient would be **totally blind** rather than selectively form-blind. The absence of a "pure form agnosia from cortical damage" is therefore not decisive evidence against specialisation; it may simply reflect anatomy.

#### Colour processing: is V4 a colour centre?

The claim that **V4** is specialised for colour has been tested in three ways: brain-damaged patients, brain imaging, and **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)**, which creates a brief, reversible "lesion".

**Patients.** If V4 and its neighbours do colour, damage mostly confined to them should leave little or no colour perception but relatively intact form, motion and fine detail. Something close to this is seen in **achromatopsia** (also called **cerebral achromatopsia**). **Bouvier and Engel (2006)** carried out a **meta-analysis** of all known cases. Almost all of them had damage to a small region of **ventral (lower) occipital cortex in or near V4**. But two details weaken the "colour centre" reading: the loss of colour was often only **partial**, which implies V4 is not the only colour area, and most patients also had **significant impairments of spatial vision**, which implies the damaged region does more than colour.

**Imaging.** **Goddard et al. (2011)** showed observers movie segments in full colour or without colour; **ventral V4** was substantially more active for the coloured versions. Earlier, **Wade et al. (2002)** had found V4 active in colour processing, but **V1 and V2** were activated as well.

**TMS.** **Banissy et al. (2012)** had participants respond to a diamond-shaped target. Responses were faster when the target was preceded by a **prime of the same colour**. When TMS was applied to **V4**, the colour priming effect **disappeared**, because TMS reduced colour processing there. Unlike imaging, this shows V4 is **causally** involved, not merely active.

**The book's verdict.** V4 and adjacent areas are certainly involved in colour, but V4 is **not a "colour centre"**, for three reasons:

1. V4 is a relatively large area that also handles **spatial processing, shape and depth** (**Roe et al., 2012**).
2. Most people with achromatopsia, and monkeys with V4 lesions, keep **some** colour ability (**Heywood & Cowey, 1999**).
3. Several areas **outside V4**, including **V1 and V2**, also process colour.

> **Trap.** Achromatopsia is a **cortical** loss of colour perception (damage in or near V4), with form and motion relatively spared. It is **not** colour blindness of the retinal kind (dichromacy, missing cone classes, 2.4). An item that says achromatopsia is caused by missing cones is wrong, and an item that says V4 is "the" colour centre ignores the 7th edition's three objections.

#### Motion processing: V5/MT, MST and akinetopsia

**V5** (also called motion area **MT**) is heavily involved in motion. Imaging studies show V5/MT is **active** during motion processing, but activation alone cannot show the area is **necessary**. **McKeefry et al. (2008)** tested necessity with **TMS** over V5/MT: it made stimuli seem to move **more slowly** than they did and impaired observers' ability to **discriminate between speeds**.

The strongest patient evidence comes from **akinetopsia**, in which stationary objects are seen fairly normally but **motion perception is grossly impaired**.

> **Key case, LM (Zihl et al., 1983).** LM was a woman with **bilateral damage to V5/MT**. She could locate stationary objects by sight, her colour discrimination was good and her binocular vision was normal. Motion, however, was severely disturbed. When she poured a drink, the liquid looked frozen in place, so she could not see the level rising and did not know when to stop pouring. In a room with several people walking about, they seemed to jump from one position to another rather than move between them.

**S.A. Cooper et al. (2012)** reported a **61-year-old** woman with akinetopsia who saw static objects normally, but saw people's smooth movements as a series of **"freeze frames"**, and people near the opening doors of a train appeared to move in slow motion.

V5/MT is not the only motion area. **MST** (medial superior temporal area) lies next to and just above V5/MT. **Vaina (1998)** studied **two patients with MST damage**. Both performed normally on some motion tests but had other motion-related problems. One of them, **RR**, often collided with people, corners and objects, especially **moving** ones such as people walking. This suggests MST helps with the **visual guidance of walking** (using motion information to steer through the world, rather than simply seeing that something moves).

#### First-order versus second-order motion

A finer distinction within motion:

- In **first-order** displays, the moving shape differs in **luminance** (the intensity of reflected light) from its background, for example a dark shape on a light background.
- In **second-order** displays, the moving shape and the background have the **same luminance**; the shape is defined by something else, such as texture. Everyday examples are rare; wind moving through a field of grass is one.

Do the two depend on different mechanisms? The evidence points both ways.

**Evidence for different mechanisms:**

- **Ashida et al. (2007)** used **adaptation** of brain activation. When the same kind of display is repeated, activation in a region falls because the same neurons are being driven again. Repeating first-order displays produced a big drop in **MT and MST** activation, and repeating second-order displays did the same. But there was **no adaptation** when a first-order display was followed by a second-order one or vice versa. So the two kinds of display drove **different sets of neurons**.
- **Rizzo et al. (2008)** found **22 patients** with a deficit for first-order but not second-order motion, and **1 patient** with the reverse. This is a **double dissociation**, the classic neuropsychological signature of separate processes.

**Evidence for the same mechanism:**

- **Hong et al. (2012)** found **direction-selective** responses to both types of motion in several areas (**V1, V2, V3, V3A, V4 and MT+**), so none of these areas is dedicated to one type, and the **patterns** of activation for the two types were similar.
- **Cowey et al. (2006)** applied TMS to **V2/V3** or to **V5/MT+**, and both types of motion perception were impaired.

**Reconciliation.** The book concludes that first- and second-order motion probably depend on **similar but not identical** mechanisms, which allows both sets of findings to be true at once.

> **Trap.** First-order motion is defined by a **luminance** difference; second-order motion has **no luminance difference** from the background. Rizzo et al.'s **22 versus 1** patients is cited as a **double dissociation** (supporting separate mechanisms); Hong et al. (2012) and Cowey et al. (2006) are the "same mechanism" side. The book's resolution is "similar but not identical".

### The binding problem

Functional specialisation creates an obvious difficulty. If colour, form and motion are analysed in different places, how does the brain put them back together so that you see **one red ball moving left**, rather than redness, roundness and leftward motion floating separately? This is the **binding problem**. **Feldman (2013)** defines it as the question of how items coded by distinct brain circuits are combined for perception, decision and action.

#### Approach 1: there is less to bind than Zeki thought

One way to shrink the problem is to show that features are not as separately processed as Zeki claimed. **Seymour et al. (2009)** showed observers **red or green** dots rotating **clockwise or anticlockwise**. Brain activity showed that particular **colour-motion conjunctions** were coded in many areas: **V1, V2, V3, V3A/B, V4 and V5/MT+**. Binding of colour with motion therefore happens extensively and **early**, not only at some late meeting point.

#### Approach 2: there are several binding problems

**Feldman (2013)** argued that "the" binding problem is really several. One is how visual **features** are bound together. Another is how information is bound across **successive eye movements**, so that the world looks stable even though the image on the retina jumps with every saccade. Seen this broadly, many research areas become relevant:

- Working out which parts of the current input belong to the **same object** (the Gestalt laws of grouping, Chapter 3).
- **Selective attention**, especially work on **visual search** (finding a target among distractors, Chapter 5), which shows that attention plays a key part in combining features that are close together in time and space.

#### Approach 3: binding by synchrony

The **binding-by-synchrony hypothesis** (for example **Singer & Gray, 1995**) proposes that detectors responding to features of the **same object fire in synchrony**, while detectors responding to features of **different objects** do not. The brain would then "know" which features go together by which cells are firing in step. Consistent with this, widespread **synchronisation** of neural activity accompanies **conscious visual awareness** (**Melloni et al., 2007; Gaillard et al., 2009**).

The book judges the hypothesis **oversimplified**, for two reasons. First, processing of an object is spread across widely separated brain areas and passes through several stages, so **precise** synchrony across all of them is implausible. Second, we usually see **several objects at once**, and it is hard to see how synchrony alone would keep their processing apart. **Guttman et al. (2007)** offered an alternative: perception depends on **patterns of neural activity over time**, not on precise synchrony.

#### Where the binding problem stands

The book's summary: there are several binding problems, most of them hard (Feldman, 2013), but there has been progress. **Selective attention** clearly matters. Feature binding is usually **associated** with synchronised activity across brain areas, though the association is often **imprecise**, and there remains the separate question of why and how the synchrony arises in the first place.

### Evaluation of functional specialisation theory

**Strengths.** Zeki's theory has been deservedly influential. It is an ambitious attempt to give a simple framework for a remarkably complex system, and its claim that **motion processing proceeds somewhat independently** of other visual processing has received reasonable support (LM and the other akinetopsia cases, TMS of V5/MT).

**Three main limitations:**

1. **Visual areas are less specialised than the theory implies.** **Heywood and Cowey (1999)** tabulated the percentage of cells in six visual areas that respond selectively to **orientation, direction of motion, disparity and colour** (the book's Figure 2.8). Cells in several areas respond to orientation, disparity and colour. The only clear specialisation was for **direction of motion in MT**.
2. **Early processing in V1 and V2 is more extensive than Zeki suggested.** **Hegdé and Van Essen (2000)** found many V2 cells responding to complex shapes.
3. **The binding problem has not been solved.** In fairness, there are probably several binding problems and nobody has solved them, but selective attention appears to be of major importance (Feldman, 2013).

> **Trap.** The one area that **does** show the specialisation Zeki claimed, in Heywood and Cowey's (1999) data, is **MT**, for **direction of motion**. V4 (colour) is the standard counter-example: large, multi-purpose, and not necessary for all colour vision. If an item asks which of Zeki's claims has the best support, it is the relative independence of **motion** processing.

## 2.3 Two Visual Systems: Perception and Action

### What is vision for?

Ask what the visual system is for, and the traditional answer is that it gives us an internal, usually conscious, representation of the outside world. **Milner and Goodale (1995, 2008)** argued that this answer is incomplete, because there are **two visual systems** serving **two different purposes**. Their theory developed earlier proposals such as **Bridgeman et al. (1979)**.

1. **Vision-for-perception** (the **"what"** system), based on the **ventral stream**. This is the system people usually mean by "seeing". You use it to decide whether the animal in front of you is a cat or a buffalo, or to take in a beautiful landscape. Its job is to **identify** objects.
2. **Vision-for-action** (the **"how"** system), based on the **dorsal stream**. You use it to **guide movements**: running to return a tennis ball, or grasping a cup. To grasp an object you must compute its **orientation and position relative to your own body**, and because you and the object are often moving relative to each other, those values must be computed **immediately before** the movement starts.

**Milner (2012)** summarised the contrast like this. The dorsal stream's main role is fast, **bottom-up**, moment-to-moment visual guidance of movement as it happens ("online"). The ventral stream, working together with **top-down** information from visual and semantic memory, builds perceptual representations that support **recognition**, visual thought, planning and memory.

#### The four core characteristics (Schenk & McIntosh, 2010)

| | **Ventral stream** | **Dorsal stream** |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | Vision-for-**perception** | Vision-for-**action** |
| **Coding frame** | **Allocentric**: object-centred, independent of the observer's viewpoint | **Egocentric**: body-centred, dependent on the observer's viewpoint |
| **Duration of representation** | **Sustained** over time | **Short-lasting** |
| **Conscious access** | Typically (not always) leads to **conscious awareness** | Does **not** lead to conscious awareness |

Two further differences are assumed by the theory:

- Processing in the **dorsal** stream is **faster** than in the ventral stream.
- **Ventral** processing depends **more on input from the fovea** (the central retina used for detail) than dorsal processing does.

The logic of the coding difference is worth understanding rather than memorising. To recognise a mug, what matters is the mug's shape in its own terms; it is the same mug whether it is to your left or right, so recognition needs an **allocentric** description that stays stable. To pick the mug up, what matters is exactly where the handle is **relative to your hand right now**, and that description is useless a second later once you or the mug have moved. So the action system needs **egocentric**, **short-lived** coding, and the perception system needs **object-centred**, **durable** coding.

> **Trap: "what versus where" versus "what versus how".** Older textbooks and older answer keys describe the dorsal stream as the **"where"** pathway (classic background: this labelling comes from Ungerleider and Mishkin's 1982 monkey work, which treated the dorsal stream as coding spatial location). The 7th edition follows **Milner and Goodale**, for whom the dorsal stream is the **"how"** pathway, for **vision-for-action**, and the book notes the stream was "previously described as" the where pathway. The ventral stream is "what" in both schemes. If an option offers "dorsal = where", it reflects the older key; "dorsal = how / action" is the 7th-edition answer. Both place the dorsal stream ending in **posterior parietal cortex** and the ventral stream ending in **inferotemporal cortex**.

### Findings: brain-damaged patients

The theory makes a clear neuropsychological prediction. Damage to the **dorsal** stream should leave vision-for-perception reasonably intact but **severely impair vision-for-action**. Damage to the **ventral** stream should do the reverse. Together these would form a **double dissociation**: two patient groups, each impaired on the task the other can do.

#### Optic ataxia (dorsal damage)

**Optic ataxia** is a disorder of precise **visually guided movement**, even though the patient's vision and their ability to move their arms are essentially intact. It follows damage to the **posterior parietal cortex** (the book's Figure 2.9, from Vesia and Crawford, 2012, shows that the patients' lesions overlap most in the superior and inferior parietal lobules, the superior occipital gyrus and the precuneus).

**Perenin and Vighetto (1988)** found that patients with optic ataxia had great difficulty **rotating the hand correctly** when reaching towards and into a large **oriented slot**. This fits the theory: dorsal damage, impaired visually guided action.

But optic ataxia does not fit the simple picture neatly, for three reasons.

1. **Reaching and grasping dissociate.** Slightly different regions of posterior parietal cortex are associated with **reaching** and with **grasping** (**Vesia & Crawford, 2012**), and some patients have more trouble with one than the other.
2. **Not every aspect of action is impaired.** **Jakobson et al. (1991)** studied **VK**, a woman with optic ataxia who had difficulty **grasping** objects even though her **initial planning** of the action was essentially intact.
3. **Perception is not fully intact, and action is not uniformly impaired.** **Pisella et al. (2009)** argued the "intact perception, impaired action" description is oversimplified. When patients could see their own hand, their visually guided action was severely impaired only in **peripheral vision**; in **central vision** there was much less impairment, which fits the observation that many optic ataxic patients can **drive** effectively.

#### Visual form agnosia (ventral damage): patient DF

**Visual form agnosia** is a severe difficulty in perceiving **shape** (what an object is) despite visual information reaching the cortex, combined with a reasonable ability to make accurate visually guided actions (agnosias are covered more fully in Chapter 3). The most studied case is **DF**.

- **Lesion and imaging (James et al., 2003).** DF's damage lies in the **ventral stream**, in the **lateral occipital complex** (the book's Figure 2.10). Her ventral stream was **no more active** for drawings of objects than for scrambled line drawings, which means it was not processing shape. Her **dorsal** stream, by contrast, was **highly active** when she grasped objects.
- **Recognition versus reaching (Milner et al., 1991).** Although her visual acuity was reasonable, DF could not identify **any** drawings of common objects. Yet she could reach out and grasp a pencil accurately whatever its orientation.
- **The posting task (Goodale & Milner, 1992).** DF held a card and faced a circular block with a slot cut in it. Asked to turn the card to **match** the slot's orientation (a perceptual judgement), she failed. Asked to move her hand forward and **post** the card through the slot (an action), she performed well. The same orientation information was available to action but not to perception.
- **Coloured objects (Dijkerman et al., 1998).** DF could not accurately **tell apart** several differently coloured objects (an object-recognition problem, consistent with ventral damage). But she **reached out and touched** them as accurately as healthy people, using their positions **relative to her own body** (egocentric, dorsal coding).
- **Irregular shapes (Goodale et al., 1994).** DF had two tasks with irregularly contoured shapes: **discriminate** between two of them, or **grasp** them firmly between thumb and index finger. She was very poor at discrimination; the authors concluded she had no difficulty placing her fingers on suitable opposition points when grasping.

**The reanalysis (Himmelbach et al., 2012).** Himmelbach and colleagues argued that last conclusion went too far. They reanalysed DF's grasping data from Goodale et al. (1994) against **20 healthy controls** (the book's Figure 2.11 plots the percentage of errors for discriminating and for grasping). DF's grasping was **substantially worse** than the controls', and comparisons on other reaching and grasping tasks gave the same picture. DF's action is better than her perception, but it is **not normal**.

**Summary of the patient evidence.** The differences between visual form agnosia and optic ataxia are real and theoretically important, but the picture is not tidy: **both kinds of patient have some problems with both perception and action**. That is the main reason the "double dissociation" is described later as not clear-cut.

> **Trap: optic ataxia versus visual form agnosia.** Optic ataxia = **dorsal** (posterior parietal) damage; can **see and recognise** the object but **cannot reach or grasp it accurately** (Perenin & Vighetto, 1988; VK). Visual form agnosia = **ventral** (lateral occipital) damage; **cannot recognise or match** the shape but can **reach for, grasp and post** to it (DF). The direction errors in MCQs are almost always a swap of these two. A further twist: in the slot tasks, Perenin and Vighetto's optic ataxics **failed to orient the hand while reaching**, whereas DF **succeeded at posting** but **failed at matching**.

### Visual illusions

#### The paradox and Milner and Goodale's answer

Hundreds of studies have examined visual illusions. Two famous ones:

- **Müller-Lyer illusion** (book's Figure 2.12): two vertical lines of equal length look different in length because of the fins at their ends; one looks clearly longer, though a ruler shows they are the same.
- **Ebbinghaus illusion** (Figure 2.13): a central circle surrounded by **small** circles looks **larger** than an identical central circle surrounded by **large** circles. (Put the other way round, as the book does: the circle ringed by larger circles looks smaller.)

There are many more, and their existence raises a paradox. If our perceptual processes are so easily fooled, how has the species done so well?

**Milner and Goodale (1995)** offered a neat answer. Almost all illusion studies have tested the **vision-for-perception** system (people judge or report how long or big something looks). But when we walk along a cliff edge or dodge traffic, we mainly use the **vision-for-action** system, which they argued gives **accurate** information about where we are relative to objects. This yields a striking prediction: **actions** directed at illusory figures (pointing, grasping) should be **unaffected** by the Müller-Lyer, Ebbinghaus and similar illusions, even though the same people's perceptual judgements are fooled.

#### Findings

**Pointing at the Müller-Lyer (Bruno et al., 2008).** A **meta-analysis** of **33 studies** in which observers **pointed rapidly** at one of the Müller-Lyer figures (designed to engage vision-for-action) found a mean illusion effect of **5.5%**. For comparison, **11 studies** using standard **perceptual** procedures such as verbal estimates of length gave a mean illusion effect of **22.4%**, about **four times** larger. This clearly supports the perception-action distinction. But note the gap between "reduced" and "absent": the theory seems to predict **no** illusion at all for rapid pointing, and 5.5% is not zero.

**The hollow-face illusion (Króliczak et al., 2006).** A realistic **hollow mask** viewed from the front looks like a normal face bulging towards you (book's Figure 2.14). Króliczak and colleagues placed a small target (a magnet) on either the hollow mask or a normal face and compared three tasks:

| Task | System assumed to be used | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Draw** the target's position | Vision-for-perception | **Strong illusion** |
| Make a **fast flicking** finger movement to the target | Vision-for-action | **Very accurate**, essentially illusion-free |
| Make a **slow pointing** movement to the target | Vision-for-action (expected) | Illusion **fairly strong** |

The first two rows are exactly what the theory predicts. The third is not: a slow pointing movement should still be an action. Króliczak et al.'s explanation is that when an action is **preceded by conscious cognitive processing** (time to think about it), it draws on the **vision-for-perception** system as well as the vision-for-action system.

**Grasping is different from pointing (Franz & Gegenfurtner, 2008).** With three-dimensional versions of illusions, actions can include grasping. Franz and Gegenfurtner reviewed Müller-Lyer studies and found:

| Condition | Mean illusion effect |
|---|---|
| **Perceptual** tasks | **11.2%** |
| Grasping with **full visual guidance** of the hand | **4.4%** |
| Grasping **without** being able to monitor the hand | **9.4%** |

So grasping is not uniformly immune: when the hand cannot be watched, the illusion effect is close to the perceptual one. The book's interpretation is that the **action programming** needed for grasping requires the **ventral** stream. Note that the 7th edition presents action as showing **reduced**, not zero, illusion effects, and treats the residue as a problem for the original theory.

### Action: planning plus motor responses

The illusion findings already show that vision-for-action does not rely only on the dorsal stream. **Milner and Goodale (2008)** themselves accepted that most grasping tasks involve **some ventral processing** as well as dorsal. The ventral stream is especially likely to be involved when:

1. **Memory** is needed (for example, there is a delay between the stimulus disappearing and the start of the grasp).
2. There is **time to plan** the movement (as in Króliczak et al.'s slow pointing condition).
3. **Planning which movement** to make is necessary.
4. The action is **unpractised or awkward**.

The studies that show this:

- **Memory helps optic ataxics (Milner et al., 2003).** Two patients with optic ataxia (dorsal damage) reached and grasped either **immediately** or **a few seconds after** the target object had disappeared. Surprisingly, they did **better** when relying on **memory**. Milner and colleagues' explanation is that the delay forced them to use their **intact ventral stream**, which holds sustained representations, instead of their damaged dorsal stream, which only works in real time.
- **Effective versus appropriate grasping (Creem & Proffitt, 2001).** You can grasp a toothbrush **effectively** by its bristles, but **appropriate** grasping takes it by the **handle**. Appropriate grasping requires **stored knowledge** about the object and so, Creem and Proffitt argued, the ventral stream. As predicted, disrupting participants' ability to retrieve object knowledge harmed **appropriate** grasping much more than **effective** grasping.
- **Planning versus motor programming (van Doorn et al., 2007).** Participants saw a rod of varying length forming part of a Müller-Lyer figure and had to pick it up end to end, choosing a **one-handed or two-handed** grip. The **choice** of grip (a planning decision) was influenced by the illusion: they switched to two hands at **shorter** rod lengths when the fins pointed **outwards** (the configuration that makes a line look longer) than when they pointed inwards. But their **maximum grip size** (motor programming) was **unaffected**. So the ventral stream seems to guide **action selection**, and the dorsal stream the **motor programming**.
- **Small task changes matter (Canal-Bruland et al., 2013).** In two experiments with a **one-tailed** Müller-Lyer figure, participants either **estimated the distance** to the end of the shaft (perception) or **threw a beanbag** to that point (action). Standing at the base of the figure, they showed the usual pattern: an illusion in verbal estimates but not in throwing. Standing **1.5 metres behind** the base, they showed **comparable illusion effects on both** measures. Why this apparently minor change matters is unclear.

### Dorsal stream: conscious awareness?

The theory also claims that **dorsal-stream processing is not accessible to consciousness**. This is hard to test, because on most visually guided tasks the two streams both contribute. A simple task helps: **reaching for a target while avoiding obstacles** (for example two rods). **Milner (2012)** reviewed evidence that this task can be done by the **dorsal stream alone** and **without awareness**.

- **Patient VE (McIntosh et al., 2004).** VE was a man who had a **right-hemisphere stroke** and as a result showed **extinction**: when stimuli were presented in both visual fields at once, he was largely unaware of the one in his **left** field. In an obstacle-avoidance task (the book's Figure 2.15: the hand moves from a start position to a target strip of grey tape, with one or two rods placed in between), VE's reach **took full account** of a rod in his left field even on trials where he said he had **not seen** it. His action used visual information he was not conscious of.
- **Optic ataxics cannot avoid obstacles (Schindler et al., 2004).** Two patients with optic ataxia (extensive dorsal damage) showed **no** ability to adjust their reaching to avoid obstacles. Put together with VE, this suggests the obstacle-avoidance computation is dorsal: intact dorsal plus no awareness (VE) works; damaged dorsal (optic ataxia) fails.
- **Healthy observers (Roseboom & Arnold, 2011).** Visual stimuli were followed by **masks** so that participants could not see them. Although they had **no conscious awareness** of the stimuli's orientation, they **learned to orient their grasp** reasonably appropriately. Visually guided action can therefore occur without awareness, probably via the dorsal stream.

This may seem strange, since when you reach for something you are usually fully aware of the object, any obstacles and your hand. The resolution is that awareness is **not essential** for visually guided action, but it is probably **useful**: Roseboom and Arnold's participants performed well **above chance**, yet still clearly **worse** than they would have with visible stimuli.

> **Trap: extinction is not blindsight and not neglect.** VE had **extinction**: he could detect a single left-field stimulus but lost awareness of it when a competing stimulus was presented on the right at the same time. Blindsight (2.6) follows **V1** damage and involves a true field defect with above-chance forced-choice responding. **Neglect** (Chapter 5) is a failure to attend to the contralesional side even when only one stimulus is present. All three can involve "responding to what you deny seeing", which is why they are confused.

### Overall evaluation of the two-systems approach

**Strengths.** The theory has been very influential, and its central claim of two visual systems ("what" and "how") is probably **broadly correct**. Three kinds of support:

1. Patients with **optic ataxia** (dorsal damage) and **visual agnosia** (ventral damage) show the predicted **double dissociation**.
2. **Illusion** studies often find the surprising but predicted result that **action** (pointing, sometimes grasping) is almost immune to illusory effects.
3. The claim that dorsal processing is **not accessible to consciousness** has reasonable support (VE, Roseboom & Arnold).

**Five limitations:**

1. **Too much emphasis on independence, too little on interaction.** **Cloutman (2013)** reviewed the evidence and concluded that information passes **directly between the two pathways at many stages and locations**.
2. **How can they interact, given how differently they work?** One proposal is the **"frame and fill"** approach (**Chen et al., 2007**): fast, coarse **dorsal** processing supplies the **frame**, and slower, more precise **ventral** processing supplies the **fill**.
3. **Action does not depend almost entirely on the dorsal stream.** This original claim, since **abandoned by Milner and Goodale (2008)** themselves, is wrong. The dorsal stream probably works alone, or nearly alone, only for **rudimentary actions to simple targets** (**Schenk & McIntosh, 2010**); the ventral stream is involved whenever **planning and programming** are needed.
4. **The double dissociation is not clear-cut.** Some visual form agnosics have severe **action** problems too (**Himmelbach et al., 2012**, on DF), and some optic ataxics have impaired **perception** for peripheral stimuli (**Pisella et al., 2009**).
5. **Firm predictions are hard to make.** Most visual tasks use **both** streams, and small task changes shift the balance between them (**Canal-Bruland et al., 2013**).

> **Trap: older versus newer versions of the theory.** An older exam key may treat Milner and Goodale's model as "two independent systems: ventral for perception, dorsal for action, and action is immune to illusions". The 7th edition's version is **two interacting systems**: the dorsal stream alone handles only simple, immediate actions; the ventral stream joins in when memory, planning, object knowledge or awkward movements are involved; and action shows **reduced**, not absent, illusion effects. A "which is NOT a limitation" item will typically include one genuine strength (for instance "patients show the predicted double dissociation") among the limitations.

## 2.4 Colour Vision

### Why have colour vision at all?

An old black-and-white film is perfectly easy to follow, so colour is clearly not essential for making sense of a scene. The book gives three reasons it is still worth having.

1. **Figure-ground separation.** Colour often makes an object **stand out from its background**. The reverse case shows the same point: chameleons change colour to **blend in** with their surroundings, which makes them harder for predators to detect.
2. **Recognising and categorising objects.** Colour helps you judge, for example, whether fruit is under-ripe, ripe or over-ripe.
3. **Predictive coding.** **Predictive coding** means processing mainly the **unpredicted** parts of the sensory input (**Huang & Rao, 2011**). If you expect ripe fruit, colour lets you rapidly pick out anything that does not fit the prediction, such as a patch of discolouring.

### Three qualities of colour

"Colour" has three main qualities:

| Quality | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Hue** | The colour itself, what distinguishes red from yellow or blue |
| **Brightness** | The perceived **intensity** of the light |
| **Saturation** | How **vivid or pale** a colour is; it is affected by how much **white** is mixed in |

> **Trap.** **Saturation** is vividness versus paleness (the amount of white present), not lightness. **Brightness** is perceived intensity. A pastel pink and a vivid red can have the same hue; they differ in **saturation**.

### Trichromacy theory

The **cones** of the retina are specialised for colour; each contains a light-sensitive **photopigment**. According to **trichromatic** (three-colour) **theory** there are **three kinds of cone**:

| Cone type | Most sensitive to | Responds most to stimuli usually seen as |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-wavelength (S)** | Short wavelengths | **Blue** |
| **Medium-wavelength (M)** | Medium wavelengths | **Yellow-green** |
| **Long-wavelength (L)** | Long wavelengths | **Orange-red** |

How do we see the many other colours? Most stimuli activate **two or all three** cone types to different degrees, and the colour we see is set by the **relative** amount of activity in each type. No single cone "knows" what colour it is looking at; colour is read out from the **ratio** of activity across the three.

(Classic background: this is the Young-Helmholtz theory, from Thomas Young and Hermann von Helmholtz in the nineteenth century; Indian papers often ask for it by that name.)

#### Colour deficiencies fit trichromacy

Many colour deficiencies are what trichromacy theory would predict. Most colour-deficient people have **dichromacy**: one cone class is **missing**.

| Type | Missing cones |
|---|---|
| **Protanopia** | **Long**-wavelength (red) cones |
| **Deuteranopia** | **Medium**-wavelength (green) cones |
| **Tritanopia** | **Short**-wavelength (blue) cones |

A memory aid that also tells you the order: proto-, deutero- and trito- mean first, second and third, and the cone classes go **red (L), green (M), blue (S)**.

#### Why three cones?

Three cone types make a very **efficient** system: with so few types we can still discriminate **millions** of colours. But three is not the only workable design; many animal species have **more than three** cone types, so good colour vision can be achieved in several ways.

### Opponent processes

#### What trichromacy cannot explain

Trichromacy theory has two gaps. It says nothing about what happens **after** the cones are activated, and it cannot explain **negative afterimages**. Stare at a coloured square for several seconds, then look at a white surface, and you see a ghost square in the **complementary** colour (complementary colours are pairs that make white when combined). A **green** square leaves a **red** afterimage; a **blue** square leaves a **yellow** one.

#### Hering's three opponent processes

**Hering (1878)** explained afterimages by proposing **three opponent processes** in the visual system. Each is a single channel that signals one colour when it responds in one direction and the opposite colour when it responds in the other:

1. **Red-green channel**: green one way, red the other.
2. **Blue-yellow channel**: blue one way, yellow the other.
3. **Achromatic channel**: white at one extreme, black at the other.

**Why opponency is useful.** Evidence (**Tailor et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2002**) indicates that these three opponent dimensions give **maximally independent** representations of colour information. Because each channel carries information the others do not, the code is very **efficient** for chromatic stimuli.

#### Evidence for opponent processes

1. **Opponent cells** have been found in monkeys (**DeValois & DeValois, 1975**).
2. The theory **explains negative afterimages.** Prolonged viewing of a colour, say red, drives the relevant opponent process to one extreme. When you then look at a white surface, the process swings to its **other extreme**, and you see the opposite colour, green.
3. The theory **explains some colour deficiencies.** **Red-green deficiency** occurs when the long- or medium-wavelength cones are damaged or missing, so the red-green channel cannot be used. **Blue-yellow deficiency** occurs when people lacking the short-wavelength cones cannot use the blue-yellow channel effectively.

### Dual-process theory (Hurvich & Jameson, 1957)

**Hurvich and Jameson (1957)** combined the two theories into a **dual-process theory**. The **three cone types** of trichromacy are the first stage; their signals are sent to **opponent cells**, which form **three channels** (the book's Figure 2.16, from Mather, 2009). How each channel is computed:

| Channel | Computed from | Signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Achromatic** (light-dark) | **Sum** of **medium** and **long** cone activity (non-opponent) | Lightness |
| **Blue-yellow** | **(Medium + long)** compared with **short** | Blue or yellow, depending on the direction of the difference |
| **Red-green** | **Medium** compared with **long** | Red or green, depending on the direction of the difference |

The two theories, which were historically rivals, are therefore both right, at **different stages**: trichromacy describes the **receptors**, opponent processing describes the **next stage of coding**.

> **Trap: trichromatic versus opponent-process versus dual-process.** Trichromacy (three **cone** types, colour from their relative activity) explains **colour matching and dichromacy** but **not afterimages**. Opponent-process theory (**Hering, 1878**; three **channels**: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white) explains **afterimages** and red-green / blue-yellow deficiencies. **Dual-process theory (Hurvich & Jameson, 1957)** says cones feed opponent channels, so both are correct at different stages. Hering's date is **1878**; Hurvich and Jameson's is **1957**; the monkey opponent cells are **DeValois and DeValois (1975)**.

#### Evaluation

There is much experimental support for opponent-process theory, but it is **greatly oversimplified** (**Solomon & Lennie, 2007; Conway et al., 2010**). Two awkward facts:

1. The **proportions** of the different cone types **vary considerably** across people, yet this has surprisingly **little effect** on their colour perception.
2. The **arrangement** of cone types in the eye is **fairly random**, which ought to make it hard for opponent processes to compare neighbouring cone types reliably, yet colour vision works.

The conclusion is that colour vision is far more complicated than the classic theories assumed.

### Colour constancy

**Colour constancy** is the tendency for a surface or object to be seen as the **same colour** even when the **wavelengths in the illuminant** (the light source shining on it) change. Its existence proves that colour vision does **not** depend only on the wavelengths reflected from objects, because those wavelengths change with the lighting while the perceived colour does not.

**Why it matters.** Without colour constancy, familiar objects would change colour dramatically every time the lighting changed (daylight, tube light, a yellow bulb), which would make rapid, accurate recognition very difficult.

**Why it is impressive.** Constancy feels effortless, but the input is a mess. The book's Figure 2.17 (from Brainard and Maloney, 2011) is a photograph of what anyone would call a **blue mug**; sampling the light at different points on its surface reveals many other colours. The light reaching the eye from each point depends on the mug's own surface, on the illuminant, and on light **reflected onto the mug from other objects**. The visual system has to undo all of that.

#### How good is colour constancy?

- **Natural conditions (Granzier et al., 2009a).** Observers learned names for **six** uniformly coloured papers that were similar to one another, then tried to identify single papers shown at various indoor and outdoor locations with very different lighting. They identified **55%** correctly. That sounds modest, but given how similar the papers were and how different the lighting was, it is good performance.
- **Judgement versus experience (Reeves et al., 2008).** Reeves and colleagues distinguished what we **experience** from what we **judge** about the world. As you walk towards a fire it feels hotter and hotter, but your judgement of how hot the fire is need not change. Similarly, constancy was **high** when observers judged whether two stimuli under different illuminants were **objectively** the same surface, but much **lower** when they rated the **subjective** similarity of hue and saturation. The visual system can be used flexibly, and the instructions determine which use is measured.
- **2-D versus 3-D scenes.** One might expect better constancy with rich, naturalistic **three-dimensional** scenes than with flat displays, because they carry more information. In practice that is **not** found (**Foster, 2011**). **de Almeida et al. (2010)** found **comparably high** constancy for two- and three-dimensional stimuli, perhaps because observers attend to only a small fraction of the extra information 3-D scenes provide.

#### Mechanism 1: estimating the illumination?

If observers could estimate the illuminant accurately, they could discount it and achieve constancy. They sometimes use illuminant information, but they are generally **very insensitive** to changes in illumination (**Foster, 2011**).

**Granzier et al. (2009b)** tested this directly. Observers estimated a **lamp's colour** from the light reflected by the scene it lit, and also judged the colour of a **surface** in the scene. Two findings: surface-colour judgements were **much more accurate** than lamp-colour judgements, and there was **no correlation** between how accurately a person judged the lamp and how accurately they judged the surface. So colour constancy does **not** seem to depend on estimating the illuminant.

#### Mechanism 2: local colour contrast and cone-excitation ratios

**Land's retinex theory (e.g., Land, 1986)** proposed that we perceive a surface's colour by comparing how well it reflects short, medium and long wavelengths with how well **adjacent surfaces** do. In other words, we use **local colour contrast**: colour is judged relative to the neighbours, so a change in illuminant that affects everything equally cancels out.

**Kraft and Brainard (1999)** put objects (a tube wrapped in tin foil, a pyramid, a cube and others) in a box and changed the illumination substantially. By removing cues one at a time they showed how much each contributes:

| Viewing condition | Colour constancy |
|---|---|
| **Full viewing** | **83%** |
| **Local contrast** unavailable | **53%** |
| **Local and global contrast** unavailable | **39%** |
| Also no **reflected highlights** from glossy surfaces | **11%** |

The most important single factor was **local contrast**. **Global contrast**, in which the cone responses from the target are compared with those across the **whole scene**, also mattered, as did **specular highlights**.

**Foster and Nascimento (1994)** developed Land's idea into an influential theory based on **cone-excitation ratios**. Their key discovery can be seen with two surfaces and two illuminants. Suppose that switching from illuminant 2 to illuminant 1 makes the long-wavelength (red) cones respond **three times** as strongly to surface 1. Then the same **threefold** change occurs for surface 2. Because the illuminant changes both surfaces by the same factor, the **ratio** of cone responses between the two surfaces stays essentially **invariant** across illuminants. A visual system that tracks these ratios can therefore cancel out the illuminant.

Support: much evidence favours the importance of cone-excitation ratios (**Foster, 2011**); for example, **Nascimento et al. (2004)** found that the level of constancy observed across conditions could be **predicted** from cone-excitation ratios.

Limitation: the theory works elegantly in **simple** environments but has limited value in **complex** ones (**Brainard & Maloney, 2011**). Reflections from other objects (as on the blue mug) and **multiple light sources** at once both make constancy harder, and the theory does not fully explain how observers cope with them.

#### Mechanism 3: familiarity

Knowledge of objects' typical colours (bananas are yellow) also affects constancy. **Hansen et al. (2006)** had observers adjust the colour of photographs of **fruit** until each looked **grey**. They **over-adjusted**: when a banana was actually grey it still looked slightly yellow to them, so they pushed it towards a slightly **bluish** hue (the opponent of yellow) to make it look grey. Objects tend to be seen in their **typical** colour even when the actual colour differs. This is a **top-down** contribution.

#### Mechanism 4: chromatic adaptation

**Chromatic adaptation** is the reduction, over time, in the visual system's sensitivity to a particular illuminant. Standing outside after dark, you notice how **yellow** the artificial light in people's windows is; after a while inside a room lit by that light, it no longer looks yellow. **R.J. Lee et al. (2012)** exposed observers to sudden changes between **sunlight** and **skylight** illumination. The results were complex, but some aspects of adaptation took about **six seconds**. This fairly fast reduction in the impact of a lighting change improves constancy.

#### Mechanism 5: cells that show constancy

- **Zeki (1983)** found cells in monkey **V4** that responded strongly to a **red** patch lit by **red** light, but did **not** respond when the red patch was replaced by a green, blue or white patch, even though the light then reflected was dominated by the same long wavelengths and would generally look red. These cells were responding to the **surface's actual colour**, not to the reflected wavelengths.
- **Kusunoki et al. (2006)** found similar results when they changed the **background illumination** around monkey V4 neurons, concluding that these neurons show the property of colour constancy and so can reflect colour **perception**.

> **Trap: colour constancy versus chromatic adaptation.** **Colour constancy** is the **outcome** (a surface keeps its perceived colour under different illuminants). **Chromatic adaptation** is **one mechanism** contributing to it (reduced sensitivity to the prevailing illuminant over time, partly within about **six seconds**, R.J. Lee et al., 2012). Other contributors are **local and global contrast** (Land's retinex theory; Kraft & Brainard, 1999), **cone-excitation ratios** (Foster & Nascimento, 1994), **familiar colour** (Hansen et al., 2006) and **V4 cells** (Zeki, 1983; Kusunoki et al., 2006). Estimating the illuminant is **not** a major contributor (Granzier et al., 2009b).
#### Evaluation of colour constancy research

**Strengths.** Colour constancy is complex and observers often fall well short of complete constancy, so it is not surprising that the visual system uses an **"all hands on deck"** approach, with several factors contributing. Of special importance are **cone-excitation ratios**, which stay nearly invariant across lighting changes. **Top-down** factors such as memory for familiar colours also play a part, and the discovery of **V4 cells** that show constancy has advanced understanding of the brain mechanisms.

**Limitations:**

1. There is **no comprehensive theory** of how the various factors **combine** to produce constancy.
2. Most research uses relatively **simple** visual environments, so constancy in complex environments is poorly understood.
3. More research is needed on why the degree of constancy depends so heavily on the **exact instructions** given to observers (the Reeves et al., 2008, problem).

## 2.5 Depth Perception

### The problem and the idea of a cue

The image on each retina is **two-dimensional**, yet we see a **three-dimensional** world laid out in depth. Turning one into the other is a major achievement, and an essential one: picking things up, deciding whether it is safe to cross a road and avoiding cliff edges all need a 3-D representation of the surroundings.

Depth perception relies on many **cues**. **Ernst and Bülthoff (2004)** define a cue as any sensory information that gives rise to a sensory estimate. Two properties of cues shape the rest of the section:

1. **Every cue is ambiguous**, so it would be unwise to rely totally on any one.
2. **Cues often conflict.** In a cinema or on a television, some cues (for example the **stereo** cues from your two eyes) say that everything is at the **same distance**, the screen, while others (perspective, shading) say some things are nearer than others.

In everyday life, movement of the observer or of objects also provides depth information, and some depth cues are not visual at all (touch, hearing). The book concentrates on **visual cues that are available even when observer and objects are stationary**, and divides them into three kinds:

| Kind | Needs | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Monocular** | **One eye** (but usable with both) | Information in a single retinal image; the world still looks deep with one eye closed |
| **Binocular** | **Both eyes together** | Comparison of the two retinal images |
| **Oculomotor** | The eye muscles | Sensations from contraction of the muscles around the eye; uses **kinaesthesia** (the muscle sense) |

> **Trap: monocular, binocular, oculomotor.** **Motion parallax** is **monocular** (it works with one eye and head movement), even though it involves motion. **Vergence** and **accommodation** are **oculomotor**, not binocular, in the 7th edition's scheme (accommodation even works with one eye). The only **binocular** cue discussed is **binocular disparity**, which produces **stereopsis**.

### Monocular cues

Monocular cues are also called **pictorial cues**, because artists use them to create the impression of depth on a flat canvas.

#### Linear perspective

Parallel lines running directly away from the viewer seem to get **closer together** as they recede, like the edges of a long straight road. This convergence gives a powerful impression of depth in a flat drawing.

How well perspective works depends on **viewing distance**. **Todorović (2009)** used an engraving by the Dutch artist **Jan Vredeman de Vries** (1604) to show this: viewed from a distance the drawing looks distinctly odd, but viewed from **very close** it produces a convincing 3-D effect. A perspective drawing is "correct" only from the viewpoint it was constructed for.

#### Texture gradient

Most surfaces (carpets, cobbled roads) have texture. When a textured surface slants away from you, the texture elements get smaller and more densely packed with distance. The **rate of change** of texture density from the front to the back of the slanting surface is a **texture gradient** (**Gibson, 1979**). **Sinai et al. (1998)** found that observers judged the distance of objects up to **seven metres** away well when the ground in between was **uniformly textured**, but **systematically overestimated** distances when there was a **gap** in the texture, such as a ditch.

#### Shading

Flat two-dimensional surfaces do not cast shadows, so **shading** signals a 3-D object. **Ramachandran (1988)** showed a display of many similar circular patches, some lit by one light source and the rest by another. Observers wrongly assumed there was a **single** light source, and so assigned different depths to different patches: some **dents** were seen as **bumps**. The assumption of a single light source determined how the shading was read.

#### Interposition

When a nearer object **hides part of** a farther one, that is **interposition** (occlusion). Its strength shows in **Kanizsa's (1976) illusory square** (book's Figure 2.20). Four circles each have a wedge cut out, arranged so the wedges form the corners of a square. Observers see a **square lying in front of four circles**, even though most of the square's contours are not actually drawn. The visual system prefers the interpretation in which something nearer is occluding the circles.

#### Familiar size

If you know how big an object really is, the size of its retinal image tells you how far away it is. The cue misleads when the object is **not** its usual size.

**Ittelson (1951)** had observers look through a **peephole**, which restricted them to **monocular** vision, at **playing cards** of different sizes, all placed at **2.28 metres**. Perceived distance was determined almost entirely by familiar size: observers assumed every card was normal-sized and read its retinal image accordingly. The 7th edition reports the two key values as **1.38 metres** and **4.56 metres** (pairing the half-size card with 1.38 m and the double-size card with 4.56 m).

> **Trap: the direction of the Ittelson effect.** Work it out from the logic. A **double-size** card at 2.28 m casts the retinal image a normal card would cast at **half** the distance, so it should look **nearer**; a **half-size** card casts the image of a normal card at **twice** the distance (4.56 m is exactly double 2.28 m), so it should look **farther**. The classic report of Ittelson's study (classic background) gives exactly that: double-size card seen at about 1.38 m, half-size card at about 4.56 m. The 7th edition's sentence pairs the numbers the other way round. If an item asks for the direction, go with the logic: **bigger-than-normal looks nearer, smaller-than-normal looks farther**; the numbers to remember are **2.28 m (actual), 1.38 m and 4.56 m**.

#### Blur

The book singles out **blur** as a cue whose importance has been "curiously underestimated". There is **no blur at the fixation point**, and blur increases with distance from fixation in depth, **more rapidly for nearer** distances than for farther ones. **Held et al. (2012)** argued that **binocular disparity** (below) is the more useful cue **close to** the fixation point, whereas **blur** is more useful than disparity **further from** it. Their findings fitted these assumptions (book's Figure 2.21 plots how effective each cue is at different distances). Blur and disparity are therefore **complementary**: each is strong where the other is weak.

#### Motion parallax

**Motion parallax** is movement of one part of the retinal image **relative to** another. When you move your head, **nearby** objects appear to move a lot in the **opposite** direction, while **distant** objects appear to move a little in the **same** direction. **Rogers and Graham (1979)** showed that motion parallax alone, with **all other cues removed**, can support **accurate** depth judgements.

#### Pictorial depth on flat displays is underestimated

Perspective, texture and interposition let observers see depth in 2-D pictures, but research with computer-generated 2-D displays has often found that **depth is underestimated** (**Domini et al., 2011**). The reason is that a flat display also supplies **cues to flatness** (binocular disparity, accommodation and vergence all signal one flat surface), and these may weaken the pictorial cues to depth.

### Oculomotor and binocular cues

The pictorial cues work just as well for a person with one eye. Depth perception also uses **oculomotor** cues, based on sensing the contraction of the muscles around the eyes.

- **Vergence**: the eyes **turn inwards** more to fixate a **very close** object than a distant one. The degree of convergence signals distance.
- **Accommodation**: the **lens thickens** to focus on a **close** object, changing its optical power; the effort involved signals distance.

Both are **very limited**. Each provides only **one value at a time**, so each can tell you the distance of only **one object** (the one you are fixating). And both are useful only for **close** objects, and even then are not very accurate.

#### Binocular disparity and stereopsis

The binocular cue is far more powerful. Because the eyes are a few centimetres apart, the images on the two retinas differ slightly; this is **binocular disparity**. The depth perception it produces is **stereopsis**.

Stereopsis is very powerful at **short** distances but fades fast: the disparity in the retinal images of an object falls by a **factor of 100** as its distance increases from **2 to 20 metres**. So stereopsis becomes much less effective beyond a few metres.

**Is stereo information used in object recognition?** It has sometimes been assumed that stereoscopic information is available early and feeds object recognition. **Bülthoff et al. (1998)** found otherwise: **scrambling** the stereoscopic information did **not** impair recognition of familiar objects, and observers did not even seem to notice the depth information was scrambled. Their **expectations** about the structure of familiar objects outweighed the misleading stereo information.

#### Autostereograms

A key step in stereopsis is **matching** corresponding features in the two eyes' inputs. Mismatches produce illusions, and **autostereograms** (the "Magic Eye" pictures) exploit this. An **autostereogram** is a flat image that contains depth information and looks three-dimensional when viewed in the right way. The **same repeating 2-D pattern** goes to each eye; if **vergence and accommodation are dissociated** (the eyes converge at one distance while focusing at another), two **neighbouring** repeats of the pattern are fused as though they were the same feature, and an object appears at a different depth from the background. A glance shows only a flat pattern; the 3-D image appears only if you stare while deliberately keeping the picture out of focus.

**Gómez et al. (2012)** asked why some people are much better at seeing autostereograms than others. Individual differences in **binocular disparity, vergence and accommodation** all predicted success or failure.

**Where disparity is processed.** **Parker (2007)** reviewed the brain areas that process disparity and concluded that disparity processing is **more detailed and sophisticated** in the **ventral** stream than in the dorsal stream.

> **Trap: vergence versus accommodation versus disparity.** **Vergence** = the eyes **rotate inward** for near objects. **Accommodation** = the **lens thickens** for near objects. Both are **oculomotor**, give one value at a time, and are useful only at **close** range. **Binocular disparity** = the **difference between the two retinal images**; it gives **stereopsis**, is powerful close up, and falls by a **factor of 100 from 2 to 20 m**. Autostereograms depend on **dissociating vergence from accommodation**.

### Cue integration

#### Additivity, selection, and reliability weighting

We usually have several depth cues at once, so how are they combined? **Bruno and Cutting (1988)** described two possibilities:

- **Additivity**: information from **all** cues is added together.
- **Selection**: information from only **one** cue is used.

More complex combinations are also possible. **Jacobs (2002)** argued that, to be accurate, we should give **more weight to reliable cues** than to unreliable ones, and since a cue reliable in one context may not be in another, the weights should be **flexible**. He derived two hypotheses:

1. **Less ambiguous** cues (for instance those giving **consistent** information) are treated as **more reliable**.
2. A cue is treated as reliable if the inferences drawn from it **agree with those from other cues**.

Advances in **virtual reality** made these hypotheses testable, because VR lets researchers control each cue precisely and put cues into conflict.

#### Findings

- **Hypothesis 1 (Triesch et al., 2002).** In a VR task, observers tracked an object defined by **colour, shape and size**. On each trial **two** of these attributes were **unreliable** (their values changed often). Over the course of each trial observers gave **increasing weight** to the reliable, consistent attribute and less to the unreliable ones.
- **Hypothesis 2 (Atkins et al., 2001).** Observers viewed and grasped **elliptical cylinders** in VR, with three cues to depth: **texture**, **motion** and **haptic** (touch). When haptic and texture cues agreed and motion disagreed, observers increasingly used **texture** and reduced their use of motion; when haptic and motion agreed and texture disagreed, they increasingly relied on **motion**. Whichever **visual** cue agreed with **touch** was preferred, and the preference grew with practice. Touch acted as the standard against which visual cues were calibrated.

**Small conflicts: weighted additivity.** Most research suggests that observers follow **additivity**, taking account of most or all cues, but **weight** the more reliable ones more heavily (**Landy et al., 2011**). This applies particularly to the many studies with only **small** conflicts between cues.

**Large conflicts: selection.** When cues conflict **strongly**, observers rely heavily or even exclusively on **one** cue, the **selection** strategy. This is sensible: if one cue says 10 metres and another says 90 metres, averaging them to 50 metres would be wrong on both counts. The cinema is the everyday case: we use perspective and texture to see a 3-D scene and **ignore** the disparity cue that says everything is on a flat screen.

- **Girshick and Banks (2009)** studied **slant** perception with **binocular disparity** and **texture gradient**. With a **small** conflict, observers used **both** cues. With a **large** conflict, perceived slant was determined **entirely by one** cue (disparity or texture). Observers were **not consciously aware** of the large conflict.

**Is the combination optimal?**

- **Lovell et al. (2012)** studied **binocular disparity** and **shading**. Disparity was the more informative cue overall, but the experimenters made it **less reliable** over trials. Observers combined the cues **optimally**, consistently giving more weight to whichever cue was more reliable.
- **Knill and Saunders (2003)** studied **texture** and **stereo** cues to surface slant. Texture becomes more informative as slant **increases**, so they predicted greater use of texture at **greater slants**, and that was confirmed. They also found **individual differences**: people best able to use texture weighted it heavily, and likewise for stereo. Overall the combination was **close to optimal**.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** A great deal is known about the cues used to judge depth. Information from different cues is usually combined to give **accurate** depth perception, often **additively**, but **one cue dominates** in some situations, notably strong conflict. As Jacobs (2002) argued, more weight goes to cues that are reliable and **consistent** with other cues, and the weighting is **flexible**, so a cue that has stopped being reliable is down-weighted. Overall, cues are weighted in a way that produces accurate perception.

**Limitations:**

1. **Laboratory versus life.** Everyday depth judgements are made with **many** cues and **no large conflicts**; laboratory studies often give **few** cues that **conflict strongly**. That unfamiliarity may sometimes produce suboptimal performance and limit generalisation (**Landy et al., 2011**).
2. **Untested assumption.** Research generally assumes the information from **single** cues is accurate, but this is **rarely tested**.
3. **How reliability is learned.** Observers learn fairly quickly which cues are reliable, which requires comparing cues against one another, and the processes involved are not well understood.

> **Trap: additivity versus selection.** **Small** cue conflict → **weighted additivity** (all cues used, reliable ones weighted more; Landy et al., 2011; Lovell et al., 2012; Knill & Saunders, 2003). **Large** conflict → **selection** of one cue (Bruno & Cutting, 1988; Girshick & Banks, 2009), with no conscious awareness of the conflict. The two strategy names come from **Bruno and Cutting (1988)**; the two reliability hypotheses come from **Jacobs (2002)**.

### Size constancy

**Size constancy** is the tendency for an object to look the **same size** whether its retinal image is large or small. A person walking towards you casts a steadily growing retinal image, yet does not seem to grow.

Many factors contribute, but **apparent distance** is especially important: a distant object with a tiny retinal image can still be judged large. Size and distance judgements are therefore tightly linked. Several other factors are reviewed before the size-distance relationship itself.

#### Familiar size versus distance (Haber & Levin, 2001)

**Haber and Levin (2001)** argued that the perceived size of objects usually depends on **memory of their familiar size** rather than only on perceptual information about distance. First they showed that participants could estimate the sizes of common objects very accurately **from memory alone**. Then they showed observers objects at a **close** (0 to 50 metres) or **distant** (50 to 100 metres) range and asked for size judgements. The objects came in three categories:

1. Objects of nearly **invariant** size (a tennis racquet, a bicycle).
2. Familiar objects of **variable** size (a television set, a Christmas tree).
3. **Unfamiliar** stimuli (ovals, triangles).

A **familiar-size** account predicts accuracy falling from invariant to variable to unfamiliar objects; a **distance** account predicts better accuracy for **all** categories at **close** range, where distance is judged better. The results (book's Figure 2.22) showed that familiar size matters, but judgements of **unfamiliar** objects were still **fairly accurate** (which familiar size cannot explain), and **viewing distance had practically no effect**.

#### Action ability: the golfers (Witt et al., 2008)

**Witt et al. (2008)** found that **good golfers** saw the hole as **larger** than less skilled golfers did when putting, and that golfers facing a **short** putt saw the hole as **larger** than those facing a long putt. They concluded that objects look larger when we can **act effectively** on them. However, **A.D. Cooper et al. (2012)** obtained evidence that these effects involve **memory rather than perception**.

#### The body size effect (Van der Hoort et al., 2011)

**Van der Hoort et al. (2011)** demonstrated the **body size effect**: the size of a body you mistakenly perceive as your own changes the perceived size of things around you. Participants wore head-mounted displays connected to cameras so that they saw the world from the viewpoint of a **doll**, which was either **small** or **large**. With the **small** doll's body, other objects looked **larger and further away** than with the large doll's. The effects were **stronger** when participants were induced to feel the doll's body was their own, which was done by touching the participant's body and the doll's body **at the same time**. The conclusion is that size and distance perception both depend partly on our lifelong experience of seeing the world **from our own body's perspective**; the body acts as a measuring stick.

#### Perceived distance drives perceived size: the Ames room

If size judgements depend on perceived distance, size constancy should **fail** when perceived distance is very different from actual distance. The **Ames room** (**Ames, 1952**; book's Figure 2.24) is built to make that happen. Its floor slopes and its back wall is not at right angles to the side walls, so one back corner is much farther from the viewer than the other. That extra distance is disguised by making the far corner correspondingly **taller**, so that through a **peephole** the room produces the same retinal image as an ordinary rectangular room.

Cues suggesting a normal room are so strong that observers wrongly assume two adults standing in the two back corners are at the **same distance**. Since the nearer adult casts a much larger retinal image, observers see that person as much **larger**. A person walking back and forth along the back wall appears to **grow and shrink**. Perceived distance is driving perceived size.

Familiarity can break the illusion. The book tells the story (from Ian Gordon) of a researcher's wife who came to the laboratory, saw her husband inside the room, and at once remarked that the room must be distorted. A very familiar person's size is a stronger prior than the room's apparent shape.

**Glennerster et al. (2006)** produced an even more dramatic effect in **virtual reality**. Participants walked through a virtual room that **expanded or contracted** considerably. Although **motion parallax** and their own movement gave plenty of information that the room was changing, **no participant noticed**. They made large errors in judging the sizes of objects at longer distances, because the strong expectation that a room does not change size made their perception of distance very inaccurate.

**Nguyen et al. (2011)** showed the same size-distance link in reverse. In a virtual tunnel, two poles were presented. During training their size stayed **constant** while observers judged their distance. Then the poles changed size: when they became **smaller**, observers **overestimated** their distance, and when they became **larger**, observers **underestimated** it. The familiar size learned in training controlled perceived distance.

#### Evaluation

Size perception and size constancy depend **mainly on perceived distance**, and the strongest evidence is that **distance misperceptions** (the Ames room, virtual environments) produce **systematic size distortions**. The relationship runs both ways: perceived distance is also strongly influenced by perceived or **familiar size** (Nguyen et al., 2011). Many other factors (familiar size, body size, the ability to act) influence size perception too.

What is missing is a **comprehensive theory**: little is known about the relative importance of these factors, the circumstances in which each matters more or less, or how they combine.

> **Trap: the Ames room.** Size constancy **fails** here because perceived **distance** is wrong: both people are seen as equally far away, so the nearer one looks **bigger**. The room has a sloping floor and an angled back wall and is viewed through a **peephole**; the illusion does not depend on binocular cues or on the people really differing in size.

## 2.6 Perception Without Awareness

### The question

Can we perceive aspects of the visual world **without being aware** that we are doing so? This is the question of **unconscious perception**, or **subliminal perception**: processing of a stimulus that is **below the threshold of conscious awareness** but can still influence behaviour. Common sense says no. The book says the evidence strongly says **yes**, but warns that the terms need care: a **thermostat** responds appropriately to changes in temperature, so in a loose sense it shows "unconscious perception", which is obviously not what psychologists mean. The interesting claim is that **the same system that normally produces conscious seeing** can also do substantial processing when awareness is absent.

The most important evidence comes from **blindsight** in patients with damage to **V1** (early visual cortex). **Ko and Lau (2012)** describe blindsight as the rare ability of V1-damaged patients to carry out visual tasks while claiming not to consciously see the relevant stimuli. The section covers blindsight first, then subliminal perception in healthy people.

### Blindsight

#### Origins: Riddoch's soldiers

During the **First World War**, many British soldiers were blinded by gunshot wounds that destroyed their **primary visual cortex (V1, BA17)**. They were treated by **George Riddoch**, a captain in the Royal Army Medical Corps, who reported that they **responded to motion** in parts of the visual field where they claimed to be blind (**Riddoch, 1917**). The name **blindsight** captures the apparent paradox.

#### What blindsight patients can do

**Farah (2001)** summarised the preserved abilities:

- **Always preserved to some degree:** **detecting and localising light**, and **detecting motion**.
- **Preserved in many patients:** discriminating **orientation, shape, direction of movement and flicker**.
- **Preserved in some cases:** colour vision mechanisms.

### In the real world: blindsight patient DB

Much of the early work on blindsight was done with **DB**, studied in detail at **Oxford** by **Larry Weiskrantz**, who coined the term "blindsight" (Weiskrantz, 2010, gives an overview).

**Cause.** DB had part of his **right** primary visual cortex (BA17) **surgically removed** to relieve frequent, severe **migraine**. This left him blind in the **lower part of his left visual field** (right-hemisphere damage, left-field loss, as the chiasma anatomy in 2.2 predicts).

**What he could do in the blind field.** DB could **detect** the presence of various objects and **point** to their approximate location. He could tell **moving** from **stationary** objects and **vertical** from **horizontal** lines.

**What he could not do.** He could **not** tell apart **rectangles of different sizes**, or **triangles with straight versus curved sides**. The pattern suggests he processed only **low-level features** and could not **discriminate form**.

**His experience.** Despite these abilities, DB reported **no conscious experience** in the blind field. **Weiskrantz et al. (1974)** reported that when he was shown a video of himself reaching for objects and judging the orientation of lines (played to his intact field), he was **openly astonished**.

**The stray-light objection and its answer.** **Campion et al. (1983)** pointed out that DB and similar patients are only **partially** blind, and proposed the **stray-light hypothesis**: patients succeed because light from the stimulus **scatters** off the surroundings onto parts of the visual field that **still work**. The test case is the **blind spot**, the region of the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eye and where there are no receptors at all. If stray light explained DB's performance, a stimulus on the blind spot should scatter light just as well, and he should do reasonably well there. In fact DB was **completely unable** to detect objects presented to his blind spot. Since his "blindsight" vanished exactly where the eye itself is blind, it cannot be explained by light leaking to functioning retina.

> **Trap.** DB's damage was **surgical** (removal of part of right V1 for **migraine**), not a war wound or a stroke; the **war wounds** belong to **Riddoch's (1917)** soldiers, and **GY's** damage came from a **car accident** in childhood. The **blind spot** test is the evidence **against** Campion et al.'s **stray-light hypothesis**.

#### How blindsight is assessed

Two measures are used together:

1. A **forced-choice** test, in which patients must **guess** (stimulus present or absent? which location?) or **point** at stimuli they say they cannot see.
2. The patient's **subjective report** that they cannot see stimuli in the blind region.

**Blindsight is defined by the combination**: **no** self-reported visual perception together with **above-chance** forced-choice performance.

**Do not overstate it.** **Cowey (2004)** warned against the impression that blindsight is normal vision with the experience taken away: on the contrary, it is marked by **severely impoverished** discrimination.

#### What V1 contributes

Blindsight patients usually have extensive V1 damage, but V1 itself probably does **not directly produce** visual awareness. **ffytche and Zeki (2011)** studied two patients with V1 lesions, **FB** and **GN**. Their blind-field experience was **degraded** compared with their sighted field, but both had **visual awareness** of blind-field stimuli, **especially moving ones**. V1's main job appears to be to **process and pass on** information to higher areas, so damage to it has **knock-on effects**, greatly reducing activation in later visual areas (**Silvanto, 2008**). Awareness is lost largely because the areas downstream are starved of input, not because V1 is the seat of awareness.

#### Which routes carry blindsight?

If V1 is destroyed, information must reach the rest of the brain another way. Two routes have been identified:

- **Superior colliculus (Tamietto et al., 2010).** In **GY**, **grey** stimuli in the blind field altered his responses to a consciously seen stimulus in his intact field. The effect was associated with activation in the **superior colliculus**, a **midbrain** structure. With **purple** stimuli, which hardly activate the superior colliculus, the effect **disappeared**. The colliculus is therefore part of a sensory-to-motor route **outside conscious visual experience**.
- **Lateral geniculate nucleus (Schmid et al., 2010).** In monkeys, blindsight depends partly on the **LGN**, which has **direct projections** to visual cortex areas **BA18 and BA19** that **bypass V1**.

> **Trap: the two bypass routes.** Blindsight routes that avoid V1: retina → **superior colliculus** (midbrain) → onward (Tamietto et al., 2010, with the grey versus purple stimuli in GY); and **LGN → BA18/BA19 directly**, skipping BA17 (Schmid et al., 2010, monkeys). Purple stimuli were used because they **barely activate the superior colliculus**.

### Findings from blindsight patients

**Avoiding subjective report altogether (van der Stigchel et al., 2010).** Self-reports might be inaccurate, so it helps to measure blindsight without them. **Two** blindsight patients made **eye movements** to a target in their **sighted** field, presented alone or together with a **distractor** in the **blind** field. The **direction** of their eye movements was influenced by the distractor although they were not aware of it.

**GY.** GY is one of the most studied blindsight patients. He has extensive damage to **left-hemisphere V1** and a smaller area of damage in the **right parietal** region, caused by a **car accident in childhood**.

**Inclusion and exclusion (Persaud & Cowey, 2008).** GY was shown a stimulus in the **upper or lower** part of his visual field. On **inclusion trials** he was told to report the location it appeared in. On **exclusion trials** he was told to report the **opposite** location (say "up" if it appeared below). The logic: if you are **conscious** of where the stimulus was, you can follow the exclusion instruction and say the other location; if information is available only **unconsciously**, it will still push you towards the **real** location even when you are trying to avoid it. GY tended to give the **real** location on **both** kinds of trial, so he had access to location information but **without conscious awareness** of it (book's Figure 2.25 shows the estimated conscious and unconscious contributions in each field). Healthy people showed a **large difference** between inclusion and exclusion performance, showing conscious access.

**Equating performance (Persaud et al., 2011).** Persaud and colleagues adjusted the stimuli to GY's intact and blind fields until his accuracy in judging the location of a vertical grating was **the same** in both. Even so, he reported **awareness** on far more trials in the intact field (**43%**) than in the blind field (**3%**). Most importantly, targets in the **intact** field produced substantially more activation in **prefrontal and parietal** areas. Same performance, different awareness, different brain activity.

**Graded awareness in GR (Overgaard et al., 2008).** The blindsight patient **GR** had to decide whether a **triangle, circle or square** had been shown in her blind field. Awareness was measured in two ways.

With a **four-point Perceptual Awareness scale**, accuracy tracked awareness closely:

| GR's rating | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| **Clear image** | **100%** |
| **Almost clear image** | **72%** |
| **Weak glimpse** | **25%** |
| **Not seen** | **0%** |

Combining "clear" and "almost clear", GR reported awareness on **54%** of trials and was correct on **83%** of those.

With a simple **yes/no** question, she seemed far **less** aware: she said she had seen the stimulus on only **21%** of trials, and was correct on **86%** of those.

The implication is that with a **sensitive** measure, **degraded conscious vision** sometimes underlies blindsight patients' above-chance performance. The claim that blindsight patients have **no** conscious experience in the blind field may sometimes be **wrong**; it may partly reflect the crudeness of yes/no questions.
### What do blindsight patients experience?

It is harder than it seems to say what blindsight patients actually experience. **GY** compared his experience to that of a sighted person who, with eyes shut against bright sunlight, can still tell the direction in which a hand is waved in front of them (**Beckers & Zeki, 1995**). On another occasion, asked about **qualia** (sensory experiences), he said that they happened only on very easy trials with very bright stimuli, and then added that he was not sure he really had them even then (**Persaud & Lau, 2008**).

#### Sub-types of blindsight (Danckert & Rossetti, 2005)

Because patients differ in what they retain, **Danckert and Rossetti (2005)** proposed three sub-types:

| Sub-type | What the patient can do | Stream used |
|---|---|---|
| **Action-blindsight** | Some ability to **grasp or point** at objects in the blind field. The **most studied** sub-type. **Baseler et al. (1999)** found GY showed activation in the **dorsal** stream but not the ventral stream to blind-field stimuli | **Dorsal** ("where") stream |
| **Attention-blindsight** | Can **detect objects and motion** and has a **vague conscious feeling** of objects while reporting not seeing them | Some use of the **dorsal** stream and **motor** areas |
| **Agnosopsia** | **Denies any** conscious awareness, but shows some ability to discriminate **form and wavelength** | Some use of the **ventral** stream |

#### Type 1 and Type 2 blindsight (Weiskrantz)

**Weiskrantz (e.g., 2004)** made a simpler two-way division:

- **Type 1** blindsight (similar to **agnosopsia**): **no** conscious awareness of blind-field stimuli.
- **Type 2** blindsight (similar to **attention-blindsight**): **some** awareness of blind-field stimuli.

The example of Type 2 is patient **EY**, who sensed a definite pinpoint of light, yet said it did not actually look like a light at all (**Weiskrantz, 1980**). Type 2 sounds suspiciously like **residual conscious vision**. A further complication is that patients tested many times may learn to use **indirect cues**: for example, partial success at guessing whether a stimulus moves left or right might depend on vague awareness of their **own eye movements**.

> **Trap: Type 1 versus Type 2, and the three sub-types.** **Type 1 = no awareness** (≈ agnosopsia); **Type 2 = some awareness** (≈ attention-blindsight; patient EY). **Action-blindsight** uses the **dorsal** stream and is the most studied; **agnosopsia** uses the **ventral** stream. The three sub-types are **Danckert and Rossetti (2005)**; Type 1 and 2 are **Weiskrantz**.

#### Are blindsight patients just very cautious? (Ko & Lau, 2012)

**Ko and Lau (2012)** argued that blindsight patients may have **more conscious visual experience** than usually believed. Their key proposal is that these patients adopt an unusually **conservative criterion** for detection, so they answer "no" almost every time they are asked whether they see something. The excess caution may arise because **prefrontal damage** impairs the ability to set the detection criterion appropriately. If so, the gap between what patients **report** and how they **perform** on forced-choice tests would partly be a **response bias**, not purely an absence of experience.

Support: Overgaard et al.'s (2008) finding that GR was much more reluctant to say she had seen stimuli under a yes/no question than her four-point ratings implied; and evidence that blindsight patients often have **prefrontal damage** as well as V1 damage.

(Classic background: "criterion" here is the signal detection theory sense; a conservative criterion means requiring a lot of evidence before saying "yes", which lowers both hits and false alarms without changing sensitivity.)

### Evaluation of blindsight research

**Reasons to accept blindsight as genuine:**

1. It has been shown in studies that appear to overcome the problems of **subjective, possibly distorted reports** (for example **Persaud & Cowey, 2008**, with inclusion and exclusion).
2. Some evidence does **not depend on verbal reports at all** (**van der Stigchel et al., 2010**, eye movements).
3. **Neuroimaging** shows that many blindsight patients have activation mainly or only in the **dorsal** stream (**Danckert & Rossetti, 2005**), and the dorsal stream is strongly associated with **non-conscious** processing (**Milner, 2012**), linking blindsight to the two-systems theory of 2.3.
4. There is evidence for subliminal processing of **emotional** information in such patients, known as **affective blindsight** (Chapter 15).

**Limitations:**

1. Patients **differ considerably**, and several seem to have **some conscious awareness** in the supposedly blind field.
2. Many probably have **more** conscious experience than yes/no judgements suggest, because they are **excessively cautious** about saying they saw something (**Ko & Lau, 2012; Overgaard, 2012**).
3. **GY**, one of the most studied patients, has **nerve-fibre connections** in his visual system that **healthy people lack** (**Bridge et al., 2008**). Some of his abilities may be specific to his reorganised brain, which limits generalisation to healthy people.

### Subliminal perception in healthy people

#### The Vicary hoax

In **1957** **James Vicary**, a market researcher whose business was struggling, announced that sales of popcorn and Coca-Cola had jumped after he repeatedly flashed messages urging viewers to eat popcorn and drink Coke during screenings of the film **Picnic**. Each flash supposedly lasted **1/300th of a second**, far too brief to be consciously seen. He later **admitted the study was fabricated**. The episode is still cited, but only as a warning.

#### Two thresholds (Merikle et al., 2001)

How do we decide whether someone consciously perceived a stimulus? **Merikle et al. (2001)** distinguished two criteria:

| Threshold | Defined by |
|---|---|
| **Subjective threshold** | The person's **failure to report** conscious awareness of the stimulus |
| **Objective threshold** | The person's **inability to make accurate forced-choice decisions** about it (for example, guessing at above-chance level whether it was a word) |

The **objective** threshold is the more **stringent**: below it, the person cannot even guess correctly. Evidence of processing below the objective threshold is therefore stronger evidence of unconscious perception.

**Two problems with both measures.**

1. **Reingold (2004)** pointed out that a valid measure must capture **all** the perceptual information available to consciousness and **only** conscious information. That is a demanding requirement.
2. It is hard, perhaps impossible, to prove that either measure shows **zero** awareness. For the subjective threshold, **Lamme (2010)** argued that limits of **attention and memory** often cause people's reports to **leave out** some of what they consciously experienced.

> **Trap: subjective versus objective threshold.** **Subjective** = the person **says** they saw nothing. **Objective** = the person **cannot perform above chance** on a forced-choice discrimination. The objective threshold is **lower and stricter**. Blindsight is defined at the **subjective** level (denies seeing, but performs **above chance** on forced choice); that is, blindsight patients are **below the subjective but above the objective** threshold.

#### Findings

**Masked number priming (Naccache et al., 2002).** Participants decided quickly whether a clearly visible target digit was **smaller or larger than 5**. Unknown to them, a **masked digit** was flashed for **29 ms** just before the target. The masked digit was either **congruent** (same side of 5 as the target) or **incongruent**. In one experiment, a **cue** signalling that the target was about to appear was present or absent. Two findings:

1. There was **no evidence of conscious perception** of the masked digits: no participant reported seeing any (subjective measure), and guessing whether the masked digit was above or below 5 was at **chance** (objective measure).
2. Responses to the targets were **faster on congruent trials** when the **cue was present**. So the masked digit had been processed, up to the level of its **numerical meaning**, without awareness.

**The exclusion logic in healthy people (Persaud & McLeod, 2008).** Persaud and McLeod argued that only information perceived **with awareness** can be used to **control** action. They flashed the letter **"b" or "h"** for **10 ms** (short) or **15 ms** (long). In the key condition, participants had to respond with the letter that was **not** shown. A participant aware that "b" appeared would deliberately say "h"; a participant unaware of it could not inhibit the letter that had in fact been processed.

| Exposure | Correct (non-presented letter) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **15 ms** | **83%** | Reasonable **conscious** awareness: they could exclude the seen letter |
| **10 ms** | **43%**, significantly **below chance** | The letter was **processed** (it biased them towards saying it) but **without awareness**, so they could not exclude it |

Below-chance performance is the striking part: random guessing would give 50%, so doing worse than chance means the unseen letter actively pulled responses towards itself.

**Comparing awareness measures (Sandberg et al., 2010).** Very few studies compare different measures of awareness. Sandberg and colleagues briefly presented one of **four shapes**, followed by a mask. Observers first identified the shape, then gave one of three subjective measures: (1) the clarity of their experience on the **Perceptual Awareness Scale (PAS)**; (2) **confidence** in their decision; or (3) **wagering** money on being right (post-decision wagering).

1. All three measures showed that **above-chance performance could occur without awareness**.
2. The **PAS** predicted accuracy **better** than the other two.
3. The PAS also revealed **more** conscious experience than the others, so it was the most **sensitive**.

The best way to measure conscious experience, then, is to ask people directly about the **content of their experience** (the PAS), not about their confidence or their willingness to bet.

**Graded or all-or-none? (Windey et al., 2014).** Sandberg's results suggested awareness is **graded**. **Windey et al. (2014)** argued that this is not always so. On each trial a coloured digit was flashed briefly. In the **low-level** task participants decided whether it was **red or blue**; in the **high-level** task, whether it was **smaller or larger than 5**. Awareness was **graded** for the low-level task but **all-or-none** for the high-level task. They explained this using **global workspace theory (Baars, 1988)**: early, non-conscious processing is carried out by many **independent specialised processors** (colour, motion and so on), while conscious awareness is typically associated with **synchronised activity across several brain areas** (Chapter 16). That abrupt, large-scale change in brain activity, mostly tied to **high-level** processing, gives all-or-none awareness.

**Brain-imaging and ERP evidence.** Neuroimaging and ERP studies, discussed fully in Chapter 16 (for example **Gaillard et al., 2009; Lamy et al., 2009; Melloni et al., 2007**), consistently show that stimuli observers are **unaware of** still activate **several brain areas**, as global workspace theory predicts. In one study (**Rees, 2007**), activity in face-processing and object-processing areas was recorded while **invisible** pictures of **faces or houses** were shown; the identity of the picture could be predicted from the activation patterns with almost **90%** accuracy. Even unseen stimuli can be processed fairly thoroughly.

#### Evaluation of subliminal perception research

**Strengths.** Subliminal perception was once very controversial, but there is now **convincing** evidence for it, both **behavioural** (**Naccache et al., 2002; Persaud & McLeod, 2008**) and from **brain activity** (**Melloni et al., 2007; Lamy et al., 2009**). Visual stimuli can be processed substantially, **up to and including the semantic level**, without awareness.

**Limitations:**

1. Apart from **Sandberg et al. (2010)**, there has been surprisingly little interest in **comparing the validity** of different subjective measures of awareness.
2. More studies need to collect **behavioural and neuroimaging** measures together, to clarify the strengths and weaknesses of each as an index of awareness.
3. More research is needed on **when** conscious visual experience is **graded** and when it is **all-or-none**.

## How the chapter fits together

Six threads run through all six modules.

1. **Specialisation, then integration.** The anatomy (P and M pathways, V1 to V5/MT, ventral and dorsal streams) keeps dividing vision into specialised parts, and every section then has to explain how the parts are put back together: the binding problem in 2.2, the interaction of the two streams in 2.3 ("frame and fill"), opponent channels built from three cone types in 2.4, the combination of depth cues in 2.5. The 7th edition's consistent answer is that the parts are **less separate** than the original theories claimed (Heywood & Cowey's cell counts; Seymour et al.'s early colour-motion conjunctions; Cloutman's cross-talk between streams).
2. **Strong original theories, softened by evidence.** Zeki's functional specialisation, Milner and Goodale's independent streams, and opponent-process theory all survive in a weakened form. V4 is not a colour centre; the dorsal stream acts alone only for simple actions; opponent processing is "greatly oversimplified". Exam questions on evaluation reward knowing exactly which claim was softened and by which study.
3. **Dissociations are the main method, and they are rarely clean.** Achromatopsia, akinetopsia, the first-order versus second-order motion patients, optic ataxia versus visual form agnosia, and blindsight are all used as dissociations. In each case the book then shows the dissociation is partial (achromatopsics keep some colour; DF's grasping is not normal; optic ataxics have peripheral perceptual problems; blindsight patients have some awareness).
4. **Perception and action use vision differently.** The definition at the start (perception also guides action) becomes Milner and Goodale's theory, reappears in the illusion research (action is less fooled), in the obstacle-avoidance work (action uses unseen information) and in blindsight (action-blindsight relies on the dorsal stream).
5. **The visual system uses context and knowledge, not just the retinal image.** Simultaneous contrast, colour constancy (local contrast, cone-excitation ratios, familiar colour), familiar size, the Ames room, the body size effect, and Bülthoff et al.'s scrambled stereo all show perception going beyond the light that reaches the eye.
6. **Awareness is not the same as processing.** Recurrent processing and awareness (2.2), dorsal processing without awareness (2.3), blindsight and subliminal priming (2.6) all show that a lot of visual processing happens without consciousness; the measurement problem (subjective versus objective thresholds, yes/no versus graded scales, response criteria) decides how much. This thread leads directly into Chapter 16.

## Worked example

*Three patients are referred to a neuropsychology clinic on the same day. Patient A, a 58-year-old man, had bilateral strokes in the posterior parietal cortex. He reads and names objects well, but when he reaches for a cup in his peripheral vision his hand goes to the wrong place and his grip is badly shaped. Patient B, a 35-year-old woman, suffered damage to the lateral occipital region after a hypoxic episode. She cannot name line drawings of common objects or say whether two irregular shapes are the same, yet she walks around the ward without bumping into things and picks up a pen without hesitation. Patient C, a 66-year-old woman, had a stroke affecting her right primary visual cortex. She says she is completely blind in her left visual field. The referring physician asks which tests would separate these patients and what the results would mean.*

**Start by placing each patient in the two-systems framework.** Patient A's lesion is in the **dorsal** stream, and his profile (good recognition, poor visually guided reaching) is **optic ataxia**. Patient B's lesion is in the **ventral** stream, and her profile (poor shape recognition, preserved action) is **visual form agnosia**, the DF pattern. Patient C has a **V1** lesion with a hemifield loss, the setting in which **blindsight** is looked for.

**Test 1: the slot task, in two versions.** Give each patient a card and a slot set at different orientations. In the **matching** version, they must turn the card to the slot's angle without moving towards it (a perceptual judgement). In the **posting** version, they must reach out and post the card. The theory predicts Patient B fails matching but succeeds at posting (Goodale & Milner, 1992), and Patient A shows the reverse, misrotating his hand as he reaches (Perenin & Vighetto, 1988). If the two patients show this pattern, you have a **double dissociation** in your own clinic.

**But do not over-read it.** Score the posting performance against healthy controls, not just against her own matching. Himmelbach et al. (2012) showed that DF's grasping, once compared with 20 controls, was substantially worse than normal; Patient B's "intact" action may be only relatively intact. For Patient A, test separately in **central** and **peripheral** vision: Pisella et al. (2009) found that optic ataxics with sight of their hand were badly impaired mainly in the periphery, which is why many can still drive. The answer to whether Patient A can drive safely may depend on that split.

**Test 2: a delay.** Ask Patient A to reach for an object a few seconds **after** it has been removed from view. Milner et al. (2003) found that optic ataxic patients reached **better** from memory, because the delay recruits the intact ventral stream. For Patient B a delay should make performance **worse**, because her action depends on the short-lived, real-time dorsal system.

**Test 3: the toothbrush.** Patient B may grasp a toothbrush **effectively** but not **appropriately** (by the handle), because appropriate grasping needs stored object knowledge in the ventral stream (Creem & Proffitt, 2001).

**Test 4: obstacle avoidance and blindsight in Patient C.** Patient C denies seeing anything on the left. Do not accept the verbal report as the whole story. First, use **forced choice**: present a light in the blind field at one of two locations and ask her to guess, or ask her to point. Above-chance accuracy combined with denial of seeing is the definition of blindsight. Second, use measures that bypass report: an eye-movement task with a blind-field distractor (van der Stigchel et al., 2010), or an obstacle-avoidance task with a rod placed on the left (as with VE, McIntosh et al., 2004). Third, rule out stray light by placing stimuli on the **blind spot**, where stray-light explanations predict success but genuine blindsight does not (the DB test). Fourth, use the **inclusion/exclusion** procedure: if she reports the true location even when told to report the opposite, the information is available but not conscious (Persaud & Cowey, 2008).

**How you ask about awareness changes the answer.** If you ask Patient C only "did you see anything, yes or no?", she may say no on almost every trial; on a four-point awareness scale she may report a "weak glimpse" on many of them, and her accuracy may track those ratings, as GR's did (Overgaard et al., 2008). Ko and Lau (2012) would add that, especially if she also has prefrontal damage, she may simply be using a very **conservative criterion**. A clinician who concludes "no residual vision" from yes/no questions alone may be wrong.

**What you should not conclude.** That Patient C has "normal vision without awareness" (Cowey, 2004: blindsight is severely impoverished); that Patient A's problem is motor (his arm moves normally, the deficit is in using vision to guide it); or that Patient B is "blind" (she uses vision for action every time she avoids a chair).

## MCQ traps

1. **Felleman and Van Essen (1991)**: about **25** visual areas. **Thorpe et al. (1996)**: photographs shown for **20 ms**, animal detected in the EEG by about **150 ms**.
2. **Rods**: about **125 million**, peripheral, **dim light**; one ganglion cell pools **hundreds of rods** but only **a few cones**.
3. **Retina-geniculate-striate pathway**: retina → **LGN (thalamus)** → **V1** → V2; **retinopy** is the book's word for retinotopic mapping.
4. **Optic chiasma**: only the **inner (nasal)** halves cross, so each visual **field** (not each eye) goes to the **opposite** hemisphere.
5. **P pathway**: colour and fine detail, mainly **cones**, linked to the **ventral** stream. **M pathway**: movement, mainly **rods**, linked to the **dorsal** stream. Koniocellular function is poorly understood.
6. Ventral stream ends in **inferotemporal** cortex, dorsal in **posterior parietal** cortex; ventral areas are **more than twice as large**.
7. **V1 = BA17**, **V2 = BA18**; **Hegdé and Van Essen (2000)**: **one-third** of V2 cells respond to complex shapes.
8. **Lamme (2006)**: feedforward sweep, then **recurrent** processing reaching V1 within **120 ms**; **Boehler et al. (2008)** linked strong recurrent processing to awareness.
9. **Zeki's areas**: V3/V3A form (moving shapes), **not** colour; **V4 colour** (plus orientation); **V5/MT motion** (in macaques all V5 cells motion-responsive, none colour-responsive).
10. **Achromatopsia** is **cortical** (in or near V4; **Bouvier & Engel, 2006**, meta-analysis), often **partial**, with spatial-vision problems. It is not dichromacy.
11. **V4 is not a colour centre**: it also handles space, shape and depth (Roe et al., 2012); V4 lesions spare some colour (Heywood & Cowey, 1999); V1 and V2 also process colour. **Banissy et al. (2012)**: TMS over V4 removed colour priming.
12. **Akinetopsia**: **LM** (Zihl et al., **1983**), **bilateral V5/MT**, tea looked frozen; **S.A. Cooper et al. (2012)**: a **61-year-old**, "freeze frames". **McKeefry et al. (2008)**: TMS over V5/MT **slowed** perceived speed.
13. **MST** (Vaina, 1998, two patients): **RR** collided with moving people; MST guides **walking**.
14. **First-order** motion = **luminance**-defined; **second-order** = equal luminance. **Rizzo et al. (2008)**: **22 versus 1** patients = double dissociation; the book's verdict is **similar but not identical** mechanisms.
15. **Binding**: definition from **Feldman (2013)**; **binding-by-synchrony** = **Singer and Gray (1995)**; alternative = **Guttman et al. (2007)**; **Seymour et al. (2009)**: colour-motion conjunctions coded as early as V1.
16. **Heywood and Cowey (1999)**: the only clear specialisation was **direction of motion in MT**.
17. **Milner and Goodale**: ventral = **perception / "what"**; dorsal = **action / "how"** (older keys: "where", from Ungerleider & Mishkin, 1982).
18. **Schenk and McIntosh (2010)**: ventral **allocentric, sustained, usually conscious**; dorsal **egocentric, short-lived, not conscious**. Dorsal is **faster**; ventral is more **fovea**-dependent.
19. **Optic ataxia** = **posterior parietal**; Perenin and Vighetto (1988) hand rotation into a slot; **VK** (Jakobson et al., 1991) planning intact, grasping impaired; Pisella et al. (2009) deficit mainly **peripheral**.
20. **DF** = visual form agnosia, **lateral occipital complex** (James et al., 2003); posting versus matching (**Goodale & Milner, 1992**); **Himmelbach et al. (2012)**: grasping worse than **20 controls**.
21. **Bruno et al. (2008)**: **33** pointing studies **5.5%** versus **11** perceptual studies **22.4%** (about four times). **Franz and Gegenfurtner (2008)**: perceptual **11.2%**, guided grasp **4.4%**, unmonitored grasp **9.4%**.
22. **Króliczak et al. (2006)**, hollow face: drawing shows the illusion, fast flick does not, **slow pointing does** (conscious planning recruits the ventral stream).
23. **Milner et al. (2003)**: optic ataxics reached **better after a delay**. **Creem and Proffitt (2001)**: **appropriate**, not effective, grasping needs the ventral stream. **van Doorn et al. (2007)**: illusion changed **grip choice**, not **grip size**.
24. **Canal-Bruland et al. (2013)**: standing **1.5 m** behind the figure made beanbag throwing show the illusion too.
25. **VE** (McIntosh et al., 2004) had **extinction** after a right-hemisphere stroke and avoided an unseen rod; **Schindler et al. (2004)**: optic ataxics could not avoid obstacles; **Roseboom and Arnold (2011)**: masked orientation still shaped the grasp.
26. **Frame and fill** = **Chen et al. (2007)**; **Cloutman (2013)**: direct transfer between streams at many stages.
27. **Trichromacy**: S (blue), M (yellow-green), L (orange-red). **Protanopia** lacks L, **deuteranopia** lacks M, **tritanopia** lacks S.
28. **Hering (1878)**: three opponent channels, explain **afterimages** (green → red, blue → yellow); **DeValois and DeValois (1975)**: opponent cells in monkeys; **Hurvich and Jameson (1957)**: achromatic = M + L, blue-yellow = (M + L) versus S, red-green = M versus L.
29. **Granzier et al. (2009a)**: **six** papers, **55%**; **Granzier et al. (2009b)**: lamp and surface accuracy **uncorrelated**, so constancy does **not** rest on estimating the illuminant.
30. **Kraft and Brainard (1999)**: **83%** full, **53%** without local contrast, **39%** without local or global, **11%** without highlights too. **Land (1986)** = retinex; **Foster and Nascimento (1994)** = cone-excitation ratios.
31. **Hansen et al. (2006)**: a grey banana still looked **yellowish**, so it was pushed towards **blue**. **R.J. Lee et al. (2012)**: some chromatic adaptation in about **six seconds**. **Zeki (1983)**, **Kusunoki et al. (2006)**: constancy cells in **V4**.
32. **Cue classes**: motion parallax and blur are **monocular**; vergence and accommodation are **oculomotor**; disparity is **binocular** and gives stereopsis.
33. **Ittelson (1951)**: cards at **2.28 m**, peephole; distances **1.38 m** and **4.56 m**. Logic: **double-size looks nearer, half-size looks farther** (the 7th edition's sentence pairs them the other way).
34. **Sinai et al. (1998)**: texture judgements good within **7 m**, a ditch causes **overestimation**; **Rogers and Graham (1979)**: parallax alone suffices; **Held et al. (2012)**: disparity best **near** fixation, **blur** farther away.
35. Disparity falls **100-fold** from **2 to 20 m**; **Bülthoff et al. (1998)**: scrambled stereo did **not** harm recognition; **Parker (2007)**: disparity processing richer in the **ventral** stream.
36. **Bruno and Cutting (1988)**: additivity versus selection; **Jacobs (2002)**: two reliability hypotheses (Triesch et al., 2002; Atkins et al., 2001); **Girshick and Banks (2009)**: large conflict → **one cue**, unnoticed.
37. **Haber and Levin (2001)**: distance had **practically no effect**; **Witt et al. (2008)**: better golfers see a **bigger hole**; **Van der Hoort et al. (2011)**: small doll body → objects **larger and farther**.
38. **Ames (1952)** room: wrong perceived distance → wrong size; **Glennerster et al. (2006)**: **nobody** noticed the virtual room changing; **Nguyen et al. (2011)**: shrinking poles looked **farther**.
39. **Riddoch (1917)**: WWI soldiers; **DB**: surgery for **migraine**, lower-left field; the **blind spot** result refutes **Campion et al.'s (1983)** stray-light hypothesis.
40. **GY** (childhood **car accident**): superior colliculus route (Tamietto et al., 2010, grey versus purple); awareness **43% versus 3%** at matched accuracy (Persaud et al., 2011); atypical fibres (Bridge et al., 2008). **Schmid et al. (2010)**: LGN → **BA18/19** bypass in monkeys.
41. **GR** (Overgaard et al., 2008): **100/72/25/0%** by rating; aware on **54%**, **83%** correct; yes/no **21%**, **86%** correct.
42. **Danckert and Rossetti (2005)**: action-blindsight (dorsal, most studied), attention-blindsight, **agnosopsia** (ventral). **Weiskrantz**: **Type 1** none, **Type 2** some awareness (**EY**). **Ko and Lau (2012)**: conservative criterion, prefrontal damage.
43. **Vicary (1957)**: **1/300 s**, *Picnic*, **fabricated**. **Merikle et al. (2001)**: subjective threshold (no report) versus the stricter **objective** threshold (chance forced choice).
44. **Naccache et al. (2002)**: **29 ms** masked digit, congruency effect only **with a cue**. **Persaud and McLeod (2008)**: **10 ms → 43%** (below chance), **15 ms → 83%**.
45. **Sandberg et al. (2010)**: **PAS** beats confidence and wagering. **Windey et al. (2014)**: graded for colour, **all-or-none** for magnitude (global workspace, **Baars, 1988**). **Rees (2007)**: unseen faces versus houses decoded at almost **90%**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Retinal ganglion cells** | The retinal neurons whose axons carry the retina's output to the brain |
| **Retinopy** | The point-to-point mapping of retinal locations onto the surface of visual cortex (retinotopy) |
| **Retina-geniculate-striate pathway** | Main route from eye to cortex: retina → lateral geniculate nucleus → V1 (striate cortex) |
| **Optic chiasma** | Junction of the optic nerves where fibres from the inner half of each retina cross to the other hemisphere |
| **Parvocellular (P) pathway** | Channel most sensitive to colour and fine detail, fed mainly by cones |
| **Magnocellular (M) pathway** | Channel most sensitive to movement, fed mainly by rods |
| **Receptive field** | The patch of retina (or visual space) where light alters a given neuron's firing |
| **Lateral inhibition** | Suppression of a neuron's activity by active neighbours; sharpens edges |
| **Simultaneous contrast** | Identical patches look lighter on dark surrounds and darker on light ones |
| **Feedforward sweep / recurrent processing** | Fast upward pass through visual areas / later feedback from higher to lower areas, linked to awareness |
| **Functional specialisation** | Zeki's claim that separate cortical areas handle separate attributes (form, colour, motion) |
| **Achromatopsia** | Cortical loss of colour perception, with form and motion relatively preserved |
| **Akinetopsia** | Cortical loss of motion perception, with stationary objects seen fairly normally |
| **First-order / second-order motion** | Motion of a shape defined by a luminance difference / by something other than luminance |
| **Binding problem** | How features analysed in different brain areas are combined into one coherent percept |
| **Binding-by-synchrony** | Hypothesis that cells coding features of one object fire in synchrony |
| **Ventral stream** | Pathway to inferotemporal cortex serving object perception, recognition and lasting representations |
| **Dorsal stream** | Pathway to posterior parietal cortex serving visually guided action |
| **Allocentric coding** | Coding of objects in their own terms, independent of the viewer's position |
| **Egocentric coding** | Coding of objects relative to the viewer's body |
| **Optic ataxia** | Impaired visually guided reaching and grasping despite fairly intact visual perception (dorsal damage) |
| **Visual form agnosia** | Severe impairment of shape perception with relatively preserved visually guided action (ventral damage) |
| **Double dissociation** | Two patients (or groups) each impaired on the task the other performs normally |
| **Extinction** | Loss of awareness of a contralesional stimulus only when a competing ipsilesional stimulus is present |
| **Hue / brightness / saturation** | The colour itself / perceived intensity / vividness versus paleness (amount of white) |
| **Trichromacy theory** | Colour is coded by the relative activity of three cone types (short, medium, long wavelength) |
| **Dichromacy** | Colour deficiency in which one of the three cone classes is absent |
| **Negative afterimage** | Illusory complementary colour seen after staring at a colour (green → red, blue → yellow) |
| **Opponent processes** | Hering's three paired channels: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white |
| **Dual-process theory** | Hurvich and Jameson's synthesis: cone signals feed three opponent channels |
| **Colour constancy** | A surface keeps its perceived colour across changes in illumination |
| **Illuminant** | The light source falling on a surface or object |
| **Retinex theory** | Land's account: colour judged by comparing a surface's reflectance with that of neighbouring surfaces |
| **Cone-excitation ratios** | Ratios of cone responses between surfaces, which stay nearly constant across illuminants |
| **Chromatic adaptation** | Decline in sensitivity to the prevailing illuminant's colour over time |
| **Monocular cues** | Depth cues usable with one eye (also with two) |
| **Binocular cues** | Depth cues that need both eyes working together |
| **Oculomotor cues** | Depth cues from sensed contraction of the eye muscles (kinaesthesia) |
| **Texture gradient** | The rate at which texture density changes across a surface slanting away from the viewer |
| **Motion parallax** | Relative movement of parts of the retinal image during observer movement; near things move more |
| **Vergence** | Inward turning of the eyes to fixate near objects, used as a distance cue |
| **Accommodation** | Thickening of the lens to focus near objects, used as a distance cue |
| **Binocular disparity** | The slight difference between the two retinal images of a scene |
| **Stereopsis** | Depth perception produced by binocular disparity |
| **Autostereogram** | A flat repeating image that appears three-dimensional when viewed without focusing on it |
| **Additivity / selection** | Combining all depth cues / relying on a single cue (Bruno & Cutting, 1988) |
| **Haptic** | Relating to touch |
| **Size constancy** | An object looks the same size despite changes in its retinal image size |
| **Body size effect** | Misperceiving one's own body size changes the perceived size (and distance) of other objects |
| **Ames room** | Distorted room that looks rectangular through a peephole, making people appear to change size |
| **Subliminal perception** | Processing of a stimulus below the threshold of awareness that still affects behaviour |
| **Blindsight** | Above-chance responses to visual stimuli without conscious visual experience after V1 damage |
| **Stray-light hypothesis** | Campion et al.'s claim that blindsight reflects light scattered onto working parts of the field |
| **Action-blindsight / attention-blindsight / agnosopsia** | Danckert and Rossetti's blindsight sub-types (dorsal action; vague feeling of objects; ventral form and wavelength discrimination) |
| **Type 1 / Type 2 blindsight** | Weiskrantz's division: no awareness / some awareness of blind-field stimuli |
| **Subjective threshold** | Level below which a person reports no awareness of a stimulus |
| **Objective threshold** | Level below which a person cannot discriminate the stimulus above chance |
| **Perceptual Awareness Scale (PAS)** | Graded rating of the clarity of one's experience; the most sensitive awareness measure in Sandberg et al. (2010) |
| **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** | Magnetic pulses over the scalp that briefly disrupt a cortical area, creating a temporary "lesion" |

## Likely exam points

1. Trace the pathway from retina to V1, including the optic chiasma, the LGN and retinopy, and say which visual field reaches which hemisphere.
2. Compare rods and cones, and explain how ganglion-cell convergence accounts for their differences.
3. Contrast the P and M pathways and their (approximate) links to the ventral and dorsal streams, with the book's caveats.
4. Explain receptive fields and lateral inhibition, using simultaneous contrast as the example.
5. Distinguish the feedforward sweep from recurrent processing (Lamme, 2006) and link recurrent processing to awareness.
6. State Zeki's functional specialisation theory, his two reasons for it (Zeki, 2005) and the functions of V1/V2, V3/V3A, V4 and V5/MT.
7. Evaluate the claim that V4 is a colour centre, using achromatopsia (Bouvier & Engel, 2006), imaging and TMS (Banissy et al., 2012).
8. Describe akinetopsia (LM; S.A. Cooper et al., 2012) and the TMS evidence that V5/MT is necessary for motion perception.
9. Discuss whether first- and second-order motion use different mechanisms (Ashida et al., 2007; Rizzo et al., 2008; Hong et al., 2012; Cowey et al., 2006).
10. Explain the binding problem and evaluate the binding-by-synchrony hypothesis.
11. Give three limitations of Zeki's theory (Heywood & Cowey, 1999; Hegdé & Van Essen, 2000; binding).
12. State Milner and Goodale's two-visual-systems theory and the four characteristics listed by Schenk and McIntosh (2010).
13. Distinguish the older "what versus where" account from the "what versus how" account.
14. Compare optic ataxia and visual form agnosia, with Perenin and Vighetto (1988), VK and Pisella et al. (2009) on one side and DF on the other.
15. Describe the DF studies (Milner et al., 1991; Goodale & Milner, 1992; Dijkerman et al., 1998; Goodale et al., 1994) and Himmelbach et al.'s (2012) reanalysis.
16. Explain why action might be immune to visual illusions, and evaluate the evidence (Bruno et al., 2008; Króliczak et al., 2006; Franz & Gegenfurtner, 2008).
17. List the circumstances in which the ventral stream contributes to action (Milner & Goodale, 2008) with supporting studies (Milner et al., 2003; Creem & Proffitt, 2001; van Doorn et al., 2007).
18. Evaluate the claim that dorsal-stream processing is not conscious (VE; Schindler et al., 2004; Roseboom & Arnold, 2011).
19. Give the strengths and five limitations of the two-systems approach, including "frame and fill".
20. Explain trichromacy, dichromacy and the three named types of dichromacy.
21. Explain negative afterimages with opponent-process theory, and outline Hurvich and Jameson's dual-process theory.
22. Define colour constancy and explain why it is both important and difficult.
23. Evaluate the contributions of illuminant estimation, local and global contrast, cone-excitation ratios, familiarity, chromatic adaptation and V4 cells to colour constancy.
24. Classify depth cues as monocular, oculomotor or binocular, with examples and their limits.
25. Explain binocular disparity and stereopsis, including the effect of distance and the autostereogram.
26. Explain how depth cues are combined (additivity versus selection; Jacobs's reliability hypotheses) with supporting studies.
27. Evaluate the evidence that size constancy depends on perceived distance (Ames room; Glennerster et al., 2006; Nguyen et al., 2011) and on other factors (Haber & Levin, 2001; Witt et al., 2008; Van der Hoort et al., 2011).
28. Define blindsight, describe how it is assessed, and outline the DB case and the stray-light objection.
29. Describe the evidence on GY and GR, and what it implies about residual awareness in blindsight.
30. Compare Danckert and Rossetti's three sub-types with Weiskrantz's Type 1 and Type 2.
31. Explain Ko and Lau's (2012) criterion account of blindsight.
32. Distinguish subjective and objective thresholds and their problems (Reingold, 2004; Lamme, 2010).
33. Describe the behavioural evidence for subliminal perception (Naccache et al., 2002; Persaud & McLeod, 2008) and the comparison of awareness measures (Sandberg et al., 2010).
34. Discuss whether awareness is graded or all-or-none (Windey et al., 2014) and summarise the neuroimaging evidence (Rees, 2007).
