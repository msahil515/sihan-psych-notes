# Chapter 9: Reading and Speech Perception

**What this chapter is really about.** Reading a word and hearing a word both feel instantaneous, which is exactly why they are hard to study. In a fraction of a second you turn a pattern of ink, or a continuous and messy pressure wave, into one identified word with a sound, a spelling and a meaning. This chapter asks how. Two questions run through every model in it. The first is **how much of word recognition is driven bottom-up by the stimulus and how much top-down by knowledge and context**: the interactive activation model, semantic priming, the autonomous versus interactionist debate, TRACE and the cohort model are all answers to it. The second is **whether the system works by separate routes or by one interactive network**: the dual-route cascaded model versus the triangle model for reading aloud, E-Z Reader (serial) versus SWIFT (parallel) for eye movements, and Ellis and Young's three routes for repeating heard words. The brain-damaged patients (surface, phonological and deep dyslexics; pure word deafness, word meaning deafness, transcortical sensory aphasia, deep dysphasia) are not side material. They are the evidence on which the models stand or fall, which makes this a high-yield chapter for a clinical psychology paper.

In the book the chapter is preceded by the short Part III introduction to all three language chapters. It raises three big questions that Indian papers like to set as one-liners: is language unique to humans, is it innate (Chomsky's Universal Grammar against Christiansen and Chater), and does the language you speak shape how you think (the Whorfian hypothesis)?

**How to read this chapter.** 9.1 is the Part III opener and stands alone. 9.2 sets reading against listening. 9.3 to 9.6 are about reading: methods and the role of sound (9.3), recognising printed words (9.4), reading aloud and the dyslexias (9.5, the centrepiece of the reading half) and eye movements (9.6). 9.7 to 9.11 are about speech: what is special about it (9.7), the listener's problems (9.8), context (9.9), the three big theories (9.10, the centrepiece of the speech half) and patients (9.11). Learn the three routes and three dyslexias of 9.5 until you can draw them, and note now that the **route numbers run in opposite directions** in the reading model (Route 1 is the non-lexical sound-out route) and the speech-repetition model (Route 3 is the non-lexical route).

## 9.1 Part III Opener: Language, Innateness and Thought

### What language is, and why psychology came to it late

Harley (2013) gives the working definition: language is a system of **symbols and rules** that lets us communicate. The symbols are words, spoken or written, each standing for something else; the rules govern how words are ordered into sentences. Communication is the main function, but **Crystal (1997)** listed **eight** functions. Besides communicating, the book's examples are thinking, recording information, expressing emotion ("I love you"), pretending to be an animal ("Woof! Woof!") and expressing group identity (singing together in church).

There was little research on language before the late 1950s. The behaviourists, notably **Skinner (1957)**, treated speech as conditioned responses that had been rewarded; on that view language is nothing special and another species could acquire it given the right reinforcement. **Noam Chomsky (1957, 1959)** argued that this was hopelessly inadequate: language has unique features, above all grammar (syntax), and only humans can acquire it. His work triggered a surge of research (Harley & McAndrews, 2015) and made language central to cognitive psychology. (*Classic background:* Chomsky, 1959, is his famous review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*.)

### Is language unique to humans?

The strongest case for language in another species is **Savage-Rumbaugh's** work with **Panbanisha**, a **bonobo** (a great ape) born in **1985**, who died in **2012** after a lifetime of language training in captivity. She used a keypad of about **400 lexigrams** (geometric symbols); a computer turned each sequence of key presses into synthetic speech. By age **14** she had a vocabulary of about **3,000 words** and was good at combining symbols in grammatically correct order (asking for an iced coffee, for instance). **Lyn (2007)** found that she and her half-brother **Kanzi** understood English at about the level of a **2½-year-old child** but had little grasp of grammar.

A standard objection is that ape utterances are not spontaneous and only concern the here and now. **Lyn et al. (2011)** studied three great apes, including Panbanisha and Kanzi, and found that **74%** of their utterances were spontaneous; they referred to the past about as often as young children and produced *more* utterances about future intentions.

The conclusion is balanced. Great apes master many components of language and can sometimes refer to past and future, but they produce few novel sentences, their sentences are very simple, and they understand little or no grammar. They do not acquire language as humans do. Chomsky (quoted in Atkinson et al., 1993) argued that a biologically valuable capacity lying unused in animals until now would be "an evolutionary miracle", like finding an island of humans who could be taught to fly.

> **Trap.** Panbanisha and Kanzi are **bonobos** using a **lexigram keyboard**, not chimpanzees using sign language, even though the book's summary sentence speaks of what "chimpanzees" cannot do. Older keys feature *classic background* cases instead: **Washoe** (a chimpanzee taught American Sign Language by the Gardners) and **Nim Chimpsky** (Terrace). Pairing Panbanisha with sign language is the distractor.

### Is language innate? Chomsky's Universal Grammar

Chomsky claims we possess an innate **Universal Grammar**: grammatical principles common to every human language. In his words (Baptista, 2012), Universal Grammar is simply the name for our genetic structure. Candidate **linguistic universals** are:

- **Lexical categories**: nouns, verbs and adjectives in every language.
- **Word order**: subject-verb-object (S-V-O) or subject-object-verb (S-O-V).
- **Recursion**: embedding clauses within sentences, so that finite means generate infinitely many sentences.

Recursion is easiest to see by building a sentence. "The doctor saw the patient" becomes "The doctor, who had just finished a night shift, saw the patient", which becomes "... saw the patient, who had been waiting since dawn", and nothing in the grammar stops the process.

**Hauser et al. (2002)** clarified Chomsky's position by splitting the language faculty:

| | **Narrow language faculty** | **Broad language faculty** |
|---|---|---|
| Contents | Unlearned capacities **specific to language**, including Universal Grammar | The narrow faculty **plus** an auditory system, a motor system, working memory, attention, intelligence and so on |
| Specific to language? | Yes | The added systems are **necessary** for acquiring language but **not specific** to it |

Chomsky's ideas have changed, and above all **Universal Grammar has shrunk dramatically**: it now consists **mainly of recursion**.

Why propose it at all? Chomsky gave three reasons: it explains why **only humans** develop language fully; it explains the **broad similarities** among languages; and the speech children hear is **too limited** to explain how astonishingly fast they acquire language (*classic background:* the **poverty of the stimulus** argument).

> **Trap.** Older textbooks describe Chomsky's innate mechanism as the **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)** and present Universal Grammar as a rich set of principles. The 7th edition frames it through Hauser et al.'s (2002) **narrow versus broad faculty** and stresses that Universal Grammar has **shrunk to mainly recursion**. Asked what Universal Grammar consists of on Chomsky's current view, answer recursion.

### Christiansen and Chater's (2008) critique

**Christiansen and Chater (2008)** rejected Chomsky's approach. Their five points:

1. **Languages differ enormously**, which fits badly with Universal Grammar and linguistic universals.
2. It is **mystifying** that natural selection could give us genes tuned to abstract features of languages we have never met.
3. **Languages change too fast** for genes to track: the whole Indo-European family arose from a common source in **under 10,000 years** (Baronchelli et al., 2012).
4. **Language has been shaped by the brain**: it reflects pre-existing learning and processing mechanisms that are not specific to language.
5. Children find language easy because **humans invented it** to fit human abilities.

Hold the contrast as a reversal of direction: for Chomsky the brain was shaped (genetically) for language; for Christiansen and Chater language was shaped (culturally) to fit the brain.

### Findings: how universal are the universals?

**Diversity.** The major European languages resemble each other, but across all **6,000 to 8,000** languages the differences are large. **Evans and Levinson (2009)** concluded that there are "vanishingly few universals" in the strict sense.

**Recursion.** **Everett (2005)** claimed the Amazonian language **Pirahã** lacks recursion; **Nevins et al. (2009)** rejected the claim, and the dispute is unresolved.

**Lexical categories.** Evans and Levinson (2009) argued that some languages lack noun, verb or adjective, citing an Austronesian language (Chamorro; the name is garbled in the converted source). **Chung (2012)** analysed it in detail and found it **does** have all three, concluding that such claims arise because languages are **understudied**.

**Word order** comes closest to a universal. **Greenberg (1963)** found the subject precedes the object in **98%** of a large sample of languages, sensibly since the subject is usually the sentence's focus; **S-V-O** was most common, then **S-O-V**. **Sandler et al. (2005)** studied the **Al-Sayyid**, an isolated Bedouin community in an Israeli desert with much congenital deafness, which in recent generations developed **Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language**. It uses **S-O-V**, unlike the other languages its users hear.

What do these findings mean? Grammatical structure can form very fast, plausibly because agreeing on word order helps communication. **Culbertson et al. (2012)** showed the human drive to impose structure: learners given a miniature artificial language with an inconsistent mixture of word orders mostly inferred a grammar **more regular than their input**.

**Bickerton (1984)** proposed the **language bioprogram hypothesis**, close to Chomsky's view: children will create a grammar even when barely exposed to a proper language. **Senghas et al. (2004)** studied **deaf Nicaraguan children** at special schools, who developed a gesture system that grew into a basic sign language passed on to later intakes. Since it resembles neither Spanish nor hearing children's gestures, it seems a genuinely new language.

Together these findings show a **strong innate motivation to acquire language**, including grammatical rules, and to communicate, but they do **not** strongly support a Universal Grammar. An innate *drive* to build structure is not the same as innate *content* specifying the structure.

### Genetic evidence: the KE family and FOXP2

Genetic research shows that innate factors matter (Graham & Fisher, 2013): individual differences in language ability are large and partly genetic. In the **KE family** in London, across **three generations** about **50%** of members have had severe language problems: difficulty understanding speech, slow ungrammatical speech and poor grammaticality judgement. The disorder was traced to one gene, **FOXP2** (Lai et al., 2001), with mutations found **only in affected members**. FOXP2 is probably a hub in several gene networks whose disruption impairs language-related brain areas.

> **Trap.** "FOXP2 is the language gene" is the classic distractor. Other genes (**ATP2C2**, **CMIP**) are also associated with specific language impairment, and the FOXP2 sequence is found in **numerous vertebrate species** (Graham & Fisher, 2013). FOXP2 plays a role in language but is far from being "the language gene".

### The poverty of the stimulus, revisited

Chomsky **underestimated the input**. Children hear **child-directed speech** (Eysenck, 2013): very short, simple sentences, spoken slowly, with a restricted vocabulary. Children whose parents use a lot of it develop language faster (**Rowe, 2008**). He also **overestimated the speed**: for about two years after children begin to talk, their speech is very limited (**Bannard et al., 2009**), relying on a few familiar verbs and repeating what they have just heard. (*Classic background:* older books call child-directed speech **"motherese"**; it reappears in 9.7 as evidence that speech is musical.)

### Evaluation of Chomsky's approach

**Strengths.** Evidence strongly suggests language is **uniquely human** (Berwick et al., 2013); **specific genes** influencing acquisition have been identified; and brain areas heavily associated with language, especially in the **left hemisphere**, fit his approach (Berwick et al., 2013).

**Limitations.**

1. Languages differ **far more** than he predicted.
2. Universal Grammar is now **very restricted**, leaving very few universals, which greatly weakens the original theory.
3. The claim that children's input is too impoverished has **little support**.
4. Language probably reflects **non-language cognitive processes** such as perception and attention (Christiansen & Chater, 2008).
5. Languages probably have grammatical structure because it **eases communication**, not because of a Universal Grammar.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of Chomsky's approach?" The non-limitations are the strengths: language appears uniquely human, specific genes affect acquisition, and left-hemisphere language areas exist.

### The Whorfian hypothesis: does language shape thought?

**Benjamin Lee Whorf (1956)**, a **fire prevention officer for an insurance company** whose hobby was linguistics, proposed the **Whorfian hypothesis**: language **determines, or at least influences**, thinking. Its core is **linguistic relativity**, the idea that speakers of different languages think differently. Wolff and Holmes (2011) distinguish three strengths of claim:

| Position | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Differences between languages **inevitably** cause differences in thought |
| **Intermediate** | Language influences **certain aspects** of cognition, such as perception and memory |
| **Weakest** | Language creates **preferences** for ways of thinking that disappear easily when not useful |

> **Trap.** Older keys call it the **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** (after Whorf's teacher Edward Sapir, *classic background*) and split it into **linguistic determinism** (strong: language determines thought) and **linguistic relativity** (weak: language influences thought). The 7th edition uses "linguistic relativity" for the general idea that speakers of different languages think differently. Under either scheme, the evidence supports only a **weak** version.

### Findings on language and thought

**Colour.** **Categorical perception** means discriminating stimuli across a category boundary more easily than within one (developed for speech in 9.7), and it is assumed to depend partly on language. Russian has separate basic words for dark blue (**siniy**) and light blue (**goluboy**). **Winawer et al. (2007)** had participants judge which of two test colours matched a siniy target. Russian speakers were **faster when the distractor was goluboy** than when it was another shade of siniy; English speakers, for whom all the stimuli were simply "blue", showed no such effect. (Roberson and Hanley, 2010, review language and colour.)

**Time.** English describes duration with **distance** metaphors ("a long meeting"); Greek uses **amount** metaphors (a meeting that "lasts much"). **Casasanto (2008)** showed participants a line growing across a screen or a container filling with liquid, and asked how long the growth or filling took. As predicted, English speakers' estimates were swayed by **line length**, Greek speakers' by **how full the container was** and not by distance.

**Motion.** English encodes **manner of motion** (hopping, running) more prominently than Spanish, and **Kersten et al. (2010)** found English monolinguals outperformed Spanish monolinguals at categorising novel animated objects by manner of motion (Figure III.1, percentage correct).

**Objects versus substances.** **Li et al. (2009)** showed objects made of a substance, such as a plastic whisk. English speakers attended to the **object**, Mandarin and Japanese speakers to the **substance**. But when participants simply rated how likely they were to think of items as objects or as substances, the groups **did not differ**. The effects were **task-specific** and far smaller than Whorf predicts.

**Number.** The **Pirahã** have no words for exact quantities, not even "one". **Frank et al. (2008)** found they could still make **exact quantity matches**, even with large numbers, but were **inaccurate when the quantity had to be remembered**. Language is not essential for some numerical tasks, but as an efficient code it helps memory.

> **Trap.** The Pirahã appear twice: **Everett (2005)** used their language against **recursion** as a universal; **Frank et al. (2008)** used their lack of **number words** to test the Whorfian hypothesis.

### Evaluation of the Whorfian hypothesis

Native language affects colour discrimination, time estimation and categorisation. Wolff and Holmes (2011) found no evidence that language "closes doors" on thought, though it may "fling others wide open", for example by **enhancing memory** (Frank et al., 2008) and **sharpening categorical perception** (Winawer et al., 2007). There is little or no support for the **strong** version. Language creates **tendencies** to attend to, perceive or remember things in certain ways, but these are often mere preferences that vanish when the task changes (Li et al., 2009). Only a **rather weak** version is supported.

*Classic background:* older books cite **Berlin and Kay (1969)** on universal basic colour terms and **Heider (1972)** on the **Dani**, who have only two colour terms yet remembered focal colours better than non-focal ones; both were used against the strong version.

### Looking ahead: four language skills, three chapters

There are **four main language skills**: listening, reading, speaking and writing. People are not uniformly good or bad at all four, even in a first language (many fluent talkers find writing hard), and still less in a second: the book's first author can read French newspapers and easy novels but struggles with rapid spoken French and speaks it poorly. **Chapter 9** covers the basic processes of **reading and listening**, especially identifying individual words, and what patients reveal. **Chapter 10** covers **comprehension** of sentences and discourse, including **parsing** (working out grammatical structure), inferences and mental models. **Chapter 11** covers **speaking and writing**; far more is known about speech production, because we speak much more than we write. The skills are interdependent: speakers use comprehension to monitor what they say, and listeners use production processes to predict what the speaker will say next (**Pickering & Garrod, 2007**).

## 9.2 Introduction: Reading Versus Listening

Many comprehension processes do not care which sense the words came through: told that you had done exceptionally well in your cognitive psychology exam, you would understand it the same way read or heard. Those shared processes are Chapter 10's business. This chapter deals with the **front end**, where reading and listening really differ.

| | **Reading** | **Listening** |
|---|---|---|
| Availability of the word | Each word is visible **as a whole** | Spoken words are **spread out in time** and **transitory** |
| Word boundaries | Marked by spaces | Hard to tell where one word ends and the next begins |
| Clarity of the signal | Relatively unambiguous | **More ambiguous** |
| Everyday conditions | Readers are rarely distracted by other visual stimuli | Other speakers and noise often interfere |
| Memory load | Earlier words can be re-read | Earlier words are **gone**, so demands are greater |

The ambiguity point has a famous demonstration: words cut out of recorded sentences and played alone were recognised only **about half the time** (**Lieberman, 1963**).

Listening has advantages too. Speech carries **prosodic cues**, information about structure and intended meaning in the speaker's **pitch, intonation, stress and timing**; questions, for instance, usually end with rising intonation. Text has only punctuation, which is often (not always) less informative. Speakers also gesture.

Patients confirm that the two skills are partly separate: some brain-damaged adults understand speech but cannot read, while others read well but cannot understand speech. So reading and speech perception involve **somewhat different brain areas and cognitive processes**. The chapter takes reading first, then speech.

> **Trap.** **Lieberman (1963)** is the splicing study (isolated words recognised about half the time). **Liberman et al. (1967)** is the **motor theory of speech perception** (9.10). Examiners swap the spellings.

## 9.3 Reading: Introduction

### Why English is a special case

Adults lacking effective reading skills are seriously disadvantaged, so understanding reading matters for helping poor readers. Reading needs perceptual and cognitive processes plus good knowledge of language and grammar.

Most research has used **English**, and **Share (2008)** argued that these **"Anglocentricities"** matter, because the mapping between spelling (**orthography**) and sound (**phonology**) is much **less consistent** in English than in most languages. **Caravolas et al. (2013)** found that young children learning English learned to read **more slowly** than children learning the more consistent **Spanish or Czech** (Figure 9.1 tracked estimated reading ability over **30 months** from a mean starting age of **66 months**). Yet **letter-sound knowledge** and **phoneme awareness** were comparably strong predictors of reading progress in all three languages, so the underlying processes need not differ much.

> **Trap.** Caravolas et al. (2013): English children were **slower**, but the **same predictors** (letter-sound knowledge, phoneme awareness) worked across English, Czech and Spanish. "Reading English involves fundamentally different processes" is not the conclusion.

### Research methods

**Lexical decision and naming.** In the **lexical decision task** participants decide rapidly whether a letter string is a word; in the **naming task** they read a printed word aloud as fast as possible. Both distort normal reading times through their response demands, and it is hard to identify the processes behind the response. **Balota et al. (1999)** pointed out that reading involves **orthography**, **phonology**, **semantics**, **syntax** and **higher-level discourse integration**, and that tasks weight these differently:

| Task | Emphasises the link between |
|---|---|
| **Naming** | Orthography and **phonology** |
| **Lexical decision** | Orthography and **semantics** |
| **Normal reading** | All of these **plus** syntax and integration, which hardly matter for the two tasks |

**Eye movements** give an unobtrusive, detailed, online record of attention-related processing; the difficulty is knowing what processing happens during each **fixation** (9.6).

**Priming.** A **prime** related to the target in spelling, meaning or sound is shown just before it. Reading *clip* does seem to involve its pronunciation: *clip* is processed faster after the non-word *klip*, even when *klip* is presented below conscious awareness (Rastle & Brysbaert, 2006).

**ERPs** time processes precisely (Hagoort & van Berkum, 2007). The **N400** is a negative wave peaking about **400 ms** after a word; a large N400 signals a **mismatch between the word's meaning and its context**.

> **Trap.** **Naming** is the **spelling-to-sound** task; **lexical decision** is the **spelling-to-meaning** task (Balota et al., 1999). Neither taps syntax or discourse integration.

### Phonological processes: does reading go through sound?

| | **Weak phonological model** | **Strong phonological model** |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Coltheart et al. (2001) | Frost (1998) |
| Claim | Phonological processing is **fairly slow** and **inessential** for word identification | A phonological representation is a **necessary product** of processing printed words even when no pronunciation is required; it is **mandatory**, perhaps **automatic** |

Why expect phonology to matter? Children speak for years before they read, and are often taught by **phonics**, linking letters and letter groups to spoken sounds (Share, 2008). Early **phonemic skills predict, and probably cause**, later word reading (**Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012**). But phonology might arrive too late to affect recognition. **Sliwinska et al. (2012)** applied **TMS** to the **supramarginal gyrus**, a phonological area, at **80 and 120 ms** after word onset, and impaired a phonological task. Phonological processing therefore starts within **80 to 120 ms**, early enough to influence recognition.

### Findings on phonology in reading

**Homophones.** **Van Orden (1987)**: asked "Is it a flower?", participants wrongly said yes far more often to **ROWS** than to **ROBS**. ROWS sounds like ROSE, so readers must have been computing its sound.

**Phonological neighbours** differ by one phoneme (*gate*: *bait*, *get*). Words with many such neighbours are fixated **for less time** in sentences (**Yates et al., 2008**). But when the neighbours are **closely related in sound to each other**, recognition is **slowed** (**Yates, 2013**), because the neighbours activate one another and **compete** with the target.

**Masked phonological priming.** A target (*clip*) follows a masked, very brief, phonologically identical non-word (*klip*); control primes are less similar in sound. In a **meta-analysis**, **Rastle and Brysbaert (2006)** found faster processing after identical-sounding primes, implying **rapid, automatic** phonology, as the strong model predicts. **Kinoshita and Norris (2012)**, noting that earlier effects were small and inconsistent, used carefully selected words and found a **larger** effect, which was **greater still when the prime also matched in spelling**: word processing **combines orthographic and phonological** information.

**Used, but not necessary.** Priming shows phonology is normally used, not that recognition must wait for it. Patient **PS** (Hanley & McDonnell, 1997) could not pronounce written words accurately and could not get from one spelling of a homophone (*air*) to its other meaning, yet he **defined printed words accurately**. **YGA** (Han & Bi, 2009) had **severely impaired phonology** but **intact understanding** of written words.

**Inner speech.** **Filik and Barber (2012)** compared readers from the **North and South of England**, who pronounce the vowel in *path* differently (longer in the South). Reading limericks, participants showed disrupted eye movements when the last word **failed to rhyme in their own accent**: inner speech resembles your own speaking voice.

**Conclusion.** Phonology is involved in reading and most findings fit the **strong** model, but patients show it is **not essential**, and inner phonology follows the reader's dialect.

> **Trap.** The chapter summary says masked priming suggests word recognition "typically depends" on prior phonology; the main text adds that this **does not prove** it must. Phonology is **normally and rapidly used** (Rastle & Brysbaert, 2006; Sliwinska et al., 2012) but **not essential** (PS, YGA). PS and YGA are evidence **against** the strong model's "necessary" claim.

> **Trap.** **Homophones** share sound, differ in spelling (*rose*/*rows*). **Phonological neighbours** differ by **one phoneme** (*gate*/*bait*). **Orthographic neighbours** differ by **one letter** (*stem*/*step*; 9.4).

## 9.4 Word Recognition

### How fast is word recognition?

College students read at about **300 words a minute**, an average of only **200 ms per word**. How long recognition itself takes is hard to say, partly because "word recognition" can mean deciding a word is **familiar**, retrieving its **name** or retrieving its **meaning**, so estimates vary.

### The interactive activation model

**McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** proposed the influential **interactive activation model**, a **computational** model with much **parallel processing**, in which **bottom-up and top-down processes interact**. Figure 9.2 drew three layers of recognition units:

1. **Feature level (bottom).** A detected feature (say, a vertical line on the right of a letter) **activates every letter unit containing it** (M, N and so on) and **inhibits** all other letter units.
2. **Letter level (middle).** An identified letter **activates every four-letter word unit with that letter in that position** and inhibits all other word units.
3. **Word level (top).** Words are recognised here, and active word units **send activation back down** to the letter units for their own letters. This is the top-down component.

### Findings: the word superiority effect

A letter string is flashed briefly, followed by a **pattern mask**, and the participant chooses which of **two letters** appeared in a given position. Performance is better when the string is a **word**: the **word superiority effect**. (*Classic background:* this is the **Reicher-Wheeler** paradigm.) The model explains it by feedback. If SEAT is shown and the choice for the third letter is A or N, the active SEAT unit boosts A and works against N; a letter inside a word gets support that a letter in a random string never gets. This top-down influence arrives about **200 ms** after word onset (**Martin et al., 2006**).

### Findings: orthographic neighbours

A word's **orthographic neighbours** are the words made by changing one letter (*stem*: *seem*, *step*, *stew*). They become active when the word is shown. **Chen and Mirman (2012)** found that neighbours **less frequent** than the target **facilitate** recognition, whereas **more frequent** neighbours **inhibit** it. Their computational version of the interactive activation model produced **facilitation when neighbours were weakly activated** and **inhibition when strongly activated**, which matches the data given that high-frequency words are more strongly activated.

### Findings: the problem of letter position

The model codes each letter rigidly in one position, so, as Norris and Kinoshita (2012) put it, "WROD is no more like WORD than is WXYD". Scrambled text should then be very hard to read, yet most people read the internet **"Cambridge email"** (which claims, in jumbled spelling, that interior letter order hardly matters) with ease. **Velan and Frost (2007)** had readers repeat sentences shown a word at a time, spelled normally or with many letter transpositions: accuracy was **86%** versus **84%**. Transpositions are much easier when the **first and last letters** stay in place (Norris & Kinoshita, 2012).

### Evaluation of the interactive activation model

**Strengths.** It has been very influential (Harley, 2013), was an early demonstration of a **connectionist** account of word processing, explains the **word superiority** and **orthographic neighbour** effects, and its emphasis on **top-down** processing is well supported.

**Limitations.** (1) It has **no account of meaning**. (2) It ignores **phonology**, though phonology is often involved. (3) It gives **too much weight to letter order**, so cannot explain fluent reading with transposed letters. (4) It was built for **four-letter words**; its application to longer words is unclear.

> **Trap.** Interactive activation levels: **features, letters, words**. TRACE (9.10): **features, phonemes, words**. And there are two word superiority effects: in **reading** (letter identification in masked strings) and in **speech** (phoneme detection is faster in words; Mirman et al., 2008, for TRACE).

### Semantic priming

**Semantic priming** is faster recognition of a word after a semantically related word: people decide faster that DOCTOR is a word after NURSE than after LIBRARY (**Meyer & Schvaneveldt, 1971**). It could arise **automatically** (the prime activates related words through massive past learning) or through **controlled expectation** (the prime makes you expect a related word). Both are valid, and **Neely (1977)** separated them.

The prime was a category name, the target a letter string presented after **250, 400 or 700 ms**, and the task **lexical decision**. Participants were told that **BIRD** would mostly be followed by a **bird**, but **BODY** mostly by a **part of a building**. That dissociates relatedness from expectation:

| Condition | Example | Related? | Expected? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | BIRD followed by *robin* | Yes | Yes |
| 2 | BODY followed by *door* | No | Yes |
| 3 | BODY followed by *heart* | Yes | No |
| 4 | BIRD followed by *arm* | No | No |

Automatic facilitation should track **relatedness**; controlled processes should track **expectation**. Neely found **two** effects (Figure 9.3): a **rapid, short-lived facilitation** based on **semantic relatedness** (automatic), and a **slower, longer-lasting** effect based on **expectation**, with **facilitation** for expected targets and **inhibition** for unexpected ones (controlled). Condition 3 is the telling one: *heart* gains from automatic activation at the shortest interval but, being unexpected, suffers inhibition once expectation has had time to act.

**Sanchez-Casas et al. (2012)** confirmed that semantic priming can be **automatic**: an invisible **masked** word (*table*) still primed a strongly associated word (*chair*).

> **Trap.** Neely (1977): **automatic** = **fast**, **short-lived**, **facilitation only**; **controlled** (expectancy) = **slow**, **long-lasting**, **facilitation and inhibition**. Giving inhibition to the automatic process is the swap.

### Sentential context effects

Readers might use context to **predict** the next word, so that context shapes its **early** processing, or they might passively access its meaning first and apply context afterwards. Prediction has increasing support (Kutas et al., 2011).

**Penolazzi et al. (2007)** used sentences such as "He was just around/near the corner", where *corner* is expected after *around* but not after *near*. ERPs to *corner* differed **within 200 ms**, fast enough for context to be affecting **lexical access**.

**DeLong et al. (2005)** gave the strongest evidence. Their sentences made one noun highly predictable: on a windy day a boy goes out to fly *a kite* (predictable) or *an airplane* (unpredictable) in the park. The **N400** was larger to *airplane* than *kite*, but that alone could reflect either prediction or easier **integration** of *kite* after it arrived. The decisive result was a larger N400 to the **article *an*** than to ***a***. The articles do not differ in meaning; *an* is only odd because it rules out the expected noun, so readers must **already have predicted** *kite*.

Such constraining contexts are rare in ordinary reading. **Szewczyk and Schriefers (2013)** showed that readers also predict **broad categories** (for example, that the next noun will be inanimate), so context yields **specific or general** expectations. **Dikker and Pylkkänen (2013)** showed participants a drawing and then a noun phrase; when the drawing made the noun predictable (an apple, then *the apple*), areas for **semantic** and **visual word-form** processing became active **before the noun appeared**, so predictive context triggers widespread top-down processing.

> **Trap.** In DeLong et al. (2005) the **noun** N400 effect is ambiguous (prediction or integration); the **article** (*a*/*an*) effect is the one that proves **prediction**.

## 9.5 Reading Aloud

### The puzzle

Read aloud: **CAT, FOG, COMB, PINT, MANTINESS, FASS**. Nothing in the letters tells you that *comb* has a silent *b* or that *pint* breaks the pattern of *hint*, *mint* and *lint*; that knowledge must be stored, word by word, in long-term memory. That cannot explain *mantiness* and *fass*, which you have never seen; perhaps you pronounce them **by analogy** with real words (*fass* like *mass*), or perhaps letter-to-sound **rules** generate a pronunciation. Pronounceable non-words are **pseudowords** (the book mostly says non-words).

Brain damage to different parts of the language system produces **different reading disorders**, and two **computational** approaches try to explain both normal reading aloud and these disorders: the **dual-route cascaded (DRC) model** (Coltheart et al., 2001) and the **distributed connectionist triangle model** (Plaut et al., 1996; Harm & Seidenberg, 2004). Computational models suit reading because it is complex and interactive, and a running simulation shows what a set of assumptions really predicts (Norris, 2013).

| | **Dual-route cascaded model** | **Triangle model** |
|---|---|---|
| Words versus non-words | **Different processes** | The **same** interactive system |
| Character | Relatively neat and tidy; partly **rule-based** (less tidy over time) | Flexible; **all** knowledge of spelling, sound and meaning used **in parallel** |
| Key variable | **Regularity** | **Consistency** |
| Meaning | Present (Route 2) but vague | Central (one side of the triangle) |
| Learning | None | Back-propagation |

### The dual-route cascaded model

**Coltheart et al. (2001)** (reviewed by Coltheart, 2012) model both reading aloud and silent reading. Every route starts with **orthographic analysis** (identifying and grouping letters). Then comes the crucial split between a **non-lexical** route converting letters to sounds (**Route 1**) and a **lexical**, dictionary-lookup route with two sub-routes (**Routes 2 and 3**). Figure 9.4, in prose:

| Route | Pathway after orthographic analysis | Reads | Fails on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Route 1** (non-lexical) | **Grapheme-phoneme conversion** rules | Regular words, **non-words** | Irregular words (regularises them) |
| **Route 2** (lexical + semantic) | **Orthographic input lexicon** → **semantic system** → **phonological output lexicon** | Familiar words, regular or irregular | Unfamiliar words, non-words |
| **Route 3** (lexical, non-semantic) | **Orthographic input lexicon** → **phonological output lexicon** (bypasses meaning) | Familiar words, regular or irregular | Unfamiliar words, non-words |

Three properties matter. Healthy readers use both routes **in parallel**, and the routes are **not independent**, but naming familiar words mostly depends on the **lexical** route because it is **faster**. The model is **cascaded**: activation passes to the next level **before** processing at the first level is complete, unlike a **thresholded** model in which a level passes activation on only after reaching a threshold. And it is a **weak phonological** model: word identification generally does not depend on phonology (9.3).

The simulation used McClelland and Rumelhart's (1981) **interactive activation model** as its orthographic component, with the most strongly activated pronunciation across the routes determining the response. It read **99%** of **7,981 one-syllable words** and **98.9%** of **7,000 one-syllable non-words** correctly.

#### Route 1 and surface dyslexia

Route 1 alone uses **grapheme-phoneme conversion**. A **grapheme** is the written unit corresponding to a phoneme, and may be several letters: the *i* in *pig*, the *ng* in *ping*, the *igh* in *high*. A **phoneme** is the basic unit of sound. A patient using **only Route 1** should read **regular words** and **non-words** correctly but turn **irregular words** into their rule-based pronunciation, reading *pint* to rhyme with *hint*. This error is **regularisation**.

Patients apparently reliant on Route 1 have **surface dyslexia**, a specific difficulty reading **irregular (exception) words**. **KT** (McCarthy & Warrington, 1984) read **100%** of non-words, **81%** of regular words and only **41%** of irregular words, and **over 70%** of his irregular-word errors were **regularisations**.

#### Routes 2 and 3 and phonological dyslexia

Routes 2 and 3 depend on the **orthographic input lexicon**, which stores thousands of familiar written words. In Route 2 a recognised word's meaning is retrieved from the **semantic system** and its sound from the **phonological output lexicon**; Route 3 links the two lexicons directly, **bypassing meaning**. A patient with Routes 2 and 3 but no Route 1 should read **familiar words**, regular or irregular, but struggle with **unfamiliar words and non-words**.

**Phonological dyslexia** fits this well. **Caccappolo-van Vliet et al. (2004)** described two phonological dyslexics who read **over 90%** of regular and irregular words but **under 60%** of non-words.

> **Trap.** One sentence of the book calls Route 2's store an "orthographic **output** lexicon"; the next correctly calls it the orthographic **input** lexicon. The written word is the input; the **phonological output lexicon** produces the spoken form.

#### Deep dyslexia

**Deep dyslexia** involves problems with unfamiliar words, an **inability to read non-words**, and, most strikingly, **semantic reading errors** (*ship* read as "boat", *missile* as "rocket"). Coltheart et al. (2001) argued that deep dyslexics use a **completely different, right-hemisphere** reading system, whereas language is mainly left-hemisphere in most people, and so concluded that explaining any symptom of deep dyslexia lies outside the scope of the DRC. **Patterson et al. (1989)** supported the right-hemisphere idea with **NI**, a girl whose **left hemisphere was removed** and who two years later had **all the symptoms of deep dyslexia**.

> **Trap.** **Surface:** irregular words fail by **regularisation**, non-words fine (Route 1 only). **Phonological:** non-words fail, familiar words fine (Route 1 lost). **Deep:** non-words fail **plus semantic errors** (right-hemisphere reading on the DRC). "Surface" does not mean a visual problem; it means reading from the surface spelling by rule, without access to the word's stored identity. The dyslexias in this section are **acquired** dyslexias in brain-damaged patients, not developmental dyslexia.

#### Two routes? Evidence from healthy readers

**Jobard et al. (2011)** scanned highly educated people reading familiar words silently. Those with **high working-memory capacity** activated **fewer** areas (more efficient processing), and **only low-capacity** readers activated areas linked to **grapheme-phoneme conversion**.

The routes also differ in **how** they work. Grapheme-phoneme conversion is **serial, left to right**, so the time to start saying a non-word should increase with its **length**; the lexical route is **parallel**, so length should matter little for words. As predicted, length affects non-words much more than words (**Rastle et al., 2009**). **Juphard et al. (2011)** found that starting to say **three-syllable non-words** took **26%** longer than one-syllable non-words, against only **11%** for words, and syllable length affected the duration of activity in phonological areas for **non-words but not words**. Assembling a non-word's sound is **slow and serial**; retrieving a word's is **fast and parallel**.

#### Evaluation of the dual-route cascaded model

**Strengths.** It is an ambitious, original account of basic reading processes, including in patients; the two-route idea and its accounts of **surface** and **phonological dyslexia** have been very influential; and its parallel-lexical, serial-non-lexical distinction is supported by behaviour (Rastle et al., 2009) and brain activity (Juphard et al., 2011; Taylor et al., 2013). **Perry et al. (2007)** built a successor, the **CDP+ (connectionist dual-process) model**, with lexical and sublexical routes, removing problems such as the DRC's **inability to learn**.

**Limitations.**

1. Its account of **semantics is vague**, which is serious because meaning often matters: in a meta-analysis of imaging studies, **Cattinelli et al. (2013)** linked word reading to semantic areas in the **temporal lobe** and **anterior fusiform** region.
2. It **does not learn**, so cannot explain how children acquire grapheme-phoneme rules.
3. It treats word phonology as **slow** and minor, but phonology is often **rapid and automatic** (Rastle & Brysbaert, 2006).
4. It failed to explain **individual differences**, and its assumption that readers know **letter positions perfectly** proved wrong (**Adelman et al., 2014**).
5. It has **over 30 parameters**, so a good fit is unsurprising.
6. It is **not universal**: as Coltheart et al. (2001) admitted, one-syllable non-words cannot even be written in **Chinese** script or Japanese **kanji**.

### The connectionist triangle model

The **triangle model** (**Plaut et al., 1996**; **Harm & Seidenberg, 2004**) rejects separate routes for irregular words and non-words. In Plaut et al.'s formulation, all the system's spelling-sound knowledge is applied to **every** letter string, and competing pronunciations are resolved by **cooperative and competitive interactions** based on how the string relates to all known words ("all hands to the pump"). The corners of the triangle (Figure 9.5) are **orthography**, **phonology** and **semantics**, giving two paths from spelling to sound: **direct** (orthography → phonology) and **indirect** (orthography → semantics → phonology). Meaning plays a much bigger role than in the DRC.

The key property is **consistency**: how far a string's pronunciation agrees with those of similarly spelled **neighbours**. Harley (2010): **TAZE** is consistent (*gaze*, *laze*, *maze*); **TAVE** is not (*gave*, *rave*, *save*, but *have*). Consistent items should be read **faster**. The DRC instead classifies words as **regular** or **irregular** by its rules.

The triangle model's account of the dyslexias:

- **Surface dyslexia** results mainly from **damage to the semantic system**. When Plaut et al. (1996) **lesioned** the network's semantic contribution, it stayed very good on **consistent** high- and low-frequency words and on **non-words**, got worse on **inconsistent high-frequency** words and was **worst on inconsistent low-frequency** words, exactly the surface dyslexic pattern.
- **Phonological dyslexia** reflects a **general phonological impairment**.
- **Deep dyslexia** is a **more severe form of phonological dyslexia**: phonology is so impaired that the reader relies heavily on semantics, producing semantic errors.

#### Findings for the triangle model

Plaut et al. (1996) trained the network with **back-propagation** (comparing its actual output with the correct output and adjusting connections to reduce the error) on **2,998 words**. Afterwards, like adults, it named **inconsistent** words more slowly than consistent ones and **rare** words more slowly than common ones, showed much **larger consistency effects for rare words**, and pronounced **over 90% of non-words** acceptably despite **no training on non-words**.

The model also predicts that meaning helps most with **inconsistent** items. **McKay et al. (2008)** taught participants to read non-words (*bink*), some with consistent and some with inconsistent pronunciations, and taught meanings for some. Learning a meaning made reading aloud **faster for inconsistent** non-words and made **no difference** for consistent ones.

#### Evaluation of the triangle model

**Strengths.** Parallel, interactive use of orthography, semantics and phonology is well supported; the importance of **semantics** in reading aloud appears correct (McKay et al., 2008); and it has an explicit **learning** mechanism.

**Limitations.** (1) It has concentrated on **morphologically simple, often monosyllabic** words (Harley, 2013). (2) Plaut et al. (1996) admitted the semantic pathway was characterised only in the coarsest way, though **Harm and Seidenberg (2004)** largely filled the gap by implementing the semantic component. (3) Its accounts of **phonological and surface dyslexia are oversimplified**: some phonological dyslexics show **no** general phonological impairment (Caccappolo-van Vliet et al., 2004).

### Controversial topics: where the two models disagree

#### Surface dyslexia

The **DRC** blames damage to Routes 2 and 3, leaving reliance on Route 1; the **triangle model** blames **extensive semantic damage**. **Woollams et al. (2007)** studied **51 patients with semantic dementia** (severe loss of word-meaning knowledge; Chapter 7). **Surface dyslexia was present in 48**, and the **other three became surface dyslexic** as their semantic knowledge declined. Reading of **low-frequency exception words** fell in step with the loss of semantic knowledge (the book reports a large negative correlation between the two measures; the substance is that they declined together). Both models give reasonable accounts and the semantic system is clearly central; the triangle model arguably offers the **simpler** explanation.

#### Phonological dyslexia

The **DRC** says phonological dyslexics cannot use **Route 1**, a **reading-specific** deficit; the **triangle model** says they have a **general phonological deficit**. The evidence is **mixed**. Caccappolo-van Vliet et al.'s (2004) two patients were essentially intact on **non-reading phonological tasks** (for the DRC). **Coltheart (1996)** found many cases with a **general phonological impairment** (for the triangle model). **Nickels et al. (2008)**, combining modelling and patient data, found **no single locus** of impairment could explain the variety of cases. Each model explains some patients, though **many, perhaps most**, phonological dyslexics have fairly **general** phonological impairments.

#### Deep dyslexia

The **DRC** puts deep dyslexia outside its scope (right-hemisphere reading); the **triangle model** places deep and phonological dyslexia on one continuum of **severe phonological impairment** with no clear dividing line. **Jefferies et al. (2007)** found deep dyslexics poor at phonological tasks such as **phoneme addition and subtraction**, implying a general phonological impairment like that of phonological dyslexics. **Crisp et al. (2011)** found deep and phonological dyslexics alike in their substantially impaired translation of **orthography into phonology**. Semantic errors plausibly arise because damaged phonology forces reliance on semantics. The triangle account is generally persuasive, though probably not for every case (Harley, 2013).

#### Word regularity versus word consistency

The DRC predicts a **regularity** advantage; the triangle model a **consistency** advantage. Irregular words also tend to be inconsistent, so the two must be unconfounded. **Jared (2002)** used four word types:

| | Consistent | Inconsistent |
|---|---|---|
| **Regular** | *bean* | *beak* |
| **Irregular** | *both* | *bear* |

Naming times depended **more on consistency than regularity** (Figure 9.6: consistent versus inconsistent differences, at both high and low frequency, far exceeded regular versus irregular differences). This supports the triangle model over the DRC and has been replicated (Harley, 2013).

> **Trap.** **Regular** = obeys grapheme-phoneme rules (DRC). **Consistent** = sounds like its spelling neighbours (triangle). *beak* is regular but **inconsistent** (compare *steak*, *break*). Jared (2002): **consistency** matters more, which the chapter summary repeats.

#### Pronouncing non-words

The DRC models an idealised skilled reader and so, as Pritchard et al. (2012) put it, gives a "single, ideal response" to each non-word. The triangle model predicts **more variable** pronunciations for **inconsistent** non-words, which **Zevin and Seidenberg (2006)** supported. **Pritchard et al. (2012)** had **47 participants** read **1,475 non-words**: each non-word received on average **8.4 different pronunciations**, and on average only **61.2%** of participants gave the most common one, far more flexibility than the DRC allows.

**Buetler et al. (2014)** showed this flexibility responds to **language context**. German spelling-to-sound mapping is far more consistent than French, so the non-lexical route should suit German and the lexical route French. **German/French bilinguals** reading non-words among German or French words used the **non-lexical route more in the German context** and the **lexical route more in the French context**, a strategic adjustment harder to explain on the DRC than on the triangle model.

#### Converging models: the neuroimaging evidence

The two models have evolved and now differ less. **Taylor et al. (2013)** meta-analysed **36** neuroimaging studies of reading. Both models predict that some areas respond more to words and others to non-words, and so they did:

| Contrast | Areas | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Words > non-words** | **Anterior fusiform gyrus** | Orthographic lexicon and/or semantics |
| **Words > non-words** | **Angular** and **middle temporal gyri** | Phonological lexicon and/or semantics |
| **Non-words > words** | **Posterior fusiform** and **occipito-temporal** cortex | Basic orthographic processing |
| **Non-words > words** | **Inferior parietal** cortex and **inferior frontal gyrus** | Converting spelling into sound |
| **Irregular > regular** | **Inferior frontal gyrus** | Extra effort deciding the pronunciation |

Taylor et al. mapped six components (non-lexical orthographic processing, spelling-sound conversion, semantic processing, orthographic lexicon, phonological output computation, phonological lexicon) onto the brain and onto both models (Figure 9.7). The findings fit **both** models broadly, perhaps the DRC's more detailed assumptions slightly better. But neuroimaging is **correlational**: greater activation for words does not show that an area is **necessary** for reading words.

## 9.6 Reading: Eye-Movement Research

### The basic facts

At any moment most of the text you are processing comes from the word you are fixating, though some comes from neighbouring words. The eyes do not glide; they jump in **saccades** separated by **fixations**:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Saccade duration | **20 to 30 ms** |
| Fixation duration | **200 to 250 ms** (about 250 ms in the key-term definition) |
| Saccade length | About **8 letters or spaces** |
| **Regressions** (backward saccades) | **10%** of saccades |
| Content words fixated | **80%** |
| Function words (*a*, *the*, *and*, *or*) fixated | **20%** |

Saccades are **ballistic** (their direction cannot change once launched), and information is extracted **only during fixations**.

### The perceptual span and parafoveal processing

The **moving window technique** (Rayner et al., 2012) mutilates all text except a window around the fixation point that moves with the eyes; comparing window sizes shows how much text the reader actually uses. The **perceptual span** (effective field of view) depends on text difficulty and print size. In English, read left to right, it extends **three or four letters to the left** but **up to 15 letters to the right**; some languages, such as Hebrew, are read right to left. In **Chinese** it is only **one character left** and **two or three right** (**Inhoff & Liu, 1998**), reflecting how complex Chinese characters are.

So readers use **parafoveal** information from around the fovea (the region of sharpest vision). The **boundary technique** proves it: a preview word to the right of fixation changes into the target word during the saccade towards it, without the reader noticing. Fixation on the target is **shorter when preview and target were the same**, because **visual and phonological** information had already been extracted parafoveally (Rayner et al., 2012).

Unfixated words tend to be **common, short or predictable**. In the **spillover effect**, a word is fixated **longer when the word before it was rare**.

> **Trap.** The **moving window** measures the span's **size**; the **boundary technique** shows **preview** is used. Spillover lengthens fixation on the word **after** a rare word, not on the rare word itself.

### The E-Z Reader model

The most influential eye-movement theory (among many: Rayner & Reichle, 2010) is the **E-Z Reader model**, first proposed by **Reichle et al. (1998)** and revised since (Rayner et al., 2012).

The obvious model, fixate a word until it is processed and then move on, fails twice over. Programming an eye movement takes **85 to 200 ms**, so readers would waste that time on every word; and readers **skip** words, which would be impossible if nothing were known about a word before fixating it. E-Z Reader's key assumption solves both: the **next eye movement is programmed after only partial processing of the current word**. Rare words take longer, leaving **less spare time** to preview the next word (hence **spillover**), and a next word processed quickly enough (for example because it is predictable) is **skipped**. The reader can attend to **two** words in a fixation, but the model is **serial**: only **one** word is processed at any moment.

The rival **SWIFT** model (Saccade-generation With Inhibition by Foveal Targets; Engbert et al., 2005; Engbert & Kliegl, 2011) is **parallel**: fixation durations are influenced by the **previous and next** words as well as the current one, and a typical perceptual span of about **18 letters** can often hold all three.

The six assumptions of E-Z Reader:

1. Readers check the **familiarity** of the currently fixated word.
2. **Finishing this frequency check** (the **first stage** of lexical access) **triggers the eye-movement programme**.
3. Readers then carry out the **second stage**, retrieving the word's **semantic and phonological** forms. (**Lexical access** is entering the lexicon to retrieve detailed information about a word.)
4. **Finishing the second stage** triggers a **covert (internal) attention shift** to the next word.
5. Both stages finish faster for **common** than rare words, and the difference is larger for the second stage.
6. Both stages finish faster for **predictable** than unpredictable words.

Predictions follow (Figure 9.8 showed frequency effects according to the model). Common words are fixated more briefly than rare ones (assumptions 2 and 5), as found repeatedly. The gap between finishing lexical access and moving the eyes is used to process the next word parafoveally; a rare fixated word shrinks that gap, so the following word is fixated longer (**spillover**). A word is **skipped** when its lexical access finishes during fixation of the previous word, most likely for **common, predictable or short** words (assumptions 5 and 6).

> **Trap.** The **first** stage (frequency check) triggers the **eye-movement programme**; the **second** stage (full lexical access) triggers the **attention shift**. Swapping the triggers is the standard distractor. Attending to two words per fixation does not make the model parallel.

### Findings on E-Z Reader

The model's main assumptions are well supported (Rayner et al., 2012).

- **Fast frequency access:** word frequency affected ERPs within **110 ms** of word onset (**Reichle et al., 2011**).
- **Two stages:** **Sheridan and Reingold (2013)** predicted that **faint** presentation would disrupt only stage 1 and **case alternation** (*tAbLe*) only stage 2, and the results were as predicted.
- **Preview:** **Reingold et al. (2012)** swapped *table* for *banjo* during the saccade towards it in a sentence about selling a table at a garage sale. Readers fixated *banjo* longer than *table*, because it had received no processing before being fixated.
- **Other scripts:** **Rayner et al. (2007)** found that Chinese readers, whose text has **no spaces** and mostly **two-character** words, showed eye-movement patterns like English readers; reading rates are comparable too (**Reilly & Radach, 2012**).
- **Serial or parallel?** A **parafoveal-on-foveal effect** is an influence of the **next** word on fixation of the **current** word; if real, both words are processed at once, favouring SWIFT. Findings are **mixed** (Schotter et al., 2012). **Pynte et al. (2004)** found **shorter** fixations on a word when the next word contained a **spelling mistake** (orthographic), and next-word **frequency** can also produce the effect (Harley, 2013), but effects are typically **small** and sometimes non-significant. Reading is **predominantly serial with limited parallel processing**.
- **Order:** most versions (other than the latest) assume words are processed in the correct order and predict trouble when they are not. Readers often read out of order (**10%** of eye movements are regressions), yet this has only **limited, short-lived** effects (**Kennedy & Pynte, 2008**).

### Evaluation of E-Z Reader

**Strengths.** (1) It identifies major determinants of fixations, such as **frequency** and **predictability**. (2) Its **two-stage** lexical processing is supported. (3) It shows close links between **eye and mind**, clearest when they decouple during **mind wandering**, when fixations become longer, erratic and less sensitive to word frequency (**Reichle et al., 2010**). (4) It performs well against rivals (Rayner & Reichle, 2010) and applies beyond English.

**Limitations.** (1) It concerns **early** processes such as lexical access and says little about **higher-level** integration across a sentence. (2) **Parafoveal-on-foveal effects**, though small, imply some **parallel** processing. (3) Readers do not process words strictly in order, and cope well when they do not; neither is predicted. (4) Predictability and frequency sometimes have **additive** and sometimes **interactive** effects on fixations (**Hand et al., 2010**), and the model makes no clear prediction. (5) It focuses **too narrowly on eye-movement data** and needs integrating with general theories of reading. (6) It does not readily accommodate the activation of **related words** (Chen & Mirman, 2012).

> **Trap.** Timings: saccade **20 to 30 ms**; fixation **200 to 250 ms**; eye-movement programming **85 to 200 ms**; frequency in ERPs by **110 ms**. Span: **3 to 4 letters left, up to 15 right** in English; about **18 letters** in SWIFT's argument; Chinese **1 left, 2 to 3 right**.

## 9.7 Speech Perception: Introduction

### Is speech special?

Speech perception is the most important kind of auditory perception, though music matters too. One view (**Trout, 2001**) is that humans are born with **specially designed speech-perception mechanisms**; many theorists instead argue for a **general mechanism** handling speech and non-speech sounds alike.

**Brandt et al. (2012)** went further, describing spoken language as a special type of **music**. Two findings fit. **Child-directed speech** is high-pitched, slow and rhythmical, and so sounds musical. And a looped recording of speech heard over and over often starts to **sound like singing** as you stop attending to its meaning (**Tierney et al., 2013**); music-related brain areas were **more active** for repeated speech heard as song than for repeated speech not heard as song.

### Categorical perception

Play listeners a series of sounds changing in small, equal steps from **/ba/** to **/da/**. Instead of hearing a gradual change, they show **categorical perception**: an intermediate sound is heard as **one phoneme or the other**, with a sharp switch at the boundary, and two sounds straddling the boundary are much easier to tell apart than two equally different sounds within a category.

**Raizada and Poldrack (2007)** presented pairs of sounds for same-or-different phoneme judgements. Listeners perceived categorically, and the **difference in brain activation** to the two sounds was **strongly amplified** when they lay on **opposite sides** of the boundary. Two limits: categorical perception is **stronger for consonants than vowels** and depends partly on **task demands** (Scott & Evans, 2010).

It is **not unique to speech**. **Locke and Kellar (1973)** found that **musicians** showed much clearer categorical perception than non-musicians when classifying chords as **A minor or A major**, and **McMurray et al. (2008)** confirmed categorical perception in musicians. This may explain why phoneme perception is so categorical: everyone is an **expert listener** to their own language's phonemes.

> **Trap.** Older textbooks offered categorical perception as proof that "speech is special". The 7th edition treats it as a product of **expertise** shared with music (Locke & Kellar, 1973; McMurray et al., 2008), **stronger for consonants than vowels**, and **task-dependent**.

### Cognitive neuroscience: speech, music and other sounds

**Overlap.** **Husain et al. (2006)** found speech and non-speech sounds activated similar regions in **primary and non-primary temporal cortex**, **intraparietal cortex** and the **frontal lobe**, with only minor differences. Speech and music are closely connected at **three levels: sound, structure and meaning** (**Slevc, 2012**), which partly explains the overlap.

**Separation.** **Rogalsky et al. (2011b)** compared **sentences**, **scrambled sentences** and **novel melodies**. Both speech and music activated the **superior temporal lobe** and overlapped in parts of **auditory cortex**, but their activation **patterns** were distinguishable **even within the overlap**, and several areas did not overlap. A **temporal lobe network** responded to sentences (not scrambled ones) and **not at all to music**. Earlier studies probably found more overlap because their complex tasks recruited **higher-order processes** such as working memory for both.

**Patients** show a double dissociation: some have **intact speech but impaired music perception**, others the reverse (**Peretz & Coltheart, 2003**).

**The motor system.** The **speech-production system** is usually active during speech perception and plays an important role (Möttönen & Watkins, 2012). **Möttönen et al. (2013)** used **TMS** to disrupt the **lip representations** in motor cortex: processing of **speech sounds** was impaired but processing of **piano tones** was not. Motor processes seem **more important for speech** than for non-speech perception.

### The processing stages

Figure 9.9 (from **Cutler & Clifton, 1999**) set out the stages of listening:

1. **Selecting** the speech signal from other sounds (such as other voices).
2. **Decoding**: extracting discrete units such as **phonemes** from the signal.
3. **Word identification**, which is of special importance.
4. **Utterance interpretation**: building a coherent sentence meaning from the words and their order.
5. **Integration** with preceding speech into an overall model of the speaker's message.

**Phonemes or syllables?** **Goldinger and Azuma (2003)** argued the unit is **flexible**. Listeners heard non-word lists recorded by speakers told that either **phonemes** or **syllables** were the basic units of speech. Listeners detected **phoneme** targets faster than syllable targets when the speaker believed in phonemes, and the reverse when the speaker believed in syllables.

**Why word identification is hard.** All English words are built from **about 35 phonemes**, so most spoken words resemble many others phonemically.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition says **about 35** phonemes for English; older books and keys usually give **about 40 to 44** (*classic background*; the count depends on dialect and on how diphthongs are counted). If both appear, 35 is the 7th-edition answer.

## 9.8 Listening to Speech

### Adverse conditions and the two kinds of masking

Listeners face enormous **variability** (any phoneme or word can be pronounced in many ways) and frequent **adverse conditions**, which **Mattys et al. (2012)** defined as anything that lowers intelligibility on a task relative to optimal listening conditions. **Mattys et al. (2009)** distinguished two types:

| | **Energetic masking** | **Informational masking** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Distracting **sounds** degrade the target words | **Cognitive load** (for example, a second task) makes perception harder |
| Process mainly affected | **Bottom-up** | **Top-down** |

Energetic masking (several people talking at once, traffic noise) is a serious everyday problem that laboratory listeners rarely faced until recently, prompting **Mattys and Liss (2008)** to remark that laboratory phenomena show what the system can do with highly constrained input.

> **Trap.** **Energetic** = competing **sound**, hits **bottom-up** processing; **informational** = **cognitive load**, hits **top-down** processing (Mattys et al., 2009).

### The listener's specific problems

- **Segmentation**: speech has **few silences**, so it is hard to tell where one word ends and the next begins, as anyone listening to an unfamiliar foreign language notices.
- **Coarticulation**: each phoneme's production is shaped by the **preceding and following** sounds; Harley (2010) notes that the /b/ in *bill*, *ball*, *able* and *rub* differs acoustically each time. It **increases variability**, but it is also a **cue** that helps listeners **predict the next phoneme**.
- **Speaker differences** (dialect, rate) are handled well. **Kraljic et al. (2008)** proposed two explanations: listeners may detect **acoustic invariants** common to all speakers, or use active **perceptual learning** to adapt to an unusual speaker. Perceptual learning is important but **selective**: learning a drunk friend's slurring would be pointless, and Kraljic et al. found **no perceptual learning** when a speaker sounded odd because she had a **pen in her mouth**. Listeners adapt only to deviations characteristic of the speaker.
- **Expectations**: **Magnuson and Nusbaum (2007)** played a single speaker to everyone, but listeners told to expect **two speakers with similar voices** performed **worse**, having spent resources coping with two voices that did not exist.
- **Speed**: speech runs at about **ten phonemes per second**.

Listeners cope by using **multiple sources of information flexibly**: **bottom-up** cues from the signal and **top-down** knowledge and context (such as the speaker's previous utterance).

### Segmentation cues

Segmentation uses **acoustic-phonetic** cues (coarticulation, stress) and cues from **knowledge** (of words) and **immediate context** (Mattys et al., 2012).

- **Possible words.** A stretch with **no vowel** cannot be an English word. **Norris et al. (1997)** found *apple* hard to detect in *fapple* (the leftover [f] could not be a word) but easy in *wuffapple* (*wuff* could be).
- **Stress.** Most English content words are stressed on the **first syllable**, so word strings without first-syllable stress get missegmented: "conduct ascents uphill" is heard as "a duck descends some pill".
- **Coarticulation and lengthening.** Coarticulation is **stronger within words than between them**, and segments and syllables at the **start and end** of words are **lengthened** relative to the middle (**Kim et al., 2012**).
- **Sentence context.** In **Kim et al. (2012)**, speakers said the critical stretch almost identically in "Lovers are meant to *adore* each other" and "The hallway leads to *a door* at the end"; lacking acoustic cues, listeners relied heavily on **context** to decide whether they had heard one word or two.

### Mattys et al.'s (2005) hierarchical approach

**Mattys et al. (2005)** ranked the cues in three tiers (Figure 9.10, where triangle width showed each tier's importance):

| Tier | Cue type | Examples | Used when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Lexical** | Syntax, word knowledge | **Preferred** when all cues are available |
| **2** | **Segmental** | Coarticulation, **allophony** | Lexical information is lacking or poor |
| **3** | **Metrical prosody** | Word **stress** | Tiers 1 and 2 are hard to use |

**Allophony** is the existence of several similar sounds (**allophones**) for one phoneme: the /p/ in *pit* differs from the /p/ in *spit*. Stress is the last resort partly because it **misleads** whenever a word's first syllable is **unstressed** (Cutler & Butterfield, 1992).

Degrading the signal flips the ranking, as the hierarchy predicts. In **Mattys (2004)**, **coarticulation** beat **stress** as a boundary cue in **intact** speech, but **stress** beat coarticulation in **impoverished** speech. In **Mattys et al. (2005)**, **lexical** cues beat **stress** with **no noise**, but **stress** beat lexical cues **in noise**.

> **Trap.** Preference order is **lexical > segmental > stress**, but in **noise or degraded speech** stress becomes the most useful cue. "Listeners always rely most on stress" is wrong, and so is "stress is never used".

**Masking and segmentation cues.** Compare **coarticulation** with **transitional probabilities** between syllables (a low probability that one syllable follows another often marks a boundary). With intact speech, segmentation depends more on coarticulation (**Fernandes et al., 2007**). **Energetic masking** reduced the usefulness of **coarticulation** much more than that of transitional probabilities (**Fernandes et al., 2007**), whereas **informational masking** reduced the usefulness of **transitional probabilities** more (**Fernandes et al., 2010**). Disrupting bottom-up and top-down processing has different effects.

### The McGurk effect

Even listeners with normal hearing rely heavily on **lip-reading**. **McGurk and MacDonald (1976)**, as the 7th edition describes it, made a video of someone repeatedly saying "ba", then replaced the sound with a voice saying "ga" in synchrony with the lips still showing "ba". Listeners heard **"da"**, a **blend** of visual and auditory information.

The effect was long assumed to be **automatic and bottom-up**, but it is **stronger** when the critical word is in a **semantically congruent** sentence (**Windmann, 2004**), so **top-down** processes contribute. **Soto-Faraco and Alsius (2009)** introduced a **temporal mismatch** between the visual and auditory streams and still obtained the effect **when participants were aware of the mismatch**, which they took to show the processes are not necessarily entirely automatic. Listeners use visual information even when it **impairs** perception; in normal listening, it usually **enhances** it.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition pairs **lips "ba" with voice "ga"** giving "da". Most other textbooks and older keys (*classic background*) give the reverse pairing: **voice "ba" dubbed onto lips saying "ga"** is heard as "da". If asked which combination yields "da", the conventional key is **auditory /ba/ + visual /ga/**. Constant across versions: a **fusion** into a syllable that was neither seen nor heard.

> **Trap.** **McGurk** = audiovisual **fusion** of conflicting lips and voice. **Phonemic restoration** (9.9) = context **filling in** a phoneme replaced by a cough. **Ganong** (9.9) = an **ambiguous** phoneme heard as whichever phoneme makes a **real word**.

## 9.9 Context Effects

### Two positions on when context acts

**Context** is relevant information not contained in the auditory signal currently arriving: earlier parts of the sentence, or knowledge of the language and its words. Context clearly affects spoken word recognition (Samuel, 2011); the dispute is **when and how**. Harley (2013) identified two extremes:

| | **Interactionist account** | **Autonomous account** |
|---|---|---|
| When context acts | **Early**, during processing | **Late**, after word recognition |
| Can it change perception? | Yes, it may influence word **perception** itself | No: it only helps **evaluate and integrate** the output of lexical processing, never **generate** it |

**Brock and Nation (2014)** showed listeners **four objects**, played a sentence, and asked them to click on any object mentioned, while recording eye movements:

| Condition | Sentence | Critical object shown |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Competitor constraining | "Alex fastened the button" | *butter* |
| 2. Competitor neutral | "Alex chose the button" | *butter* |
| 3. Unrelated neutral | "Alex chose the button" | *lettuce* |
| 4. Target neutral | "Joe chose the button" | *button* |

*Butter* sounds like *button*, but nobody fastens butter. Listeners looked at the competitor **far less** in condition 1 than in condition 2, and the difference appeared **early** (Figure 9.11 tracked gaze over the first **1,000 ms**). Context acts almost immediately, as the **interactionist** account predicts.

### The phonemic restoration effect

**Warren and Warren (1970)** replaced one phoneme of a sentence with a meaningless noise (a **cough**). The damaged word was always the same sound, "*eel", and only the sentence's last word varied:

| Sentence ending | Heard as |
|---|---|
| ... the *eel was on the **axle** | *wheel* |
| ... the *eel was on the **shoe** | *heel* |
| ... the *eel was on the **table** | *meal* |
| ... the *eel was on the **orange** | *peel* |

Since the sound never changed, only context can explain what was heard, and the disambiguating word came **after** the damaged one. Listeners in the **phonemic restoration effect** are typically **unaware** that anything was deleted. (*Classic background:* the original demonstration, Warren, 1970, replaced the first /s/ of "legislatures" with a cough.)

Its cause is controversial (Samuel, 2011). **Samuel (1981, 1996)** found **more restoration in real words than non-words**, so **lexical** knowledge enhances restoration. **Shahin et al. (2009, 2012)** found two processes in brain activity. **Sensory repair** is largely **unconscious**: successful (not failed) restoration came with **suppressed auditory cortex responses** to the noise's onset and offset. **Subjective continuity** uses **top-down expectations**, so that listeners seem to "perceive" the missing phoneme.

### The Ganong effect

**Ganong (1980)** asked whether categorical phoneme perception (9.7) is affected by lexical context. On a continuum from a **word** (*dash*) to a **non-word** (*tash*), an ambiguous initial phoneme was more often heard as the one that made a **real word**: the **Ganong effect**, or **lexical identification shift**.

Lexical context might act **directly on perception** or on **post-perceptual decision** processes. **Myers and Blumstein (2008)** found evidence for **both**: the effect was linked to more activation in the **superior temporal gyri** (auditory perception; **interactionist**) and in **frontal and midline** areas associated with **executive** processes (decision; **autonomous**).

> **Trap.** In **phonemic restoration** the phoneme is **missing** and context **fills it in**; in the **Ganong** effect it is **present but ambiguous** and lexical status **biases** it. Both are explained by TRACE's top-down feedback (9.10), and both give **mixed** support to the two accounts.

### Interactionist versus autonomous: the evidence

Listeners anticipate upcoming words rapidly (Kutas et al., 2011). **Van Berkum et al. (2005)** used **Dutch**, in which adjectives carry **gender** marking that must agree with the noun. In one story a burglar easily finds a family's hidden safe, located of course behind a ... (the expected noun, *painting*, is **neuter**). The Dutch adjective for *big* followed, in **neuter** or **common** gender. **ERPs to the adjective** differed according to whether its gender fitted the predicted noun, so story context affected processing **before the predicted word was heard**.

**Wild et al. (2012)** played sentences as **clear** speech or as **degraded but potentially intelligible** speech, accompanied by **matching text** or **random consonant strings**. Degraded speech was rated **clearer** with matching text. The interactionist account allows context to affect early processing in **primary auditory cortex**; the autonomous account does not. Context **was** linked to activation in primary auditory cortex. With **clear** speech there was **no** such context effect, which gives the autonomous view some support. The likely reason is timing: degraded speech is perceived slowly, giving context time to reach primary auditory cortex, whereas clear speech is perceived too fast for that.

> **Trap.** For Wild et al.'s clear-speech condition the book's text says "primary **motor** cortex"; the argument concerns **primary auditory cortex**, so read it as a misprint. Context reached primary auditory cortex with **degraded** speech, not clear speech.

**Sohoglu et al. (2014)** found that a degraded spoken word sounded **clearer** when **preceded** by written text containing it. What if the text came **after**? The **autonomous** account (context acts late) predicts it should still help; the **interactionist** account (context changes **acoustic processing**) predicts little or no benefit. The results were much more supportive of the **interactionist** account.

### Summary and conclusions

The two accounts are hard to separate. Phonemic restoration and the Ganong effect support **both**, but growing evidence that context affects **early** stages (van Berkum et al., 2005; Wild et al., 2012; Sohoglu et al., 2014) favours the **interactionist** position. The qualification: early top-down effects are **much more likely with degraded speech** (Wild et al., 2012). With clear speech they may be **too slow** to matter (Mattys et al., 2012), and **unnecessary** when bottom-up information is ample.

## 9.10 Theories of Speech Perception

These theories try to explain how spoken words are identified, including the segmentation and context findings above. After a surprising empirical point (spelling affects listening) come the three main theories:

| Theory | Source | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Motor theory** | Liberman et al. (1967) | Listeners recruit the **motor** processes of speech production to perceive speech |
| **TRACE** | McClelland & Elman (1986) | A connectionist network in which **bottom-up and top-down** processes interact throughout |
| **Cohort model** | Marslen-Wilson & Tyler (1980); revised by Marslen-Wilson (1990) | A **cohort** of candidates activated by the word's onset is whittled down over time; the revision gives more weight to **bottom-up** processing |

### Orthographic influences on hearing words

It seems implausible that **spelling** would matter when you are only listening, yet it does. In an **auditory lexical decision** task, **Perre and Ziegler (2008)** found listeners **slower** with words whose sound-spelling mapping is **inconsistent**: a mismatch between phonological and orthographic information disrupts spoken word processing.

Does orthography act **automatically**, affecting **lexical access**, or only afterwards? ERPs help. **Perre et al. (2009)** found an orthographic consistency effect **330 ms** after word onset, early enough for orthography plausibly (not certainly) to influence lexical access. **Pattamadilok et al. (2011)**, with a task of deciding whether spoken words had a given final syllable, found consistency affecting ERPs at **175 to 250 ms**, again suggesting influence **before** lexical access.

> **Trap.** Reading recruits **sound** (9.3); listening recruits **spelling** (Perre & Ziegler, 2008; Perre et al., 2009, 330 ms; Pattamadilok et al., 2011, 175 to 250 ms). The finding is later used against TRACE, which has no role for orthography.

### The motor theory of speech perception

**Liberman et al. (1967)** started from the variability problem: how can listeners perceive words accurately when the signal for each phoneme varies so much? Their **motor theory** held that listeners **mimic the speaker's articulatory movements**, and that this motor signal carries much **less variable** information than the acoustic signal. **Pulvermüller and Fadiga (2010)** similarly argued that language processing relies on **action-perception circuits** linking auditory areas with **premotor and motor** areas. The **mirror neuron system**, whose neurons fire both when performing and when observing an action and which includes premotor areas, may extend this to understanding speech. (*Classic background:* Liberman and Mattingly, 1985, revised the theory so that the objects of perception are the speaker's **intended phonetic gestures**.)

#### Findings

- **Möttönen et al. (2013)** applied TMS to the **lip area** of motor cortex during speech and found, using ERPs, **impaired auditory processing of changes in speech sounds**.
- **D'Ausilio et al. (2012)** noted that most such evidence came from **noisy** conditions. Listeners identified consonants made with the **lips** or the **tongue**, in **noise** or **quiet**, with TMS to the lip or tongue motor area (TMS can **facilitate** as well as disrupt, depending on stimulation). TMS had **no effect in quiet**; **in noise**, consonants were detected **faster** when TMS hit the area matching their articulator.
- **Osnes et al. (2011)** found much more **premotor** activation for **degraded** than for clear speech.
- **Hickok et al. (2011)** tested patients with **Broca's aphasia**, who have severe speech-production problems; they performed **well** on simple perception tasks such as same-different syllable judgements. **Rogalsky et al. (2011a)** found **good comprehension** after damage to the **mirror neuron system**.
- **Hickok et al. (2008)** exploited the strong **left-lateralisation** of the **motor** speech system (the auditory speech system is not so lateralised). **Anaesthetising the entire left hemisphere** silenced the motor speech system, so participants **could not speak**, yet they matched heard words to pictures **reasonably well**. (*Classic background:* this is the **Wada** procedure.)
- **Krieger-Redwood et al. (2013)** applied TMS to **premotor cortex**, **posterior superior temporal gyrus** (pSTG, auditory and speech processing) or **occipital pole** (visual control) during a **phonemic** task and a **semantic** task. Premotor TMS disrupted the **phonemic** task but **not** the semantic task; pSTG TMS disrupted **both** (Figure 9.12). Comprehension depends more on non-motor areas such as the superior temporal gyrus; motor areas have a **limited** role in **phoneme** processing.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Motor areas such as premotor cortex matter when a task requires **explicit perception or manipulation of phonemes**, and they matter more under **demanding** conditions.

**Limitations.**

1. With **clear** speech, comprehension needs **practically no** motor involvement, limiting the theory's reach.
2. **Non-motor** areas matter much more, as in the **dual-stream model** (**Specht, 2014**; Figure 9.13). The **ventral "what" stream** (areas such as the **superior** and **middle temporal gyri**; one stream in **each** hemisphere) is strongly involved in most aspects of speech perception, from phoneme processing to comprehension, voice, prosody, semantics and syntax. The **dorsal "how" stream** (**premotor** areas and **Broca's area**, in the **left** hemisphere; auditory-motor integration) plays a much more **limited** role. The two streams interact.
3. It is unclear **how** auditory input could produce useful motor processing; Harley (2013) noted there is no apparent way to translate an articulatory hypothesis into the same format as the heard speech so that the two could be compared.

> **Trap.** The chapter summary claims "some evidence that patients with damage to motor areas have impaired speech perception". Every patient study in the main text points the other way (Hickok et al., 2011; Rogalsky et al., 2011a; Hickok et al., 2008). Answer from the main text: motor damage leaves perception **largely intact**; motor areas help mainly with **noise** and **explicit phoneme tasks**.

> **Trap.** Liberman's **motor theory of speech perception** is not Kimura's **motor theory of cerebral asymmetry** (Pinel Chapter 16). And the **ventral** stream is **"what"** and does most of the work; the **dorsal** stream is **"how"**.

### The TRACE model

**McClelland and Elman (1986)** proposed **TRACE**, a connectionist network in which **bottom-up and top-down processes interact flexibly**, so all information sources are used at once. Its assumptions (Figure 9.14):

1. Nodes exist at three levels: **features** (such as voicing and manner of production), **phonemes** and **words**.
2. Feature nodes connect to phoneme nodes, phoneme nodes to word nodes.
3. **Between**-level connections run in **both directions** and are **always facilitatory**.
4. **Within**-level connections are **inhibitory**.
5. Nodes influence one another in proportion to their **activation** and **connection strength**; as excitation and inhibition spread, a **pattern of activation** develops.
6. The word recognised is the candidate with the highest **activation**.

Bottom-up activation runs **features → phonemes → words** and top-down activation **words → phonemes → features**, interacting throughout.

> **Trap.** TRACE's **between-level** links are **excitatory and bidirectional**; its **within-level** links are **inhibitory**. "Top-down connections are inhibitory" is the standard wrong option.

#### Findings supporting TRACE

- **Word superiority in phoneme monitoring.** Top-down activation should make phonemes easier to detect in **words**. **Mirman et al. (2008)** had listeners detect **/t/ or /k/** in words and non-words, with words on **80%** or **20%** of trials (more words should draw more attention to the word level). The word superiority effect appeared in **all** conditions and was **larger at 80%** (Figure 9.15), implying top-down processing.
- **Ganong effect**: explained by **top-down activation from the word level**.
- **Lexically guided learning.** **Norris et al. (2003)** found that an ambiguous sound was categorised as **/f/** by listeners who had heard it ending **/f/-words**, and as **/s/** by those who had heard it ending **/s/-words**: top-down learning shaped phoneme categories.
- **Categorical perception**: **mutual inhibition** between phoneme units sharpens boundaries in a **"winner takes all"** process.
- **Word frequency.** High-frequency words are recognised faster (Harley, 2013), which TRACE can explain through **higher resting activation**, implying effects on **early** processing. **Dufour et al. (2013)** found an ERP frequency effect for spoken words as early as **350 ms**.

#### Findings problematic for TRACE

- **Too much top-down.** **Frauenfelder et al. (1990)** asked listeners to detect a phoneme in non-words closely resembling words, such as *vocabutaire* for the French *vocabulaire*. TRACE predicts that top-down activation from *vocabulaire* should impair detection of the /t/; it **did not**. **McQueen (1991)** had listeners categorise **ambiguous final phonemes** that completed either a word or a non-word; the predicted word bias appeared only with **degraded** stimuli.
- **Context TRACE ignores.** **Rohde and Ettlinger (2012)** used a pronoun ambiguous between *he* and *she* in "Abigail annoyed Bruce because ___ was in a bad mood" and "Luis reproached Heidi because ___ was getting grouchy". After *annoyed* the pronoun usually refers to the **subject**, after *reproached* to the **object**, so *she* was predicted, and heard, in **both**. Listeners use **verb** information to make inferences.
- **Not everything goes through phonemes.** **Davis et al. (2002)** asked whether listeners had heard a **short word** (*cap*) or the **first syllable of a longer word** (*captain*). With identical phonemes TRACE implies the task is very hard, but listeners did **well** using **syllable duration**, which TRACE ignores.
- **Orthography** influences spoken word identification (Perre & Ziegler, 2008; Pattamadilok et al., 2011), and TRACE makes **no allowance** for it.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** (1) It gives reasonable accounts of **phonemic restoration**, **categorical perception**, the **Ganong effect** and the **word superiority effect in phoneme monitoring**. (2) It combines bottom-up and top-down contributions with **explicit** assumptions. (3) It predicts some **word-frequency** effects accurately (e.g., Dahan et al., 2001). (4) It copes extremely well with **noisy input** (Harley, 2008), because top-down processes matter more as input degrades (consistent with Wild et al., 2012; Sohoglu et al., 2014).

**Limitations.** (1) It is **narrow**, saying little about **comprehension**. (2) It **exaggerates top-down effects** (Frauenfelder et al., 1990; McQueen, 1991): it implies listeners will usually hear a **mispronunciation** as the best-fitting real word, yet mispronunciations can **strongly disrupt** perception (**Gaskell & Marslen-Wilson, 1998**). (3) With so many assumptions it may be **"too powerful"**, able to accommodate any result (Harley, 2013). (4) It **ignores** orthography (Perre & Ziegler, 2008) and **non-phonemic** information such as syllable duration (Davis et al., 2002). (5) Despite its top-down emphasis, it misses **context** effects such as the use of verb information (Rohde & Ettlinger, 2012).

### The cohort model

The **cohort model** concentrates on how recognition unfolds **over time** and gives **bottom-up** processing more weight than TRACE does. The original version (**Marslen-Wilson & Tyler, 1980**) assumed:

1. Early in a word, **all words matching the sounds heard so far** become active, forming the **cohort**, whose members **compete**.
2. Words drop out when they **stop matching** the input or conflict with **semantic or other context**: *crocodile* and *crockery* start in the same cohort, and *crockery* goes when the /d/ is heard.
3. This continues until word information and context leave **one** candidate. The **uniqueness point** is where only one word remains consistent with the acoustic signal.

**Revisions** (e.g., **Marslen-Wilson, 1990**) made cohort membership **a matter of degree**, with **graded activation**, and allowed the initial cohort to include words with **similar** initial phonemes. Under the original all-or-none rule, a word with an unclear first phoneme could never be recognised, but **Frauenfelder et al. (2001)** found that French listeners hearing *focabulaire* **did** activate *vocabulaire*, though they took time to overcome the mismatch.

The model has **three stages**: **access** (the cohort is activated), **selection** (one word is chosen) and **integration** (its semantic and syntactic properties are combined with the sentence). Context acts at **selection** in the **original** model but only at **integration** in the **revised** model.

| | **Original (1980)** | **Revised (1990 onwards)** |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort membership | All-or-none | **Graded** |
| Initial cohort | Same initial phoneme | **Similar** initial phonemes allowed |
| Where context acts | **Selection** | **Integration** only |

> **Trap.** Findings showing that context **fails to stop** a contextually wrong candidate being activated (Zwitserlood, 1989; Friedrich & Kotz, 2007) support the **revised** model, in which context acts only at integration.

#### Findings

- **The uniqueness point matters.** **O'Rourke and Holcomb (2002)** compared spoken words with **early** (average **427 ms**) and **late** (**533 ms**) uniqueness points in lexical decision; the **N400** came about **100 ms earlier** for early-uniqueness words.
- **But perhaps not at natural speed.** **Radeau et al. (2000)** found the uniqueness point mattered at **slow (2.2 syllables/s)** and **medium (3.6 syllables/s)** rates but **not fast (5.6 syllables/s)**, close to normal conversation.
- **Context does not block activation.** In **Zwitserlood (1989)**, listeners made a **visual** lexical decision straight after hearing part of a word. After *cap___* (consistent with *captain* and *capital*), decisions were faster for words related to **either** (*ship*, *money*). Even when a preceding passage about men mourning at a grave biased the fragment towards *captain*, **capital was still activated**, as the revised model predicts.
- **Friedrich and Kotz (2007)** played sentences ending in a fragment, about a woman needing her *can___* to light up the dark, followed by a written word related in **form and meaning** (*candle*), **meaning only** (*lantern*), **form only** (*candy*) or **neither** (*number*). ERPs showed a **form-based cohort** at **220 ms**: *candy* was active despite the context.
- **Strong context acts early.** **Van Petten et al. (1999)** played a frame about Sir Lancelot sparing a man who begged for ___, then after **500 ms** a **congruent** (*mercy*) or **incongruent** (*mermaid*) word. N400s differed **200 ms before the uniqueness point**, earlier than the model expects, probably because the contexts were **very strongly predictive**.
- **Weber and Crocker (2012)** played German sentences such as "The woman irons the ___", where **Bluse** (*blouse*) is likely, the similar-sounding **Blume** (*flower*) implausible, and **Wolke** (*cloud*) an unrelated distractor. Listeners fixated the **target picture** more than the others **before the final word was heard**, increasingly so afterwards (Figure 9.16), yet the **similar-sounding** word was also fixated more than the distractor just after the final word: phonological competitors are activated **somewhat even against context**, as the model predicts.
- **Competition or prediction?** **Gagnepain et al. (2012)** contrasted **competition** among cohort members (mismatching words lose activation), which the cohort model assumes, with **prediction** of upcoming speech segments (words behind successful predictions gain activation). **Auditory cortex** activity fitted **prediction** better.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** (1) Recognition does involve processing **several competitors**. (2) Processing is **sequential** and changes as the word unfolds. (3) The **uniqueness point** is very important. (4) Context effects **often** (not always) occur at **integration**. (5) The **revised** versions, with graded membership, fit the evidence better than the original.

**Limitations.** (1) Context sometimes acts **before integration**, especially when **strongly predictive** (Van Petten et al., 1999; van Berkum et al., 2005; Weber & Crocker, 2012) or when speech is **degraded** (Wild et al., 2012), though the **"continuous integration"** of input and context emphasised by **Gaskell and Marslen-Wilson (2002)** can accommodate this. (2) It **de-emphasises meaning**: **Tyler et al. (2000)** found high **imageability** (ease of picturing a word's referent) aided recognition **only when the cohort was large**, and **Zhuang et al. (2011)** found more activation in speech-perception areas for high- than low-imageability words under the same condition, so selection depends on **semantic** as well as phonological factors. (3) It gives little weight to **prediction** (Gagnepain et al., 2012).

> **Trap.** **TRACE** = connectionist, **strong top-down** feedback, problem of **too much top-down** (Frauenfelder et al., 1990; McQueen, 1991). **Cohort** = time-course, more **bottom-up**, organised around **competition** and the **uniqueness point**, problems of **early context** (Van Petten et al., 1999) and neglected **prediction** (Gagnepain et al., 2012).

## 9.11 Cognitive Neuropsychology

### Ellis and Young's (1988) framework

This module asks what **brain-damaged patients** reveal about speech perception, focusing on **repeating a spoken word immediately after hearing it**. It uses the framework of **Ellis and Young (1988)** (Figure 9.17), with **five components**:

1. The **auditory analysis system** extracts phonemes and other sounds from the speech wave.
2. The **auditory input lexicon** holds the spoken words the listener knows, **without their meanings**.
3. The **semantic system** stores word meanings.
4. The **speech output lexicon** provides words' spoken forms.
5. The **phoneme response buffer** provides the distinctive speech sounds.

Its most striking claim is that a heard word can be repeated by **three different routes**.

### The auditory analysis system and pure word deafness

Damage confined to the auditory analysis system should impair phonemic processing, and so perception of **words and non-words alike**, while leaving **speech production, reading, writing**, hearing and perception of **non-verbal sounds** (coughs, whistles) intact. That is **pure word deafness**.

Its defining feature is **selectivity to speech**, and the evidence on this is **mixed**: **Pinard et al. (2002)** found impaired **music and/or environmental sound** perception in **94% of 63 patients**. Speech differs from most sounds in demanding the tracking of **rapid changes**, so perhaps the real deficit is with **rapid temporal change** in any sound. **Slevc et al. (2011)** supported this: their patient **NL**, with pure word deafness, had particular difficulty discriminating **speech or non-speech** sounds differing in rapid temporal changes.

### The three-route framework

All three routes run from the **auditory analysis system** to the **phoneme response buffer**:

| Route | Components in between | Used for | Accesses meaning? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Route 1** | **Auditory input lexicon → semantic system → speech output lexicon** | **Familiar** words | **Yes** |
| **Route 2** | **Auditory input lexicon → speech output lexicon** | **Familiar** words | **No** |
| **Route 3** | A **rule-based system** converting acoustic information into speakable form | **Unfamiliar** words and **non-words** | **No** |

Someone relying on Route 2 could repeat familiar words accurately **without knowing their meaning**; Route 3's conversion rules let listeners repeat **unfamiliar words and non-words**.

> **Trap.** The book's text says Routes 1 and 2 are for "**unfamiliar** words"; this is a misprint for **familiar**, as its following sentences make clear.

> **Trap.** **Route numbers run in opposite directions.** In the reading model (DRC, 9.5), **Route 1 is non-lexical** (grapheme-phoneme rules). In Ellis and Young's repetition framework, **Route 1 is lexical-semantic** and **Route 3 is non-lexical**. Both models share the same logic: a semantic lexical route, a non-semantic lexical route and a rule-based sub-lexical route.

### Findings: patient patterns

**Word meaning deafness (mainly Route 2).** Such a patient should **recognise** familiar words without understanding them, **tell words from non-words** (input lexicon intact) and have trouble repeating **unfamiliar words and non-words**. **Word meaning deafness**, a selective failure to understand **spoken** but not written language, fits; few cases are known.

- **Dr O** (**Franklin et al., 1996**) distinguished words from non-words excellently and repeated **words far better than non-words (80% versus 7%)**. His **auditory comprehension was impaired** but **written comprehension intact**, so his semantic system was probably undamaged.
- **BB** (**Bormann & Weiller, 2012**) could tell words from non-words but was severely impaired at matching **pictures to spoken words**, while matching pictures to **written** words normally. Her semantics were intact; she could not reach them from speech.

**Transcortical sensory aphasia (mainly Route 3).** Such a patient should **repeat** words and non-words but **understand** very little. **Kim et al. (2009)** described a man who repeated spoken words and sentences but had severely impaired **auditory and reading comprehension**, suggesting damage **within the semantic system**. (*Classic background:* in the classical aphasia scheme this is a **fluent** aphasia with **poor comprehension** and strikingly **preserved repetition**.)

**Deep dysphasia.** Patients have extensive problems with both perception and production, make **semantic errors** in repetition (saying *sky* for *cloud*) and repeat words and non-words very poorly. **JR** (**Ablinger et al., 2008**) had **severely impaired speech perception** yet only **slightly impaired reading aloud** of words and non-words. Deep dysphasia could reflect damage to **all three routes**, but **Jefferies et al. (2007)** argued, more plausibly, for a **general phonological impairment** that pushes patients to rely on meaning, producing semantic errors. Their deep dysphasics showed poor **phonological production** in repetition, reading aloud and picture naming; very poor **phonological manipulation** (for example **phoneme subtraction**: "cat" without its first sound); and **speech perception** problems such as poor **rhyme** judgements. This is the same explanation Jefferies et al. gave for **deep dyslexia** (9.5).

| Condition | Words vs non-words | Repeats words | Repeats non-words | Understands speech | Understands print | Framework account |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pure word deafness** | Impaired | Impaired | Impaired | No | **Yes** | **Auditory analysis system** damaged |
| **Word meaning deafness** | **Intact** | **Good** (Dr O 80%) | **Poor** (Dr O 7%) | No | **Yes** | Mainly **Route 2**; speech cannot reach intact semantics |
| **Transcortical sensory aphasia** | (not reported) | **Good** | **Good** | No | **No** | Mainly **Route 3**; **semantic system** damaged |
| **Deep dysphasia** | (not reported) | Poor, **semantic errors** | Very poor | Impaired | (JR read aloud fairly well) | All three routes, **or** general phonological impairment |

> **Trap.** Word meaning deafness and transcortical sensory aphasia both combine **poor speech comprehension** with **good word repetition**. **Written comprehension** separates them: **intact** in word meaning deafness (Dr O, BB), **impaired** in transcortical sensory aphasia (Kim et al., 2009). So does non-word repetition: poor in the first, good in the second.

> **Trap.** **Deep dyslexia** = semantic errors **reading** print (*ship* as "boat"); **deep dysphasia** = semantic errors **repeating** speech (*cloud* as "sky"). Jefferies et al. (2007) explain both by a general phonological impairment. The chapter summary's "transcranial sensory aphasia" is a misprint for **transcortical**.

### Evaluation of the three-route framework

**Strengths.** It is along the right lines: there is **evidence for all three routes**, and pure word deafness, word meaning deafness and transcortical sensory aphasia relate readily to it.

**Limitations.** (1) Some patients' symptoms are hard to map onto it; for deep dysphasia it is disputed whether **all three routes** are damaged or there is mainly a **general phonological impairment**. (2) It identifies the **components** but not how they **work**, for example what happens inside the semantic or auditory analysis systems.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **Bottom-up versus top-down, and when top-down wins.** The interactive activation model's word-to-letter feedback, Neely's controlled expectancy, DeLong's predicted article, the interactionist account of context, and TRACE are all claims about top-down influence. The chapter's recurring answer is that top-down processes matter **most when bottom-up input is weak**: context reaches primary auditory cortex only for degraded speech (Wild et al., 2012), motor areas help only in noise (D'Ausilio et al., 2012), stress becomes the best segmentation cue only in noise (Mattys et al., 2005), and TRACE's word bias appears only with degraded stimuli (McQueen, 1991).
2. **Separate routes versus one interactive network.** DRC versus triangle for reading aloud, E-Z Reader (serial) versus SWIFT (parallel) for eye movements, and Ellis and Young's three routes for repetition. In each case the "routes" view explains patient dissociations neatly, the "network" view explains graded effects (consistency, variable non-word pronunciations, parafoveal-on-foveal effects), and the two have converged over time (Taylor et al., 2013).
3. **Sound and spelling cross over.** Reading recruits phonology rapidly (Van Orden, 1987; Rastle & Brysbaert, 2006; Sliwinska et al., 2012) though not necessarily (PS, YGA); listening recruits orthography (Perre & Ziegler, 2008; Pattamadilok et al., 2011). Models that keep the two modalities sealed (TRACE; the DRC's slow phonology) lose points for it.
4. **Patients are the test bed.** KT, Caccappolo-van Vliet's two patients, NI, PS and YGA for reading; NL, Dr O, BB, JR and Kim et al.'s patient for speech; and double dissociations between reading and listening and between speech and music. The question to ask of each is which component, or which route, is spared.
5. **A general phonological impairment explains a lot.** The triangle model's account of phonological and deep dyslexia and Jefferies et al.'s (2007) account of deep dysphasia make the same move: when phonology is badly damaged, the patient leans on meaning, and semantic errors follow.
6. **Prediction is everywhere.** DeLong et al. (2005), Dikker and Pylkkänen (2013), van Berkum et al. (2005), Weber and Crocker (2012), Gagnepain et al. (2012) and Pickering and Garrod (2007) all show listeners and readers running ahead of the input.
7. **English is not the world.** The Anglocentricity critique (Share, 2008), slower reading acquisition in English (Caravolas et al., 2013), kanji and Chinese script falling outside the DRC, the narrower Chinese perceptual span, the German/French route switch (Buetler et al., 2014) and, in the opener, the diversity that undermines Universal Grammar and the weak, task-specific effects of language on thought.

## Worked example

*A 63-year-old retired school principal is referred for two years of worsening difficulty "finding and understanding words". MRI shows left anterior temporal atrophy. On reading aloud she reads non-words almost perfectly and regular words well, but makes many errors on low-frequency irregular words, reading* pint *to rhyme with* mint *and sounding out* yacht. *Common irregular words such as* have *are mostly correct. She cannot say what a yacht is. A second patient seen the same week, a 66-year-old man, has had a left temporal stroke: he cannot follow conversation, yet reads the newspaper and understands it, can tell spoken real words from non-words, repeats familiar words well but repeats non-words very poorly.*

**What is her reading disorder?** **Surface dyslexia**: regular words and non-words are spared, exception words fail, and the errors are **regularisations**, the signature seen in KT (McCarthy & Warrington, 1984).

**How would each model explain it?** The **DRC** says the lexical Routes 2 and 3 are damaged, leaving her dependent on Route 1 (grapheme-phoneme conversion). The **triangle model** says her **semantic system** is damaged, so inconsistent words lose the semantic support they need. Her profile and its cause favour the second reading: loss of word meaning (she cannot define *yacht*) with an atrophy pattern typical of **semantic dementia**, and Woollams et al. (2007) found surface dyslexia in **48 of 51** semantic dementia patients, with the other three developing it as semantic knowledge declined.

**What finer pattern should you predict?** Plaut et al.'s (1996) semantically lesioned network: good on consistent words of any frequency and on non-words, worse on inconsistent high-frequency words, **worst on inconsistent low-frequency** words. That matches her sparing of common irregulars like *have*. Testing Jared's (2002) four word types would also show whether **consistency** predicts her errors better than **regularity**, as the triangle model expects. The prognostic point follows from Woollams et al.: as her semantic knowledge declines, her exception-word reading should worsen in step.

**What is his disorder?** Poor speech comprehension, intact written comprehension, intact word versus non-word discrimination, good word repetition and poor non-word repetition: **word meaning deafness**, the pattern of Dr O (80% versus 7%; Franklin et al., 1996) and BB (Bormann & Weiller, 2012). In Ellis and Young's (1988) framework he is relying mainly on **Route 2** (auditory input lexicon to speech output lexicon, bypassing meaning), with Route 3 impaired, while his semantic system, reachable through print, is intact.

**What does he not have?** Not **pure word deafness**, because his auditory analysis and input lexicon work (he can tell words from non-words). Not **transcortical sensory aphasia**, because that would impair **written** comprehension too and spare **non-word** repetition (Kim et al., 2009). Not **deep dysphasia**, unless his repetition errors turn out to be **semantic** substitutions (*sky* for *cloud*); in that case Jefferies et al.'s (2007) tests of phoneme subtraction and rhyme judgement would check for a general phonological impairment.

**One more check.** Had he shown a pure-word-deafness picture instead, you would test environmental sounds and music (impaired in **94% of 63** such patients; Pinard et al., 2002) and rapid temporal discrimination (patient NL; Slevc et al., 2011) before calling the deficit speech-specific.

## MCQ traps

1. **Panbanisha** was a **bonobo** (1985 to 2012) using about **400 lexigrams**, with about **3,000 words** by age **14**; **Lyn et al. (2011)**: **74%** of ape utterances spontaneous. Not a chimpanzee, not sign language.
2. **Universal Grammar** has shrunk to **mainly recursion**; **Hauser et al. (2002)** split the **narrow** (language-specific, includes Universal Grammar) from the **broad** faculty (plus working memory, attention, motor and auditory systems). Older keys say **LAD**.
3. **Christiansen and Chater (2008)**: language was **shaped by the brain**, not the brain for language; Indo-European arose in **under 10,000 years** (Baronchelli et al., 2012).
4. **Greenberg (1963)**: subject before object in **98%** of languages, **S-V-O** then **S-O-V**; **Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language** uses **S-O-V** (Sandler et al., 2005).
5. **Pirahã** twice: **Everett (2005)** on **recursion** (disputed by Nevins et al., 2009); **Frank et al. (2008)** on **number words** (exact matching intact, memory for quantity impaired).
6. **KE family**: about **50%** affected over **three generations**; **FOXP2** (Lai et al., 2001) is **not** "the language gene" (ATP2C2, CMIP; shared with many vertebrates).
7. **Bickerton (1984)** = **language bioprogram hypothesis**; **Senghas et al. (2004)** = deaf **Nicaraguan** children creating a new sign language.
8. **Whorf (1956)** was a **fire prevention officer**; evidence supports only a **weak** version; Russian **siniy/goluboy** (Winawer et al., 2007); effects vanish with task change (Li et al., 2009).
9. **Casasanto (2008)**: English time as **distance**, Greek as **amount**.
10. **Lieberman (1963)**: spliced-out words recognised about **half** the time. **Liberman et al. (1967)**: **motor theory**.
11. **Naming** taps orthography-to-**phonology**; **lexical decision** taps orthography-to-**semantics** (Balota et al., 1999).
12. **Strong phonological model** = **Frost (1998)**; **weak** = **Coltheart et al. (2001)**, and the DRC is weak-phonological.
13. **Sliwinska et al. (2012)**: TMS to **supramarginal gyrus** at **80 and 120 ms** shows phonology begins within 80 to 120 ms.
14. **Van Orden (1987)**: "Is it a flower? **ROWS**" errors show phonological recoding. **PS** and **YGA** show meaning **without** phonology.
15. **Interactive activation model** (McClelland & Rumelhart, 1981): **features, letters, words**; **four-letter** words; top-down effects at about **200 ms** (Martin et al., 2006).
16. **Orthographic neighbours**: **less** frequent ones **facilitate**, **more** frequent ones **inhibit** (Chen & Mirman, 2012). Transposed-letter reading **86% versus 84%** (Velan & Frost, 2007).
17. **Neely (1977)**: intervals **250, 400, 700 ms**; **automatic** = fast, short-lived facilitation; **expectancy** = slow, long-lasting, facilitation **and inhibition**.
18. **DeLong et al. (2005)**: the **article** N400 (*an* versus *a*) proves **prediction**; the noun N400 alone does not.
19. **DRC** reads **99%** of **7,981** words and **98.9%** of **7,000** non-words; **cascaded**, not thresholded; **over 30** parameters.
20. **KT** (McCarthy & Warrington, 1984): **100%** non-words, **81%** regular, **41%** irregular, **over 70%** of errors regularisations = **surface** dyslexia.
21. **Phonological dyslexia** (Caccappolo-van Vliet et al., 2004): words **over 90%**, non-words **under 60%**.
22. **Deep dyslexia**: semantic errors (*ship* as "boat"); **NI** after **left hemispherectomy** (Patterson et al., 1989); outside the DRC's scope.
23. **Juphard et al. (2011)**: length cost **26%** for non-words versus **11%** for words, showing serial non-lexical processing.
24. **Triangle model**: trained on **2,998 words** by **back-propagation**; reads **over 90%** of untrained non-words; key variable **consistency**.
25. **Jared (2002)**: **consistency** beats **regularity** (*bean*, *beak*, *both*, *bear*).
26. **Woollams et al. (2007)**: **48 of 51** semantic dementia patients surface dyslexic; the remaining **3** became so.
27. **Pritchard et al. (2012)**: **47** readers, **1,475** non-words, **8.4** pronunciations each, **61.2%** giving the commonest.
28. **Eye movements**: saccades **20 to 30 ms**, fixations **200 to 250 ms**, about **8** letters per saccade, regressions **10%**; content words **80%** fixated, function words **20%**.
29. **Perceptual span**: **3 to 4** letters left, **up to 15** right in English; Chinese **1** left, **2 to 3** right (Inhoff & Liu, 1998).
30. **E-Z Reader**: frequency check triggers the **eye movement**; lexical access triggers the **attention shift**; **serial**. **SWIFT** is **parallel**.
31. **Categorical perception** is **not** speech-specific (musicians and chords: Locke & Kellar, 1973) and is stronger for **consonants** than vowels.
32. **Energetic** masking hits **bottom-up**, **informational** masking hits **top-down** processing (Mattys et al., 2009).
33. **Mattys et al. (2005)**: **lexical > segmental > stress**, but **stress** wins **in noise**.
34. **McGurk and MacDonald (1976)**: fusion into "da". The 7th edition says lips "ba" with voice "ga"; the conventional key is **auditory /ba/ + visual /ga/**. Stronger in congruent sentences (Windmann, 2004).
35. **Phonemic restoration** (Warren & Warren, 1970: *eel heard as wheel, heel, meal, peel) is not the **Ganong effect** (Ganong, 1980: *dash/tash*, lexical identification shift) or the **McGurk** effect.
36. **TRACE**: between-level links **excitatory and bidirectional**, within-level links **inhibitory**; word superiority larger with **80%** than **20%** words (Mirman et al., 2008).
37. **Cohort**: original puts context at **selection**, revised at **integration**; **Zwitserlood (1989)** *captain/capital* supports the revised version; uniqueness points **427** versus **533 ms**, N400 **100 ms** earlier (O'Rourke & Holcomb, 2002); no uniqueness-point effect at **5.6 syllables/s** (Radeau et al., 2000).
38. **Ellis and Young (1988)**: Route **1** is lexical-semantic and Route **3** non-lexical, the reverse of the DRC's numbering; **Dr O** repeated **80%** of words versus **7%** of non-words; pure word deafness with non-speech deficits in **94% of 63** patients (Pinard et al., 2002).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Universal Grammar** | Chomsky's proposed innate set of grammatical principles shared by all languages; now mainly recursion |
| **Recursion** | Embedding clauses within sentences so that finite rules yield infinitely many sentences |
| **Narrow / broad language faculty** | Language-specific unlearned capacities / those plus non-specific systems such as working memory and attention (Hauser et al., 2002) |
| **Language bioprogram hypothesis** | Bickerton's claim that children create a grammar even with minimal exposure to a proper language |
| **Child-directed speech** | Short, simple, slow speech with restricted vocabulary used to young children |
| **Whorfian hypothesis** | The idea that language determines, or at least influences, thought |
| **Linguistic relativity** | The idea that speakers of different languages think differently |
| **Lexical decision task** | Deciding quickly whether a letter string is a real word |
| **Naming task** | Reading a printed word aloud as fast as possible |
| **Orthography** | The study of letters and spellings |
| **Phonology** | The study of the sounds of words and parts of words |
| **Semantics** | The study of meaning in words, phrases and sentences |
| **N400** | ERP negativity peaking about 400 ms after a word, larger when its meaning fits its context poorly |
| **Homophones** | Words pronounced alike but spelled differently (*sale*, *sail*) |
| **Phonological neighbourhood** | The set of words differing from a word by one phoneme |
| **Word superiority effect** | Better detection of a letter in a string that forms a word than in one that does not |
| **Orthographic neighbours** | Words formed by changing one letter of a target word |
| **Semantic priming** | Faster recognition of a word after a meaning-related word |
| **Pseudowords** | Pronounceable non-words (*mantiness*) |
| **Cascade model** | A model in which activation passes to the next level before processing at the first is complete |
| **Grapheme** | A written unit (one or more letters) corresponding to a phoneme |
| **Phoneme** | The basic unit of speech sound; words consist of one or more |
| **Regularisation** | Pronouncing an irregular word by spelling-sound rules (*pint* rhyming with *hint*) |
| **Lexicon** | A store of detailed knowledge about words (spelling, sound, meaning, syntax) |
| **Orthographic lexicon** | The part of long-term memory storing learned word spellings |
| **Surface dyslexia** | Regular words and non-words read well; irregular (exception) words impaired |
| **Phonological dyslexia** | Familiar words read well; unfamiliar words and pseudowords impaired |
| **Deep dyslexia** | Impaired reading of unfamiliar words and non-words plus semantic reading errors |
| **Consistency** | How far a string's pronunciation agrees with that of similarly spelled neighbours |
| **Saccades** | Rapid, ballistic eye movements separated by fixations of about 250 ms |
| **Fixation / regression** | A pause in which the eye is still / a saccade back along the text |
| **Perceptual span** | The effective field of view in reading: letters left and right of fixation that can be processed |
| **Spillover effect** | Longer fixation on a word when the preceding word was rare rather than common |
| **Lexical access** | Entering the lexicon to retrieve detailed information about a word |
| **Parafoveal-on-foveal effects** | Influence of the next word's characteristics on fixation duration of the current word |
| **E-Z Reader / SWIFT** | Serial eye-movement model with two-stage lexical access / parallel rival model |
| **Categorical perception** | An intermediate sound heard as one phoneme or the other rather than as in between |
| **Energetic / informational masking** | Degradation by competing sound (bottom-up) / by cognitive load (top-down) |
| **Segmentation** | Dividing near-continuous speech into separate phonemes and words |
| **Coarticulation** | Influence of the preceding sound and preparation for the next on how a phoneme is produced |
| **Allophony** | One phoneme realised as two or more similar sounds (allophones), such as /p/ in *pit* and *spit* |
| **McGurk effect** | Conflicting lip movements and speech sounds fused into a third, perceived syllable |
| **Phonemic restoration effect** | Failure to notice that a phoneme in a sentence was replaced by a non-speech sound such as a cough |
| **Ganong effect** | Bias to hear an ambiguous phoneme as the one that makes a word rather than a non-word |
| **Motor theory of speech perception** | Liberman's claim that listeners perceive speech by recruiting articulatory (motor) processes |
| **Mirror neuron system** | Neurons active both when performing and when observing an action; includes premotor areas |
| **TRACE model** | Connectionist model with feature, phoneme and word nodes linked by interactive bottom-up and top-down activation |
| **Cohort model** | Model in which a word's onset activates a cohort of candidates that is narrowed down over time |
| **Uniqueness point** | The moment in a spoken word at which only one candidate remains consistent with the input |
| **Pure word deafness** | Severely impaired speech perception with intact speech production, reading, writing and non-speech sound perception |
| **Word meaning deafness** | Selective inability to understand spoken, but not written, language |
| **Transcortical sensory aphasia** | Spoken words can be repeated but comprehension of spoken and written language is severely impaired |
| **Deep dysphasia** | Semantic errors in repeating spoken words with generally poor repetition of words and non-words |
