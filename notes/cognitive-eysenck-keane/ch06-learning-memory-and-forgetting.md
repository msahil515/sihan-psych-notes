# Chapter 6: Learning, Memory and Forgetting

**What this chapter is really about.** Most students arrive with a filing-cabinet picture of memory: information goes into a drawer and is later taken out, unless the drawer has rusted shut. Eysenck and Keane replace that picture with something more interesting and more testable. Memory is a set of interacting systems, not one store. The short-term part is a **workspace** for reasoning, reading and arithmetic, not a waiting room. What you remember depends on *how* you processed something and on *how well that processing matches* the conditions at test. Retrieving is itself one of the most powerful ways to learn. You can learn complex regularities without knowing that you have. And forgetting is not one process but at least five (decay, interference, motivated forgetting, cue failure, failed consolidation), several of them useful.

Each claim has a clinical edge. The split between short-term and long-term memory rests on brain-damaged patients (KF, PV, amnesic patients). The executive part of working memory is what fails in the **dysexecutive syndrome**. Implicit learning survives amnesia but is disturbed in **Parkinson's disease**. The **recovered memory** debate asks what therapists can do to their clients' memories. Read every model as a clinician would: what should be spared, and what lost, when one part of the system is damaged?

**How to read this chapter.** 6.1 sets out the vocabulary. 6.2 compares the **multi-store** and **unitary-store** models. 6.3, the longest and most examined section, takes **Baddeley's working memory model** component by component. 6.4 turns to **individual differences in working memory capacity**. 6.5 (**levels of processing**) and 6.6 (**the testing effect**) ask what makes long-term learning succeed. 6.7 asks whether learning can be **implicit**. 6.8 covers the theories of **forgetting from long-term memory**. Sections 6.3 and 6.8 carry the most marks, and their evaluations feed the "which is NOT a limitation of..." items that Indian papers favour.

## 6.1 Introduction

This is the first of three memory chapters. All three concern intact human memory; this one and Chapter 7 focus on laboratory research (Chapter 7 adds amnesic patients in detail), and Chapter 8 on everyday memory. The authors stress that the same theoretical questions apply to healthy and brain-damaged people, in the laboratory or in the field.

Remembering has three stages:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Encoding** | Processing carried out while the material is presented; it overlaps heavily with perception |
| **Storage** | The outcome of encoding: information now held in the memory system |
| **Retrieval** | Recovering stored information; when retrieval attempts fail, we call it **forgetting** |

These processes run within the **architecture** of the memory system (its stores or components and their connections). Architecture and process cannot be separated, and retrieval presupposes earlier encoding and storage.

> **Trap.** "Forgetting" is defined here as an *unsuccessful retrieval attempt*, which is neutral about cause. Equating it with "loss of the trace" assumes the decay account, only one of the five theories in 6.8.

## 6.2 Architecture of Memory

Short-term and long-term memory look very different. Short-term memory holds **a few items** for **a few seconds**; long-term memory has **essentially unlimited capacity** and lasts **up to several decades**. **Multi-store models** are built on this distinction; **unitary-store models** argue it is much less clear-cut.

### The multi-store model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968)

**Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968)** proposed three kinds of store:

| Store | Capacity | Duration | Key property |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensory stores** | Large | Very brief | **Modality-specific** (one per sense) |
| **Short-term store** | Very limited | Seconds | Where information is attended to and rehearsed |
| **Long-term store** | Essentially unlimited | Very long | Trace strength depends on rehearsal |

The lost Figure 6.1 shows a one-way flow:

1. Environmental input enters the **sensory stores** and is held very briefly.
2. Some of it is **attended to** and passed to the **short-term store**.
3. Some of that is **transferred to the long-term store**.
4. The **amount of rehearsal** in the short-term store directly determines the **strength** of the long-term trace.

Point 4 is the assumption that levels of processing (6.5) and the testing effect (6.6) later attack.

### Sensory stores

**Iconic memory**, the visual store, holds information for about **500 milliseconds**: Sperling (1960) argued it decays within that time, though this may be an underestimate. It matters because perceptual mechanisms always operate on the icon, which extends the time visual information is available (for example, during reading).

> **Classic background:** Sperling's **partial-report** method showed that people who tried to report a whole brief letter grid managed only about four or five letters, but when a tone after the display named one row they could report most of it, so much more of the display had briefly been available. The advantage faded if the tone came later than a fraction of a second.

Iconic memory has traditionally been called **preattentive**, and Atkinson and Shiffrin accepted this: attention acts only after the sensory stores. The 7th edition reports evidence against it: **Persuh et al. (2012)** found iconic storage was **severely disrupted** by a simultaneous attention-demanding task.

**Echoic memory** is the auditory equivalent. It explains the experience of asking "What did you say?" and then realising, before the speaker repeats it, that you know what was said. **Ioannides et al. (2003)** measured brain responses to tones: echoic memory lasted **longer in the left hemisphere than the right** (up to about **five seconds versus two**), probably reflecting left-hemisphere dominance for language. Stores exist for the other senses too, but they matter less and are little studied.

> **Trap.** Iconic about **500 ms**; echoic about **2 seconds** (key term; the text says "a few seconds"), with Ioannides et al. finding **5 s left, 2 s right**. And iconic memory is **not purely preattentive** (Persuh et al., 2012), against the older textbook claim and Atkinson and Shiffrin's assumption.

### Short-term memory

#### Capacity: from seven items to three or four chunks

In **digit span**, a random digit series is heard and repeated immediately in order (letter and word spans work the same way). The longest series recalled without error is usually about **seven** (**Miller, 1956**). The book gives two reasons not to treat seven as the true capacity:

1. **Items are not chunks.** A **chunk** is a group of items bound together and treated as one unit. In a random span task each item is its own chunk, but the ten letters P-S-Y-C-H-O-L-O-G-Y make one chunk and are recalled perfectly.
2. **Span is inflated** by **rehearsal** and **long-term memory**.

**Mathy and Feldman (2012)** made "chunk" more precise: people compress a string into the fewest distinct sequences they can, and those sequences are the chunks. On that definition, people recalled **three or four chunks** in order. **Chen and Cowan (2009)** used chunks of single words or previously learned word pairs and blocked rehearsal with **articulatory suppression** (repeating "the"): only **three chunks** were recalled.

> **Trap.** **Miller (1956): about 7 plus or minus 2 items.** **7th edition: about 3 to 4 chunks** once rehearsal and long-term memory are controlled. *Classic background:* the "four chunks" figure is usually credited to **Cowan (2001)**. Many older keys still mark 7 as STM capacity; read whether the stem wants Miller's figure or the modern one.

#### Items in short-term memory are not equal

The multi-store model treats all items as equally important, which **Nee and Jonides (2013)** call an oversimplification. In **probe tasks** (a list, then a probe: was it in the list?), responses are **fastest when the probe is the most recently rehearsed item** (**McElree, 2006**). The item in the **focus of attention** is privileged.

#### Losing information: decay versus interference

Information might **decay** without rehearsal, or suffer **interference** from earlier trials or from material in the retention interval. **Berman et al. (2009)** favoured interference: words from the previous trial disrupted the current one, but **lengthening the intertrial interval had no effect** (decay predicts it should help), whereas **reducing interference** largely removed the disruption. **Campoy (2012)** noted that this design could not detect decay within **3.3 seconds** of presentation, and found **strong decay below 3.3 seconds**. Conclusion: **decay at short intervals, interference at longer ones**.

> **Classic background:** in the **Brown-Peterson** task (Brown, 1958; Peterson & Peterson, 1959) a trigram was followed by counting backwards in threes, and recall fell steeply over about 18 seconds, first read as decay. Keppel and Underwood (1962) showed there was little forgetting on the first trials, implicating **proactive interference** from earlier trials.

#### A double dissociation

If the stores are separate, brain damage should sometimes impair one and spare the other, in both directions. It does.

- **Amnesic** patients (Chapter 7) have severe long-term deficits but usually **intact short-term memory** (**Spiers et al., 2001**).
- **KF** (**Shallice & Warrington, 1970**) had normal long-term learning and recall but a **very small digit span**. His problem mainly concerned **letters, words and digits**, not **meaningful sounds or visual stimuli** (e.g., Shallice & Warrington, 1974), a material-specific deficit that already points to separate verbal and non-verbal short-term systems (6.3).

> **Classic background:** the amnesic side is classically illustrated by **HM** (Scoville & Milner, 1957; Chapter 7), with normal digit span but no new lasting conscious memories. The **serial position curve** gives a behavioural argument: **primacy** (early items, rehearsed into long-term memory) and **recency** (last items, still in the short-term store); a short filled delay abolishes recency but spares primacy (Glanzer & Cunitz, 1966).

> **Trap.** **KF** = impaired STM, intact LTM (the multi-store dissociation patient). **PV** (6.3) also has a span of two but is the **phonological loop / vocabulary-learning** patient. Amnesic patients show the opposite pattern to both.

### Evaluation of the multi-store model

**Strengths.** Hugely influential; the distinctions among three kinds of store are still widely accepted; several kinds of evidence support the short-term/long-term distinction, the strongest being patients impaired on only one.

**Limitations.**

1. **Oversimplified.** Both stores are treated as **unitary**. The short-term store has since become a four-component **working memory** system, and there are several long-term systems (Chapter 7).
2. **Not a gateway.** Information in short-term memory has *already* contacted long-term memory (**Logie, 1999**): you can rehearse "IBM" as one chunk only because you know what IBM is.
3. **Consciousness is not required.** Equating short-term contents with consciousness implies only conscious material reaches long-term memory, but **implicit learning** (6.7) shows otherwise.
4. **Items are unequal.** The attended item is accessed fastest (McElree, 2006).
5. **Rehearsal is overrated.** Only a small fraction of what we store was ever rehearsed.

> **Trap.** "It cannot explain the double dissociation in patients" is **not** a limitation; the dissociation is the model's best evidence.

### The unitary-store model (Jonides et al., 2008)

**Jonides et al. (2008)** argued that short-term memory consists of **temporary activations of long-term memory representations** (or of very recently perceived items), especially those in the **focus of attention**. Atkinson and Shiffrin stressed differences between the two memories; unitary theorists stress similarities. Word span is about **seven** for random words but can reach **20** when the words form sentences (**Simon, 1974**), because large chunks draw on long-term knowledge.

How can amnesic patients then have normal short-term but impaired long-term memory? Jonides et al. said their real problem is **forming novel relations** (for example, binding an item to its context) in both kinds of memory; ordinary short-term tasks rarely need relational storage. So amnesic patients should fail **relational** short-term tasks. Neurally, the **hippocampus and surrounding medial temporal lobes** form novel relations and should be engaged by any short-term task requiring them, whereas multi-store theorists see these structures as mainly long-term.

#### Findings

- **For:** amnesic patients with medial temporal damage often do poorly even at short study-test intervals (**Jeneson & Squire, 2012**). **Hannula et al. (2006)** repeated scenes exactly or with one object moved; hippocampal amnesic patients were worse than controls **even at very short intervals**.
- **Against:** long-term memory may have contributed there. **Jeneson et al. (2011)** reduced the **memory load** to keep long-term memory out, and hippocampal patients performed **as well as controls**.
- **Imaging:** hippocampal activity in short-term tasks (Jeneson & Squire, 2012) may just reflect long-term encoding. **Bergmann et al. (2012)** examined word pairs remembered short-term but **later forgotten**: their short-term learning showed **no hippocampal activation**.
- **Refinement:** **Race et al. (2013)** tested face recognition after **eight seconds** (lost Figure 6.2 also shows a 15-second delay and compares controls, all amnesic patients, hippocampus-only patients and patients with wider damage). **Hippocampus-only** patients were **intact**; patients with **wider medial temporal damage** were **impaired**.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Activated long-term memory does play an important role in short-term memory; there is some (controversial) support for hippocampal involvement in short-term relational memory, and more promising evidence that related **medial temporal areas** contribute (Race et al., 2013).

**Limitations.** (1) Short-term memory is more than activation: we **manipulate** activated knowledge, as in **backward digit recall** or **generating novel images** (**Logie & van der Meulen, 2009**). (2) Most amnesia studies show **far greater** hippocampal involvement in long-term memory, which fits the multi-store view. (3) When short- and long-term processes are separated, imaging shows **little hippocampal involvement** (Bergmann et al., 2012). Summary verdict: **partially correct**, but the two memories differ more than it allows.

> **Trap.** The unitary-store model does **not** predict impaired digit span in amnesia; it explains intact span (no novel relations needed) and predicts deficits only on **relational** short-term tasks.

## 6.3 Working Memory

What is short-term memory actually *for*? Holding a phone number while dialling is no longer a convincing answer, since phones store numbers. **Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974)** gave a better one: we use short-term memory while performing **complex tasks**, because every multi-step task produces intermediate results that must be held while later steps are worked out (mental arithmetic is the clearest case). Their key insight was that short-term memory is essential to many tasks that are **not memory tasks at all**. They therefore replaced the passive short-term store with **working memory**, a set of limited-capacity components that both *hold* and *work on* information.

### The four-component model

The current version (**Baddeley, 2012**; lost Figure 6.3) has four components:

| Component | Role | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Central executive** | Modality-free, limited-capacity controller; Baddeley calls it "an attentional system" (2012, p. 22); stores nothing | 1974 |
| **Phonological loop** | Briefly processes and stores **speech-based** information; preserves word **order** | 1974 |
| **Visuo-spatial sketchpad** | Briefly stores and manipulates **visual and spatial** information | 1974 |
| **Episodic buffer** | Briefly stores **integrated** information from the loop, the sketchpad and long-term memory | **Baddeley (2000)**, about **25 years** later |

The **central executive** is the most important component; it resembles attention and is used by any demanding task. The loop and the sketchpad are **slave systems** it uses for specific purposes. In the figure, perception feeds the loop and sketchpad, both feed the buffer, the executive sits over all three, and the buffer connects to long-term memory (and is assumed to accept input from other senses too).

> **Trap.** The **original** model (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974) had **three** components; the **episodic buffer** (Baddeley, 2000) makes **four**. Older keys asking for "the three components" will not list the buffer. Working memory is also not a synonym for the short-term store: it **processes** as well as stores.

### The dual-task logic

The three original components each have limited capacity and work fairly independently. So: (1) two tasks that need the **same** component cannot both be done well together; (2) two tasks that need **different** components can be done together as well as separately. Pairing a main task with secondary tasks that load known components reveals which components the main task uses.

> **Key study, Robbins et al. (1996).** Weaker and stronger chess players chose moves while doing one of four secondary tasks: **repetitive tapping** (control); **random number generation** (loads the **central executive**); **pressing keypad keys in a clockwise order** (loads the **sketchpad**); or repeating **"see-saw"** (articulatory suppression, loads the **loop**). Move quality suffered with the executive and sketchpad tasks but **not** with articulatory suppression, and the effects were **similar for stronger and weaker players**.

So chess move selection uses the **central executive and sketchpad, not the loop**, and skill does not change which components are used. Tapping controls for simply doing two things at once. **Articulatory suppression** works by occupying the loop's **articulatory control process** with a repeated simple sound ("the the the"), which blocks rehearsal.

### The phonological loop

Following **Baddeley (1990)** (lost Figure 6.5), the loop has two parts: a **passive phonological store**, tied to **speech perception**, and an **articulatory process**, tied to **speech production**, which carries out **rehearsal** and gives access to the store.

> **Classic background:** in the standard account heard speech enters the phonological store directly, while printed words must first be recoded into speech by the articulatory process. That is why suppression affects visually presented lists more strongly.

#### The phonological similarity effect

Immediate serial recall is worse when the items **sound alike**. For lists such as FEE, HE, KNEE, LEE, ME, SHE versus BAY, HOE, IT, ODD, SHY, UP, ordered recall was **25 percent worse** for the similar list (**Larsen et al., 2000**), as a speech-based code predicts. But the effect is more complex than the model allows. **Acheson et al. (2010)** showed **semantic** processes contribute too. **Schweppe et al. (2011)** noted that the model is **underspecified** about whether **acoustic** (similar sound) or **articulatory** (similar movements) similarity matters; the effect depended **more on acoustic** similarity, but articulatory similarity had a significant effect when recall was **spoken** and not when it was **written**. The model predicts none of this.

#### The word-length effect

Word span is **greater for short than long words**, and the evidence for this is extensive (Baddeley, 2012).

> **Key study, Baddeley et al. (1975).** The effect appeared with **visual** and **auditory** presentation. When participants did **articulatory suppression by repeating the digits 1 to 8** during presentation, the effect **disappeared for visually presented words**, implying it depends on **rehearsal**.

> **Classic background:** the usual explanation is that long words take longer to rehearse; span roughly equals what a person can say in about two seconds.

**Jacquemot et al. (2011)** supported the rehearsal account: their patient, whose verbal rehearsal was greatly impaired, showed **no word-length effect**. But **Jalbert et al. (2011)** found a **confound** with **orthographic neighbourhood** (words of the same length differing by one letter). Short words have **more neighbours**, so the effect might really concern neighbourhood size; when one- and three-syllable words were **matched for neighbourhood size**, the word-length effect **disappeared**. The book concludes there are now some doubts that it exists.

> **Trap.** Suppression abolishing the effect for **visual** words (Baddeley et al., 1975) is a **rehearsal** argument; the effect vanishing when **neighbourhood size** is matched (Jalbert et al., 2011) is a **confound** argument against the effect itself.

#### What the loop is for: PV

**Baddeley et al. (1988)** studied **PV**, whose **digit span was two** (population average about seven) but who coped very well with daily life, **running a shop and raising a family**. **Baddeley et al. (1998)** argued the loop's real function is **language learning**. PV, a native **Italian** speaker, learned pairs of **unrelated Italian words** as well as healthy people, but when learning **Russian words with their Italian translations** she showed **no learning at all over ten trials**. Familiar words can be linked without the loop; a **new sound pattern** must be held in the loop long enough to be learned. **Papagno et al. (1991)** agreed: articulatory suppression **greatly slowed foreign vocabulary learning** but barely affected learning Italian words.

The loop may also support **action control**, the "inner voice" used to resist temptation. **Tullett and Inzlicht (2010)** reduced loop use by suppression (repeating **"computer"**) on a simple task with low or high action-control demands; suppression reduced control and substantially increased errors **only when demands were high**.

> **Trap.** Each study used a different suppression token: digits **1 to 8** (Baddeley et al., 1975), **"the"** (Chen & Cowan, 2009), **"see-saw"** (Robbins et al., 1996), **"computer"** (Tullett & Inzlicht, 2010). The logic is the same: occupy the articulatory process.

### The visuo-spatial sketchpad

The sketchpad stores and manipulates **visual patterns** and **spatial movement**: visual processing is remembering **what**, spatial processing remembering **where**. We use it to find our way around or while watching television. The key question is whether visual and spatial processing form one system or separable ones. **Logie (1995)** proposed two components:

| Component | Handles | Extra role |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual cache** | Visual **form and colour** | Passive store |
| **Inner scribe** | **Spatial and movement** information | Rehearses the cache and passes its contents to the central executive |

Later developments are discussed by **Logie and van der Meulen (2009)**.

> **Trap.** The **visual cache** is the passive store; the **inner scribe** is the active, spatial component that **rehearses** it, mirroring the phonological store and articulatory process.

#### Findings

**Smith and Jonides (1997)** showed two stimuli and then a probe. The **spatial** task asked whether the probe matched a **location**, the **visual** task whether it matched a **form**. With identical stimuli, the spatial task produced more **right-hemisphere** activity and the visual task more **left-hemisphere** activity. **Zimmer (2008)** reviewed the anatomy: **occipital and temporal** areas for visual processing, **parietal** areas (especially the **intraparietal sulcus**) for spatial processing.

> **Key study, Klauer and Zhao (2004).** A **spatial** task (memory for **dot locations**) and a **visual** task (memory for **Chinese characters**) were each combined with **colour discrimination** (visual interference) or **movement discrimination** (spatial interference). Spatial interference hurt the spatial task more, and visual interference hurt the visual task more (lost Figure 6.6): a crossover supporting separate components.

**Vergauwe et al. (2009)** argued that these tasks were **too easy to need much attention**. With **more demanding** versions, each kind of interference had **comparable** effects on both tasks. So interference is **specific** when tasks are undemanding but **general** when they draw on the executive's attention; the components look separate only to the extent that general attention is not needed.

#### Evaluation

The sketchpad has **somewhat separable** visual and spatial components. Unknown: how the two are **integrated**, how the sketchpad **interacts with the episodic buffer**, and, as **Baddeley (2012)** admits, how **rehearsal** in the sketchpad works.

### The central executive

The **central executive** resembles an attentional system, is involved in nearly all complex activities (problem solving, doing two tasks at once), and **stores nothing**. The **prefrontal cortex** is assumed to be its main site: **Mottaghy (2006)** reviewed studies in which **rTMS** of the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (BA9/46)** impaired many complex tasks. But executive processes do **not depend on the prefrontal cortex alone**: many patients with **diffuse trauma, multiple sclerosis or vascular cognitive impairment** have poor executive functioning with **little or no frontal damage** (**Stuss, 2011**).

**Executive processes** organise and coordinate cognition towards current goals. **Baddeley (1996)** speculatively proposed **four**: **focusing** attention, **dividing** attention between two streams, **switching** attention between tasks, and **interfacing with long-term memory**. Agreement on the number and nature of executive processes has proved elusive (Baddeley, 2012).

#### Miyake et al.'s three executive functions

**Miyake et al. (2000)** gave a battery of executive tasks and grouped tasks that **correlated positively**, taking each group to share a process. They found three **related but separable** functions:

| Function | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inhibition** | Deliberately overriding dominant responses and resisting distraction | **Stroop task**: name the ink colour of BLUE printed in red, suppressing "blue" |
| **Shifting** | Switching flexibly between tasks or mental sets | Alternately multiplying two numbers and dividing one by the other |
| **Updating** | Monitoring and rapidly adding or deleting working memory contents | Tracking the latest item in each of several categories |

**Miyake and Friedman (2012)** developed this into the **unity/diversity framework**: each function is what is **common to all three** plus what is **unique** to it (lost Figure 6.7). **Friedman et al. (2008)** found **no unique variance for inhibition** once common variance was removed: inhibition correlates almost perfectly with the common factor, which Miyake and Friedman describe as the ability to keep task goals active and use them to bias lower-level processing. Friedman et al. also studied **monozygotic and dizygotic twins** and found **substantial genetic contributions** at both unity and diversity levels. **Collette et al. (2005)** found each function activated a **different prefrontal area** (diversity) while all tasks activated shared regions such as the **left lateral prefrontal cortex** (unity). **Hedden and Gabrieli (2010)** (lost Figure 6.8) found **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and basal ganglia** strongly linked to **both** inhibition and shifting; **right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex** and **bilateral temporo-parietal junction** more active for **inhibition**; and only **modest** evidence of shifting-specific areas. Overlap, with some diversity, especially for inhibition.

> **Trap.** In the unity/diversity framework it is **inhibition** that has **no unique component**; shifting and updating keep function-specific variance.

#### The dysexecutive syndrome

Patients whose executive is damaged by **frontal** lesions have the **dysexecutive syndrome** (**Baddeley, 1996**). **Godefroy et al. (2010)** listed impaired **response inhibition**, **rule deduction and generation**, **maintenance and shifting of sets**, and **information generation**. Such patients usually struggle to **hold a job** and manage daily life (**Chamberlain, 2003**).

**Stuss and Alexander (2007)** called the notion flawed because it implies frontal damage impairs **all** executive functions. They accepted a **global** syndrome after **widespread** frontal damage, but in patients with **limited** prefrontal damage they found three processes, each tied to a **different frontal region**:

| Process | What it involves | Effect of damage |
|---|---|---|
| **Task setting** | **Planning**; setting up stimulus-response relationships, as when first learning to drive or planning a wedding | Not specified in the book |
| **Monitoring** | Checking the adequacy of one's performance | More **variable** performance and more **errors** |
| **Energisation** | **Sustained attention** | **Slow** responding on all speeded tasks |

These overlap reasonably with Miyake's functions (task setting and monitoring, like inhibition and shifting, involve cognitive control). **Stuss (2011)** confirmed the three and added **metacognition/integration**, an orchestrating function that includes distinguishing what one **knows** from what one **believes**, supported by patients with **BA10 (frontopolar)** damage (**Burgess et al., 2007**).

> **Key study, Gläscher et al. (2012).** Brain-damaged patients did many tasks, including the **Iowa Gambling Task** (value-based decision making: choose from **four** decks and learn which are "good" and which "bad"). Two networks emerged (lost Figure 6.9): a **cognitive control network** (**dorsolateral prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex**) for inhibition, conflict monitoring and switching, and a **value-based decision-making network** (**orbitofrontal, ventromedial and frontopolar cortex**) strongly linked to the **limbic system**.

> **Trap.** Match author to list: **Baddeley (1996)** focusing, dividing, switching, interfacing with LTM; **Miyake et al. (2000)** inhibition, shifting, updating; **Stuss and Alexander (2007)** task setting, monitoring, energisation, plus **metacognition/integration (Stuss, 2011)**; **Gläscher et al. (2012)** two networks.

#### Evaluation

The executive consists of **related but separable** processes, as the unity/diversity framework says, on behavioural and imaging evidence; a single dysexecutive syndrome is misleading, since different processes depend on different prefrontal regions. Limitations: the **task-impurity problem** (most tasks involve several processes; Miyake & Friedman, 2012), and continuing uncertainty about which processes count as executive (**dual-task coordination**, **value-based decision making**, **metacognition/integration**?).

### The episodic buffer

The buffer is **episodic** because it holds **integrated chunks** about episodes in a **multi-dimensional code** combining visual, auditory and other information, and a **buffer** because it links the other components with each other and with **perception** and **long-term memory**. Its capacity is about **four chunks** (Baddeley, 2012). **Baddeley (2000)** added it for two reasons: the original components were **too separate**, with nowhere for verbal and visuo-spatial information to be **combined**; and people recall about **five unrelated words** but **up to 16 words in sentences** (**Baddeley et al., 1987**), far beyond the loop's capacity but within that of a four-chunk store.

Its relation to the executive has changed. **Baddeley (2000)** had the executive **controlling access** to the buffer, so storing integrated information needed executive processing; **Baddeley (2012)** holds that information can be stored there **without direct executive involvement**.

#### Findings

**Baddeley and Wilson (2002)** proposed that immediate recall of sentences up to about **15 words** depends on buffer capacity plus an efficient executive to chunk the sentence. So even **severely amnesic** patients, with almost no **delayed** prose recall, should have good **immediate** prose recall if their executive is intact, and immediate recall was indeed **much better in amnesic patients with little executive deficit**. Later work did not confirm the executive's role. **Baddeley et al. (2009)** found an executive secondary task impaired sentence and word-list recall **similarly**. **Allen et al. (2012)** asked people to remember single features (colour, shape) or colour-shape **combinations** while sometimes **counting backwards**; the effect of counting was **comparable** either way, so feature binding appears **automatic**, occurring before information enters the buffer.

#### Evaluation

The buffer widens the model by giving the loop and sketchpad shared storage, and it is now seen as far less dependent on the executive: in Baddeley's (2012, p. 17) words, "an essentially passive structure on which bindings achieved elsewhere can be displayed". Unresolved: **how** loop and sketchpad information are combined there, and whether **smell or taste** information really enters it (no research).

> **Trap.** **Baddeley (2000):** executive controls the buffer. **Baddeley (2012):** the buffer is **passive**; binding happens elsewhere, often automatically. "An active binding system controlled by the executive" is the superseded view.

### Overall evaluation of the working memory model

**Strengths over the short-term store.** (1) Its **scope** is wider: it handles **processing and storage** and is involved in all complex tasks. (2) It explains **partial short-term deficits** after brain damage confined to one component (as in PV). (3) It makes verbal rehearsal an **optional** loop process rather than the central mechanism. (4) Its main assumptions have survived about **40 years** of change in the field.

**Limitations.** (1) **Oversimplified**: many kinds of information (**smell, touch, taste**) are left out, and there is evidence for separate **eye-centred, hand-centred and foot-centred** spatial working memory (**Postle, 2006**); taken to its logical end, Postle argues, the model would need **hundreds** of domain-specific buffers. (2) The **number and nature of executive processes** remain unclear. (3) The **interactions** among the components, especially how the buffer integrates information from the others and from long-term memory, need much more research.

## 6.4 Working Memory Capacity

Baddeley's model asks what working memory's components are. A second tradition asks why people **differ** in working memory and what follows. **Working memory capacity** is how much a person can **process and store at the same time** (Chapter 10 applies it to comprehension).

### Measuring it

**Daneman and Carpenter (1980)** devised **reading span**: people read sentences for **comprehension** (processing) while remembering each sentence's **final word** (storage). Reading span is the largest number of sentences whose final words the person recalls **more than 50 percent of the time**. The assumption is that comprehension uses only a **small share** of a high-capacity person's resources, leaving more for holding the words. **Operation span** does the same with arithmetic, for example "IS (4 × 2) − 3 = 5? TABLE": answer each sum and remember all the words. It is the maximum number of items for which all the words are recalled, and it **correlates highly with reading span**.

> **Trap.** Reading and operation span combine **processing with storage**; plain digit span (storage only) is not a capacity measure in this sense.

Capacity is **not fixed**. It falls with **anxiety or stress** (**Eysenck et al., 2007**) and rises with **relaxation training** (**Kargar et al., 2013**), perhaps because relaxation reduces anxiety. Clinically, an anxious patient's poor score may partly reflect state rather than trait.

### Capacity and intelligence

Working memory capacity correlates about **+0.6 with intelligence**, and much more strongly with **fluid intelligence** (rapid grasp of novel relationships) than with **crystallised intelligence** (knowledge, skills and experience) (**Unsworth, 2010**). Capacity reflects processing and temporary storage, which novel problems demand; crystallised intelligence reflects what has already been acquired.

> **Trap.** Capacity goes with **fluid**, not crystallised, intelligence. Do not confuse **+0.6** (capacity with intelligence) with **Kaufman et al. (2010)** in 6.7 (**+0.44** explicit learning, **+0.16** implicit learning).

### Engle and Kane's two-factor theory

Most theories credit high-capacity people with better **executive and attentional** processes. In **Engle and Kane's (2004) two-factor theory**, they are better at (1) **maintaining task goals** and (2) **resolving response competition or conflict**. The overlap with Baddeley's model is mainly with the **central executive**. Why do the loop and sketchpad barely appear in this research? **Logie (2011)** suggests individual differences in those components are **small** compared with differences in the executive, so task differences mostly reflect executive functioning. High-capacity people unsurprisingly do better on complex tasks; the theory's sharper prediction is that they should also do better on **simple** tasks **when keeping the goal in mind is hard**.

### Findings

- **McCabe et al. (2010)** found **high correlations** between capacity and executive-function measures, both reflecting **executive attention** (keeping goals active and resolving interference).
- **External distraction.** **Sorqvist (2010)**: the sound of **aircraft flying past** disrupted prose recall more in **low-capacity** people. **Yurgil and Golob (2013)**: high-capacity people had **smaller ERPs to auditory distractors** and **larger ERPs to targets than non-targets**, whereas low-capacity people responded similarly to both, indicating better **goal maintenance** and **early top-down control**.
- **Internal distraction.** **McVay and Kane (2012b)** used a sustained-attention task (respond fast to frequent targets, withhold to rare non-targets). Low-capacity people did worse, largely because they reported more **mind wandering**.
- **Goal neglect.** **Unsworth et al. (2012)** used the **anti-saccade task**: a cue flashes on one side and the target appears on the **opposite** side, so the reflexive look must be suppressed. High-capacity people did better overall (a **response-conflict** effect). When each person's reaction times were split into **quintiles**, high-capacity people were faster **only in the slowest quintile** (lost Figure 6.10): low-capacity people lost the goal on roughly **20 percent of trials**, not on every trial.

> **Trap.** Unsworth et al. (2012) found no across-the-board slowing; the difference was confined to the **slowest quintile**, the signature of **intermittent goal neglect**.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** High- and low-capacity people differ in **attentional control**: high-capacity people resist **external and internal** distraction and show **less goal neglect**, and capacity predicts performance on a wide range of tasks. **Limitations.** (1) The approach is **less comprehensive** than Baddeley's model. (2) Because capacity correlates so highly with **fluid intelligence**, findings attributed to capacity may really reflect fluid intelligence.

## 6.5 Levels of Processing

The multi-store model said the amount of rehearsal determines trace strength. **Craik and Lockhart (1972)** argued instead that what matters is **how** information is processed at learning: attentional and perceptual processes determine what is stored. Processing ranges from **shallow** physical analysis (detecting letters) to **deep** **semantic** analysis; the more meaning is processed, the deeper the level. Their assumptions: depth has a **large effect** on memorability, and deeper analysis yields **more elaborate, longer-lasting and stronger** traces.

> **Classic background:** Craik and Lockhart contrasted **maintenance rehearsal** (Type I: repetition at the same level, adding little to retention) with **elaborative rehearsal** (Type II: deeper processing, which aids retention), a direct challenge to the idea that rehearsal *amount* sets trace strength.

### Depth and elaboration

> **Key study, Craik and Tulving (1975): depth.** Learning was **incidental** (no warning of a test). Words received a **shallow graphemic** task (upper or lower case?), an **intermediate phonemic** task (rhymes with a target?) or a **deep semantic** task (fits the blank in a sentence?). **Recognition** was **more than three times higher** after deep than shallow processing.

Since nobody was trying to memorise, the differences can only come from the orienting task: memory depends on the **nature of processing**, not the **intention to learn**.

> **Key study, Craik and Tulving (1975): elaboration.** Words were judged against **simple** frames ("She cooked the ____") or **complex** frames ("The great bird swooped down and carried off the struggling ____"). **Cued recall was twice as high** with complex frames.

Richness of processing at a given depth (**elaboration**) therefore matters as well as depth.

### Transfer-appropriate processing

> **Key study, Morris et al. (1977).** Participants answered **semantic** or **rhyme** questions about words and then took a **standard recognition** test or a **rhyming recognition** test (choose new words that **rhyme with** list words). Deep beat shallow on the standard test, but **shallow beat deep on the rhyme test**.

This contradicted the central prediction of levels theory. Morris et al.'s **transfer-appropriate processing** says retrieval succeeds when learning processes are **relevant to the test's demands**: rhyme information was what the rhyme test needed. **Mulligan and Picklesimer (2012)** replicated this and showed the rhyme-test effect depended on **recollection** of context: rhyme cues more often brought back contextual details for **shallowly** processed items.

> **Trap.** Transfer-appropriate processing (Morris et al., 1977) and **encoding specificity** (Tulving, 1979; 6.8) both say memory depends on the learning-test match; the book's distinction is that transfer-appropriate processing focuses more directly on the **processes**. And in Morris et al., shallow won **only on the rhyme test**.

### Explicit and implicit memory

Levels effects are strong on **explicit** tests (recall, recognition: conscious recollection). **Implicit memory** does not depend on conscious recollection (Chapter 7); in **word-fragment completion** (c _ p p _ _) it shows up as more completions with previously seen words. **Challis et al. (1996)** found the levels effect generally **smaller in implicit memory** and **especially small for word-fragment completion**. **Parks (2013)** explained this through transfer-appropriate processing: shallow processing is **more perceptual and less conceptual**, so the depth advantage should **shrink** when the test is perceptually demanding. Varying the perceptual demands of recognition tests confirmed that the effect **decreased as perceptual demands increased**.

### Distinctiveness

A trace is **distinctive** when it differs from other traces because it was processed differently. **Hunt (2013, p. 10)** defines distinctive processing as "the processing of difference in the context of similarity", illustrated by a **purple car with lemon-yellow polka dots** in ordinary traffic.

> **Key study, Eysenck and Eysenck (1980).** Nouns with **irregular pronunciation** (comb, with its silent "b") were sometimes pronounced distinctively (sounding the "b"). That processing is **shallow** (phonemic) but **distinctive**, and **recognition** in that condition was **as good as** after **semantic** processing.

Depth and distinctiveness can therefore be pulled apart. **Hunt and Rawson (2011)** found that people knowledgeable about **American football** recognised football items better, partly because knowledge helped them **organise** the material and partly because it increased **distinctiveness**, helping them **reject familiar but wrong items**.

> **Trap.** Eysenck and Eysenck (1980) measured **recognition**, and the distinctive condition was **shallow**: distinctiveness can compensate for shallow depth.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** (1) Processes at learning do strongly affect long-term memory (**Roediger, 2008**), and saying so challenged the multi-store approach. (2) Learning and remembering are treated as **by-products of perception, attention and comprehension**. (3) The approach identified **elaboration** and **distinctiveness**. (4) **Roediger and Gallo (2001)** credit it with generating a powerful set of experimental techniques.

**Limitations.** (1) It **underestimated the retrieval environment** (Morris et al., 1977; Mulligan & Picklesimer, 2012). (2) The **relative importance** of depth, elaboration and distinctiveness is unclear. (3) None of the three terms has been **defined or measured precisely** (Roediger & Gallo, 2001). (4) We do not know **why** deep processing works so well or why the effect is **small in implicit memory**; the summary calls the theory **not explanatory**.

> **Classic background:** limitation 3 is the classic **circularity** criticism (associated with Baddeley, 1978): without an independent measure, depth is inferred from good memory and then used to explain it.

## 6.6 Learning Through Retrieval

Most people assume that learning happens during study and that tests only **measure** it; Pyc and Rawson (2010, p. 335) state this common view in order to reject it. In fact, practising **retrieval** during learning improves long-term memory **more than studying and restudying**: the **testing effect**, which is generally surprisingly strong (**Karpicke, 2012**). **Dunlosky et al. (2013)** evaluated **ten** techniques claimed to help students in exams (including summarising, imagery for text and restudying) and judged **repeated testing the most effective** of them all.

### The classic demonstration

> **Key study, Roediger and Karpicke (2006).** Students learned a prose passage by **repeated study** (read **four** times, no test), **single test** (read **three** times, recall once) or **repeated test** (read **once**, recall **three** times). The final test came after **five minutes** or **one week**. At five minutes **repeated study was best**. At one week the pattern **reversed**: recall was **50 percent higher** after repeated testing than repeated study, although the repeated-study students had **predicted** they would recall more.

The benefit is **long-term**, which is exactly why learners are fooled: on an immediate test restudy looks better, and learners' own predictions point the wrong way.

> **Trap.** At **five minutes restudy won**; the testing effect appeared at **one week**.

### Why testing works

**Mediator effectiveness hypothesis (Pyc & Rawson, 2010).** Testing leads learners to use **better mediators**, the linking ideas in **paired-associate learning**. To learn Swahili-English **wingu-cloud** you might use **plane**, then retrieve the chain wingu, plane, cloud.

> **Key study, Pyc and Rawson (2010).** After an initial study trial, one group had **restudy-only** trials and another **test-restudy** trials (cued recall, then restudy); participants reported their mediators. A week later the final test gave the **cue only (C)**, the **cue plus their own mediator (CM)**, or the **cue plus a prompt to recall the mediator (CMR)**. In C, test-restudy won (the testing effect). In CM, test-restudy still won, showing **more effective mediators**. In CMR, test-restudy won by far, because these participants **remembered their mediators** much better (lost Figure 6.11); when they failed to retrieve the mediator, target recall was poor.

**Pyc and Rawson (2012)** found people **changed mediators more often** under test-restudy practice, and especially **after retrieval failures**. Retrieval tells you which mediators fail, so you replace them; restudy gives no such feedback.

**Retrieval effort hypothesis (Pyc & Rawson, 2009).** Testing helps more when retrieval is **hard**. Increasing the **number of items between successive practice retrievals** of an item made final recall **significantly better**. The two hypotheses combine: difficult retrieval increases the need for effective mediators. Testing also involves **more effortful semantic processing**: language regions such as the **left inferior frontal gyrus** are more active during testing than restudy (**van den Broek et al., 2013**).

> **Trap.** **2009** = retrieval **effort** (harder is better, not easier); **2010** = mediator **effectiveness**; **2012** = mediator **change** after failure.

### How far does it generalise?

Testing mostly strengthens **specific responses**, but there is some evidence of broader benefit (**Carpenter, 2012**): concepts learned by testing supported better **inferences** (**Butler, 2010**). It can also narrow learning.

> **Key study, Peterson and Mulligan (2013).** Participants learned rhyming pairs such as force-horse, tape-grape, wife-knife, swear-bear, cork-fork, vow-cow, teach-peach and moon-spoon, whose targets fall into **categories** (four-footed animals, fruit, kitchen utensils). After initial study, one group **restudied** and another practised **retrieval**; the final test was **free recall of the targets without cues**. The retrieval group, absorbed in retrieving each target, was **less likely to notice the categories**, showed **less clustering** (lost Figure 6.12), and testing proved **less effective** than restudy.

Retrieval effort can constrain what is learned, strengthening item-specific links at the expense of relations among items.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** The effect is **strong**, widely replicated, usable with almost **any material**, and **much larger** than people expect; mechanisms (better mediators, especially under **difficult** retrieval) are being identified. **Limitations.** (1) Mediator research mostly uses **paired associates**, so generality is unclear. (2) **Other factors** may contribute. (3) Most accounts assume testing helps under almost any circumstances, so cases where it is **worse than restudy** (Peterson & Mulligan, 2013) are a challenge.

## 6.7 Implicit Learning

**Implicit learning** is learning complex information **without conscious awareness of what has been learned**. **Cleeremans and Jiménez (2002, p. 20)** define it as becoming sensitive to environmental regularities (1) **without intending** to learn them, (2) **without awareness** of learning, and (3) in a way that leaves knowledge **hard to express**. It overlaps with **implicit memory** (Chapter 7) and there is no clear boundary; implicit learning research tends to use **complex, novel** tasks, implicit memory research **simple, familiar** stimuli.

> **Trap.** The 7th edition says there is **no clear distinction** between implicit learning and implicit memory; the practical difference is **complex novel tasks** versus **simple familiar materials**.

### Reber's five assumptions

**Reber (1993)** proposed five differences between implicit and explicit learning, **none definitively established**: **age independence** (little affected by age or development), **IQ independence**, **robustness** (spared by disorders such as amnesia that impair explicit systems), **low variability** (smaller individual differences) and **commonality of process** (shared by most species).

**Age.** Implicit learning is often essentially intact in older adults (**Simon et al., 2012**), but in Simon et al.'s own study of implicit **associative** learning, older adults fell **increasingly behind** as training continued and showed **less striatal activation**.

**IQ.** **Kaufman et al. (2010)**: intelligence correlated **+0.44 with explicit** but only **+0.16 with implicit** learning. **Janacsek and Nemeth (2013)**: **working memory capacity** was typically related to **explicit** but **not implicit** learning. Both support IQ independence.

#### In the real world: skilled typists and implicit learning

The typical American college student has about **ten years** of typing experience and types around **70 words a minute**, yet many typists cannot say where letters are; the book's first author, with some **7 million words** typed for publication, has only a vague idea. Learning to type may start explicitly, but skilled typing looks implicit.

**Snyder et al. (2014)** tested typists averaging **11.4 years** of experience. On a **blank keyboard**, they placed only **14.9 letters (57.3 percent)** correctly (lost Figures 6.13 and 6.18, the latter giving each letter's percentages located, omitted and misplaced). When **simulated typing** was prevented by having them press a key sequence during the task, accuracy **fell significantly**, so genuine explicit knowledge is even lower. After **two hours** of training on the **Dvorak** layout, their explicit knowledge of Dvorak was **comparable** to that of QWERTY after a decade. Implicit knowledge is accessed **fast**, which touch-typing at **five or six keystrokes per second** needs; explicit lookup would be far too slow.

### Assessing implicit learning

Showing that someone improved on a task but reports no awareness is not enough, because people fail to report knowledge for several reasons (**Shanks, 2010**). One is the **retrospective problem** (**Shanks & St John, 1994**): they may have been aware at the time but forgotten by the final questionnaire. Shanks and St John set two criteria. The **information criterion**: the awareness test must ask about the knowledge **actually responsible** for the improvement. The **sensitivity criterion**: the test must be **sensitive to all the relevant knowledge**, or conscious knowledge will be underestimated.

In the **process-dissociation procedure**, people who have experienced a repeating sequence either try to **guess** the next stimulus (**inclusion**) or to **avoid** producing it (**exclusion**). Implicit knowledge cannot be controlled, so if learning is **wholly implicit**, inclusion and exclusion performance are **similar**. If it is partly **explicit**, people use it in inclusion and withhold it in exclusion, so **inclusion exceeds exclusion**; the difference estimates explicit learning. The method assumes conscious and unconscious influences are **independent**, which is controversial, but **Joordens et al. (2010)** found support for that assumption.

> **Trap.** **Inclusion greater than exclusion = explicit knowledge; inclusion equal to exclusion = implicit only.**

### Findings

In the **serial reaction time task**, a stimulus appears at one of several locations and the matching key is pressed fast; unknown to participants, the locations follow a **complex repeating sequence**, often followed near the end by a block with a **new** sequence. People speed up, then slow sharply on the new sequence, and usually report **no awareness** of any sequence (**Shanks, 2010**). But awareness is often **partial**. **Wilkinson and Shanks (2004)** gave **1,500 trials (15 blocks)** or **4,500 trials (45 blocks)**; process dissociation then showed predictions **better in inclusion than exclusion** (lost Figure 6.14), revealing explicit knowledge. **Gaillard et al. (2009)** found the same, with conscious knowledge **growing with practice**.

> **Key study, Haider et al. (2011).** Using **several** awareness measures, they ran a version of the task in which a colour word appeared in the same (congruent) or a different (incongruent) ink, and participants pressed the one of **six** coloured squares matching the **word**. Unknown to them, the correct square followed the sequence **1-6-4-2-3-5**. **34 percent** showed a **sudden drop in reaction time** (predicted explicit learners) and **66 percent** did not (predicted implicit only); the RT-drop group learned much more (lost Figure 6.15). In process dissociation the RT-drop group scored **80 percent on inclusion versus 18 percent on exclusion**, while the no-drop group was **equally low** on both. **91 percent** of the RT-drop group but **0 percent** of the others described the sequence perfectly.

Three independent measures agreed on who was aware.

**Attention.** If implicit learning needs no demanding resources, two implicit tasks should not interfere. **Jiménez and Vázquez (2011)** found **no interference** between the serial reaction time task and a second implicit task, but interference **appeared** once the reaction time task became **explicit**, since explicit learning uses limited attention.

### Neuroimaging

Conscious awareness is most consistently linked with the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** and **anterior cingulate** (**Dehaene & Naccache, 2001**), so these should be more active in **explicit** learning, whereas the **striatum** (part of the **basal ganglia**, including the **caudate nucleus** and **putamen**; lost Figure 6.16) is linked with **implicit** learning. Findings have been **inconsistent** (Shanks, 2010), partly because most tasks mix the two. **Destrebecqz et al. (2005)** separated them with process dissociation: **striatum** went with the **implicit** component, **prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate** with the **explicit** one. **Gheysen et al. (2011)** (fMRI) found the **striatum** involved in implicit **perceptual** and **motor** sequence learning, with the **hippocampus** also engaged in the motor task.

**Penhune and Steele (2012)** modelled motor sequence learning (lost Figure 6.17): the **striatum** learns **stimulus-response associations** and **motor chunking**; the **cerebellum** builds an **internal model** for performance and **error correction**; the **motor cortex** **stores** the sequence; each region's involvement **changes with the stage** of learning. **Wessel et al. (2012)** found that **50 percent** of participants became explicitly aware during training, and in them a region of coordinated activity centred on the **right prefrontal cortex** expanded around the **onset of explicit learning**.

### Brain-damaged patients

**Amnesia.** Amnesic patients with **medial temporal** damage do badly on explicit tests but often normally on implicit ones (Chapter 7). Separate systems predict **intact implicit** with **impaired explicit** learning; this has often been reported, though implicit learning is **sometimes** impaired (review: **Wilkinson et al., 2009**). **Van Tilborg et al. (2011)** found **normal** implicit serial reaction time learning in amnesic patients, supporting Reber's **robustness** assumption.

**Parkinson's disease** is a progressive **basal ganglia** disorder (rigidity, tremor, mask-like face), so it should impair implicit but spare explicit learning. **Wilkinson et al. (2009)** found implicit learning **impaired**, as predicted, but explicit learning **impaired too**, against prediction. **Foerde and Shohamy (2011)** noted several studies of impaired **explicit** learning and **variable** implicit learning, which suffered especially with **error-correcting feedback**, suggesting the basal ganglia help to **use feedback**. They concluded that **entirely separate** systems are an oversimplification: the **medial temporal lobes** and **basal ganglia** **interact** in complex ways.

> **Trap.** The tidy double dissociation (amnesia spares implicit, Parkinson's spares explicit) is only half supported: Parkinson's patients were impaired on **both** (Wilkinson et al., 2009), with implicit deficits clearest **with feedback**.

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** (1) The implicit/explicit distinction is supported by behavioural, imaging and patient evidence. (2) **Prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate** go more with explicit learning, the **striatum** with implicit, within **complex networks** (Penhune & Steele, 2012). (3) Awareness assessment has improved; **multiple** measures are best and often agree (Haider et al., 2011). (4) Most learning has **both** implicit and explicit aspects (**Sun et al., 2009**), varying across people, tasks and **stages of training** (Wessel et al., 2012); finding some explicit learning no longer implies there was no implicit learning.

**Limitations.** (1) Learning is usually a **mixture**, so the implicit share is hard to measure. (2) The two kinds of process probably **interact** in unclear ways. (3) Imaging is **inconsistent**: basal ganglia tend to go with implicit and prefrontal areas with explicit learning, with many exceptions, partly because implicit tasks vary so much (**Reber, 2013**).

## 6.8 Forgetting from Long-Term Memory

Short-term forgetting was covered in 6.2. This section deals with **long-term** forgetting: first the classic data, then why forgetting can be useful, then five families of theory (**decay**, **interference**, **repression and motivated forgetting**, **cue-dependent forgetting**, **consolidation and reconsolidation**). The theories are **not mutually exclusive**; each names a real cause, and most forgetting involves several.

### Ebbinghaus and the forgetting curve

**Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885/1913)**, his **own and only participant**, learned lists of meaningless **nonsense syllables** and **relearned** each after between **21 minutes and 31 days**. His measure was the **savings method**: how many **fewer trials** relearning needed than original learning. Savings reveal memory that a recall test would miss.

> **Classic background:** savings are usually expressed as (original trials minus relearning trials) divided by original trials, times 100; the syllables are usually described as consonant-vowel-consonant trigrams.

Forgetting was **very rapid in the first hour**, then **slowed considerably** (lost Figure 6.19). **Rubin and Wenzel (1996)**, analysing many forgetting functions, confirmed the pattern and argued for a **logarithmic** function; **Averell and Heathcote (2011)** favour a **power** function.

> **Trap.** Method: **savings** (relearning), one participant (himself). Shape: steep loss in the **first hour**, then much slower; **logarithmic** (Rubin & Wenzel, 1996) or **power** (Averell & Heathcote, 2011), never linear.

**Forgetting is often useful** (**Schacter et al., 2011**). What we need to know keeps changing: last year's lecture timetable or a friend's old address must be updated, which means forgetting the old version.

**Explicit versus implicit.** Most research concerns **explicit** (declarative) memory. Forgetting appears **slower in implicit memory** (e.g., **Tulving et al., 1982**). **Mitchell (2006)** had people identify pictures from **fragments**, some seen in an experiment **17 years** earlier; previously seen pictures were identified better, evidence of **very-long-term implicit memory**, yet explicit memory of the earlier experiment was poor (one 36-year-old man apologised that he could not remember it at all).

### Decay

**Decay** is forgetting due to "a gradual loss of the substrate of memory" (Hardt et al., 2013, p. 111). Plausible as it is, most theorists have **ignored** it. **Hardt et al. (2013)** revived it: we form countless **trivial** memories each day, so some process must clear them, and they propose a **decay process**, operating **mostly during sleep** and especially active in the **hippocampus**. Brain research supports this, but overwhelming evidence shows that **other factors** also contribute substantially.

#### In the real world: is perfect memory useful?

In **Jorge Luis Borges's (1964)** story **"Funes the memorious"**, a fall from a horse leaves Funes remembering everything in full detail. It is a disability: recalling a day takes **a whole day**, and he can barely **think**, troubled even that the same word "dog" covers the same animal seen from the side at one minute and from the front the next. Thinking needs generalisation, and generalisation needs discarded detail.

The nearest real case was **Solomon Shereshevskii (S)**, a **journalist** whose editor noticed he could repeat anything **word for word** and sent him to **Luria**. S learned lists of **more than 100 digits** and recalled them **perfectly years later**; Luria (1968) could find no limit to the capacity or durability of his memory. The basis was vivid **imagery** and exceptional **synaesthesia** (one sense evoking another). But the images collided with one another and with the speaker's voice until everything blurred; he often failed to **recognise faces and voices** of people he knew, because each encounter was stored in its own detail; Luria likened his mind to "a junk heap of impressions". S eventually ended up in an **asylum**. The box is the human counterpart of Hardt et al.'s argument: memory that cannot discard cannot generalise or update.

> **Trap.** **S** (Luria, 1968) could not forget; amnesic patients, KF and PV have memory deficits. **Funes** is **fictional** (Borges, 1964). **Synaesthesia** powered S's imagery; it is not a memory disorder.

### Interference: proactive and retroactive

**Interference theory** dominated much of the twentieth century. It describes two forms:

| Type | Interfering material | Impaired memory | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proactive interference (PI)** | **Earlier** learning (often similar) | Memory for **later** learning | Old disrupts new |
| **Retroactive interference (RI)** | **Later** learning or processing during the retention interval | Memory for **earlier** learning | New disrupts old |

The idea goes back to **Hugo Munsterberg** in the nineteenth century. He moved his pocket watch to a different pocket and then kept fumbling in the old one whenever asked the time: an old response to the same stimulus intruded on the new one, which is **PI**.

> **Classic background:** the paired-associate designs behind the lost Figure 6.20 pair the same stimuli (A) first with responses B, then with C. **PI:** experimental group learns A-B then A-C and is tested on **A-C**; controls rest, then learn and are tested on A-C. **RI:** experimental group learns A-B then A-C and is tested on **A-B**; controls learn A-B, rest, and are tested on A-B.

Both forms are **greatest when two different responses are attached to the same stimulus**. Strong RI also occurs in **eyewitness testimony**, where post-event information distorts memory of the event (Chapter 8).

> **Trap.** **Pro-** = forwards: **old** learning disrupts **new** (Munsterberg). **Retro-** = backwards: **new** learning disrupts **old** (misinformation, a foreign language crowding out native words). Name the memory being retrieved: an older intruder is proactive, a newer one retroactive.

#### Proactive interference

PI typically reflects **competition** between correct and incorrect responses, strongest when they share a stimulus. **Jacoby et al. (2001)** asked whether the correct response is too **weak** or the incorrect one too **strong**; PI was due **much more to the strong incorrect response**, so a major cause is the difficulty of **excluding wrong responses** from retrieval. **Bergström et al. (2012)** used **ERPs** and found an **early automatic** component reflecting conflict between correct and incorrect responses, followed by a **controlled** component reflecting intentional retrieval strategies.

**Bäuml and Kliegl (2013)** argued that PI arises because memory search is **too broad**, sweeping in earlier, now-irrelevant material; restricting the search should reduce it.

> **Key study, Bäuml and Kliegl (2013).** Three lists were followed by free recall of the **last**. In the **remember** (PI) condition all three were to be learned; in the **forget** condition participants were told after lists one and two to **forget** them; **controls** learned one list only. Recall: **68 percent** (control), **41 percent** (remember), **68 percent** (forget). The forget instruction **eliminated PI**, and retrieval was **as fast** as in the control condition (lost Figure 6.21), consistent with a narrower search.

In a second experiment, **imagining one's childhood home** between two lists changed **mental context** and cut PI by **almost 40 percent**. **Wahlheim and Jacoby (2011)** found people became **aware** of PI with practice, gave **more study time** to vulnerable items and **focused retrieval** better, reducing intrusions. PI is partly under the learner's control.

#### Retroactive interference

Try to picture a long-standing friend as they looked **five years ago**: current knowledge of their face gets in the way. Travellers say a foreign language crowds out words of their own. **Misra et al. (2012)** found **Chinese-English bilinguals** named pictures in **Chinese** more **slowly** after naming the same pictures in **English**, and ERPs suggested they were **inhibiting the English names**. **Lustig et al. (2004)**, mirroring Jacoby for PI, found RI in paired-associate learning was due mainly to the **strength of the incorrect response**.

RI is usually greatest when new learning **resembles** old learning, yet **Dewar et al. (2007)** found significant RI even when the retention interval contained only **detecting differences between pictures** or **detecting tones**. RI can thus arise from (1) **mental effort** during the interval or (2) **similar new learning**, and the first is probably more common in daily life. **Dewar et al. (2010)** found **amnesic** patients' prose recall **much better** after an **unfilled** interval than one spent detecting tones. Almost all this work is on explicit memory, though **Eakin and Smith (2012)** found clear RI in **implicit** memory with paired associates.

> **Trap.** For both PI (Jacoby et al., 2001) and RI (Lustig et al., 2004) the main cause was the **strong incorrect response**. RI needs no similar learning: **mental effort** suffices (Dewar et al., 2007).

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Strong evidence for both PI and RI and growing understanding of their processes; people are not **passive victims** of interference but use **active strategies** against it (Wahlheim & Jacoby, 2011). **Limitations.** (1) Little is known about interference in **implicit** memory. (2) The theory explains **why** forgetting happens but **not why its rate slows** over time. (3) We need to know **why and how** strategies reduce interference; imagining a childhood home helps (Bäuml & Kliegl, 2013), and better strategies may exist.

### Repression

**Sigmund Freud (1856 to 1939)** claimed that **threatening or traumatic** memories often **cannot reach consciousness**, a phenomenon he called **repression**, which could be **active and intentional** or **automatic**. The evidence offered for it is **recovered memories**: traumatic childhood memories forgotten for years and remembered in adulthood, often, Freud found, **during therapy**. Some experts (e.g., **Loftus & Davis, 2006**) argue that **most recovered memories are false**. For a clinical psychologist this is a question about whether therapy itself can manufacture memories of abuse.

#### Inside versus outside therapy

**Lief and Fetkewicz (1995)** studied adults who **admitted** that their recovered memories had been false or imagined: **80 percent** had therapists who had **directly suggested** childhood sexual abuse. Memories recovered **inside** therapy therefore seem more likely to be false.

> **Key study, Geraerts et al. (2007).** Three groups who reported childhood sexual abuse: **suggestive therapy** (memories first recovered **inside** therapy), **spontaneous recovery** (recovered **outside** therapy) and **continuous memory** (never forgotten). Genuineness was approximated by **corroborating evidence** (for example, the abuser's confession), found for **45 percent** (continuous), **37 percent** (outside therapy) and **0 percent** (inside therapy).

Spontaneously recovered memories were corroborated almost as often as never-forgotten ones; therapy-recovered memories never were. **Geraerts (2012)** adds that women with **spontaneous** recoveries can **suppress unwanted memories** and sometimes **forget having remembered** something before, whereas women whose memories were recovered **in therapy** are **susceptible to false memories**.

**Conclusions.** Recovered memories seem to be of two types: those recovered **inside** therapy, often **false**, arising from **therapists' suggestions** plus the client's **susceptibility to false memories**; and those recovered **outside** therapy, often **genuine**, triggered by relevant **retrieval cues** (such as revisiting the place of the abuse). Why would genuine abuse be forgotten for years? Only if it was **traumatic**, as Freud assumed, is this puzzling, and only **8 percent** of women with recovered memories saw the experiences as **traumatic or sexual at the time** (**Clancy & McNally, 2005/2006**); most found them **confusing or uncomfortable**, which could plausibly be set aside. In McNally and Geraerts's (2009, p. 132) words, "A genuine recovered CSA memory does not require repression, trauma, or even complete forgetting."

> **Trap.** The book does **not** conclude that all recovered memories are false, nor that they prove repression. Outside-therapy memories are often **genuine** but need **no repression**; inside-therapy memories are often **false**. Numbers: **80** (Lief & Fetkewicz), **45 / 37 / 0** (Geraerts et al.), **8 percent** (Clancy & McNally).

### Motivated forgetting

Freud's approach was confined to traumatic and distressing memories; recent work takes a **broader** view. Forgetting upsetting memories might **reduce anxiety**, and much stored information is simply **outdated**: looking for your car, you gain nothing by remembering where you parked it last week. Intentional forgetting can be **adaptive**, a sharp break with the traditional view that forgetting is **passive** and beyond our control.

#### Directed forgetting

**Directed forgetting** is poorer memory caused by **instructions to forget** presented material. In the **item method**, each word is followed by a **remember** or **forget** cue, and later memory for all words is tested: to-be-forgotten words are remembered **worse**. The forget cue probably produces **selective rehearsal** of the remember items (**Geraerts & McNally, 2008**), and **inhibition** also seems involved, since successful forgetting goes with activation in **right frontal** areas associated with inhibition (**Rizio & Dennis, 2013**).

#### Suppression: the think/no-think paradigm

**Anderson and Green (2001)** devised the **think/no-think paradigm**:

1. **Learning** of cue-target pairs (**Ordeal-Roach**, **Steam-Train**).
2. **Think/no-think phase**: for some cues participants **think of** the target (**respond**), for others they **keep it out of mind** (**suppress**); some cues are not shown (**baseline**).
3. **Test** with the **original cue** (**same probe**: Ordeal) or a **novel category cue** (**independent probe**: Insect-R for Roach).

If suppression works, suppress items should be recalled worse than respond items and, crucially, worse than **baseline** items. A **meta-analysis of 47 experiments** (**Anderson & Huddleston, 2012**) strongly supported both predictions (lost Figure 6.22, with both probe types). The independent probe reaches the target by a **new route**, so poor recall there cannot simply reflect a blocked link from the original cue.

**Strategies.** **Thought substitution** (linking a different word to the cue) is common; **direct suppression** (focusing on the cue and blocking the target) is another. **Bergström et al. (2009)** found both **equally effective** at reducing recall, though other evidence suggested direct suppression suppressed the traces **more**.

**Mechanism.** Anderson argues that **negative control** is central: the learned response must be **inhibited**. **Depue et al. (2010)** found suppression **increased dorsolateral prefrontal** activation (inhibitory control) and **decreased hippocampal** activation (memory formation), so suppression engages inhibitory processes that partly **restrict the formation of new long-term memories**. **M.C. Anderson et al. (2004)** tested the **executive deficit hypothesis**: people with the **greatest bilateral dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal** activation were **best** at memory inhibition. Since **depression** involves **deficient executive control**, depressed people should suppress poorly, and they **generally fail** to suppress in the paradigm (Anderson & Huddleston, 2012), which gives the work obvious clinical relevance.

**Evaluation.** Most people **can** actively suppress unwanted memories; successful inhibition involves **increased dorsolateral prefrontal** and **decreased hippocampal** activity; the ability is **low in depression**. But Anderson has probably **exaggerated inhibition** (**Raaijmakers & Jakab, 2013**): inhibition theory assumes suppression **weakens** the memory, whereas suppressed items may be hard to recall because of **interference** from other memories, such as the substitute thoughts.

> **Trap.** **Repression** (Freud): traumatic material, automatic or intentional, studied via **recovered memories**. **Directed forgetting**: **instruction to forget**, item method, selective rehearsal plus right-frontal inhibition. **Think/no-think suppression** (Anderson & Green, 2001): deliberate exclusion of a cued memory, DLPFC up, hippocampus down. Keep each finding with its paradigm.

### Cue-dependent forgetting

We usually blame forgetting on weak traces, but often the trace is intact and the **right cue** is missing: **cue-dependent forgetting**. You may not recall a friend's street name yet recognise it at once in a short list.

**Endel Tulving (e.g., 1979)** argued forgetting occurs when the trace and the information available at retrieval **match poorly**: the **encoding specificity principle**. Formally (1979, p. 478), retrieval probability is "a monotonically increased function of informational overlap" between retrieval information and stored information; monotonically increasing means the function rises and never falls. It resembles **transfer-appropriate processing** (6.5), which focuses more directly on **processes**. Tulving assumed that the **context** of an event is stored with it, so memory is best when **retrieval context matches learning context**; context may be **external** (the environment) or **internal** (such as **mood**). **Eysenck (1979)** added that a cue must also help us **discriminate** the correct trace from incorrect ones, not merely overlap with it.

#### Findings

**Recall can beat recognition.** Recognition is normally easier, but encoding specificity predicts that recall should win whenever the **recall cue** overlaps more with the trace than the **recognition cue** does.

> **Key study, Muter (1978).** Participants circled surnames (DOYLE, THOMAS) they recognised as belonging to someone **famous before 1950**, then received **recall cues** of a description plus first name ("author of the Sherlock Holmes stories: Sir Arthur Conan ____"; "Welsh poet: Dylan ____"). They **recognised 29 percent** but **recalled 42 percent**.

"DOYLE" alone matches the stored knowledge poorly; the description plus first name matches it well.

**Mood** is often stored in the trace, so forgetting is reduced when mood at test matches mood at learning: **mood-state-dependent memory** (Chapter 15).

> **Key study, Godden and Baddeley (1975).** **Deep-sea divers** heard **40 words** on a **beach** or **10 feet underwater** and recalled them in the same or the other setting. **Recall** was **much better when the settings matched** (lost Figure 6.23). With **recognition** (**Godden & Baddeley, 1980**) there was **no context effect**, probably because the test items themselves are such powerful cues that they swamp context.

> **Trap.** The divers effect is about **match**, not land beating water, and it held for **recall (1975)** but not **recognition (1980)**, which is also one of the five limitations of Tulving's approach.

**Imaging.** **Park and Rugg (2008)** tested pictures and words with **congruent** (word-word, picture-picture) or **incongruent** cues; memory was better with **congruent** cues. At study, later-recognised words benefited more from "word-like" processing when tested with word cues, and pictures from "picture-like" processing when tested with picture cues: memory is best when **study processing resembles test processing**. **Bauch and Otten (2012)** (**ERPs**) found encoding-retrieval overlap mattered for **pictures but not words**, concluding that overlap may **not be a universal** principle of the neural basis of memory.

**Cue overload.** Following Eysenck (1979), a cue's power depends on its **overlap** with the trace and on how **uniquely** it points to one item. Having learned **park-grove**, you are cued with *park*. If the list's other responses are also *park* associates (tree, bench, playground, picnic), the cue is **overloaded** (high overload); if not, it points uniquely to *grove* (low overload), which also makes the target more **distinctive** (6.5).

> **Key study, Goh and Lu (2012).** Overlap was varied with **intra-list** cues (maximal: park-grove cued by park), **strong extra-list** associates (moderate: airplane-bird cued by feather) and **weak extra-list** associates (little: roof-tin cued by armour), each under **high** or **low overload** (lost Figure 6.24). **Overlap mattered**, but **overload mattered too**: recall was much better when cues were **uniquely** associated with one response, and under **high overload** intra-list cues were **not** the best cues, contrary to encoding specificity.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Trace-cue overlap often does determine retrieval; the principle is supported by **imaging** and **mood-state-dependent memory**; its emphasis on **external and internal context** has proved right.

**Limitations.**

1. **Overlap is overrated**: remembering also means **rejecting** wrong items, so a cue's **discriminative** power matters (Eysenck, 1979; Goh & Lu, 2012; Nairne, 2015).
2. **Retrieval is not simple matching**: answering "What did you do six days ago?" involves **problem solving**; remembering is **dynamic and reconstructive** (Nairne, 2015).
3. **Circularity**: informational overlap is **rarely measured** and is inferred from the very performance it is meant to explain.
4. **Matches seldom yield recollection**: we meet many events that match earlier episodes, yet few trigger conscious recollection (Nairne, 2015).
5. **Recall and recognition differ**: Tulving assumed context affects both alike, but effects are often **larger for recall** (Godden & Baddeley, 1975, 1980).

> **Trap.** "It ignores context" is **not** a limitation (context is its strength), nor is "no neuroimaging support" (Park & Rugg, 2008, support it; Bauch & Otten, 2012, qualify it).

### Consolidation and reconsolidation

No theory so far explains why the **rate** of forgetting **slows** over time. **Consolidation theory** may. **Consolidation** is a long-lasting **physiological** process, lasting **several hours or more**, that fixes information in long-term memory; while it runs, new memories are **fragile**. The **hippocampus** is thought to be vital early on, with memories later stored in the **neocortex** (including the temporal lobes). Imaging agrees (**McKenzie & Eichenbaum, 2011**): hippocampal activation is **greater for recent** than remote memories, cortical activation the reverse. The central prediction is that memories still consolidating are **especially vulnerable**; in Wixted's (2004, p. 265) phrase, "New memories are clear but fragile and old ones are faded but robust."

#### Five lines of support

1. **The forgetting curve.** Greater vulnerability of recent memories predicts fast early forgetting that then slows, the Ebbinghaus shape.
2. **Retrograde amnesia**, impaired memory for events **before** amnesia onset. Hippocampal damage should hit memories formed **shortly before onset** hardest and **remote** memories least, and generally does (**Manns et al., 2003**). Patients without this **time-graded** pattern usually have damage extending into the **neocortex** (**Squire & Wixted, 2011**).
3. **Alcohol.** **Blackouts** in heavy drinkers probably reflect **failed consolidation** of memories formed while drunk. **Moulton et al. (2005)** found alcohol **shortly before learning impaired** memory but alcohol **shortly after learning improved** it, probably because it blocks formation of **new** memories that would interfere with consolidating the earlier ones.
4. **Early retroactive interference.** New memories should be more susceptible to RI, and with **dissimilar** interfering material RI is often **greater early** in the retention interval (Wixted, 2004).
5. **Sleep.** Consolidation in sleep can enhance memory (review: **Oudiette & Paller, 2013**); cueing memories during sleep with **auditory or olfactory** cues **promotes consolidation** and improves later recall. Oudiette and Paller see sleep consolidation as central to maintaining our lifelong store of memories.

> **Classic background:** the time-graded pattern in point 2 is traditionally called **Ribot's law** (Ribot, 1882).

> **Trap.** **Retrograde** amnesia = loss of memories from **before** onset (consolidation theory's prediction concerns this); **anterograde** amnesia = inability to form **new** memories after onset (HM's core deficit, Chapter 7). Alcohol **before** learning impairs, **after** learning improves (Moulton et al., 2005).

#### Reconsolidation

Several theorists (e.g., **Hardt et al., 2010; Nadel et al., 2012**) argue that **reactivating** a consolidated memory makes it **fragile** again, so it must be **reconsolidated**, and while fragile it can be **updated or altered**. That is useful when old knowledge needs updating but can produce **misremembering**. Learn something at **Time 1**, reactivate it while learning more at **Time 2**, and some Time 2 information may be absorbed into the Time 1 trace.

> **Key study, Hupbach et al. (2007, 2008).** Some participants were **reminded** of Time 1 learning just before Time 2 learning (asked to describe the general procedure of what they had done on Monday). Later, recalling Time 1, they **misattributed some Time 2 information to Time 1**. Unreminded participants did this much **less**, as their Time 1 traces were less likely to have been reactivated.

> **Key study, Chan and LaPaglia (2013).** Participants watched a film of a **fictional terrorist attack**. Some then **recalled 24 specific details** (for example, a terrorist using a **hypodermic needle** on a flight attendant: **reactivation**); others did a distractor task. All then met **misinformation** (a **stun gun**) or **neutral** information, followed by a **recognition** test (lost Figure 6.25). Misinformation caused **substantial forgetting only after reactivation**; unreactivated traces were **not disrupted**.

Retrieval normally strengthens memory (6.6), but a reactivated trace is also an editable one, which matters for eyewitness interviewing (Chapter 8).

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Consolidation theory explains the **slowing of forgetting**, predicts **greater retrograde amnesia for recent memories** and **strongest RI soon after learning**, and points to a role for **sleep**; reconsolidation explains **updating** and corrects the theory's (perhaps excessive) emphasis on **permanent** storage.

**Limitations.** (1) Forgetting does not depend **only** on consolidation; poor **encoding-retrieval overlap** also matters. (2) It cannot explain why **PI and RI peak when two responses share one stimulus**. (3) Links between consolidation and **cognitive processes** in forgetting remain underdeveloped (the summary says it **de-emphasises cognitive processes**). (4) Reconsolidation probably does **not occur on every retrieval**, mainly when **something can be learned** during retrieval (**Sevenster et al., 2012**).

> **Trap.** **Consolidation** stabilises a **new** trace; **reconsolidation** re-stabilises a **reactivated old** trace, which can then be updated or distorted. The theory that explains the **slowing** of forgetting is **consolidation**; interference theory explicitly does not.

## How the chapter fits together

Eight threads tie the modules into one argument.

1. **From boxes to processes.** Passive stores (6.2) give way to a working *system* (6.3), to individual differences in *control* (6.4), and to memory as a by-product of *processing* (6.5). Each step replaces a place where memories sit with operations performed on them.
2. **Double dissociations and patients.** Amnesic patients versus **KF**; **PV**'s normal life but absent foreign-vocabulary learning; frontal patients with separable executive deficits; amnesic patients with spared implicit learning; Parkinson's patients impaired on implicit *and* explicit learning. Always ask what the damage spares as well as what it removes.
3. **Learning and test must match, and cues must discriminate.** Transfer-appropriate processing, Parks's shrinking levels effect, encoding specificity, the divers and Park and Rugg's congruent cues all make memory depend on encoding-retrieval fit; Eysenck (1979) and Goh and Lu (2012) add that a good cue must also point uniquely to its target.
4. **Attention and executive control are everywhere.** Attention disrupts iconic storage; the executive is "an attentional system"; capacity is largely goal maintenance; visuo-spatial interference becomes general under attentional load; explicit but not implicit learning suffers dual-task interference; people actively control PI; memory suppression depends on prefrontal control and fails in depression.
5. **Retrieval both strengthens and endangers.** Testing beats restudy because retrieval exposes and replaces weak mediators, yet a reactivated trace becomes fragile and can be rewritten by misinformation (Chan & LaPaglia, 2013).
6. **Implicit versus explicit runs throughout.** It undermines the multi-store equation of STM with consciousness, explains small levels effects in implicit memory, underlies typing skill, persists for 17 years (Mitchell, 2006) and maps loosely onto striatum versus prefrontal cortex, though most learning is a mixture.
7. **Forgetting is often adaptive.** Funes and S show the cost of total retention; decay clears trivia during sleep; motivated forgetting discards the outdated; reconsolidation updates.
8. **The 7th edition revises older claims.** Seven items become three or four chunks; three components become four; iconic memory is not purely preattentive; the word-length effect may be a confound; the buffer is now passive; there is no single dysexecutive syndrome; Parkinson's is not a pure implicit deficit; recovered memories are neither all false nor proof of repression. When an old key and the book disagree, know both.

## Worked example

*On a neuropsychology posting you assess four adults. **Mrs A**, 58, had an encephalitis that damaged both medial temporal lobes, hippocampus and surrounding cortex. Her digit span is 7, but thirty minutes after hearing a story she recalls none of it, and she does not remember meeting you yesterday. On a serial reaction time task she speeds up across blocks, slows when the sequence changes, and reports noticing no pattern. **Mr B**, 44, had a left-hemisphere stroke: digit span 2, normal delayed recall of a story and a route, still runs his shop, but has failed to learn the names of his new medicines. **Mr C**, 36, has bilateral frontal contusions: normal span, adequate delayed recall when cued, but he is disorganised at work, makes erratic errors and cannot alternate between two simple rules. **Ms D**, 29, has begun "recovering" memories of childhood abuse after her therapist suggested abuse explained her symptoms. What do the cases tell you, and what do you predict and advise?*

**Mrs A and Mr B together** form a **double dissociation**: impaired LTM with intact STM (the amnesic pattern; Spiers et al., 2001) and impaired STM with intact LTM (the KF pattern; Shallice & Warrington, 1970), the multi-store model's strongest evidence.

**Mr B** looks like **PV** (Baddeley et al., 1988): a damaged **phonological loop** with intact everyday functioning. Predictions: if, like KF, his deficit is verbal, **visuo-spatial** short-term memory should be relatively spared; if rehearsal is damaged, he should show little **word-length effect** (as in Jacquemot et al., 2011). His failure with medicine names is exactly what the loop predicts: PV learned Italian-Italian pairs normally but no Russian-Italian pairs in ten trials (Baddeley et al., 1998), because new **sound forms** need the loop. So teach the names through what he already knows (written labels, familiar descriptions, meaningful **mediators** linking each name to its purpose, as in Pyc and Rawson, 2010) and use retrieval practice rather than rereading.

**Mrs A's short-term memory** may not be normal on every task. Her damage extends beyond the hippocampus, and **Race et al. (2013)** found hippocampus-only patients normal on eight-second face recognition but patients with **wider medial temporal damage** impaired. Conversely, her **immediate** story recall may be good despite zero delayed recall if her executive is intact (Baddeley & Wilson, 2002), so test immediate and delayed recall separately. Her serial reaction time result is **implicit learning**, as in Van Tilborg et al. (2011) and Reber's **robustness** assumption; a **process-dissociation** test (inclusion roughly equal to exclusion) would rule out forgotten explicit knowledge (the **retrospective problem**). She can still acquire routines through practice she will not remember. For her **pre-illness** memories, consolidation theory predicts **time-graded retrograde amnesia** (Manns et al., 2003), possibly flattened because her damage may reach the neocortex (Squire & Wixted, 2011). Do not confuse this with her **anterograde** deficit, shown when she forgot meeting you.

**Mr C** should not simply be labelled "dysexecutive". Following Stuss and Alexander (2007), his erratic errors suggest poor **monitoring** and his rule-switching failure impaired **shifting** (Miyake et al., 2000); test **energisation** (uniform slowing?) and **inhibition** (Stroop) separately. Good cued but weaker free recall suggests a problem **organising retrieval** rather than storing. The **executive deficit hypothesis** (M.C. Anderson et al., 2004) predicts poor **memory suppression**, and weak controlled retrieval (Bergström et al., 2012) predicts more intrusions from earlier learning. Expect reduced **working memory capacity** with **intermittent goal neglect** rather than constant failure (Unsworth et al., 2012). And remember that executive deficits elsewhere do not prove frontal damage (Stuss, 2011).

**Ms D** should be neither dismissed nor confirmed. Memories recovered **inside suggestive therapy** are the type most often **false**: 80 percent of retractors had therapists who suggested abuse (Lief & Fetkewicz, 1995), and none of the therapy-recovered memories in Geraerts et al. (2007) was corroborated, against 37 percent recovered outside therapy and 45 percent continuous; such women are also more **susceptible to false memories** (Geraerts, 2012). This does not prove any single memory false, and genuine memories recovered outside therapy need neither repression nor trauma (Clancy & McNally, 2005/2006). The lesson concerns technique: a therapist should not suggest abuse as an explanation, because suggestion is the condition for false recovered memories, and repeated reactivation of a suggested event is what reconsolidation research shows can reshape memory.

## MCQ traps

1. **Iconic about 500 ms** (Sperling, 1960); **echoic about 2 s**; **Ioannides et al. (2003): 5 s left versus 2 s right** hemisphere.
2. Iconic memory is **not purely preattentive** (**Persuh et al., 2012**), against Atkinson and Shiffrin.
3. **Miller (1956): about 7 items**; modern estimate **3 to 4 chunks** (Mathy & Feldman, 2012); **Chen and Cowan (2009): 3 chunks** with "the" suppression.
4. Word span **20 in sentences (Simon, 1974)**; immediate recall **5 unrelated words versus up to 16 in sentences (Baddeley et al., 1987)**.
5. **Berman et al. (2009):** interference, intertrial interval irrelevant; **Campoy (2012):** decay **below 3.3 s**.
6. **KF (Shallice & Warrington, 1970):** tiny span, normal LTM; deficit for letters, words, digits, not meaningful sounds.
7. **PV:** span **2** (Baddeley et al., 1988); no Russian-Italian learning over **ten trials** (Baddeley et al., 1998); ran a shop, raised a family.
8. The most recently rehearsed item is accessed fastest (**McElree, 2006**).
9. STM is not a gateway: its contents have already contacted LTM (**Logie, 1999**, "IBM").
10. **Unitary-store model (Jonides et al., 2008):** amnesic patients should fail **relational** STM tasks, not digit span.
11. **Race et al. (2013):** hippocampus-only damage **intact** at 8 s; wider MTL damage **impaired**. **Jeneson et al. (2011):** low load, **no deficit**.
12. **Baddeley and Hitch (1974): three** components; **Baddeley (2000)** added the **episodic buffer**: **four**.
13. **Robbins et al. (1996):** chess uses **executive and sketchpad, not loop**; same in strong and weak players.
14. Phonological similarity: **25 percent worse** (**Larsen et al., 2000**); more **acoustic** than articulatory (**Schweppe et al., 2011**).
15. Word-length effect abolished for **visual** words by suppression with digits **1 to 8** (**Baddeley et al., 1975**); vanishes when **orthographic neighbourhood** is matched (**Jalbert et al., 2011**).
16. **Visual cache = form and colour; inner scribe = spatial, movement, rehearsal** (**Logie, 1995**).
17. **Smith and Jonides (1997):** spatial **right**, visual **left**; **Zimmer (2008):** spatial = intraparietal sulcus.
18. **Klauer and Zhao (2004):** specific interference on easy tasks; **Vergauwe et al. (2009):** **general** interference on demanding tasks.
19. **DLPFC BA9/46** (Mottaghy, 2006), but executive deficits occur with **little or no frontal damage** (Stuss, 2011).
20. **Miyake et al. (2000):** inhibition, shifting, updating; **inhibition has no unique variance** (**Friedman et al., 2008**).
21. **Stuss and Alexander (2007):** task setting, monitoring, energisation; **Stuss (2011):** metacognition/integration (**BA10**). **Baddeley (1996):** focusing, dividing, switching, interfacing with LTM.
22. **Gläscher et al. (2012):** cognitive control network versus value-based network; **Iowa Gambling Task** with **four** decks.
23. Buffer: **about 4 chunks**; **passive** (Baddeley, 2012); binding automatic (**Allen et al., 2012**).
24. **Reading span** (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980) and **operation span** combine processing and storage; criterion **over 50 percent**.
25. Capacity correlates **+0.6** with intelligence, chiefly **fluid** (**Unsworth, 2010**); anxiety lowers it, relaxation training raises it.
26. **Unsworth et al. (2012):** differences only in the **slowest quintile**: goal neglect on about **20 percent** of trials.
27. **Craik and Tulving (1975):** incidental learning; deep **more than three times** better recognition; complex frames **doubled** cued recall.
28. **Morris et al. (1977):** **shallow beat deep on a rhyme test**.
29. **Eysenck and Eysenck (1980):** shallow but **distinctive** pronunciation gave **recognition** equal to semantic processing.
30. **Roediger and Karpicke (2006):** restudy best at **5 minutes**; testing **50 percent better at 1 week**.
31. **Pyc and Rawson: 2009** effort, **2010** mediators (wingu-plane-cloud), **2012** mediator change after failure.
32. **Peterson and Mulligan (2013):** testing reduced **category clustering**; worse than restudy.
33. **Kaufman et al. (2010):** IQ **+0.44 explicit**, **+0.16 implicit** learning.
34. **Snyder et al. (2014):** **11.4 years** of typing, **14.9 letters (57.3 percent)** placed; Dvorak after **two hours** comparable.
35. Process dissociation: **inclusion greater than exclusion = explicit**.
36. **Haider et al. (2011):** **34 / 66 percent**; **80 versus 18 percent**; **91 versus 0 percent**; sequence **1-6-4-2-3-5**.
37. **Striatum (caudate, putamen) = implicit; DLPFC and anterior cingulate = explicit** (Destrebecqz et al., 2005); cerebellum = error correction (Penhune & Steele, 2012).
38. **Parkinson's:** implicit **and** explicit impaired (**Wilkinson et al., 2009**); implicit deficits clearest **with feedback** (**Foerde & Shohamy, 2011**).
39. **Ebbinghaus:** himself, savings, **21 minutes to 31 days**, steepest loss in the **first hour**; logarithmic (Rubin & Wenzel, 1996) or power (Averell & Heathcote, 2011).
40. **PI = old disrupts new** (Munsterberg); **RI = new disrupts old**. Both peak when two responses share a stimulus; both mainly due to the **strong wrong response** (Jacoby et al., 2001; Lustig et al., 2004).
41. **Bäuml and Kliegl (2013): 68 / 41 / 68 percent**; childhood-home imagery cut PI **almost 40 percent**.
42. **Dewar et al. (2007):** RI from **mental effort** alone (tones, picture differences).
43. **Geraerts et al. (2007): 45 / 37 / 0 percent** corroboration; **Lief and Fetkewicz (1995): 80 percent**; **Clancy and McNally: 8 percent** traumatic at the time.
44. **Think/no-think (Anderson & Green, 2001):** suppress below **baseline**; **47 experiments** (Anderson & Huddleston, 2012); **DLPFC up, hippocampus down** (Depue et al., 2010).
45. **Muter (1978):** recognised **29 percent**, recalled **42 percent**.
46. **Godden and Baddeley:** **40 words**, beach or **10 feet** underwater; context effect for **recall (1975)**, not **recognition (1980)**.
47. **Moulton et al. (2005):** alcohol **before** learning impairs, **after** improves.
48. **Chan and LaPaglia (2013):** misinformation harmed memory **only after reactivation** (24 details recalled).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Encoding / storage / retrieval** | Processing at presentation / the retained result / recovering it when needed |
| **Multi-store model** | Atkinson and Shiffrin's (1968) sensory, short-term and long-term stores |
| **Unitary-store model** | Jonides et al.'s (2008) view of STM as temporarily activated LTM |
| **Iconic memory** | Brief visual sensory store, roughly half a second or a little longer |
| **Echoic memory** | Brief auditory sensory store, roughly two seconds |
| **Chunks** | Storage units built by binding smaller pieces of information |
| **Double dissociation** | Patient X impaired on A not B, patient Y on B not A, implying separate systems |
| **Working memory** | Limited-capacity system that both holds and processes information during complex tasks |
| **Central executive** | Modality-free, limited-capacity, attention-like controller that stores nothing |
| **Phonological loop** | Component holding speech-based material, with subvocal rehearsal |
| **Visuo-spatial sketchpad** | Component that briefly holds and manipulates visual and spatial information |
| **Visual cache** | Logie's passive sketchpad store for form and colour |
| **Inner scribe** | Logie's sketchpad component for spatial and movement information; rehearses the cache |
| **Episodic buffer** | Largely passive store of about four integrated chunks (Baddeley, 2000) |
| **Articulatory suppression** | Repeating a simple sound aloud to occupy the articulatory process and block rehearsal |
| **Phonological similarity effect** | Poorer immediate serial recall when items sound alike |
| **Word-length effect** | Smaller verbal span for long than short words |
| **Executive processes** | Processes organising and coordinating cognition towards current goals |
| **Unity/diversity framework** | Each executive function = shared common factor + function-specific part |
| **Stroop task** | Naming ink colours of colour words; slowed when word and ink conflict |
| **Dysexecutive syndrome** | Impaired central executive functioning after frontal damage |
| **Working memory capacity** | How much can be processed and stored at once; linked to fluid intelligence and attentional control |
| **Reading span** | Most sentences read for comprehension whose final words are recalled over half the time |
| **Operation span** | Most sum-plus-word items for which all words are recalled over half the time |
| **Fluid intelligence** | Rapid understanding of novel relationships |
| **Crystallised intelligence** | Use of acquired knowledge, skills and experience |
| **Goal neglect** | Occasional loss of the current task goal |
| **Levels of processing** | Craik and Lockhart's (1972) view that deeper, semantic processing gives stronger traces |
| **Transfer-appropriate processing** | Memory succeeds when learning processes suit the test's demands |
| **Explicit memory** | Memory involving conscious recollection |
| **Implicit memory** | Memory not dependent on conscious recollection |
| **Distinctiveness** | A trace's difference from other stored traces, owing to how it was processed |
| **Testing effect** | Better long-term memory when learning time is spent retrieving rather than restudying |
| **Mediator effectiveness hypothesis** | Testing helps because learners generate, retrieve and improve linking mediators |
| **Retrieval effort hypothesis** | Testing helps more when practice retrieval is hard |
| **Implicit learning** | Learning complex regularities without awareness of what was learned |
| **Process-dissociation procedure** | Inclusion versus exclusion guessing; the difference estimates explicit knowledge |
| **Serial reaction time task** | Fast responses to stimuli secretly following a repeating sequence |
| **Striatum** | Basal ganglia structure (caudate, putamen) deep in the brain, central to implicit learning |
| **Parkinson's disease** | Progressive basal ganglia disorder: rigidity, tremor, mask-like face |
| **Savings method** | Ebbinghaus's measure: reduction in trials to relearn compared with original learning |
| **Decay** | Forgetting through gradual loss of the memory's physical substrate |
| **Synaesthesia** | One sense modality evoking experience in another |
| **Proactive interference** | Disruption of memory by earlier learning |
| **Retroactive interference** | Disruption of memory for earlier learning by later learning or processing |
| **Repression** | Freud's motivated forgetting of traumatic or threatening events, especially from childhood |
| **Recovered memories** | Traumatic childhood memories forgotten for years, then recalled in adulthood |
| **Directed forgetting** | Poorer memory for material one was instructed to forget |
| **Think/no-think paradigm** | Anderson and Green's (2001) method of repeatedly suppressing or retrieving cued targets |
| **Encoding specificity principle** | Retrieval improves with overlap between cue information and trace information |
| **Cue overload** | Loss of a cue's power when it is associated with many responses |
| **Consolidation** | Physiological process, lasting hours or more, stabilising fragile new memories |
| **Retrograde amnesia** | Loss of memory for events before amnesia onset |
| **Reconsolidation** | Renewed consolidation of a reactivated trace, during which it can be updated |

13. **Miyake et al. (2000)** and the **unity/diversity framework** (Friedman et al., 2008; Collette et al., 2005; Hedden & Gabrieli, 2010).
