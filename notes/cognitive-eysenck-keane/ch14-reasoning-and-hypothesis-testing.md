# Chapter 14: Reasoning and hypothesis testing

**What this chapter is really about.** This chapter closes Part IV (Thinking and reasoning). Chapter 12 dealt with problem solving and Chapter 13 with judgement and decision making; this one asks how people draw conclusions, both when they test a hunch against evidence and when they work out what follows from what. For most of the twentieth century the yardstick was **formal logic**: a conclusion was correct if logic licensed it, and a person was rational to the extent that their reasoning matched logic. Judged that way, people look dreadful. They choose the wrong cards on Wason's selection task, accept invalid conclusions they happen to believe, test their ideas in ways that can never show them wrong, and even professional scientists hold on to hypotheses their own data contradict.

Eysenck and Keane argue that the yardstick needs examining as much as the reasoner. The field has moved towards what Evans calls a "new paradigm" psychology of reasoning, less concerned with whether people obey logic and more with how they use knowledge, probability and goals to reach conclusions that are good enough in an uncertain world. Seen that way, reasoning looks much like the judgement of Chapter 13: beliefs carry degrees of confidence that evidence nudges up or down (the **Bayesian** idea), and a fast intuitive system competes with a slow effortful one (the **dual-process** idea). The chapter ends with the question all of Part IV has been circling, **are humans rational?**, and the answer depends on what "rational" is taken to mean. For a clinical psychologist the material is directly practical: a diagnostic interview is hypothesis testing, a formulation is a chain of conditional inferences, and the biases described here are the ones that distort clinical judgement and that cognitive therapy targets in clients.

**How to read this chapter.** Section 14.1 separates inductive, deductive and informal reasoning. Section 14.2 covers hypothesis testing in Wason's 2-4-6 task and in real and simulated science, with Popper's falsification principle as the reference point. Section 14.3 is the laboratory core: conditional reasoning, the selection task and syllogisms. Section 14.4 sets Johnson-Laird's mental model theory against Evans's heuristic-analytic theory and carries most of the theory MCQs. Section 14.5 covers brain systems, 14.6 everyday (informal) argument, and 14.7 the rationality debate, with Simon's bounded rationality and Stanovich's tripartite model as its two frameworks.

## 14.1 Introduction

### Two classical kinds of reasoning

**Inductive reasoning** goes from particular cases to a general conclusion. However well supported, an inductive conclusion is only **probably** true, because the next case might break the pattern. Bertrand Russell's turkey makes the point: fed every morning of its life, it concludes that it will always be fed in the morning, an inference that every past observation supports and that fails on Christmas Eve. Nothing was wrong with the turkey's evidence; past regularities simply do not guarantee future ones.

Scientists reason like the turkey more often than they admit. A psychologist who finds in study after study that learning occurs only with reinforcement, and concludes that reinforcement is *essential* for learning, has made an inductive leap that no number of confirming studies can make certain. (Classic background: this generalisation did fail; Tolman's latent learning in rats is the standard counterexample.)

**Deductive reasoning**, the tradition running from Aristotle onwards, derives a conclusion that follows **necessarily** from premises assumed to be true. Grant that Tom is taller than Dick and Dick taller than Harry, and Tom must be taller than Harry; nobody needs measuring.

> **Trap.** Two contrasts are often merged. **Inductive versus deductive** concerns the direction and guarantee of the inference. **Valid versus true** is a separate matter: a deductive conclusion is **valid** if it follows from the premises, whatever the facts. "All psychologists can fly; Sihan is a psychologist; so Sihan can fly" is valid and false. "Deductive conclusions are always true" is wrong; they are guaranteed true only *if the premises are true*.

### Deduction resembles problem solving but has a different origin

A deductive task feels like a Chapter 12 problem: the goal is clear and the route is not obvious. The difference is that deductive problems were built from **formal logic**, so each has an answer a logician could derive mechanically. That a task was *constructed* from logic does not mean people *solve* it with logic, and a central message of the chapter is that they rarely do.

### Informal reasoning

The chapter also covers **informal reasoning**, the reasoning of everyday argument: whether a news story is credible or a medicine worth taking. Concern about the gap between artificial laboratory puzzles and real argumentation led researchers to study everyday reasoning processes directly.

### The "new paradigm" and the Bayesian link

**Evans (2012)** describes the resulting "new paradigm psychology of reasoning", in which deduction and logic are no longer central. Reasoning is now seen as sharing processes with **judgement and decision making**, so Chapter 13's theories apply to it. The key import is the **Bayesian approach**: beliefs carry **subjective probabilities** (for example, that someone is dishonest), and each new piece of information moves them up or down. You will meet it again in the selection task (Oaksford), informal argument (Hahn and Oaksford) and the rationality debate (Oaksford and Chater).

| | **Inductive** | **Deductive** | **Informal** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | Cases to a generalisation | Premises to what follows | Knowledge and evidence to a persuasive claim |
| Status of conclusion | Probable, never certain | Certain *if* premises true | Graded by strength |
| Role of prior knowledge | Central | Irrelevant in principle | Central |
| Normative standard | Probability | Binary logic | Probability, argument strength |
| Typical task | 2-4-6 task, simulated science | Conditionals, selection task, syllogisms | Argument evaluation, fallacies |

> **Trap.** Both the 2-4-6 task and the selection task were devised by **Peter Wason**, which is why examiners swap them. The 2-4-6 task tests **inductive** rule discovery with number triples. The selection task uses four cards and an "if...then" rule; it is usually filed under deduction, but Eysenck and Keane say it is more accurately **hypothesis testing with a conditional rule**.

## 14.2 Hypothesis Testing

### Popper: confirmation versus falsification

**Karl Popper (1968)** separated two ways of testing a hypothesis: **confirmation** (looking for evidence that it is right) and **falsification** (designing tests that could show it is wrong). Confirmation can never be completed, because the next observation might overturn the hypothesis, whereas one clear disconfirmation is logically decisive. Popper drew two conclusions. **Falsifiability**, the possibility in principle of being shown wrong, is what marks off science from non-science such as religion and pseudo-science (his examples included **psychoanalysis**). And scientists *ought* to try to falsify their hypotheses. The section asks whether people, scientists included, actually do so. Mostly they seek confirmation, and that is not always the mistake it seems.

> **Trap.** **Falsifiability** is a property of a theory (it *can* be refuted); **falsification** is the strategy of trying to refute it. The demarcation criterion is falsifiability, not "verification", which was the logical positivists' criterion that Popper opposed (classic background).

### Wason's 2-4-6 task

**Peter Wason (1960)** told participants that the triple **2-4-6** fitted a simple relational rule. They proposed triples of their own, each with a reason, and were told after each whether it fitted; they could announce the rule whenever they liked. The rule was **any three numbers in ascending order**. Only **21%** of university students were right at their **first** announcement and **28%** **never** found it, and later studies have mostly found similarly poor performance.

#### Confirmation bias, and a myth about Wason

The usual explanation is **confirmation bias**, seeking information that agrees with one's hypothesis. Someone who decides the rule is "the second number is double the first and the third is triple it" proposes 3-6-9, 10-20-30 and so on. Each also fits the true rule, each gets a "yes", and confidence grows in a hypothesis that is far too narrow. The informative triples (1-7-50, 9-6-2) never get tried.

Eysenck and Keane correct a common story: Wason's 1960 paper did **not** explain the failures as confirmation bias. That is a **myth**. Only some years later did he offer an equivalent idea, which he called **"verification bias"**: participants kept verifying their overly specific first hypothesis instead of replacing it with the general rule.

#### Positive testing is not the same as confirmation

Wason ignored what participants **expected**. **Positive testing** means proposing an instance of your hypothesis; **confirmation** means seeking support for it. They coincide only if you believe the hypothesis. If you suspect it is wrong, proposing a non-instance and expecting "no" is the confirming move. Behaviour on the 2-4-6 task is better described as a strong preference for **positive testing**, and the theoretical analysis below shows this is usually sensible.

#### Findings

**Ownership (Cowley & Byrne, 2005).** If people confirm because they are loath to give up their *own* idea, they should readily attack someone else's. They did: **62%** abandoned another person's hypothesis versus **25%** who abandoned their own.

**Two rules (Tweney et al., 1980).** Participants were told the experimenter had two rules, generating **DAX** and **MED** triples, that 2-4-6 was DAX, and each proposal was labelled DAX or MED. DAX meant any ascending triple; MED meant everything else. **Over 50%** were correct at their first attempt. The relabelling works because people can keep positive testing and still learn: by confirming MED triples (6-4-2 is MED) they collect exactly the evidence that would disconfirm a narrow DAX hypothesis, without having to aim deliberately for "no".

**The key dimension (Gale & Ball, 2012).** These authors argued the active ingredient is noticing the **ascending versus descending** dimension. With 2-4-6 always the DAX example, they varied the MED example: when it was **6-4-2**, **75%** found the DAX rule; when it was **4-4-4**, only **23%**. The one clear difference between solvers and non-solvers was how many **descending** triples they produced.

**Hypothesis generation (Cherubini et al., 2005).** Most research studies hypothesis *testing*; this study looked at *generation*. People try to keep as much of the example's information as possible in their first hypothesis, which is therefore much more specific than the rule, so their tests almost always get "yes". With two information-rich examples (**6-8-10** and **16-18-20**) people hypothesised "increasing by twos" and only **30%** succeeded; with less rich examples (**6-8-10** and **9-14-15**) they formed broader hypotheses and **70%** succeeded.

**Feedback frequency (Russo & Meloy, 2008).** With feedback after **every** trial participants tested **4.6** hypotheses on average and **39%** found the rule; with feedback only after each **block of five**, they tested **9.4** and **62%** succeeded. Less frequent feedback encouraged more flexible thinking.

#### Theoretical analysis: why positive testing usually works

Most hypotheses are **sparse**: they apply to fewer than half the items in their domain (**Navarro & Perfors, 2011**). **Perfors and Navarro (2009)** asked people to list rules for the numbers **1 to 1,000**; **83%** of the rules (prime numbers, two-digit numbers) applied to **fewer than 20%** of them. With a sparse hypothesis, testing a non-instance almost always yields "no" whether you are right or wrong, so it teaches little; testing an instance can yield an informative "no". In Perfors and Navarro's phrase, the world is biased towards answering "no", so asking for a "yes" is the best way to learn. Positive testing is therefore normally **optimal**. The 2-4-6 task penalises it because its rule is unusually **general**: a narrow hypothesis sits wholly inside the true rule, so positive tests can never expose it.

> **Trap.** "Positive testing is irrational" and "positive testing = confirmation bias" are both wrong on the 7th edition's account. Positive testing is usually optimal because most hypotheses are sparse; it fails on the 2-4-6 task only because Wason's rule is so broad.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** The task has fascinated researchers since 1960, has shown both strengths and limits of inductive reasoning, and is relevant to how scientists test hypotheses.

**Limitations.**

1. **Unrealistic feedback.** It is immediate and accurate but says only "yes" or "no", never why. Real scientific feedback is richer but often delayed and sometimes wrong.
2. **An unusually general rule.** Most real hypotheses cover a small fraction of cases, where positive testing works well.
3. **Overstated confirmation bias.** It shrinks when the hypothesis is someone else's (Cowley & Byrne, 2005), and scientists behave the same way: at a **1977** conference on the **levels-of-processing** approach attended by the book's first author, nearly every paper aimed to expose that approach's problems.

### Hypothesis testing in simulated and real research environments

#### Popper revisited

Most experts regard Popper as partly right but simplistic (**Okasha, 2002**). A result that seems to contradict a hypothesis may mean the hypothesis is false, or it may reflect poor design or inaccurate data, so falsification is rarely decisive in practice.

#### How scientists generate ideas

The popular image of discovery as genius and sudden insight, quoted by **Trickett and Trafton (2007)**, is largely wrong. **Klahr and Simon (2001)** showed that scientists mostly use **weak methods**, very general strategies usable on almost any problem, including **trial and error** and **means-ends analysis** from everyday problem solving.

- **Kulkarni and Simon (1988):** scientists rely on the **unusualness heuristic**, using **unexpected** findings to steer theory and research.
- **Zelko et al. (2010):** every leading biomedical scientist interviewed (in several countries) had one **main heuristic**, such as challenging conventional wisdom, working step by step, or running many trial-and-error experiments.
- **Trickett and Trafton (2007):** scientists use **"what if" reasoning** about imagined situations, as when **Einstein** at **16** imagined riding a beam of light, a thought that eventually fed into relativity. **Trickett et al. (2009)** found the same among **astronomy** and **computational fluid dynamics** experts facing anomalous data, who ran **conceptual simulations** of what hypotheses implied.

#### Dunbar's simulated genetics laboratory

**Dunbar (1993)** had participants discover, in a computer-simulated molecular genetics lab, how genes are controlled by other genes, a problem whose real solution won a **Nobel prize**. Participants were led towards **activation**; the true mechanism was **inhibition**. Those who sought data consistent with activation failed; the **20%** who succeeded tried to **explain the discrepant findings**. Most worked through one candidate activator gene after another, and only once every activation version had failed did some turn to the inconsistent data.

#### In the real world: hypothesis testing by scientists

Real scientists typically "**confirm early and disconfirm late**" (**Manktelow, 2012**), which is often optimal: early confirmation shows a new hypothesis can explain a range of findings, and later disconfirmation points towards a better one.

- **Gorman (1995)** found confirmation bias in **Alexander Graham Bell**, who stuck with **undulating current and electromagnets** after liquid devices had worked well, then abandoned the liquid devices, leaving the way open for **Edison** to develop the forerunner of modern telephones.
- **Mitroff (1974)** found that **Apollo** lunar geologists spent most of their time trying to confirm their hypotheses, because they valued **commitment** as a motivator and often blamed negative results on **faulty instruments**.
- **Fugelsang et al. (2004)** studied molecular biologists working on gene control in **bacteria, parasites and viruses**:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Results examined | **417** |
| Inconsistent with prediction | **223** (over half) |
| Inconsistencies blamed on **methods** | **88%** |
| Inconsistencies leading to **theory change** | **12%** |
| Inconsistencies followed up (mostly with changed methods) | about **two-thirds** |
| Follow-ups in which the inconsistency **replicated** | **55%** |
| Replicated inconsistencies leading to theory change | **61%** |

The scientists look stubborn, but almost **half** of the inconsistent findings did not replicate, so refusing to revise a theory on a single surprising result, while revising it once the result recurred, was defensible.

> **Trap.** The book spells the name both "Fuselsang" and "Fugelsang"; it is **Fugelsang**. Direction: theory change **12%** after a first inconsistency, **61%** after a replicated one. The chapter summary says scientists often revise their assumptions when an inconsistent finding is obtained **twice**.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Scientists clearly rely on **heuristics**, which undercuts the genius view of discovery. Popper's emphasis on falsification is **oversimplified** (**Dunbar & Klahr, 2012**): confirmation is often appropriate (for a new theory), and decisive falsification is impractical when apparently falsifying results often fail to replicate (Fugelsang et al., 2004), a problem likely to be worse in a **statistical** science such as psychology.

**Limitations of simulated environments.** They lack the **commitment** that drives scientists to defend their own theories and attack others', and participants sometimes work **alone** (as in Dunbar, 1993) whereas scientists work in teams. Using Dunbar's task, **Okada and Simon (1997)** found **pairs outperformed individuals**: they entertained hypotheses more often, considered more alternatives and discussed more ways of justifying ideas. Real research is shaped by **motivational and social factors** that simulations largely omit.

## 14.3 Deductive Reasoning

Deductive conclusions follow with certainty once the premises are granted. The book concentrates on the two problem types built from traditional logic, **conditional** and **syllogistic** reasoning, with the **Wason selection task** between them; theories follow in 14.4.

### Conditional reasoning

#### Origins in propositional logic

**Conditional reasoning** is, roughly, reasoning with "if". It comes from **propositional logic**, in which symbols stand for whole propositions joined by **logical operators** (*and*, *or*, *if...then*, *if and only if*). With P for "the patient has delirium" and Q for "attention is impaired", *if...then* yields **if P then Q**. Two features separate this from ordinary language. Propositions have only **two truth values**, true or false, with no room for "sort of raining" on a misty day. And the meaning of "if" is fixed by logic, not by speakers' intentions or listeners' knowledge.

#### The four inferences

| Name | Second premise | Conclusion | Valid? | Example (if it rains, the match is cancelled) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Modus ponens (MP)** | P | Q | **Valid** | It rains, so the match is cancelled |
| **Modus tollens (MT)** | Not-Q | Not-P | **Valid** | Not cancelled, so it did not rain |
| **Affirmation of the consequent (AC)** | Q | P | **Invalid** | Cancelled, so it rained |
| **Denial of the antecedent (DA)** | Not-P | Not-Q | **Invalid** | No rain, so not cancelled |

AC and DA fail because "if P then Q" makes P *sufficient* for Q, not the *only* route to it: a match can be cancelled by a bomb scare as well as by rain. The book's examples make the same point (being upset because one lost one's job, not because Susan is angry; getting wet in a swimming pool, not in the rain). MT is valid although it feels harder: if rain guarantees cancellation and there was no cancellation, there cannot have been rain.

> **Trap.** **Antecedent** = the "if" part (P); **consequent** = the "then" part (Q). Valid: **affirming the antecedent** (MP) and **denying the consequent** (MT). Invalid: **affirming the consequent** and **denying the antecedent**. One 7th-edition sentence lists "denial of the consequent" among the invalid inferences; that is a slip, because denying the consequent is MT.

#### Natural language is not propositional logic

Traditional research used **disinterested reasoning**, in which the reasoner's goals and preferences play no part, unlike almost all everyday reasoning. In natural language "if P then Q" is often heard as "**if and only if** P, then Q". A parent who says "If you finish your homework, you can watch the match" will be understood to mean that unfinished homework means no match, so the "invalid" DA inference is exactly what was meant. (The book's example is five dollars offered for mowing the lawn.)

#### Findings

**The basic pattern (Evans et al., 1993).** Nearly everyone makes **MP**; far fewer make the equally valid **MT**, and many call MT conclusions invalid. The invalid **DA** and **AC** are accepted much of the time.

> **Trap.** MP and MT are both valid, but performance is **MP much better than MT**. The book's sentence about which invalid form is accepted more often is garbled by the slip above; other sources generally report AC accepted at least as often as DA, so do not lean on that ordering.

**Counterexamples and working memory (De Neys et al., 2005).** Logic says counterexamples should be ignored for valid inferences, since the conclusion follows regardless. De Neys et al. varied the number of counterexamples (**few vs many**) and participants' **working memory capacity** (**low vs high**). The number of counterexamples strongly affected acceptance of the **valid** inferences, MP and MT, against the dictates of logic, and **high-capacity** participants reasoned better. (Figure 14.1 plotted MP, MT, DA and AC acceptance across these conditions; only these two directional conclusions should be taken from it.)

**Folk axioms (Bonnefon et al., 2012).** Reasoners add inferences of their own. Told that a woman will be very ill if she eats oysters, most people infer she will **not eat** them, an inference drawn from **folk axioms** such as "people act in their own interest". Such inferences often lead to acceptance of invalid conclusions, and classical logic, which ignores knowledge, has no place for them.

**Two strategies for AC (Markovits et al., 2013).** Compare "if a stone hits a window, it breaks; the window is broken; so a stone hit it" with "if a finger is cut, it bleeds; the finger is bleeding; so it was cut". Both are invalid, but the second is accepted far more often.

- The **statistical strategy** is fast, intuitive and non-logical: estimate how likely the conclusion is given world knowledge. A bleeding finger has probably been cut; a broken window could have many causes.
- The **counterexample strategy** is slower and more demanding: look for a case where the premises hold and the conclusion fails, and accept the conclusion only if none comes to mind. Counterexamples are easy for the window and hard for the finger, but whenever one is found the invalid conclusion is correctly **rejected**.

With limited versus unlimited time, the counterexample strategy fell from **49%** of trials (unlimited) to **1.7%** (limited), while the statistical strategy rose from **19%** (unlimited) to **55%** (limited). Time pressure almost abolished the effortful strategy, a bridge to the dual-process theories of 14.4.

#### Summary

People rarely reason logically on conditionals, as their acceptance of AC and DA shows. They use knowledge of **other people's goals and preferences** and of **causes** to draw inferences, which is sensible in real life but "illogical" in the laboratory. Performance also depends on **ability and willingness** to process effortfully and on **time available**. Much conditional reasoning is **closer to decision making than to classical logic** (Bonnefon et al., 2012).

### The Wason selection task

#### The standard version

Also devised by **Peter Wason**, the task is usually studied as deduction but is better described as **hypothesis testing with a conditional rule**. Four cards each have a letter on one side and a number on the other; the visible faces are **R, G, 2 and 7**. The rule: **if there is an R on one side, there is a 2 on the other.** Participants must pick only the cards that need turning to decide whether the rule is true. Most pick **R and 2**. The correct answer is **R and 7**, given by only about **5 to 10%** of university students.

| Card | Logical role | Could it falsify the rule? |
|---|---|---|
| **R** | P | **Yes**: a non-2 on the back breaks the rule |
| **G** | Not-P | No: the rule says nothing about non-R cards |
| **2** | Q | No: an R on the back fits; anything else is irrelevant |
| **7** | Not-Q | **Yes**: an R on the back breaks the rule |

The 2 card can only ever show the rule *might* be right. Choosing 7 is the selection-task equivalent of **modus tollens**; choosing 2 corresponds to affirming the consequent.

#### Why people fail

**Matching bias (Evans, 1998).** People select the cards that **match the items named in the rule**. Negated rules separate matching from logic, and **Evans and Ball (2010)** reanalysed data from four versions: *if T then 4*; *if R then not 1*; *if not M then 5*; *if not G then not 1*. All showed substantial matching bias. With "if R then not 1", for example, the correct cards (R and 1) are also the named ones, so people "succeed" for the wrong reason.

**Misunderstanding the procedure (Stenning & van Lambalgen, 2004).** Many participants may not realise they must make all choices before seeing any card's other side. Only **3.7%** were correct with standard instructions, but **18%** when explicitly told there would be no feedback before all choices were made.

#### The probabilistic, information-gain account (Oaksford, 1997)

Oaksford argued that the logical answer conflicts with what is usually most sensible. To test "all swans are white", logic says examine swans and **non-white birds**. But swans are few and non-white birds are overwhelmingly numerous, so checking non-white birds would take forever and rarely find a swan; checking **white birds** is more informative. A good tester should weigh the **probabilities** of different cases. Applied to "if P then Q", the account predicts choosing the **Q card** when Q is expected to be **rare** and the **not-Q card** when Q is **common**, which maximises **information gain**. **Oaksford et al. (1997)** set Q cards at **17%, 50% or 83%** and, as predicted, people chose **far more Q cards when Q was rare**. Choosing 2, on this view, suits a world where properties named in rules are usually uncommon.

> **Trap.** Two explanations of the same wrong answer. **Evans: matching bias**, a non-logical surface heuristic. **Oaksford: information gain**, a rational probabilistic strategy that the artificial task fails to reward. The swans example belongs to Oaksford.

#### Improving performance: deontic rules

The standard rule is **indicative** (how things are). A **deontic** rule states what must be done ("if P, you must do Q") and concerns detecting **violations** such as cheating; it is easier because the need to look for a rule-breaking case is explicit. In **Sperber and Girotto's (2002)** version, Paolo, worried about being cheated online, records on each order card whether he **paid** (one side) and whether he **received** the item (other side). The faces show "item paid for", "item not paid for", "item received" and "item not received". **68%** correctly chose **"item paid for" and "item not received"**.

> **Trap.** Older Indian textbooks and keys usually illustrate deontic facilitation with studies the 7th edition does not use. Classic background: **Griggs and Cox (1982)** used a drinking-age rule, and **Cosmides (1989)** explained facilitation by an evolved **cheater-detection** mechanism for social contracts. The 7th edition's study is **Sperber and Girotto (2002)** (**68%**), and its explanation is that deontic rules make the need to **disprove** the rule explicit.

#### Improving performance: motivation

**Dawson et al. (2002)** used the rule that people **high in emotional lability die early**, with cards "low emotional lability", "high emotional lability", "early death" and "late death". The correct choice is **high lability** (P) and **late death** (not-Q). Participants led to believe they were **low** in lability solved it **9%** of the time; those led to believe they were **high**, and so motivated to disprove a rule predicting their own early death, solved it **38%** of the time.

#### Summary

A small minority, mostly of **high intelligence**, reason deductively and solve the standard task; most fail through simple strategies such as **matching** and incomplete understanding of the task. **Deontic** rules and **personal motivation** both help by directing attention to disproving the rule.

### Syllogistic reasoning

#### Structure

Syllogisms have been studied for over **2,000 years**. A **syllogism** has **two premises** and a **conclusion** and three terms, one of which (the middle term, B) appears in both premises: all A are B; all B are C; therefore all A are C. Each statement uses one of four **quantifiers**: **all**, **some**, **no**, **some...not**. **Validity** depends only on whether the conclusion follows; real-world truth is **irrelevant**. "All students are punctual; all medical interns are students; therefore all medical interns are punctual" is valid whatever one thinks of interns. (The book uses obedient children and girl guides.)

#### Belief bias

**Belief bias** is accepting **invalid but believable** conclusions and rejecting **valid but unbelievable** ones. **Klauer et al. (2000)** crossed **believability** (e.g. "some fish are not trout" vs "some trout are not fish"), **validity** (half valid), and **perceived base rate** (told that **one-sixth** or **five-sixths** of syllogisms were valid). Three findings:

1. A **base-rate effect**: perceived probability of validity influenced judgements.
2. Strong **belief bias**.
3. A **belief-by-logic interaction**: believable conclusions improved performance on **valid** syllogisms but worsened it on **invalid** ones, so belief bias is largest where a believable conclusion is invalid.

All three show validity judgements swayed by logically irrelevant factors. **Stupple and Ball (2008)** added that people take **longer to process unbelievable premises**, suggesting a time-consuming **conflict** between belief and the assumptions they are asked to make.

> **Trap.** **Belief bias** (evaluating a syllogism's **validity** by believability) is not **confirmation bias** (**seeking** evidence that supports a hypothesis) or **myside bias** (14.6: evaluating claims or generating arguments in line with **one's own beliefs** in informal reasoning). Related family, three different tasks.

#### Surface matching (Stupple et al., 2013)

When a conclusion **matched the premises in surface features** (for instance, the same quantifier form), people accepted it more, though logic makes this irrelevant. For **valid** syllogisms acceptance was **84%** (matching) versus **76%** (non-matching); for **invalid** ones, **62%** versus **57%**. Unlike belief bias, which reflects knowledge and experience, this **matching bias** shows reasoning swayed by non-logical factors unrelated to knowledge.

> **Trap.** Classic background: older textbooks describe a similar surface effect as the **atmosphere effect** (Woodworth and Sells, 1935). The 7th edition calls it **matching bias** and cites **Stupple et al. (2013)**; an older key asking for "atmosphere effect" means this phenomenon.

#### Logic's words versus everyday words

- People often take "all As are Bs" to imply "all Bs are As", and "some As are not Bs" to imply "some Bs are not As". **Ceraso and Provitera (1971)** spelled premises out unambiguously (e.g. all As are Bs, but some Bs are not As) and performance **improved substantially**. (Classic background: such reversal is called **illicit conversion**.)
- In logic **"some"** means "**at least one, possibly all**"; in conversation it means "**some but not all**". Making the logical meaning explicit improved performance (**Schmidt & Thompson, 2008**).

#### Summary

People find syllogisms hard: **real-world knowledge** sways acceptance (belief bias), **surface matching** sways it too, and quantifiers are misread because their **logical** and **everyday** meanings differ.

## 14.4 Theories of Deductive Reasoning

Two approaches dominate: **Johnson-Laird's mental model theory** and the **dual-system** theories (introduced in Chapter 13), represented here by **Evans's heuristic-analytic theory**. The most testable contrast between them is the **order** in which logic and knowledge enter reasoning.

### Mental model theory (Johnson-Laird)

#### What a mental model is

**Johnson-Laird (1983, 2010, 2013)** holds that people reason by building **mental models** of the situations premises describe. Each model represents **one possibility**, capturing what the various ways it could arise have in common (Johnson-Laird, 2004). A tossed coin can follow endless trajectories but has only **two** models: heads and tails.

A spatial example. Premises: the kettle is left of the toaster; the jar is right of the toaster; the spoon is in front of the kettle; the cup is in front of the jar. Is the spoon left of the cup? No rule is needed; you build a layout and read it off:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| kettle | toaster | jar |
| spoon | | cup |

Since no arrangement consistent with the premises puts the spoon elsewhere, the conclusion is **valid**: validity is the absence of any model in which the premises hold and the conclusion fails. (The book's version, from Johnson-Laird, 1983, uses a lamp, pad, book, clock and vase.)

#### Main assumptions

1. A model of the situation is built and conclusions are read off it. The model is **iconic**: its structure matches what it represents.
2. The reasoner tries to build **alternative models** that falsify the conclusion; if no **counterexample** is found, the conclusion is accepted.
3. Model building draws on limited **working memory** (Chapter 6).
4. Problems needing **several** models are harder than those needing **one**, because of the extra working memory load.
5. **Principle of truth**: to reduce working memory load, people represent explicitly only **what is true**, not what is false (Johnson-Laird, 1999).

> **Trap.** The **principle of truth** is Johnson-Laird's (represent only true possibilities). The **satisficing principle** is Evans's (accept a model that is good enough), echoing Simon. Both produce errors; they are different principles.

#### Findings

**Imagery hinders (Knauff et al., 2003).** Models preserve spatial relations and may involve visual imagery, yet reasoning was **slower** with imagery, because images carry **irrelevant details** that get in the way.

**Working memory (Copeland & Radvansky, 2004).** **86%** drew the valid conclusion when premises allowed **one** model, but only **31%** with **three**. In a second study **working memory capacity correlated +0.42** with syllogistic reasoning.

**Illusory inferences (Legrenzi et al., 2003).** Participants judged whether descriptions of everyday objects (a chair, a tray) were consistent, and some problems were designed so that the principle of truth would mislead. The tray problem's structure: exactly **one** of two statements is true, (1) the object is A or B or both, (2) the object is B and C; can it be B and C? Most say yes, since B-and-C is a possibility they represented. But if it were B and C, statement 2 would be true and so would statement 1 (B suffices), making **two** true statements and violating the premise. Seeing this requires representing falsity. As predicted, illusory problems produced many errors while **control** problems, where the principle of truth sufficed, produced very high accuracy (Figure 14.4; exact values are in the lost graph). Over-reliance on the principle of truth causes errors on other problem types too (**Johnson-Laird et al., 2012**), and instructions to **falsify** the premises reduce susceptibility (**Newsome & Johnson-Laird, 2006**).

**Little counterexample search (Newstead et al., 1999).** If people searched for counterexamples they should consider more conclusions on multiple-model problems. They did not: **1.12** conclusions for **multiple-model** versus **1.05** for **single-model** syllogisms.

**Counterexamples are hard to find (Khemlani et al., 2012).** Asked who could be described as **not living adult males**, participants listed only **four** possibilities on average, though three binary features minus the one excluded combination give **seven** (dead adult males, living adult females, living boys, and so on).

**Individual differences (Bucciarelli & Johnson-Laird, 1999).** The theory downplays them, yet people built models in different orders: some from the first premise, some from the second, some from a model satisfying the conclusion.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Most predictions are confirmed: models capture what possibilities share and appear **iconic**; many errors stem from the **principle of truth** (Legrenzi et al., 2003); people find counterexamples **hard** to generate (Khemlani et al., 2012); and **working memory** limits reasoning. In **Khemlani and Johnson-Laird's (2012)** meta-analysis of syllogistic reasoning comparing **seven** theories, mental model theory best predicted the responses people gave (**95%** success) but was relatively weak at **ruling out** responses they did not give.

**Limitations.**

1. **People deduce less than the theory assumes.** Khemlani and Johnson-Laird (2012) conceded that the theory originally assumed routine search for counterexamples and that people do not search. Most fall back on easier processing.
2. **Model construction is underspecified**: background knowledge is used, but how people choose what to include is not explained.
3. **Ambiguous problems are poorly handled (Ragni & Knauff, 2013).** Given "A is right of B; C is left of A", C could be left of B or between B and A. The theory does not predict responses. Ragni and Knauff argued that people prefer the model **easiest to construct** (appending C at the end, not squeezing it in), and most reasoners duly answered, wrongly, that C is left of B. They also found reasoners usually construct only **one** model. (The book's version uses cars: Porsche, Ferrari, Beetle.)

### Dual-system theories

#### System 1 and System 2

Dual-system theories (e.g. **Kahneman, 2003**; **Evans, 2008**) contrast:

| | **System 1** | **System 2** |
|---|---|---|
| Evolutionary age | **Early** | **Recent** |
| Awareness | **Unconscious** | **Conscious** |
| Processing | **Parallel** | **Rule-based, serial** |
| Capacity | Not limited | **Limited** |
| General intelligence | **Independent** | **Linked** |

> **Trap.** It is **System 2** that is linked to intelligence and capacity-limited; **System 1** is old, parallel and independent of intelligence. Later writers (Evans & Stanovich, 2013; Stanovich in 14.7) prefer **Type 1 / Type 2 processes** to "systems"; older keys use System 1/2 for the same contrast.

#### Evans's heuristic-analytic theory

In **Evans (2006)** (Figure 14.5), **heuristic** processes (System 1) use task features, current goals and background knowledge to build a **single** hypothetical possibility or mental model. **Analytic** processes (System 2), slow and effortful, **may** then revise or replace it, or accept it. Intervention is most likely when:

- **instructions** call for abstract or logical reasoning;
- the participant is **highly intelligent**;
- **enough time** is available;
- the participant must **justify** their reasoning.

The analytic system **evaluates** models and is **not** simply a logic engine. It usually, but not always, improves performance; it often accepts conclusions that *could* be true but need not be, because it **satisfices**.

Evans's three principles of hypothetical thinking:

1. **Singularity**: only **one** model is considered at a time.
2. **Relevance**: the model considered is the **most relevant** (plausible or probable) given prior knowledge and context.
3. **Satisficing**: the analytic system **accepts the current model if adequate**, which often leads to accepting conclusions that are not necessarily true.

| | **Mental model theory** | **Heuristic-analytic theory** |
|---|---|---|
| First step | **Deduction** (build and test models) | **World knowledge and context** |
| What may modify it | Real-world knowledge | **Deduction** in the analytic system |
| Models | Several, in the counterexample search | **One** at a time |
| Importance of deduction | **Central** | **Lower** |

> **Trap.** Mirror images. **Johnson-Laird**: deduction first, knowledge may interfere. **Evans**: knowledge first, deduction may intervene. "Which theory attaches less importance to deductive reasoning?" = **Evans's heuristic-analytic theory**.

#### Serial, parallel or logical intuition? (De Neys, 2012)

The fast-then-slow sequence is **oversimplified** (Handley et al., 2011; De Neys, 2012; Evans, 2012): processes may run **in parallel**, and **logic** may operate within intuition too. **De Neys (2012)** describes three models (Figure 14.6; also Chapter 13):

| Model | Claim | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Serial** ("traditional", e.g. Evans, 2006) | Intuitive processing first, **perhaps** followed by deliberate processing | Logic only in the slow system |
| **Parallel** | Intuitive and deliberate processing from the **outset** | **Wasteful**: effortful processing always runs |
| **Logical intuition** (De Neys, 2012, 2014) | **Heuristic** and **logical** intuitive responses arise in parallel; deliberation is triggered only by their **conflict** | Logical principles can be activated fairly automatically |

#### Findings

**Heuristics vary with the task.** **Matching bias** drives the selection task, and a similar matching bias appears in **syllogisms** (Stupple et al., 2013).

**Belief bias as a marker of heuristic processing.** A believable invalid conclusion pits a heuristic "accept" against an analytic "reject", and belief bias should be stronger when only heuristic processing operates. Evidence: **Evans (2000)** found **less** belief bias with instructions stressing logic; **Stupple et al. (2011)** found high-belief-bias participants responded **faster**, especially to invalid-but-believable conclusions; **Evans and Curtis-Holmes (2005)** found belief bias **stronger under time limits**, paralleling the effect of speed on conditional errors (Markovits et al., 2013).

**Working memory and load (De Neys, 2006).** Intelligence correlates highly with **working memory capacity** (review by **Conway et al., 2003**), so high-capacity people should use analytic processing more, and a demanding **secondary task** should reduce it. De Neys gave **conflict** problems (validity and believability disagree, so analysis is needed) and **non-conflict** problems (heuristics suffice), alone or with a low- or high-load secondary task. High capacity helped **only on conflict problems**, and the demanding secondary task impaired **conflict but not non-conflict** problems, a finding replicated by **Tsujii and Watanabe (2009)**.

**Feeling of rightness (Thompson et al., 2011).** What decides whether analysis is engaged? Participants gave a quick **intuitive answer** to syllogistic and conditional problems, rated its **feeling of rightness**, then reconsidered without time limit and gave a **final analytic answer**. A **monitoring system** indexed by these ratings was proposed: **low** feelings of rightness led to long reconsideration and frequent changes, **high** ones to quick retention of the intuitive answer. And analysis was no guarantee of accuracy: analytic answers to conditionals were correct only about **65%** of the time.

**Implicit logic (De Neys et al., 2010).** Accuracy may be an insensitive index of processing (De Neys, 2012, 2014). On syllogisms with or without conflict between validity and believability, accuracy was **52%** on conflict trials (near chance) and **89%** on no-conflict trials. Yet **skin conductance** was higher on conflict trials in **all** participants: the conflict registered **below consciousness**, perhaps as a gut feeling. This favours the **logical intuition model** over heuristic-analytic and other serial models.

**Liking valid conclusions (Morsanyi & Handley, 2012).** Quick ratings of how much people **liked** conclusions favoured **valid** ones, with slight shifts in affect, suggesting intuition has logical properties. **Klauer and Singmann (2013)** found evidence that this reflected pre-existing differences in how much the conclusions were liked, so logic may not have been involved.

> **Trap.** Three De Neys studies. **De Neys et al. (2005)**: counterexamples and working memory in **conditional** reasoning. **De Neys (2006)**: working memory capacity and **secondary-task load**, conflict vs non-conflict problems. **De Neys et al. (2010)**: **skin conductance**, 52% vs 89%.

#### Evaluation

**Strengths.**

1. Reasoning uses the **same kinds of process** (heuristic, analytic) as many other cognitive tasks.
2. The **singularity, relevance and satisficing** principles are supported and explain most errors.
3. The **heuristic-analytic distinction**, with analytic processing as more **effortful**, is supported (De Neys, 2006); **belief bias** and **matching bias** show the importance of heuristics.
4. It explains some **individual differences**: high working memory capacity or intelligence helps partly through more analytic processing.

**Limitations.**

1. The distinction is **too neat and tidy** (**Keren & Schul, 2009**), though **fruitful** in practice (**Evans & Stanovich, 2013**).
2. **How the two interact** is unclear; Thompson et al.'s (2011) **monitoring mechanism** is a useful start.
3. The analytic processes, said to vary in closeness to logic (Evans, 2006), are **not clearly specified**, nor is how people choose among them.
4. Logic is assumed to require **conscious** analysis, but some evidence suggests **intuitive** logic below awareness (De Neys et al., 2010; Morsanyi & Handley, 2012; but see Klauer & Singmann, 2013).
5. **Serial** processing may be wrong; there may be more **parallel** processing.

> **Trap.** "Which is NOT a limitation of heuristic-analytic theory?" "Cannot explain belief bias" and "ignores working memory differences" are distractors; both are listed among its **strengths**.

## 14.5 Brain Systems in Reasoning

Neuroimaging of deduction has grown fast. The questions are which regions are involved, whether that depends on the problem type and the reasoner's strategy, and in what order regions are engaged.

### A left-hemisphere fronto-parietal system

**Prado et al. (2011)** meta-analysed **28 neuroimaging studies** of deductive reasoning and found a **left-hemisphere** system involving **frontal and parietal** areas. Regions most consistently active (Figure 14.8) were the **inferior frontal gyrus (IFG)**, **middle frontal gyrus (MFG)**, **medial frontal gyrus (MeFG)**, **precentral gyrus (PG)**, **basal ganglia (BG)** and **posterior parietal cortex (PPC)**.

Patient data agree. **Goel et al. (2007)** found that patients with **left** parietal damage did worse than those with **right** parietal damage on reasoning tasks in which **complete information** was given.

Why the left hemisphere? **Gazzaniga et al. (2008)** argue for a single conscious left-hemisphere system, the **interpreter**, which strives to make coherent sense of the information available to it; left-hemisphere dominance in deduction may depend partly on it. (Classic background: the concept comes from Gazzaniga's split-brain research.)

> **Trap.** The **interpreter** is **left**-hemisphere (Gazzaniga). The deductive network is left-lateralised overall, but relational reasoning recruits **both** parietal cortices, and the **right** inferior frontal cortex inhibits belief bias (Tsujii & Watanabe, 2009, below).

### Type of task

Prado et al. (2011) found that the regions involved depend heavily on the **type of argument**:

| Type | Example structure | Distinctive activation |
|---|---|---|
| **Relational** | A left of B; B left of C; so A left of C | **Parietal cortex in both hemispheres** |
| **Categorical** | All As are Bs; all Bs are Cs; so all As are Cs | **Left IFG (BA9/44)**, linked to **rule-based** processing |
| **Propositional** | If A then B; A; so B | **Left precentral gyrus (BA6)**, perhaps **attentional and motor** processes; least clear-cut findings |

**Why parietal cortex for relational reasoning?** Relational arguments lend themselves to **spatial** representation (A B C laid out in a row), and spatial processing depends mostly on **parietal cortex**. **Waechter et al. (2013)** found that patients with **parietal** damage (**BA7, BA40**), but not those with **anterior prefrontal** damage (**BA10**), were worse than controls at relational reasoning. **Sack et al. (2002)** showed that **TMS** over the **right posterior parietal cortex** impaired visuo-spatial processing.

Prado et al. concluded that deduction does **not** rest on a **unitary** brain system, which counts against any theory proposing one mechanism for relational, categorical and propositional reasoning.

**Language (Monti & Osherson, 2012).** Their review found language heavily involved in **reading and encoding** the premises but playing **little or no role later**, when processing is mainly **visuo-spatial** or **rule-based** depending on the task.

### Individual differences

**Three strategies (Reverberi et al., 2012).** In categorical reasoning:

| Strategy | Activation | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity to **logical form** | **Left inferior lateral frontal (BA44/45)** and **superior medial frontal (BA6/8)** | Extracting and representing problem **structure** |
| Sensitivity to **validity** (accuracy) | **Left ventro-lateral frontal (BA47)** | Selecting and applying **inference rules** |
| **Heuristic** strategies | **No specific pattern** | |

Reverberi et al. concluded that deduction comprises several subcomponents interacting in complex ways.

**Belief bias and the right inferior frontal cortex (Tsujii & Watanabe, 2009).** People differ greatly in belief bias, and the more intelligent show less because they analyse more (De Neys, 2006). The **right inferior frontal cortex** seems to **inhibit incorrect heuristic responses**, so Tsujii and Watanabe predicted that greater activation there would mean **less** belief bias. Participants reasoned under **low or high** concurrent load. Accuracy (low belief bias) was **strongly associated** with right inferior frontal activation under both loads; under **high load** the region was **less active** and belief bias **much greater** (Figure 14.9).

> **Trap.** **Left IFG (BA9/44)**: categorical arguments, rule-based processing (Prado et al., 2011). **Right inferior frontal cortex**: inhibition of heuristic responses, so more activity means less belief bias (Tsujii & Watanabe, 2009). Swapping hemispheres is a classic distractor.

### Sequence of processes

Most imaging shows **which** areas are active, not **when**. **Fangmeier et al. (2006)**, guided by mental model theory, identified three stages of relational reasoning:

| Stage | Main activation | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise processing** | **Temporo-occipital** | Visuo-spatial processing |
| **Premise integration** | **Anterior prefrontal cortex** (e.g. **BA10**) | **Executive** processing |
| **Validation** | **Posterior parietal**, prefrontal **BA6** and **BA8**, **dorsal cingulate** | Parietal cortex holds **modality-independent** representations; prefrontal areas reflect demanding validation |

Problems were presented **visually**; **Fangmeier and Knauff (2009)** used **auditory** presentation and replicated anterior prefrontal involvement in **integration** and parietal involvement in **validation**.

**Bonnefond et al. (2013)** studied **modus ponens** over the first few hundred milliseconds after each premise. People **expected** the second premise to match the first (P after "if P then Q"); when it did **not**, brain activity rose about **300 ms** after it appeared. When it **did** match, the valid inference was generated (**parieto-frontal** activation at about **400 ms**) **before the conclusion was shown**, evidence of **anticipatory** processing.

### Overall evaluation

Progress has been **limited**; **Goel (2007)** remarked that imaging findings on reasoning might seem chaotic and inconsistent. Two reasons for the inconsistency are now understood: activation differs by **problem type** (Prado et al., 2011) and by **strategy** (Reverberi et al., 2012). Most studies use **complex tasks** engaging many processes and regions; remedies are to study **timing** (Fangmeier et al., 2006) or **narrower questions** such as the regions behind belief bias. Bonnefond et al.'s (2013) **anticipatory processing** contradicts the usual assumption that processing is triggered by each premise as it arrives.

## 14.6 Informal Reasoning

### How informal reasoning differs from deduction

Deduction research is **narrow**: it uses logic problems in which past knowledge is meant to be irrelevant. **Informal reasoning** draws on **knowledge and experience**, usually to argue for or against a claim, and has little to do with logic. Four differences stand out.

1. **Content matters.** In deduction only form counts. **Hahn and Oaksford (2007)** compare two arguments from ignorance with the same form: ghosts exist because nobody has proved otherwise, and a drug is safe because no evidence of harm has been found. Most people find the drug argument far more persuasive, because ghosts are implausible to begin with.
2. **Context matters.** The same argument persuades more coming from an **expert** (**Walton, 2010**).
3. **Probability, not binary truth.** Deduction assumes every proposition is true or false; informal reasoning treats claims as possibly or probably true (**Evans, 2012**). This changes how **fallacies** look (**Hahn & Oaksford, 2014**). The **straw man fallacy** misrepresents an opponent's view, and classical logic dismisses it, but **Aikin and Casey (2011)** note that one version, picking off the opponent's **weakest** arguments, can genuinely **lower the probability** that the opponent is right.
4. **Different motives.** Deduction tasks assume the reasoner wants to be logical and accurate. On **Mercier and Sperber's (2011)** argumentative theory, informal reasoning exists to devise and evaluate **arguments that persuade**, and skilled arguers seek support for their views rather than truth. Eysenck and Keane call this **somewhat narrow**: we also reason to think generally and to anticipate and plan (**Evans, 2011**).

> **Trap.** Mercier and Sperber's (2011) **argumentative theory** (reasoning is for persuading) reappears in 14.7 as an example of **instrumental rationality**. The 7th edition calls it **too narrow**, citing Evans (2011); an option saying the book fully endorses it is wrong.

**The divide is not sharp.** These differences describe what was **historically assumed**. People use knowledge and probabilities even on deductive tasks, so they reason informally there too. If the processes overlap, abilities should correlate, and **Ricco (2007)** found that the ability to detect informal fallacies such as **irrelevance** (supporting a claim with an irrelevant reason) and **slippery slope** (claiming an innocent first step leads to bad outcomes, without reasons) was associated with **deductive** performance. Since belief bias shows informal processes intruding on formal tasks, it makes sense to study them **directly** with informal tasks.

### In the real world: the neuroimaging illusion

**Weisberg et al. (2008)** gave students on an introductory **cognitive neuroscience** course good and bad explanations of psychological phenomena, some accompanied by **irrelevant neuroscience information**, and asked how satisfying each was. Neuroscience made explanations **more satisfying**, **especially bad ones** (Figure 14.10): content was having a disproportionate effect. Two reasons: neuroscience seems more "**scientific**" because of its complex, expensive technology, and brain data seem to give **direct access** to psychological processes. This **neuroimaging illusion** (Chapter 1) means neuroscientific evidence must be scrutinised as carefully as psychological evidence. (Classic background: the paper is known for the phrase "the seductive allure of neuroscience explanations".)

> **Trap.** Irrelevant neuroscience helped **bad** explanations **most**, not only good ones and not equally.

### Findings: motivation

#### Motivated inference and myside bias

Much everyday reasoning is badly flawed: the book notes that **tens of millions of Americans** believe humans did not evolve from apes, that the moon landing was faked, or that global warming is no problem. **Thagard (2011)** explains such beliefs by **motivated inference**, in which personal goals distort judgement so that wishes, not facts, drive the conclusion. Closely related is **myside bias**: evaluating statements by **one's own beliefs** rather than their merits, or generating arguments that support those beliefs.

**Stanovich and West (2007)** had college students rate the accuracy of contentious but **factually correct** propositions. Students who **regularly drank** rated "college drinkers are more likely to become alcoholics later" as less accurate than non-drinkers did, and **women** rated "the pay gap generally disappears when men and women hold the same position" as less accurate than men did: strong myside bias. **Stanovich and West (2008)** found that the **strength of prior beliefs** predicted the degree of myside bias and, crucially, that myside bias was **unrelated to cognitive ability or intelligence**, suggesting little use of **System 2**.

> **Trap.** **Belief bias** decreases with intelligence and working memory capacity (De Neys, 2006); **myside bias** is **unrelated** to intelligence (Stanovich & West, 2008) and is predicted by **prior belief strength**. "More intelligent people show less myside bias" is wrong.

#### In the real world: does climate change exist?

- **Memory (Howe & Leiserowitz, 2013).** After an unusually **hot** summer and **cold** winter, Americans most **dismissive** of global warming were only **half as likely** as the most **alarmed** to remember the summer as warmer than normal (Figure 14.11, summer 2010 and winter 2010 to 2011).
- **False consensus (Leviston et al., 2013).** Australian **disbelievers** greatly overestimated how many others shared their view, the **false consensus effect**. (Classic background: the term comes from Ross and colleagues' 1970s social psychology research.)
- **Values over literacy (Kahan et al., 2012).** **Egalitarian communitarians** (valuing equality and society) saw considerably more risk than **hierarchical individualists** (valuing hierarchy and personal responsibility), while **science literacy and numeracy** had only a **small** effect (Figure 14.12).
- **Capstick and Pidgeon (2014):** **individualism correlated +0.42** with climate scepticism and **egalitarianism -0.22**.

**Kahan (2012)** argues that one's climate position now signals **what kind of person** one is, so disagreeing with one's cultural group risks being thought weird. That motive explains why climate reasoning attends so little to scientific evidence, and why disbelievers show false consensus and distorted weather memory.

#### Motivation can help

Motivation is not always harmful: **Dawson et al. (2002)** (14.3) found that people told a rule predicted **their own early death** were far more accurate (38% vs 9%), because they were motivated to **disprove** it.

### Findings: probabilities

**Three factors in argument strength (Hahn & Oaksford, 2007).** Supporting a probabilistic view, perceived conclusion strength depended on (1) **prior conviction**, (2) **polarity**, with **positive** arguments having more impact than negative ones, and (3) **strength of evidence**. In their dialogues, a woman asks a friend whether he takes a (fictional) drug, **digesterole**, says she strongly believes it has side effects, and he agrees, citing **one** experiment. That combines a **strong prior**, a **positive** claim and **weak** evidence; other versions used a **weak** prior, a **negative** claim, or **50 experiments**. Participants judged how strongly she should now believe the drug has side effects. All three factors worked as predicted (Figure 14.13): arguments seemed stronger with a **positive** claim, a **strong** prior and **strong** evidence. The data (from Oaksford & Hahn, 2004) fitted a **Bayesian model** very well; on that approach, prior subjective probabilities are raised or lowered by evidence, so prior and evidence strength should matter greatly.

**Slippery slope arguments (Corner et al., 2011).** A **slippery slope argument** claims a small first step will set off events ending in an undesirable outcome, for instance that legalising voluntary euthanasia will lead to more "medical murder". Traditionally a fallacy, it should not be rejected wholesale on the Bayesian view: its strength depends on (1) the **probability** of the bad outcome and (2) **how bad** it would be, so strong slippery slopes have outcomes that are both **likely** and **very undesirable**. Using topics including **euthanasia** and **ID cards**, Corner et al. found **both** factors affected rated strength (Figure 14.14).

> **Trap.** Older logic texts list the slippery slope as a fallacy, full stop. The 7th edition's Bayesian position is that such arguments **vary in strength** and can be reasonable when the outcome is **probable and severe**. The same goes for the straw man (Aikin & Casey, 2011) and ad hominem (Harris et al., 2012).

### Evaluation

**Strengths.** Informal reasoning matters far more than deduction in daily life; **motivational factors** such as myside bias clearly play a major role; the **Bayesian approach**, in which **prior** subjective probabilities are updated by information into **posterior** ones, is a very useful framework that also applies to deductive tasks; and factors determining argument strength (prior belief, evidence strength) have been identified, with a Bayesian model fitting performance very well (Hahn & Oaksford, 2007).

**Limitations.**

1. **Too flexible to falsify?** **Bowers and Davis (2012)** argued the Bayesian approach is hard to falsify, partly because prior belief strength is often not measured precisely. **Griffiths et al. (2012)** replied that the approach is a **framework**, and frameworks are not falsifiable, but **specific Bayesian models** are. **Harris et al. (2012)** studied the **ad hominem argument** (dismissing a claim because of the claimant's negative characteristics), measured the factors Bayesians consider crucial, and found good support in precise **quantitative** tests.
2. **Individual differences** are little understood. **Sá et al. (2005)** found unsophisticated reasoning more common in people of **lower cognitive ability**, but the details are unclear.

> **Trap.** **Harris et al. (2012)** is the **ad hominem** study and **supports** the Bayesian approach; **Griffiths et al. (2012)** made the framework-versus-model reply to **Bowers and Davis (2012)**.

## 14.7 Are Humans Rational?

### The apparent paradox

Chapters 12 to 14 together make human thinking look poor: people fail simple items on **Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test**, often **ignore base rates**, get the **Wason selection task** wrong about **90%** of the time, and show strong **belief bias**. Yet most people cope reasonably well with everyday life. Eysenck and Keane's resolution is that the paradox is **overstated at both ends**: everyday thinking is **less** rational than we like to believe, and laboratory reasoning **less** inadequate than it looks.

### Why human reasoning is not as limited as it seems

1. **Heuristics are not simply errors.** They give fast, reasonably accurate judgements. **Maule and Hodgkinson (2002)** note that when the things being judged change over time, a precise but slow judgement is inappropriate and a quick heuristic approximation may be better.
2. **Relevance is often hidden.** People neglect **base rates** when their relevance is not explicit; when problems let people use **intuitive causal knowledge** to see why the base rate matters, performance improves greatly (e.g. **Krynski & Tenenbaum, 2007**).
3. **No social vacuum.** Many decision "errors" make sense once **accountability**, the need to justify choices to others (Chapter 13), is considered.
4. **Artificial tasks.** Syllogistic validity is independent of believability, yet real situations where background knowledge is wholly irrelevant are hard to find; and lab conclusions are certain, whereas real reasoning deals in **probabilities**.

Laboratory tasks therefore often **underestimate** reasoning ability, but blaming **all** difficulties on the problems or on poor motivation goes too far.

### Why human reasoning is limited

1. **Incentives rarely help.** **Camerer and Hogarth (1999)** reviewed **74 studies**; incentives rarely improved thinking and reasoning, so low motivation is not the explanation.
2. **Participant limitations.** On a complex base-rate task, students from a **leading** university were more often correct than those from a **second-tier** one: **40% vs 19%** (**Brase et al., 2006**).
3. **Failures survive clarification.** In **Tversky and Kahneman's (1983)** **Linda problem**, judging Linda more likely to be a **feminist bank teller** than a **bank teller** is the **conjunction fallacy** (Chapter 13), since a conjunction cannot be more probable than its parts. Spelling out that "bank teller" covered Linda whether or not she was a feminist reduced the fallacy only **somewhat**.
4. **Experts are biased.** **Medical experts** make biased judgements and decisions (e.g. **Redelmeier et al., 1995**; **Schwartz et al., 2004**).
5. **The Dunning-Kruger effect.** **Less skilled** people **overestimate** their ability **more** than skilled people; in **Dunning's (2011)** words, the incompetent have little insight into their incompetence. People blind to their own errors are unlikely to improve. The cause is that judging the correctness of one's answers needs much the same knowledge as producing correct answers. In a Dunning study on the **selection task**, participants who applied a correct rule consistently (**100%** right) and those who applied a wrong rule consistently (**0%**) **both** believed they had solved **80 to 90%** correctly. **Simons (2013)** found the effect robust: **bridge players** given feedback after each club session still **overestimated** their future performance if they were weaker players (Figure 14.15).

> **Trap.** Dunning-Kruger is not "everyone overestimates equally". The **less skilled overestimate more**, because the skill to perform is the skill to evaluate performance. Numbers: **0%** and **100%** scorers both estimated **80 to 90%** (Dunning, 2011); feedback did not cure it (Simons, 2013).

### What is rationality?

**Logic as the standard: normativism.** A long-popular view, championed by **Piaget**, **Wason** and others, held that rational thought is governed by **logic**; since most people deduce poorly, they are not very rational. This is **normativism**, which **Elqayam and Evans (2011)** define as the idea that thinking reflects a **normative system** against which it should be measured, so thought is "correct" if it conforms to classical logic. Whether logic is a suitable norm is doubtful: **Sternberg (2011)** observed that hardly any consequential problems in life have a deductive, or even meaningfully "correct", solution.

**Probability as the standard.** The book's preferred alternative is that rationality is the **effective use of probabilities**, appropriate to a world of uncertainty and imprecise information. **Oaksford and Chater (2009)** developed an influential **probabilistic approach** in which probability means **subjective degree of belief** and **prior** probabilities are revised by new information into **posterior** ones, an idea from the **Rev. Thomas Bayes** (Chapter 13) supported in several areas including informal reasoning.

> **Trap.** Older texts in the tradition of **Piaget's formal operations** and Wason's early work treat **logic** as the standard of rationality. The 7th edition calls logic an **inappropriate normative system** and prefers **probability**.

Why does actual thinking fall short of normative standards? Two frameworks answer.

### Bounded rationality (Simon)

**Herb Simon** spent much of his career on rationality (**Heukelom, 2007**). For **Simon (1945)**, behaviour is rational insofar as it selects alternatives that serve previously chosen **goals**. So informal reasoning that achieves the goal of persuading is rational even if it shows **myside bias**.

**Simon (1957)** proposed **bounded rationality**: limited processing ability is overcome with **short cuts** such as heuristics that give **workable** solutions, and thinking is bounded both by the **environment** and by **mental constraints** (limited attention, limited short-term memory). What matters is the **fit** between the two. **Simon (1990)** likened rational behaviour to **scissors**, one blade being the structure of the task environment and the other the actor's computational capacities; studying one blade alone gives a partial picture. Many "errors" thus reflect **limited capacity**, not irrationality. **Stich (1990)** called it perverse to fault people for not using strategies that would require a brain the size of a **blimp** (an airship).

> **Trap.** **Bounded rationality** (Simon, **1957**) = limited capacity forces workable short cuts; **scissors** (Simon, **1990**) = fit between environment and mind; **satisficing** = accepting the first adequate option, reused in Evans's principles. Contrast Stanovich's **cognitive miser**, who *could* do better but *prefers* the cheap strategy; the boundedly rational reasoner *cannot* do better with the resources available.

### Individual differences: intelligence and the tripartite model

**Does intelligence protect?** Only **partly** (**Stanovich, 2012**). Higher-IQ students do better on **many deductive** tasks, but intelligence often has only a **modest** effect on **judgement** tasks such as **framing**, the **sunk-cost effect** and **omission bias**. Still, **Toplak et al. (2011)** found a **+0.32** correlation between cognitive ability and performance across **15** judgement and decision tasks.

**Stanovich's (2012) tripartite model** (Figure 14.16) extends dual-process theory:

| Level | Processing | Role | Individual differences |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autonomous mind** | **Type 1** | Fast, fairly automatic processing (e.g. heuristics) | Relatively **small** |
| **Algorithmic mind** | **Type 2** | Slow, effortful; holds **mindware**; can **override** wrong heuristic responses | **Large**; tied to **fluid intelligence** |
| **Reflective mind** | **Type 2** control | Accesses **goals, beliefs, general knowledge**; **decides whether** Type 2 processing is used | **Large** |

Two features are novel. First, there are **two levels of higher control**. The algorithmic mind holds **mindware**, the rules, knowledge, procedures and strategies a person can retrieve to aid decisions and problem solving, and it can override the autonomous mind. But Type 2 processing runs only when the person **realises** it is needed and is **motivated** to start it, and that decision belongs to the **reflective mind**. Second, **fluid intelligence** (roughly, **non-verbal reasoning ability applied to novel problems**) bears directly on the algorithmic mind: high scorers have a **wider range** of Type 2 processes and use them more **efficiently**.

**Three routes to error.** A wrong answer can arise because:

1. the algorithmic mind **lacks the mindware** to override the heuristic response;
2. the mindware exists but **processing capacity** is insufficient to override Type 1 processing;
3. the mindware exists but the **reflective mind does not trigger** its use.

> **Trap.** **Reflective** mind decides **whether** to use Type 2; **algorithmic** mind **does** the override, holds mindware, and is tied to **fluid intelligence**; **autonomous** mind runs Type 1 heuristics and shows the **smallest** individual differences.

**Dysrationalia and cognitive misers.** **Stanovich (2009)** coined **dysrationalia** for the inability to think and act rationally **despite adequate intelligence**. It arises because most people, high-IQ people included, are **cognitive misers** who prefer fast, easy strategies to accurate, effortful ones (route 3 above). Miserliness, and so limited use of the reflective mind, is measured by the **Cognitive Reflection Test** (**Frederick, 2005**; Chapter 12), and **Toplak et al. (2011)** found **low CRT scorers** did relatively poorly on many judgement and reasoning tasks. (Classic background: in the best-known CRT item, a bat and ball cost $1.10 together and the bat costs $1 more than the ball; the intuitive answer of 10 cents for the ball is wrong, and the answer is 5 cents.)

> **Trap.** **Dysrationalia** is **not** low intelligence: it is **intelligent** people reasoning poorly through **cognitive miserliness**, measured by the **CRT** (Frederick, 2005), not by IQ tests. The term is **Stanovich's (2009)**.

### Conclusions: for and against

**For human rationality.**

1. **Limited capacity** (**Simon, 1957**), not deficient rationality, explains moderately effective but suboptimal reasoning.
2. **Logic is the wrong yardstick** (e.g. **Oaksford & Chater, 2009**; **Evans, 2012**): it deals in certainties and is nearly irrelevant to lives full of uncertainty, and people look far more rational against **probabilistic** models.
3. **Instrumental rationality**: thinking is rational when it achieves the reasoner's **goals** (e.g. **Simon, 1945**; **Mercier & Sperber, 2011**). Seemingly irrational reasoning may serve goals such as wishful thinking (**Thagard, 2011**), loss avoidance (**Kahneman & Tversky, 1979**) or accountability to others (**Tetlock, 2002**).

**Against human rationality.**

1. **Cognitive misers** with the necessary knowledge and skill often fail to use it: **dysrationalia**.
2. **Base-rate neglect** is widespread, a failure to reason in line with **Bayes' theorem**, which is awkward because Bayes is the very standard that rescues people from the charge of illogic.
3. **Lack of insight** into one's own errors: the **Dunning-Kruger effect**.

The chapter summary's verdict: rationality is **greater** than many tasks suggest, because apparent failures often reflect **limited capacity**, **goals** that differ from the experimenter's, or comparison with **classical logic**, which is of negligible use in everyday life. Rationality is best seen as effective use of **probabilities** within **bounded** capacity. But reasoning is still often deficient, because we have limited awareness of our own incompetence and because intelligent people are frequently **cognitive misers**.

## How the chapter fits together

1. **From logic to probability.** The chapter starts by judging reasoning against **binary logic** and ends by judging it against **probability**: positive testing is optimal for sparse hypotheses (Perfors & Navarro, 2009); selection-task choices track information gain (Oaksford et al., 1997); conditional reasoners use a statistical strategy (Markovits et al., 2013); argument strength fits a Bayesian model (Hahn & Oaksford, 2007); rationality becomes effective use of probabilities (Oaksford & Chater, 2009). Evans's (2012) "new paradigm" names the shift.
2. **Knowledge causes the "errors", and usually for good reason.** Counterexamples affect valid inferences (De Neys et al., 2005), folk axioms add inferences (Bonnefon et al., 2012), belief overrides validity (Klauer et al., 2000), and content and source shape arguments (Hahn & Oaksford, 2007; Walton, 2010). Outside the laboratory, background knowledge is almost never irrelevant.
3. **A fast default, a costly override, and a monitor.** Time effects (Markovits et al., 2013; Evans & Curtis-Holmes, 2005), capacity and load (De Neys, 2006), feeling of rightness (Thompson et al., 2011), the right inferior frontal "brake" (Tsujii & Watanabe, 2009) and Stanovich's reflective mind describe one architecture. The open question is whether logic lives only in the slow system (Evans, 2006) or also in intuition (De Neys, 2012; De Neys et al., 2010).
4. **Ownership and motivation cut both ways.** People falsify others' hypotheses but not their own (Cowley & Byrne, 2005), scientists blame methods (Mitroff, 1974; Fugelsang et al., 2004), and myside bias distorts climate reasoning (Stanovich & West, 2007; Kahan et al., 2012); yet a personal threat drives falsification (Dawson et al., 2002).
5. **Working memory is the bottleneck.** More models, worse performance (Copeland & Radvansky, 2004); capacity protects on conflict problems (De Neys, 2006); fluid intelligence powers the algorithmic mind (Stanovich, 2012); bounded rationality (Simon, 1957) states the constraint generally.
6. **No single reasoning module.** Regions differ by argument type (Prado et al., 2011), strategy (Reverberi et al., 2012) and stage (Fangmeier et al., 2006), and language serves mainly encoding (Monti & Osherson, 2012).
7. **Rationality depends on the yardstick.** Against logic people look poor; against goals and probability, much better; but base-rate neglect, miserliness and the Dunning-Kruger effect are failures by any standard.

## Worked example

*A trainee clinical psychologist, Dr P., assesses Ms R., a 27-year-old software engineer referred by her GP with the note "probable bipolar disorder: please confirm". Dr P. asks whether she has had periods of very little sleep, fast talking and overspending. She says yes to the first two: during a product launch she slept four hours a night and "talked non-stop" in meetings. When she adds that she felt exhausted and anxious throughout, not elated, Dr P. records "poor insight", writes "bipolar II, confirmed", and feels about 90% sure. Later, in therapy, Ms R. says: "If I make one mistake on this project, I'll be sacked, then I'll never get another job, and I'll end up with nothing."*

**The diagnosis is hypothesis testing.** The hypothesis began as the GP's and was quickly adopted as Dr P.'s own. On Popper's logic, symptoms that fit bipolar disorder can never confirm it, while one well-chosen observation could rule it out.

**Positive testing on a non-specific rule.** Every question was a **positive test** of bipolar disorder. That is normally reasonable, because most diagnostic hypotheses are **sparse** (Perfors & Navarro, 2009). The trouble is the 2-4-6 trouble: short sleep and rapid speech belong to a much broader "rule" that also covers stress, anxiety and stimulant use, so "yes" answers cannot discriminate. Questions that rival hypotheses answer differently were needed: mood quality, **reduced need** for sleep versus inability to sleep, episodes outside work stress.

**Affirming the consequent.** "If bipolar, then periods of little sleep; she had them; so bipolar" is **AC**, which is invalid. People accept AC when consequent and antecedent seem tightly linked (the **statistical strategy**) and reject it by searching for **counterexamples**, a strategy that collapses under time pressure (49% vs 1.7% of trials; Markovits et al., 2013). A busy clinic is a limited-time condition.

**Explaining away the anomaly.** Recording "anxious, not elated" as "poor insight" is what Fugelsang et al.'s (2004) scientists did with 88% of inconsistent results. They were partly justified, since about half their anomalies did not replicate; the clinical equivalent of replication is collateral history, mood charting or a second interview, and if the anomaly recurs the hypothesis should change (as it did for 61% of replicated anomalies).

**Belief, base rates and confidence.** A referrer's "probable bipolar" acts like a believable conclusion: **belief bias** makes acceptance easier than checking. Ignoring how much more common stress-related insomnia is than bipolar II is **base-rate neglect**, a genuine departure from Bayesian reasoning. A trainee's 90% certainty after one interview is the **Dunning-Kruger** pattern: the skill to diagnose well is the skill to judge whether one has. Medical experts are not immune (Redelmeier et al., 1995; Schwartz et al., 2004).

**What the chapter says would help.** Treat the hypothesis as **someone else's** (62% vs 25% abandonment; Cowley & Byrne, 2005). Use **supervision or a co-assessor** (pairs consider more alternatives; Okada & Simon, 1997). Make the **falsifying case explicit**, as deontic rules do (Sperber & Girotto, 2002): "What would show this is *not* bipolar disorder?" A structured checklist forces the **reflective mind** to deploy mindware the trainee already has (Stanovich, 2012); not using it would be **dysrationalia**, not ignorance.

**Ms R.'s catastrophising.** Her statement is a **slippery slope argument**. Dismissing it as a fallacy helps no one; Corner et al.'s (2011) Bayesian analysis says its strength depends on the **probability** of each step and the **severity** of the outcome, which is what cognitive therapy's questions ("How likely is dismissal for one mistake? How likely is never working again?") ask her to re-estimate. Her **prior belief** is strong, so a single reassuring example will move it little (Hahn & Oaksford, 2007); repeated evidence, 50 experiments rather than one, is needed. And because **myside bias** tracks prior belief strength, not intelligence (Stanovich & West, 2008), her ability as an engineer will not protect her.

## MCQ traps

1. **Inductive vs deductive:** induction gives only **probable** conclusions (Russell's turkey); deduction gives conclusions that follow **necessarily** *if* premises are true.
2. **Valid vs true:** validity depends only on whether the conclusion follows; a valid argument can have a false conclusion.
3. **Popper (1968):** demarcation by **falsifiability** (psychoanalysis counted as pseudo-science); recommended strategy **falsification**.
4. **Wason (1960) 2-4-6:** rule = **any ascending triple**; **21%** right first time, **28%** never. Not to be confused with the four-card **selection task**, also Wason's.
5. **Myth:** Wason did not invoke "confirmation bias" in 1960; years later he called it **verification bias**.
6. **Positive testing ≠ confirmation**, and is usually **optimal** because hypotheses are **sparse** (Perfors & Navarro, 2009: **83%** of rules for 1 to 1,000 covered **under 20%**).
7. **Cowley and Byrne (2005):** **62%** abandon another's hypothesis, **25%** their own.
8. **Tweney et al. (1980)** DAX/MED: **over 50%** right first time. **Gale and Ball (2012):** MED example **6-4-2 = 75%**, **4-4-4 = 23%**.
9. **Cherubini et al. (2005):** rich examples **30%**, less rich **70%**. **Russo and Meloy (2008):** block feedback **9.4** hypotheses, **62%**; trial feedback **4.6**, **39%**.
10. **Manktelow (2012):** "confirm early, disconfirm late".
11. **Fugelsang et al. (2004):** **417** results, **223** inconsistent, **88%** blamed on methods, **12%** theory change; **55%** replicated; then **61%** theory change.
12. **Dunbar (1993):** control by **inhibition**; the **20%** who solved it explained discrepant data. **Okada and Simon (1997):** **pairs** beat individuals.
13. **Kulkarni and Simon (1988)** unusualness heuristic; **Klahr and Simon (2001)** weak methods; **Trickett and Trafton (2007)** "what if" reasoning (Einstein at **16**); **Mitroff (1974)** Apollo geologists; **Gorman (1995)** Bell.
14. **Valid:** MP, MT. **Invalid:** AC, DA. The book once lists "denial of the consequent" as invalid; that is a slip (it is MT).
15. **MP is accepted far more than MT** (Evans et al., 1993); DA often accepted because "if" is heard as **if and only if**.
16. **Markovits et al. (2013):** counterexample strategy **49%** unlimited vs **1.7%** limited time; statistical **19%** vs **55%**.
17. **Selection task:** correct **R and 7** (P, not-Q), about **5 to 10%**; most choose **R and 2**.
18. **Matching bias = Evans** (Evans & Ball, 2010, negated rules). **Information gain = Oaksford** (swans; Q cards **17/50/83%**, more Q choices when Q rare).
19. **Stenning and van Lambalgen (2004):** **3.7%** standard vs **18%** when told no feedback would come before all choices.
20. **Deontic** rules help: **Sperber and Girotto (2002)**, **68%**. Older keys cite **Griggs and Cox (1982)** and **Cosmides (1989)** cheater detection.
21. **Dawson et al. (2002):** **9%** (believed low lability) vs **38%** (believed high, motivated to disprove).
22. **Belief bias** (validity judged by believability) vs **confirmation bias** (seeking support) vs **myside bias** (claims judged by own beliefs, informal reasoning).
23. **Klauer et al. (2000):** base-rate effect (**one-sixth vs five-sixths**), belief bias, **belief-by-logic interaction** (belief bias larger for **invalid** syllogisms).
24. **Stupple et al. (2013)** syllogism matching: valid **84% vs 76%**, invalid **62% vs 57%**; older term **atmosphere effect** (Woodworth & Sells, 1935).
25. **"Some"** = at least one, possibly all, in logic (Schmidt & Thompson, 2008); premise reversal corrected by **Ceraso and Provitera (1971)**.
26. **Principle of truth = Johnson-Laird**; **satisficing = Evans**. Illusory inferences **Legrenzi et al. (2003)**, reduced by falsification instructions (**Newsome & Johnson-Laird, 2006**).
27. **Copeland and Radvansky (2004):** **86%** one model vs **31%** three; WMC r = **+0.42**. **Newstead et al. (1999):** **1.12 vs 1.05** conclusions. **Khemlani et al. (2012):** **4** of **7** possibilities. **Knauff et al. (2003):** imagery **slows** reasoning.
28. **Khemlani and Johnson-Laird (2012):** **7** theories compared; mental model theory **95%** at predicting responses made, weak at ruling out responses not made.
29. **Order:** Johnson-Laird = deduction first; Evans (2006) = knowledge first, deduction **less important**.
30. **System 2** = recent, conscious, serial, limited, **linked to intelligence**; **System 1** = old, unconscious, parallel, **independent** of intelligence.
31. **De Neys's models:** serial, parallel (wasteful), **logical intuition** (deliberation on conflict). **De Neys et al. (2010):** **52% vs 89%** accuracy but higher **skin conductance** on conflict trials in **all** participants.
32. **De Neys (2006):** WMC helps and load hurts **only conflict** problems. **Thompson et al. (2011):** feeling of rightness; analytic answers on conditionals about **65%** correct.
33. **Prado et al. (2011):** **28** studies, **left** fronto-parietal; relational **bilateral parietal**, categorical **left IFG BA9/44**, propositional **left precentral BA6**. **Right** inferior frontal inhibits belief bias (**Tsujii & Watanabe, 2009**).
34. **Fangmeier et al. (2006):** premises **temporo-occipital**, integration **anterior PFC BA10**, validation **posterior parietal**; auditory replication **Fangmeier and Knauff (2009)**. **Bonnefond et al. (2013):** **300 ms** mismatch, **400 ms** inference before the conclusion.
35. **Myside bias is unrelated to intelligence** (Stanovich & West, 2008); belief bias falls with intelligence.
36. **Weisberg et al. (2008):** irrelevant neuroscience boosted **bad** explanations most.
37. **Climate:** dismissives **half as likely** to recall the hot summer (Howe & Leiserowitz, 2013); **false consensus** (Leviston et al., 2013); values beat science literacy (Kahan et al., 2012); individualism **+0.42**, egalitarianism **-0.22** with scepticism (Capstick & Pidgeon, 2014).
38. **Slippery slope** strength = **probability and severity** of outcome (Corner et al., 2011). **Ad hominem: Harris et al. (2012).** Framework vs model reply: **Griffiths et al. (2012)** to **Bowers and Davis (2012)**.
39. **Camerer and Hogarth (1999):** **74** studies, incentives rarely help. **Brase et al. (2006):** **40% vs 19%**. **Toplak et al. (2011):** r = **+0.32** across **15** tasks.
40. **Dunning-Kruger:** the less skilled overestimate **more**; **0%** and **100%** scorers both estimated **80 to 90%** (Dunning, 2011); persists with feedback (**Simons, 2013**, bridge).
41. **Simon:** goal-relative rationality **1945**, bounded rationality **1957**, scissors **1990**; **Stich (1990)** blimp.
42. **Stanovich:** autonomous (Type 1), **algorithmic** (mindware, override, **fluid intelligence**), **reflective** (decides whether to use Type 2); **dysrationalia** (**2009**), cognitive misers, **CRT** (Frederick, **2005**).
43. **Normativism** (Elqayam & Evans, **2011**); logic as the norm was **Piaget's and Wason's** view; the 7th edition prefers **probability**.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Inductive reasoning** | Moving from observed cases to a general rule that can be likely but never guaranteed |
| **Deductive reasoning** | Drawing a conclusion that cannot be false once the premises are granted |
| **Informal reasoning** | Everyday argument that relies on what one knows and on likelihoods, not on formal logic |
| **Validity** | Property of a conclusion that follows logically from its premises, regardless of real-world truth |
| **Falsification** | Testing a hypothesis by trying to disprove it; Popper's prescription for science |
| **Falsifiability** | A theory's potential to be shown false; Popper's criterion for science |
| **Confirmation bias** | Favouring tests and evidence likely to agree with a hypothesis one already holds |
| **Verification bias** | Wason's later term for clinging to an over-narrow first hypothesis by verifying it |
| **Positive testing** | Testing instances of one's hypothesis; confirmatory only if one believes it |
| **Sparse hypothesis** | A hypothesis covering a small fraction of its domain |
| **Weak methods** | General strategies (trial and error, means-ends analysis) used in science and everyday problems |
| **Unusualness heuristic** | Scientists' habit of treating surprising results as the lead for new ideas and studies |
| **"What if" reasoning** | Working out the consequences of an imagined situation (conceptual simulation) |
| **Propositional logic** | Logic in which symbols stand for propositions joined by operators such as and, or, if...then |
| **Conditional reasoning** | Deduction from premises of the form "if P then Q" |
| **Antecedent / consequent** | The "if" part (P) and the "then" part (Q) of a conditional |
| **Modus ponens / modus tollens** | Valid inferences: P so Q / not-Q so not-P |
| **Affirmation of the consequent / denial of the antecedent** | Invalid inferences: Q so P / not-P so not-Q |
| **Disinterested reasoning** | Reasoning in which the reasoner's goals and preferences play no part |
| **Folk axioms** | Everyday assumptions about behaviour that generate extra inferences |
| **Statistical / counterexample strategy** | Accepting a conclusion by its estimated probability / by failing to find a defeating case |
| **Wason selection task** | Four-card test of a conditional rule; correct choice is P and not-Q |
| **Matching bias** | Choosing items named in the rule, or accepting conclusions whose surface form matches the premises |
| **Information gain** | Oaksford's criterion of choosing the cards that most reduce uncertainty given how common P and Q are |
| **Indicative / deontic rule** | A rule describing how things are / a rule about obligation, focused on detecting violations |
| **Syllogism** | Two premises and a conclusion linking three terms through a middle term with all, some, no, some...not |
| **Belief bias** | Letting a conclusion's believability, not its logical validity, decide whether it is accepted |
| **Mental model** | Iconic representation of one possibility, capturing what its realisations share |
| **Principle of truth** | Mental models make true possibilities explicit and leave false ones unrepresented, saving working memory |
| **Illusory inference** | A compelling but wrong conclusion caused by neglecting false possibilities |
| **Dual-system theory** | Reasoning involves fast intuitive (System 1, Type 1) and slow analytic (System 2, Type 2) processes |
| **Heuristic-analytic theory** | Evans (2006): heuristics build one model, which analytic processes may revise or accept |
| **Singularity / relevance / satisficing** | One model at a time / the most plausible one / accepted if adequate |
| **Logical intuition model** | De Neys: heuristic and logical intuitions arise in parallel; conflict triggers deliberation |
| **Feeling of rightness** | Confidence in an intuitive answer that governs whether it is reconsidered |
| **Interpreter** | Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere system that constructs coherent sense from available information |
| **Straw man fallacy** | Attacking a distorted or weakened version of an opponent's position instead of the real one |
| **Argumentative theory** | Mercier and Sperber's claim that reasoning functions to persuade |
| **Neuroimaging illusion** | Undue persuasion by irrelevant brain-based information |
| **Motivated inference** | Distorting judgements to fit personal goals (Thagard, 2011) |
| **Myside bias** | Judging claims by whether they fit one's own views, and producing arguments mainly for one's own side |
| **False consensus effect** | Overestimating how many people share one's views |
| **Bayesian approach** | Revising subjective probabilities from prior to posterior in the light of evidence |
| **Slippery slope argument** | Arguing that a modest first step will set off a chain ending in harm; its strength depends on the outcome's probability and severity |
| **Ad hominem argument** | Dismissing a claim because of the claimant's negative characteristics |
| **Normativism** | Evaluating human thinking against a standard system (e.g. classical logic) and scoring departures as errors |
| **Instrumental rationality** | Thinking counts as rational if it achieves the reasoner's goals |
| **Bounded rationality** | Simon's view that limited capacity and the environment force short cuts that give workable solutions |
| **Dunning-Kruger effect** | Poor performers misjudge their competence upwards, and by more than strong performers do |
| **Tripartite model** | Stanovich's autonomous (Type 1), algorithmic and reflective (Type 2) minds |
| **Mindware** | Rules, knowledge, procedures and strategies retrievable to aid reasoning |
| **Fluid intelligence** | Capacity to reason non-verbally about problems one has not met before |
| **Cognitive miser** | Someone who prefers fast, easy strategies to accurate, effortful ones |
| **Dysrationalia** | Stanovich's term for irrational thinking or behaviour in people whose intelligence is adequate |
| **Cognitive Reflection Test** | Frederick's (2005) test of the tendency to override intuitive wrong answers |

13. **Oaksford's information-gain account:** swans, Oaksford et al. (1997).
14. **Facilitation:** deontic rules (Sperber & Girotto, 2002) and motivation (Dawson et al., 2002); older Griggs and Cox / Cosmides versions.
15. **Syllogisms:** structure and quantifiers; belief bias and Klauer et al.'s (2000) three findings; Stupple and Ball (2008).
16. **Other syllogism errors:** matching (Stupple et al., 2013), conversion (Ceraso & Provitera, 1971), "some" (Schmidt & Thompson, 2008).
17. **Mental model theory:** five assumptions including the principle of truth.
18. **Evidence for it:** Copeland and Radvansky (2004), Legrenzi et al. (2003), Newsome and Johnson-Laird (2006), Knauff et al. (2003).
19. **Its evaluation:** 95% predictive success but little counterexample search (Newstead et al., 1999; Khemlani et al., 2012), underspecified construction, ambiguous problems (Ragni & Knauff, 2013).
20. **System 1 vs System 2**; Evans's (2006) heuristic-analytic theory, its four conditions and three principles.
21. **Mental model vs heuristic-analytic theory:** opposite order of deduction and knowledge.
22. **De Neys's three models** and the evidence: De Neys et al. (2010), Morsanyi and Handley (2012), Klauer and Singmann (2013).
23. **Belief bias as dual-process evidence:** Evans (2000), Stupple et al. (2011), Evans and Curtis-Holmes (2005), De Neys (2006), Thompson et al. (2011).
24. **Five limitations of heuristic-analytic theory** and the "which is NOT a limitation" trap.
25. **Brain systems:** Prado et al. (2011), Goel et al. (2007), the interpreter, Waechter et al. (2013), Sack et al. (2002), Monti and Osherson (2012).
26. **Individual differences and timing in the brain:** Reverberi et al. (2012), Tsujii and Watanabe (2009), Fangmeier et al. (2006), Fangmeier and Knauff (2009), Bonnefond et al. (2013), Goel's (2007) verdict.
27. **Informal vs deductive reasoning:** content, context, probability, motive; Mercier and Sperber (2011) and why it is too narrow; Ricco (2007).
28. **Motivation:** motivated inference (Thagard, 2011), myside bias (Stanovich & West, 2007, 2008), the climate change box, the neuroimaging illusion (Weisberg et al., 2008).
29. **Bayesian informal reasoning:** Hahn and Oaksford (2007), Corner et al. (2011), and its evaluation (Bowers & Davis, 2012; Griffiths et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2012; Sá et al., 2005).
30. **Are humans rational?** Four reasons for "less limited", five for "limited" (incentives, participant limits, Linda, experts, Dunning-Kruger).
31. **Normativism vs probabilistic vs bounded rationality** (Simon 1945, 1957, 1990; Stich, 1990).
32. **Stanovich's tripartite model:** three minds, mindware, fluid intelligence, three routes to error, dysrationalia, CRT, and the final for-and-against summary.
