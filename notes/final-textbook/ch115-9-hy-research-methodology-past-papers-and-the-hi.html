  1. 'Reinforcement will have positive effect on performance,' which type of hypothesis is this? (RML 2024)

    • aNull and directional
    • bAlternate and directional
    • cNull and non-directional
    • dAlternate and non-directional
    ✓ Answer: (b) Alternate and directional
    Definition of an alternative, directional hypothesis
    The concept

    Which hypothesisThe statement predicts an effect, so it is the alternative hypothesis. The null hypothesis would predict no effect.

    Why directionalIt also says which way the effect goes (positive), so it is directional and would be tested one-tailed.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Null and directionalRight on direction, but a null predicts no effect, and this statement predicts one.
    • (c) Null and non-directionalWrong twice: the statement predicts an effect (so not null) and names its direction (so not non-directional).
    • (d) Alternate and non-directionalRight that it is the alternative hypothesis, but it names a direction (positive), so it is directional, not non-directional.

  2. Nomothetic approach of study is involved in (JEMAS 2025)

    • aQualitative method
    • bExperimental method
    • cQuasi-experimental method
    • dEthnography
    ✓ Answer: (b) Experimental method
    Nomothetic versus idiographic approach
    The concept

    What it isThe nomothetic approach seeks general laws that hold across many people.

    Why this oneThe experiment is its purest method, testing cause and effect across groups of people.

    The oppositeIdiographic work studies particular cases in depth, as qualitative methods and ethnography do.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Qualitative methodStudies particular cases in depth, so it is idiographic, the opposite of nomothetic.
    • (c) Quasi-experimental methodQuasi-experiments are nomothetic too, but the weaker choice; the true experiment is the purest nomothetic method.
    • (d) EthnographyLike other qualitative methods, ethnography studies particular cases in depth, so it is idiographic.

  3. In which method does the researcher start with a theory and then test it with observations? (RML 2023)

    • aInductive reasoning
    • bDeductive reasoning
    • cAbductive reasoning
    • dHypothetico-deductive method
    ✓ Answer: (b) Deductive reasoning
    Deductive reasoning: theory to observation
    The concept

    How it worksDeduction runs top-down: from theory, to hypothesis, to observation.

    Why this oneThe stem starts with a theory and then tests it with observations, which is exactly that direction.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Inductive reasoningInduction runs the other way, from observations up to theory.
    • (c) Abductive reasoningAbduction infers the best explanation for a surprising fact; it does not start from a theory and test it.
    • (d) Hypothetico-deductive methodThe whole cycle of conjecture and test, not the direction of reasoning asked for; the trap is the word deductive in its name.

  4. Maximization of ____ variance, minimization of ____ variance, control of ____ variance. Select the appropriate option. (JEMAS 2025)

    • asecondary, true, error
    • btrue, primary, secondary
    • cprimary, error, secondary
    • dprimary, true, error
    ✓ Answer: (c) primary, error, secondary
    Kerlinger's MAXMINCON principle
    The concept

    The principleKerlinger's MAXMINCON principle: MAXimise one kind of variance, MINimise another, CONtrol a third.

    Each partMaximise primary (experimental) variance due to the IV, minimise error variance, and control secondary (extraneous) variance.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) secondary, true, errorScrambles the order and brings in true variance, which belongs to test theory, not to this principle.
    • (b) true, primary, secondaryUses true variance (a test-theory term) and would minimise primary variance, the very variance the experimenter wants to maximise.
    • (d) primary, true, errorStarts right, but true variance belongs to test theory, error variance is minimised rather than controlled, and secondary variance is left out.

  5. Which of the following can help control experimenter's bias? (RML 2022)

    • aDouble-blinding
    • bRandomly assigning subjects to groups
    • cUsing control groups
    • dDemand characteristics
    ✓ Answer: (a) Double-blinding
    Double-blind procedure against experimenter expectancy
    The concept

    How it worksIn a double-blind design, the experimenters who treat and assess participants do not know who is in which condition.

    Why it worksBecause they do not know, their expectations cannot shape the results, which removes experimenter bias.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Randomly assigning subjects to groupsRandom assignment equalises the groups, but the experimenter still knows who got what, so expectations can still shape results.
    • (c) Using control groupsA control group gives a comparison, but the experimenter still knows who got what.
    • (d) Demand characteristicsA source of bias (cues telling participants what the study expects of them), not a control for it.

  6. A researcher is interested in the impact of anxiety on performance. To manipulate anxiety, some subjects were given plain chips to eat while others were given barbecue flavoured chips to eat. The most obvious problem with this experiment is a lack of: (RML 2022)

    • aStatistical Validity
    • bEmpirical Validity
    • cInternal Validity
    • dConstruct Validity
    ✓ Answer: (d) Construct Validity
    Construct validity of the manipulation
    The concept

    What it isConstruct validity asks whether an operation really represents the construct it is named for.

    Why this oneChip flavour does not manipulate anxiety, so the operation does not represent the construct named.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Statistical ValidityConcerns whether the statistical conclusions drawn from the data are sound; it does not name this flaw.
    • (b) Empirical ValidityAn old name for a test's criterion-related validity, so it does not name this flaw.
    • (c) Internal ValidityInternal validity fails when a rival variable explains the result, and no rival variable does here; the flaw is the manipulation itself.

  7. A researcher wants to study the prevalence of test anxiety among all school students in India. This sampling technique is known as: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • cCluster Sampling
    • dPurposive Sampling
    ✓ Answer: (c) Cluster Sampling
    Multistage cluster sampling
    The concept

    What it isCluster sampling picks intact groups at random rather than individual people.

    Why this oneIntact groups (districts, then schools) are chosen at random at successive levels, which is multistage cluster sampling.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Stratified Random SamplingStratified sampling would draw individuals from every stratum, not intact groups chosen at random level by level.
    • (b) Quota SamplingInvolves no random selection at all; the researcher simply fills set quotas of each kind of person.
    • (d) Purposive SamplingInvolves no random selection at all; the researcher hand-picks cases by judgement.

  8. Which of the following sampling methods relies on the researcher's judgment to select participants who are "typical" or "representative" of the population, and does NOT allow for the calculation of sampling error? (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aSimple Random Sampling
    • bStratified Random Sampling
    • cQuota Sampling
    • dCluster Sampling
    ✓ Answer: (c) Quota Sampling
    Non-probability sampling: quota sampling
    The concept

    Why this oneIt is the only non-probability method offered, so its sampling error cannot be calculated; the other three are probability methods.

    Key noteStrictly, a judgement-based choice of typical cases describes purposive sampling, which would be the better answer if it were offered.

    Why the other options are not the answer

  10. In experimental design, the principle that ensures every participant has an equal chance of being assigned to any group is: (Real PYQ 2025)

    • aReplication
    • bLocal control
    • cRandomization
    • dHomogeneity
    ✓ Answer: (c) Randomization
    Ronald Fisher, The Design of Experiments (1935)
    The concept

    What it isRandomisation gives every participant an equal chance of assignment to any group.

    Who said soIt is one of Ronald Fisher's three principles (The Design of Experiments, 1935), alongside replication and local control.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) ReplicationOne of Fisher's other two principles: repeating the treatment across several units, not equal-chance assignment.
    • (b) Local controlFisher's third principle, also called blocking: grouping similar units together, not equal-chance assignment.
    • (d) HomogeneityNot one of Fisher's three principles at all.

  11. Researchers studying meditation contacted over 10000 randomly selected adults in 10 nations. The results showed that those who meditated on a regular basis were less likely to suffer from stress than adults who meditate less regularly or not at all. Can we conclude that meditation lowers stress? Why? (RML 2021)

    • aYes, because the sample size was large
    • bYes, because the adults were randomly selected
    • cYes, because the adults were randomly assigned
    • dNot necessarily, because this wasn't an experiment
    ✓ Answer: (d) Not necessarily, because this wasn't an experiment
    Correlation is not causation: no random assignment
    The concept

    What they didThe researchers only measured who already meditated; nobody was randomly assigned to meditate.

    Why it mattersSo meditators may differ in other ways that lower stress, and the study cannot show that meditation is the cause.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Yes, because the sample size was largeA large sample helps generalisation, not causal inference.
    • (b) Yes, because the adults were randomly selectedRandom selection helps generalisation, not causal inference; the trap is confusing it with random assignment.
    • (c) Yes, because the adults were randomly assignedDescribes something the study did not do: nobody was randomly assigned to meditate or not.

  12. Longitudinal and cross-sectional designs can be used to study how behaviour changes as a function of age. A disadvantage of cross-sectional studies is that the result can be influenced by : (IHBAS 2024)

    • aRandom assignment
    • bSocio-economic factors
    • cCohort difference
    • dParticipant attrition
    ✓ Answer: (c) Cohort difference
    Cohort effect in cross-sectional designs
    The concept

    How it worksA cross-sectional study compares different age groups at one time.

    The flawSo generational (cohort) differences can masquerade as age changes.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Random assignmentPlays no part in age comparisons, since people cannot be assigned to an age.
    • (b) Socio-economic factorsA general confound in many studies, not the characteristic flaw of the cross-sectional design.
    • (d) Participant attritionAttrition (drop-out over time) is the problem of longitudinal studies, which follow the same people; a cross-sectional study tests each person once.

  15. In a cohort study investigating the association between a new psychological intervention and relapse rates in schizophrenia, the risk ratio is calculated as 1.0. This finding indicates that: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aThe incidence of relapse in the exposed group is double that of the unexposed group.
    • bThe intervention has a significant protective effect against relapse.
    • cThere is no difference in the risk of relapse between the intervention and control groups.
    • dThe odds of exposure are equal in both the relapse and non-relapse groups.
    ✓ Answer: (c) There is no difference in the risk of relapse between the groups
    Risk ratio in a cohort study
    The concept

    What it isA risk ratio divides the risk in one group by the risk in the other.

    Reading itSo 1.0 means equal risks: no difference between the groups.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) The incidence of relapse in the exposed group is double that of the unexposed group.Double the incidence would give a risk ratio of 2.0, not 1.0.
    • (b) The intervention has a significant protective effect against relapse.A protective effect would show as a risk ratio below 1.0; 1.0 means no effect.
    • (d) The odds of exposure are equal in both the relapse and non-relapse groups.How a case-control study's odds ratio is phrased, not what a cohort's risk ratio measures.

  25. Type II error occurs : (RML 2022)

    • awhenever a true null hypothesis is accepted
    • bwhenever a false null hypothesis is rejected
    • cwhenever a false null hypothesis is accepted
    • dwhenever a true null hypothesis is rejected
    ✓ Answer: (c) whenever a false null hypothesis is accepted
    Type II error (β): a false negative
    The concept

    What it isA Type II error (β) misses a real effect: a false negative.

    In decision termsThe null hypothesis is false, but the test accepts it.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) whenever a true null hypothesis is acceptedA correct decision: the null really is true, so accepting it is right.
    • (b) whenever a false null hypothesis is rejectedA correct decision: the null is false, so rejecting it finds a real effect.
    • (d) whenever a true null hypothesis is rejectedRejecting a true null is a Type I error (α), a false positive.

    The concept

    What it saysWith N of about 30 or more, the distribution of sample means is near normal whatever the population's shape.

  27. What is the p-value in statistical testing? (IHBAS 2024)

    • aThe probability of the null hypothesis being true
    • bThe probability of obtaining the observed data if the null hypothesis is true
    • cThe effect size of the experiment
    • dThe power of the study
    ✓ Answer: (b) The probability of obtaining the observed data if the null hypothesis is true
    Definition of the p-value
    The concept

    What it isp is the probability of data at least this extreme assuming H0 is true.

    The trapIt is not the probability that H0 is true; that reverses the conditional.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) The probability of the null hypothesis being trueReverses the conditional: p assumes H0 is true, so it cannot give the probability that H0 is true.
    • (c) The effect size of the experimentEffect size is a separate quantity: how big an effect is, which p does not tell you.
    • (d) The power of the studyPower (1 − β) is a separate quantity: the probability of detecting a real effect.

    What it isMulticollinearity means the predictors are highly intercorrelated.

    The concept

    When to useA continuous variable paired with a true (natural) dichotomy calls for point-biserial correlation.

    Exam tipAsk whether the dichotomy is natural or cut from a continuous variable: natural means point-biserial, cut means biserial.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Pearson correlation coefficientFor two continuous variables.
    • (b) Spearman's rank correlation coefficientFor ranks, not a continuous variable paired with a dichotomy.
    • (d) Biserial correlation coefficientFor an artificial dichotomy cut from a continuous variable, not a true one.

High-yield sheet

The facts this chapter's questions ask most, most-asked first. Last read before the exam.

  1. A z-score, (X − M)/SD, states a score's distance from the mean in SD units (mean 0, SD 1) so scores from different distributions can be compared; on the normal curve z = 0, +1, +1.5 and +2 lie at about the 50th, 84th, 93rd and 98th percentiles, so deviation IQs of 85, 100 and 130 sit at the 16th, 50th and 98th. asked 12 times
  2. A Type I error (α) is rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive); a Type II error (β) is retaining, or accepting, a false null hypothesis (false negative). The significance level α is the probability of a Type I error, in ANOVA as in every test. asked 11 times
  3. Stevens (1946): nominal = unordered categories (marital status, car colour); ordinal = rank order (preference or agreement); interval = equal intervals with an arbitrary zero (Celsius); ratio = equal intervals with a true zero (height, number of books). asked 9 times
  4. One-way ANOVA compares the means of three or more independent groups on one IV with a continuous DV (it also works for two, where F = t squared); repeated t-tests would inflate the family-wise Type I error. asked 9 times
  5. The Mann-Whitney U test (Mann and Whitney, 1947; the same as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) is the non-parametric equivalent of the independent-samples t-test; Wilcoxon signed-rank partners the paired t, Kruskal-Wallis the one-way ANOVA and Friedman the repeated-measures ANOVA. asked 9 times
  6. The p-value is the probability of obtaining the observed result, or one more extreme, if H0 is true; it is not the probability that H0 is true. If p ≤ α (p = .03 with α = .05), reject H0. asked 8 times
  7. Chi-square (Karl Pearson, 1900) is a non-parametric test for nominal frequency data; the test of independence asks whether two categorical variables are associated, with df = (rows - 1)(columns - 1): 3 x 4 gives 6, 4 x 5 gives 12. asked 8 times
  8. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance and measures variability; it is affected by extreme scores (the IQR and quartile deviation are not), and multiplying every score by c multiplies the variance by c² while adding a constant changes nothing (variance 4, scores × 3 + 10: variance 36). asked 7 times
  9. Cluster sampling randomly selects whole natural groups (districts, schools) and studies all or some units inside them; districts, then schools, then students is multistage cluster sampling, whereas stratified random sampling samples randomly within every stratum. asked 6 times
  10. In non-probability sampling (convenience, purposive, quota, snowball) the chance of selection is unknown, randomisation is impossible and sampling error cannot be calculated; quota sampling, the commonest option-list answer, fills proportional quotas non-randomly. asked 6 times
  11. The normal curve is symmetrical and unimodal with mean = median = mode, and about 68, 95 and 99.7 per cent of cases lie within ±1, ±2 and ±3 SD of the mean; a bimodal curve is not normal. asked 6 times
  12. The median is the average for skewed data (community income, anxiety scores), the mode the only one for nominal data; range, SD and mean deviation are measures of variability, not central tendency. asked 6 times
  13. Cohen's d, the standardised difference between two means, is small at 0.2, medium at 0.5 and large at 0.8; for r the benchmarks are .10, .30 and .50, so r = −.30 is a medium negative relationship. asked 6 times
  14. F = MS between / MS within; MS within, the pooled error variance, is the denominator. F is about 1 when H0 is true, so an F below 1 (0.99) is never significant; F rises as chance variability falls, can never be negative, and the test is one-tailed. asked 6 times
  15. Post hoc tests (Tukey's HSD, Scheffé the most conservative, Newman-Keuls, Duncan, Fisher's LSD the least conservative) are used only after a significant F; k levels give k(k - 1)/2 pairs (4 levels give 6); the Bonferroni correction divides alpha by the number of comparisons to control family-wise Type I error. asked 6 times
  16. Induction moves from specific observations to broad generalisations and builds theory (the theory-development stage; typical of qualitative research); deduction starts with a theory and tests it against observations (typical of quantitative, experimental research). asked 5 times
  17. Degrees of freedom: one-sample t = N - 1 (N 20 gives 19); independent t = N1 + N2 - 2 (25 per group gives 48); one-way ANOVA within = N - k (3 groups of 10 give 27); randomised block error = (treatments - 1)(blocks - 1) (4 and 5 give 12). asked 5 times
  18. r = -0.85 is a strong negative relationship: the sign of r gives the direction and its size gives the strength. asked 5 times
  19. A double-blind study keeps both the participants and the experimenters who treat and assess them unaware of the conditions, so it controls both experimenter bias and participant expectancy effects. asked 4 times
  20. In probability sampling every element has a known, non-zero chance of selection, so sampling error can be calculated and the sample is likely, not guaranteed, to be representative. asked 4 times
  21. Only a true experiment (manipulation, control, random assignment) licenses cause-and-effect conclusions; correlational and observational studies cannot, however large or randomly selected the sample, because of the directionality and third-variable problems. asked 4 times
  22. NNT = 1/ARR, where the absolute risk reduction is the control event rate minus the treated event rate; an NNT of 8 means treating 8 patients for one additional patient to benefit. asked 4 times
  23. Sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN), the true positive rate; specificity = TN/(TN + FP). Sensitivity 90 percent and specificity 70 percent means the test correctly identifies 90 percent of ill and 70 percent of healthy people. asked 4 times
  24. Qualitative research is naturalistic, context-specific and inductive (bottom-up evidence), seeks meaning, process and the insider's view, and claims neither objective truth nor the researcher's authority; the survey is quantitative, whereas case study, ethnography and grounded theory are qualitative. asked 4 times
  25. Random assignment (randomisation) gives every participant an equal chance of being placed in any group, making groups equivalent on known and unknown variables so the main difference is the treatment; it controls selection bias and confounding and is not random sampling. asked 3 times
  26. The independent variable is the presumed cause, manipulated or selected (having heart disease in 'residents with heart disease die earlier'); the dependent variable is the measured outcome expected to change. asked 3 times
  27. Stratified random sampling divides the population into mutually exclusive strata and samples randomly within each; it is preferred when important subgroups must be represented and is more precise than simple random sampling when strata are internally homogeneous. asked 3 times
  28. A longitudinal design studies the same people repeatedly over time and is the best design for examining change over time; its problems are attrition, practice effects, cost and time. asked 3 times
  29. A relative risk or odds ratio of 1.0 means no association between exposure and outcome; above 1 marks a risk factor, below 1 a protective one. asked 3 times
  30. Intention-to-treat analysis analyses every participant in the group to which they were randomised, regardless of adherence, drop-out or crossover, and so preserves randomisation. asked 3 times
