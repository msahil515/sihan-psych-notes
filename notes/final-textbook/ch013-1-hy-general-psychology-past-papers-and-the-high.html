  1. Functionalism, an early school of thought in psychology, was most closely associated with which psychologist? (IHBAS 2024)

    • aEdward Titchener
    • bJohn Watson
    • cWilliam James
    • dG. Stanley Hall
    ✓ Answer: (c) William James
    William James, Principles of Psychology (1890)
    The concept

    The schoolFunctionalism asked what the mind is for: how mental processes help us adapt, not what they are made of.

    Who built itJames's Principles of Psychology (1890) inspired the question; Dewey and Angell made it a formal school at Chicago.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Edward TitchenerFounded structuralism, the rival school that broke the mind into its elements by introspection.
    • (b) John WatsonFounded behaviourism (1913), which dropped the mind and studied only behaviour.
    • (d) G. Stanley HallFunctionalist in spirit, but examined for institutions such as the first American research laboratory (1883) and the APA (1892).

  4. Which is the correct order of the last four of Patanjali's eight limbs? (NIMHANS 2026 recall)

    • aDharana, pratyahara, dhyana, samadhi
    • bPratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi
    • cPratyahara, dhyana, dharana, samadhi
    • dDhyana, dharana, pratyahara, samadhi
    ✓ Answer: (b) Pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi
    Patanjali's eight limbs of yoga
    The concept

    Turning inwardThe senses are first withdrawn inward (pratyahara), then the mind is fixed on one point (dharana).

    Going deeperThe fixing becomes an unbroken flow (dhyana) and finally absorption (samadhi).

    Exam tipEach wrong option swaps two of these steps; remember the order withdraw, fix, flow, absorb.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Dharana, pratyahara, dhyana, samadhiSwaps the first two: pratyahara (withdrawing the senses) must come before dharana (fixing the mind).
    • (c) Pratyahara, dhyana, dharana, samadhiSwaps the middle two: dharana (fixing on one point) must come before dhyana, its unbroken flow.
    • (d) Dhyana, dharana, pratyahara, samadhiSwaps the first and third: it starts with dhyana, but sense withdrawal (pratyahara) comes first.

  5. Which statement is INCORRECT? (Amity 2023)

    • aCones are most prevalent in the central retina
    • bCones are sensitive to moderate to high levels of light
    • cCones provide only monochromatic information
    • dCones provide excellent acuity
    ✓ Answer: (c) Cones provide only monochromatic information
    Rods versus cones in the retina
    The concept

    Why it is falseCones, with three pigments, give colour vision, so they are not monochromatic.

    Who is monochromaticIt is the rods, with one pigment (rhodopsin), that give colourless scotopic vision.

    Why the other options are not the answer
    • (a) Cones are most prevalent in the central retinaTrue: cones are packed in the fovea, at the centre of the retina.
    • (b) Cones are sensitive to moderate to high levels of lightTrue: cones work in moderate to bright light, while the rods handle dim light.
    • (d) Cones provide excellent acuityTrue: cones give excellent acuity, sharpest in the fovea where they are packed.

  6. In signal detection theory, the ability to distinguish a stimulus from background noise is: (IHBAS 2025)

    • aSensitivity (d′)
    • bCriterion (β)
    • cROC curve
    • dJND
    ✓ Answer: (a) Sensitivity (d′)
    Signal detection theory: sensitivity, d′
    The concept

    What it isd′ is the separation between the noise and signal-plus-noise distributions.

    Why this oneIt does not depend on the observer's willingness to say "yes", so it measures the ability to detect alone.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Criterion (β)The observer's willingness to say "yes" (response bias), not the ability to detect.
    • (c) ROC curveA graph that plots hit rate against false alarm rate, not the ability itself.
    • (d) JNDThe just noticeable difference belongs to classical psychophysics, not signal detection theory.

  7. Among monocular cues for depth perception is (JEMAS 2025)

    • aConvergence
    • bRetinal disparity
    • cAssimilation
    • dInterposition
    ✓ Answer: (d) Interposition
    Monocular depth cue: interposition (overlap)
    The concept

    What it isIn interposition, an object that partly hides another is seen as nearer.

    Why monocularOne eye is enough to see the overlap, so it is a monocular cue.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) ConvergenceA binocular cue (the eyes turn inward for near objects); convergence and retinal disparity are the only two binocular cues.
    • (b) Retinal disparityThe other binocular cue: each eye gets a slightly different image, so it needs both eyes.
    • (c) AssimilationNot a depth cue at all; it is Piaget's term for fitting new experience into existing schemas.

    • breal perceptual object perceived in a distorted way
    • cvivid mental images
    • dperception without an adequate external stimulus

  9. The Stroop effect demonstrates interference in (JEMAS 2025)

    • aLong-term memory retrieval
    • bWorking memory
    • cSelective attention
    • dProcedural memory
    ✓ Answer: (c) Selective attention
    Stroop effect: selective attention
    The concept

    How it worksWord reading is automatic, so it cannot be filtered out while naming the ink colour.

    Why this oneThe task therefore shows a failure of selective attention.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Long-term memory retrievalA memory distractor: no stored memory is being retrieved from long-term memory.
    • (b) Working memoryA memory distractor: nothing is being rehearsed in working memory; the clash is in attention.
    • (d) Procedural memoryA memory distractor: procedural memory is skill memory, and no stored skill is being retrieved here.

  10. According to Freud, the ___ mind includes accessible memories, which can be recalled at will (RML 2020)

    • aConscious
    • bPreconscious
    • cUnconscious
    • dPsychic determinism
    ✓ Answer: (b) Preconscious
    Freud's topographical model of the mind
    The concept

    What it isFreud's preconscious holds memories outside current awareness that can be recalled at will.

    The other levelsThe conscious holds only what is in awareness now; the unconscious cannot be recalled at will.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) ConsciousHolds only what is in awareness now, not stored memories waiting to be recalled.
    • (c) UnconsciousIts contents cannot be recalled at will, so it does not fit "accessible memories".
    • (d) Psychic determinismA principle (no mental event is accidental), not a level of mind.

  11. Systematic desensitization technique is based upon the idea of: (IHBAS 2025)

    • aReciprocal inhibition
    • bLaw of effect
    • cHabituation
    • dAdaptation to stress
    ✓ Answer: (a) Reciprocal inhibition
    Wolpe, reciprocal inhibition
    The concept

    How it worksWolpe paired each step of an anxiety hierarchy with relaxation.

    Why it worksRelaxation is a response incompatible with anxiety, so the anxiety bond weakens: reciprocal inhibition.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Law of effectThorndike's operant principle: behaviour followed by satisfaction is strengthened.
    • (c) HabituationUnderlies exposure and response prevention, which uses no competing response such as relaxation.
    • (d) Adaptation to stressBelongs to stress theory, not to the learning principle behind desensitization.

  12. Among the following schedules of reinforcement, which typically produces the highest rate of response and is most resistant to extinction? (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aFixed Ratio
    • bVariable Ratio
    • cFixed Interval
    • dVariable Interval
    ✓ Answer: (b) Variable Ratio
    Skinner, schedules of reinforcement
    The concept

    Why ratioRatio schedules reward fast responding: the more responses, the more reinforcers.

    Why variableUnpredictability removes any reason to pause and makes it hard to tell that reinforcement has stopped, as with slot machines.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Fixed RatioFast too, but shows a pause after each reinforcer and gives up sooner in extinction, since the end of reward is easier to spot.
    • (c) Fixed IntervalProduces a scallop: little responding just after a reinforcer, speeding up as the next one nears.
    • (d) Variable IntervalGives only a moderate, steady rate, because responding faster does not earn reinforcement sooner.

  13. The famous patient H.M. (Henry Molaison) underwent surgical removal of the medial temporal lobes, including the hippocampus, to treat severe epilepsy. While he retained most of his intellectual abilities and working memory, his primary and most profound deficit was in: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aProcedural memory
    • bSemantic memory
    • cAnterograde amnesia
    • dRetrograde amnesia
    ✓ Answer: (c) Anterograde amnesia
    Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), 1953 operation
    The concept

    What was lostAfter the 1953 operation H.M. could not form new declarative memories: a profound anterograde amnesia.

    What survivedHis intelligence, digit span and childhood memories survived.

    Why the other options are wrong

    • (b) Semantic memoryOld semantic knowledge was intact; his loss was in forming new memories.
    • (d) Retrograde amnesiaPresent but limited: his retrograde amnesia covered only the years before surgery.

  14. Which of the following terms refers to difficulty recalling information due to interference from new information? (IHBAS 2024)

    • aRetroactive interference
    • bProactive interference
    • cDecay theory
    • dRetrieval failure
    ✓ Answer: (a) Retroactive interference
    Interference theory of forgetting
    The concept

    What it isIn retroactive interference, new learning reaches back to disrupt older memories.

    Memory trickRETRO: new disrupts old, acting backwards in time.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Proactive interferenceThe reverse: old learning disrupts new learning.
    • (c) Decay theoryDecay blames the passage of time, not competition from new learning.
    • (d) Retrieval failureRetrieval failure blames a mismatch of cues, not competition from new learning.

  15. In which method does the researcher gather data, look for patterns, and then generate a theory or hypothesis? (RML 2023)

    • aDeductive reasoning
    • bHypothetico-deductive method
    • cInductive reasoning
    • dAbductive reasoning
    ✓ Answer: (c) Inductive reasoning
    Inductive reasoning: data first, theory last
    The concept

    What it isInduction runs from specific observations to a probable generalisation.

    Why this oneData come first and theory last, as in most qualitative research.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Deductive reasoningDeduction runs the other way, from a theory to a tested prediction.
    • (b) Hypothetico-deductive methodAlso runs from a theory to a tested prediction: form a hypothesis, deduce a result, then test it.
    • (d) Abductive reasoningAbduction infers the best explanation of a surprising fact, rather than generalising from patterns.

High-yield sheet

The facts this chapter's questions ask most, most-asked first. Last read before the exam.

  1. Systematic desensitisation (Joseph Wolpe, Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition, 1958) rests on reciprocal inhibition: relaxation, a response incompatible with anxiety, is paired step by step with a graded anxiety hierarchy. asked 17 times
  2. Working memory (Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch, 1974) has four components: the central executive (an attentional controller that stores nothing, loaded by random number generation), the phonological loop (auditory-verbal information), the visuospatial sketchpad (visual and spatial information, including mental rotation) and the episodic buffer, added in 2000, which binds information into coherent episodes. asked 11 times
  3. The token economy is operant conditioning with tokens as secondary reinforcers, developed by Teodoro Ayllon and Nathan Azrin (The Token Economy, 1968); it is the listed therapy not derived from classical conditioning. asked 10 times
  4. Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943, 1954), bottom to top: physiological, safety, belongingness and love (social), esteem, self-actualisation. Physiological and safety needs are the lower needs; ego is not a level. asked 10 times
  5. Korsakoff's syndrome follows thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency in chronic alcoholism, often after Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, nystagmus); it damages the mammillary bodies and mediodorsal thalamus and causes dense anterograde amnesia, extensive retrograde amnesia and confabulation, sincere false stories that fill memory gaps. asked 8 times
  6. The empty chair technique belongs to Gestalt therapy (Fritz Perls, with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman), an experiential here-and-now therapy that is not the Gestalt psychology of Wertheimer, Kohler and Koffka. asked 7 times
  7. Variable ratio reinforcement gives the highest, steadiest rate and the greatest resistance to extinction; fixed ratio gives high rates with a post-reinforcement pause, fixed interval the scallop, variable interval moderate steady rates, and continuous reinforcement the fastest learning and fastest extinction. asked 7 times
  8. Extinction is the fading of a conditioned response when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS, or of an operant response that is no longer reinforced; a parent who ignores tantrums is using extinction, not operant escape. asked 7 times
  9. Reinforcement always increases behaviour and punishment decreases it; positive adds and negative removes a stimulus, so switching off a shock is negative reinforcement, while time-out and response cost are negative punishment. asked 7 times
  10. H.M. (Henry Molaison) had both medial temporal lobes, including the hippocampus and amygdala, removed by William Beecher Scoville in 1953; Scoville and Brenda Milner (1957) reported a profound anterograde amnesia for declarative memory, with normal short-term memory, limited retrograde amnesia and spared procedural learning (mirror tracing). asked 7 times
  11. Raymond Cattell (with John Horn) split g into fluid intelligence (Gf), reasoning with novel problems independently of acquired knowledge and measured best by Raven's Progressive Matrices, and crystallised intelligence (Gc), the repertoire of information, cognitive skills and strategies, measured by vocabulary and information. asked 7 times
  12. The limbic system is the classic "seat of emotion": Paul MacLean named it in 1952, enlarging James Papez's 1937 circuit, and it regulates emotion, motivation and memory; hunger and thirst belong to the hypothalamus. asked 7 times
  13. The amygdala is the structure for fear and aggression: it detects threat, drives fear conditioning and gives emotional events their extra memorability, while the hippocampus stores the facts. asked 7 times
  14. The hippocampus is the structure damaged in anterograde amnesia and is critical for consolidating new declarative memories; it is not the store of remote memories. asked 6 times
  15. Narendra Nath Sengupta set up India's first psychology laboratory (1915) and first Department of Psychology (1916) at the University of Calcutta and is the father of modern psychology in India; keys naming Bose or Uma Chaudhuri for the first laboratory are wrong. asked 5 times
  16. Humanistic psychology (Maslow, Rogers) is the third force, after psychoanalysis (first) and behaviourism (second); transpersonal psychology is the fourth force. asked 5 times
  17. The Stroop effect (John Ridley Stroop, 1935): naming the ink colour of a conflicting colour word (RED printed in green) is slowed because automatic word reading interferes; it shows a limit of selective attention (interference in reaction time), not a memory effect. asked 5 times
  18. Proactive interference: earlier learning disrupts newer learning (dialling your old phone number); retroactive interference: newer learning disrupts older memories. RML 2024 keys English grammar intruding on newly learned Spanish as retroactive, but it is proactive. asked 5 times
  19. Inductive reasoning moves from specific observations to probable generalisations (gather data, find patterns, build theory: the logic of theory development and qualitative research); deductive reasoning starts from a theory and tests its predictions against observations. asked 5 times
  20. Graham Wallas (The Art of Thought, 1926): preparation, incubation, illumination, verification; incubation (setting the problem aside) is second and illumination (the insight) third. asked 5 times
  21. Functional fixedness (Karl Duncker's candle problem, 1945) is seeing an object only in its usual use; mental set (Abraham Luchins's water jars, 1942) is reusing a strategy that worked before, and was keyed as the most basic and common obstacle to problem solving. asked 5 times
  22. Henry Murray's need-press theory (1938) underlies the Thematic Apperception Test (Morgan and Murray, 1935), a projective test whose stories reveal the teller's needs, conflicts and motives. asked 5 times
  23. Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology laboratory at Leipzig in 1879 (not Vienna, Paris or Cambridge); Galton's London laboratory (1884) was anthropometric. asked 4 times
  24. Patanjali's eight limbs, in order: yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara (withdrawal of the senses), dharana (concentration on one point), dhyana (unbroken meditation), samadhi (absorption); karma and jnana are not limbs. asked 4 times
  25. Tamas is the guna of inertia, lethargy, darkness, dullness and ignorance; a person who has stopped eating and cannot get herself to act shows tamas alone. asked 4 times
  26. Every sense except smell relays through the thalamus on its way to the cortex (vision via the lateral geniculate nucleus, hearing via the medial geniculate nucleus); the olfactory nerve (cranial nerve I) bypasses it. asked 4 times
  27. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus is the master circadian pacemaker, entrained by light through the retinohypothalamic tract; SCN lesions abolish circadian rhythms, not total sleep. asked 4 times
  28. REM (paradoxical) sleep: a desynchronised, fast, low-voltage EEG like waking, rapid eye movements, atonia of the core muscles, genital arousal and vivid dreams; EEG synchrony and delta waves belong to N3, spindles and K-complexes to N2. asked 4 times
  29. Carl Jung split the unconscious into a personal (individual) unconscious and the collective unconscious, an inherited layer of archetypes shared by all humanity, which he explained by the similar brain structure of all human races. asked 4 times
  30. William Stern coined the intelligence quotient in 1912; Lewis Terman's Stanford-Binet (1916) multiplied it by 100, giving the ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100, so a mental age of 8 at age 10 gives an IQ of 80. asked 4 times
