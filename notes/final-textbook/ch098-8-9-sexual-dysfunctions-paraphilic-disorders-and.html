Few corners of psychiatry have been redrawn as often. A man who ejaculates too soon, a person aroused by shoes, a child who insists she is a boy and a contented gay man in 1970 have all been diagnosed; only some would be today. We start with normal sexual response, take the three DSM-5-TR groups in turn, and end with homosexuality's exit from the manuals.

The sexual response cycle and the language of gender

Disorder is judged against the normal picture drawn by the gynaecologist William Howell Masters (1915 to 2001) and his research partner Virginia Eshelman Johnson (1925 to 2013). In Human Sexual Response (1966), from laboratory recordings of nearly 700 volunteers, they described a four-phase sexual response cycle. In excitement, blood engorges the genitals, producing erection or vaginal lubrication. In plateau, arousal levels off just before climax. Orgasm is a peak of rhythmic muscular contractions, with ejaculation in men. In resolution the body returns to rest, and men pass through a refractory period in which another orgasm is impossible; many women have none and can have multiple orgasms.

Helen Singer Kaplan (1929 to 1995) saw patients whose bodies worked but who did not want sex, and in Disorders of Sexual Desire (1979) put a desire phase first (desire, excitement, orgasm), giving the linear sequence of Figure 8.12; dysfunctions are still sorted by the phase they disturb. Rosemary Basson argued in 2000 that this fits many women in long relationships poorly. In her circular model a woman often starts from sexual neutrality, is receptive for the sake of intimacy, is aroused by sexual stimuli and only then feels responsive desire; satisfaction feeds back into intimacy. DSM-5 merged women's desire and arousal disorders partly for this reason.

Four terms must be kept apart. Sex is biological (chromosomes, gonads, hormones, genitals); gender identity is the inner sense of being a man, a woman or another gender; gender role is the behaviour a culture expects of each gender; sexual orientation is the gender of those one is attracted to. Androgyny, in the 1974 work of Sandra Lipsitz Bem (1944 to 2014) with her Bem Sex Role Inventory, means scoring high on both masculine and feminine traits; it describes a flexible personality, not a disorder.

Sexual dysfunctions

A sexual dysfunction is a clinically significant disturbance in the ability to respond sexually or to experience sexual pleasure. Most of DSM-5-TR's eight share one template: the problem occurs on almost all occasions (about 75 to 100 percent), has lasted at least about 6 months, causes clinically significant distress, and is not better explained by another mental disorder, severe relationship distress, a substance or a medical condition. Specifiers give the course: lifelong (present since sexual activity began) or acquired (after a period of normal function), and generalised or situational (only with certain partners, stimulation or situations). Vaginismus is involuntary tightening of the vaginal muscles that makes penetration painful or impossible; dyspareunia is pain during intercourse. Erectile disorder has the strongest medical ties: diabetes, vascular disease, smoking and age. The substance/medication-induced form follows drugs: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) most often delay orgasm or ejaculation, and antipsychotics, antihypertensives and chronic alcohol use are frequent culprits. Masters and Johnson (Human Sexual Inadequacy, 1970) described performance anxiety, fear of failing, and spectatoring, mentally watching and judging one's own performance, so that attention leaves erotic sensation and one failure breeds the next. Relationship conflict, past abuse, depression and religious prohibitions add to the picture. Premature ejaculation is the dysfunction most tied to anxiety, and is commoner in men with anxiety disorders, especially social anxiety disorder.

Treatment is in Section 7.7; here, match tool to problem. The stop-start technique of James Semans (1956), halting stimulation just before the point of no return, and Masters and Johnson's squeeze technique, firm pressure where the head of the penis meets the shaft, teach ejaculatory control. Sensate focus, graded non-demanding touch with intercourse at first forbidden, removes performance anxiety in many dysfunctions. Vaginismus is treated with relaxation and graded dilators, erectile disorder often with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil.

Exam trap

Stop-start and squeeze treat premature ejaculation, not erectile disorder, inhibited male orgasm (delayed ejaculation, the opposite problem) or vaginismus. Premature ejaculation is most commonly associated with an anxiety state: masturbation does not cause it (a myth that also fuels dhat syndrome, Section 8.1), diabetes is the classic link of erectile disorder, and personality disorder has no special tie.

Paraphilias and paraphilic disorders

DSM-5-TR calls any intense and persistent sexual interest other than in genital stimulation or fondling with physically mature, consenting human partners a paraphilia. The key DSM-5 move was to separate interest from disorder. A paraphilic disorder is a paraphilia that currently causes distress or impairment, or whose satisfaction has involved harm, or risk of harm, to others. Criterion A gives the interest, recurrent and intense for at least 6 months in fantasies, urges or behaviours; Criterion B the consequences. A shoe fetish that troubles no one is a paraphilia but not a disorder; exposing oneself to strangers meets Criterion B even without distress.

The eight named disorders (Table 8.27) fall into two groups. Anomalous activity preferences are the courtship disorders, distorted pieces of courtship (looking, showing, touching), and the algolagnic disorders, which involve pain. Anomalous target preferences are pedophilic, fetishistic and transvestic disorder. Most sets allow the specifiers in a controlled environment (such as prison) and in full remission (no acting on urges and no distress for 5 years outside such settings).

The fetish object is non-living (shoes, rubber, undergarments) or a specific non-genital body part such as feet, a focus called partialism. Other paraphilias go under other specified paraphilic disorder: necrophilia (corpses), zoophilia (animals), coprophilia (faeces), urophilia (urine), klismaphilia (enemas) and telephone scatologia (obscene calls). Hypoxyphilia, arousal from restricted breathing, is the 'with asphyxiophilia' specifier of sexual masochism disorder and can kill by accident.

Ray Blanchard coined autogynephilia in 1989: a male's recurrent, intense sexual arousal by the thought or image of himself as a woman. It anchors his controversial typology of male-to-female gender dysphoria, and in men with transvestic disorder it raises the likelihood of gender dysphoria.

Exam trap

Autogynephilia is arousal by the image of oneself as a woman; arousal by prepubescent children is pedophilia, by being humiliated is masochism, and mere arousal by oneself is not autogynephilia. An inanimate object means fetishism: necrophilia needs a corpse, frotteurism a non-consenting person, voyeurism an unsuspecting one.

Why do paraphilias form? Learning theorists cite classical conditioning: Stanley Rachman (1966) conditioned mild arousal to a picture of women's boots in the laboratory. John Money (1921 to 2006) proposed the lovemap, a childhood template of the ideal lover and erotic scene that early experience can distort. Kurt Freund (1914 to 1996), in his courtship disorder hypothesis, saw voyeurism, exhibitionism, frotteurism and preferential rape as disturbances of successive courtship phases (searching, pre-touch interaction, touching, intercourse), which is why they cluster.

Gender dysphoria and gender incongruence

Gender dysphoria in DSM-5-TR is a marked incongruence between one's experienced or expressed gender and one's assigned gender, lasting at least 6 months and causing clinically significant distress or impairment. A child needs six of eight indicators, one of which must be a strong desire to be, or insistence that one is, the other gender; others include cross-gender clothing, play and playmates and dislike of one's anatomy. An adolescent or adult needs two of six, such as a strong desire to be rid of one's sex characteristics or to be treated as the other gender. Specifiers: 'with a disorder/difference of sex development' and, for adults, 'posttransition'.

DSM-III (1980) introduced transsexualism for adults (a historic term for a person seeking social and often bodily transition to the other sex) and gender identity disorder of childhood. DSM-IV (1994) merged them into gender identity disorder, a condition in which one's experienced gender identity does not match one's assigned sex. That made the identity itself the disorder, so DSM-5 (2013) renamed it gender dysphoria, moving the focus from identity to distress. ICD-10 listed transsexualism (F64.0), dual-role transvestism (F64.1) and gender identity disorder of childhood (F64.2) under F64.

ICD-11, in force from 1 January 2022, renamed the condition gender incongruence, of adolescence or adulthood and of childhood (about 2 years' persistence in children), and moved it out of the mental disorders chapter into a new Chapter 17, Conditions related to sexual health: being transgender is not a mental illness, but a code lets people obtain gender-affirming care. The sexual dysfunctions and sexual pain disorders moved there too. The paraphilic disorders stayed in Chapter 6, now built around non-consent: exhibitionistic, voyeuristic, pedophilic, coercive sexual sadism disorder and frotteuristic, plus other paraphilic disorder involving non-consenting individuals and paraphilic disorder involving solitary or consenting individuals with marked distress or risk. Consensual fetishism, fetishistic transvestism and sadomasochism were removed (Table 8.28). DSM-5's gender dysphoria corresponds to ICD-11's gender incongruence.

Exam trap

ICD-11 gender incongruence is in Chapter 17, Conditions related to sexual health: not Chapter 6, not Chapter 5 (endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases), and not deleted. Only the paraphilic disorders stayed in Chapter 6. The same mismatch is gender identity disorder in DSM-IV and ICD-10 and gender dysphoria in DSM-5.

Gender identity is distinct from a paraphilia (what arouses a person), a sexual dysfunction (the response cycle) and androgyny (a blend of traits). Practice today is gender-affirmative, supporting identity rather than trying to change it (Chapter 7).

Homosexuality: pathologised, then depathologised

Pathologisation is classifying a behaviour or identity as a mental disorder; depathologisation is removing that classification (not changing orientation, the aim of discredited conversion therapies, nor coming out). Homosexuality is the clearest case. DSM-I (1952) placed it under sociopathic personality disturbance as a sexual deviation; DSM-II (1968) kept it among the sexual deviations. Alfred Charles Kinsey (1894 to 1956), in Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948), rated behaviour on the Kinsey scale from 0 (exclusively heterosexual) to 6 (exclusively homosexual), showing same-sex experience to be common and graded.

Key study: Hooker's blind judges (1957)

Evelyn Hooker (1907 to 1996) asked whether homosexual men are more maladjusted. She matched 30 homosexual and 30 heterosexual men, none in therapy, for age, IQ and education and gave them projective tests, including the Rorschach. Expert judges blind to group could not tell them apart beyond chance, and rated their adjustment alike. Criticism: a small, non-random sample; but earlier claims had rested on patients in treatment.

With this evidence and activist pressure, the board of the American Psychiatric Association voted in December 1973 to remove homosexuality. The seventh printing of DSM-II (1974) substituted sexual orientation disturbance for people distressed by their orientation; DSM-III (1980) renamed it ego-dystonic homosexuality, and DSM-III-R (1987) dropped it. ICD-10 (endorsed in 1990) no longer listed homosexuality, stating that orientation by itself is not a disorder, but kept F66, including egodystonic sexual orientation; ICD-11 deleted F66.

Indian context

In 2018 the Indian Psychiatric Society stated that homosexuality is not a psychiatric disorder. On 6 September 2018 the Supreme Court, in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, decriminalising consensual sex between adults of the same sex.
