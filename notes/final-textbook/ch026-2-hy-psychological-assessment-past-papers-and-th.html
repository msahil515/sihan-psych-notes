  1. The primary difference between a "speed test" and a "power test" in psychological assessment is that a speed test: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aHas items of increasing difficulty, while a power test has items of uniform difficulty.
    • bHas a strict time limit that prevents most test-takers from completing all items, while a power test has items so difficult that no one can answer them all correctly.
    • cMeasures reaction time, while a power measures cognitive ability.
    • dIs administered individually, while a power test can be administered in groups.
    ✓ Answer: (b) Has a strict time limit that prevents most test-takers from completing all items, while a power test has items so difficult that no one can answer them all correctly.
    Speed test versus power test distinction
    The concept

    Speed testA speed test uses easy items under a time limit so strict that few finish.

    Power testA power test allows generous time, but its items grow so hard that no one passes them all.

    Exam tipSpeed: easy items, tight time limit. Power: hard items, ample time.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Has items of increasing difficulty, while a power test has items of uniform difficulty.Reverses the two: items growing harder is the mark of a power test, while a speed test uses easy items.
    • (c) Measures reaction time, while a power measures cognitive ability.Confuses a timed test with reaction time; a speed test counts items finished under a time limit.
    • (d) Is administered individually, while a power test can be administered in groups.Confuses timing with individual versus group administration, which is a separate way of classifying tests.

  2. Which of the following is the most important characteristic of a psychological test? (RML 2024)

    • aObjectivity
    • bSubjectivity
    • cValidity
    • dRationality
    ✓ Answer (as keyed): (a) Objectivity
    A. K. Singh and other Indian texts (RML key)
    The concept

    Why this oneRML follows Indian texts such as A. K. Singh, which list objectivity first.

    What it meansObjectivity means a score must not depend on who gives or scores the test.

    Other papersMost textbooks and the 2014 Standards call validity the most fundamental property, so on other papers choose (c).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) SubjectivityA defect, not a virtue: subjectivity lets the scorer's judgement change the score, the opposite of objectivity.
    • (c) ValidityThe answer most textbooks and the 2014 Standards give; choose it on other papers, but RML keys objectivity.
    • (d) RationalityNot a test characteristic at all; rationality does not appear in lists of test properties.

  3. Which of the following refers to the notion of the confirmatory bias? (RML 2021)

    • aClinicians ignore information that does not support their hypothesis
    • bClinicians ignore information that does not support their stereotypes.
    • cClinicians interpret ambiguous information as supporting their hypothesis.
    • dAll of the above
    ✓ Answer: (d) All of the above
    Confirmatory bias in clinical judgement
    The concept

    What it isConfirmatory bias is the tendency to notice, weigh and interpret information so that it confirms what one already believes.

    Why all threeIgnoring contrary evidence, ignoring evidence against one's stereotypes and reading ambiguity as support are all forms of it.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Clinicians ignore information that does not support their hypothesisTrue: ignoring contrary evidence is one form of the bias, but only one of three.
    • (b) Clinicians ignore information that does not support their stereotypes.True: ignoring evidence against one's stereotypes is the same bias applied to beliefs about groups; only part of the answer.
    • (c) Clinicians interpret ambiguous information as supporting their hypothesis.True: reading ambiguity as support is the interpretive side of the bias; only part of the answer.

  4. The improvement in anxiety may sometimes spuriously improve depression. This is called: (RML 2024)

    • aHalo effect
    • bCeiling effect
    • cHawthorne effect
    • dTherapeutic effect
    ✓ Answer: (a) Halo effect
    Halo effect in rating
    The concept

    What it isIn the halo effect, one attribute colours the rating of another.

    HereThe improved anxiety colours the depression rating, so the depression rating improves although the depression has not.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Ceiling effectA ceiling effect is a test too easy to register further gains, not one rating colouring another.
    • (c) Hawthorne effectThe Hawthorne effect is change because people know they are being studied.
    • (d) Therapeutic effectA therapeutic effect is real improvement from treatment; here the depression has not actually improved.

  5. A response style or bias, in which there is tendency to agree with test items regardless of their contents is called: (RML 2024)

    • aHalo effect
    • bCentral tendency effect
    • cSocial desirability effect
    • dAcquiescence effect
    ✓ Answer: (d) Acquiescence effect
    Acquiescence response style (yea-saying)
    The concept

    What it isAcquiescence (yea-saying) is agreeing with items regardless of their content.

    How it is controlledIt is controlled by balanced keying: some items are worded so that agreeing scores in the opposite direction.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Halo effectHalo is a rater's error, not a test-taker's response style.
    • (b) Central tendency effectCentral tendency is choosing the middle of the scale, not agreeing with everything.
    • (c) Social desirability effectSocial desirability depends on content: choosing the answer that looks good.

  7. Which of the following is assessed by Cronbach's alpha? (RML 2021)

    • aConcurrent validity
    • bInter-rater reliability
    • cTest retest reliability
    • dInternal consistency
    ✓ Answer: (d) Internal consistency
    Lee Cronbach, coefficient alpha (1951)
    The concept

    What it isAlpha (Lee Cronbach, 1951) estimates how well a scale's items hang together.

    Key pointIt needs only one administration, which is what makes it a measure of internal consistency.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Concurrent validityConcurrent validity needs an outside criterion, not agreement among a scale's own items.
    • (b) Inter-rater reliabilityInter-rater reliability needs two scorers and is indexed by kappa or the intraclass correlation.
    • (c) Test retest reliabilityTest-retest reliability needs two occasions, whereas alpha uses one administration.

  8. A test developer administers a new anxiety scale to a group of college students and, two weeks later, administers the same scale again to the same group. She then correlates the two sets of scores. This process is used to establish: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aSplit-half reliability
    • bInter-rater reliability
    • cTest-retest reliability
    • dParallel forms reliability
    ✓ Answer: (c) Test-retest reliability
    Test-retest reliability, coefficient of stability
    The concept

    The designSame scale, same people, two occasions, scores correlated.

    What it givesThat correlation is the coefficient of stability, the index of test-retest reliability.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Split-half reliabilitySplit-half correlates two halves of one sitting, not two occasions.
    • (b) Inter-rater reliabilityInter-rater reliability compares scorers, not occasions.
    • (d) Parallel forms reliabilityParallel forms would need a second, equivalent version of the scale; here the same scale was repeated.

  9. Odd-even method of splitting the test to estimate split-half reliability is MOST appropriate for : (RML 2022)

    • aProjective tests
    • bNeuropsychological tests
    • cPower tests
    • dSpeed tests
    ✓ Answer: (c) Power tests
    Odd-even split-half method
    The concept

    Why power testsIn a power test items are graded and time is generous, so odd and even halves are fair parallel samples.

    Why not speed testsOn a speed test the halves match by construction and the coefficient is spuriously high.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Projective testsProjective tests lack comparable, objectively scored items to split into halves.
    • (b) Neuropsychological testsMany neuropsychological tests lack comparable, objectively scored items to split.
    • (d) Speed testsThe trap: on a speed test odd and even halves match by construction, so the coefficient is spuriously high.

  10. To determine whether a newly developed intelligence test truly accurately measures intelligence or not, it is necessary to administer the test with one that has been in the field for some time. Which of the following needs to be looked for? (JEMAS 2025)

    • aConstruct validity
    • bConcurrent validity
    • cContent validity
    • dPredictive validity
    ✓ Answer: (b) Concurrent validity
    Criterion-related validity, concurrent type
    The concept

    What it isCorrelating a new test with an established test given at about the same time is concurrent validity.

    Where it fitsIt is one type of criterion-related validity; the established test serves as the criterion.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Construct validityConstruct validity rests on a network of evidence rather than one established test.
    • (c) Content validityContent validity concerns sampling of the domain, not agreement with another test.
    • (d) Predictive validityPredictive validity needs a criterion measured later, not a test given at the same time.

  11. Which of the following statements is TRUE? (RML 2024)

    • aA test can be valid without being reliable
    • bA test can be reliable without being valid
    • cIf a test measures what it is intended to measure, then by definition it is reliable
    • dIf a test gives consistent scores when administered repeatedly then it is valid
    ✓ Answer: (b) A test can be reliable without being valid
    Reliability versus validity
    The concept

    Why this oneA test can be reliable without being valid: it can consistently measure the wrong thing.

    The ruleA test cannot be valid without being reliable, so reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) A test can be valid without being reliableThe reverse of the rule: a test cannot be valid without being reliable.
    • (c) If a test measures what it is intended to measure, then by definition it is reliableOverstates the link: a valid test must be reliable, but this is not true by definition.
    • (d) If a test gives consistent scores when administered repeatedly then it is validConsistency alone never proves validity; a test can consistently measure the wrong thing.

  12. A high false positive rate in diagnosis means : (IHBAS 2025)

    • aDiagnosis is too strict
    • bNormal people are diagnosed as disordered
    • cDiagnosis has low reliability
    • dDisordered people go undetected
    ✓ Answer: (b) Normal people are diagnosed as disordered
    False positives and specificity
    The concept

    What it isA false positive is a positive call on someone without the condition: a normal person diagnosed as disordered.

    Key linkA high false positive rate is the mark of low specificity.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Diagnosis is too strictAn over-strict diagnosis produces false negatives, the opposite problem.
    • (c) Diagnosis has low reliabilityReliability is a different property: the consistency of a diagnosis, not its error type.
    • (d) Disordered people go undetectedUndetected disordered people are false negatives, not false positives.

  13. Which statistical measure tells us how far individual data points are dispersed from the mean expressed in standard deviation units? (Real PYQ 2025)

    • aVariance
    • bZ-score (Standard score)
    • cCoefficient of variation
    • dStandard error
    ✓ Answer: (b) Z-score (Standard score)
    Standard score, z = (X − M) ÷ SD
    The concept

    The formulaz = (X − M) ÷ SD states how many SDs one score lies from the mean.

    Why it is usefulWith every score in standard deviation units, scores from different distributions can be compared.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) VarianceVariance (SD squared) describes the spread of a whole distribution, not where one score lies.
    • (c) Coefficient of variationThe coefficient of variation compares spread across distributions, not one score's distance from the mean.
    • (d) Standard errorThe standard error is the SD of a sampling distribution, not of individual scores.

  14. IQ if any body has a mental age of 8 and a chronological age of 10 is: (RML 2020)

    • a108
    • b125
    • c80
    • d92
    ✓ Answer: (c) 80
    Ratio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100
    The concept

    The formulaRatio IQ = (MA ÷ CA) × 100 = (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80.

    Sense checkA mental age below the chronological age must give an IQ below 100.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) 108Above 100, which is impossible when mental age (8) is below chronological age (10).
    • (b) 125The tempting trap: it inverts the ratio, (CA ÷ MA) × 100 = (10 ÷ 8) × 100.
    • (d) 92Below 100, but not what the formula gives: (8 ÷ 10) × 100 is exactly 80.

  15. Which of the following is a non-linear transformation of raw scores? (Recall set)

    • az-score
    • b50 + 10z
    • cStanine
    • dDeviation score (X − M)
    ✓ Answer: (c) Stanine
    Stanines, a normalising area transformation
    The concept

    What a stanine isStanines are assigned from percentile ranks in fixed proportions: 4, 7, 12, 17, 20, 17, 12, 7, 4 per cent.

    Why non-linearThis is a normalising, area transformation that forces a normal shape on the scores.

    The contrastz, the T score 50 + 10z and the deviation score X − M all apply one multiply-and-add rule to every score, so they are linear and keep the raw distribution's shape.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) z-scoreLinear: the z-score applies one multiply-and-add rule to every score, so it keeps the raw distribution's shape.
    • (b) 50 + 10zThe T score, a linear rescaling of z, so it keeps the raw distribution's shape.
    • (d) Deviation score (X − M)Linear: subtracting the mean shifts every score by the same amount and keeps the shape.

  16. In the WAIS-IV (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale - Fourth Edition), how many core subtests are there in the Verbal Comprehension Index and the Perceptual Reasoning Index respectively? (Real PYQ 2025)

    • a3 verbal, 4 performance
    • b3 verbal, 3 performance
    • c4 verbal, 3 performance
    • d2 verbal, 4 performance
    ✓ Answer: (b) 3 verbal, 3 performance
    Wechsler, WAIS-IV core subtests
    The concept

    VCI coreThe Verbal Comprehension Index core is Similarities, Vocabulary and Information; Comprehension is supplemental.

    PRI coreThe Perceptual Reasoning Index core is Block Design, Matrix Reasoning and Visual Puzzles; Figure Weights and Picture Completion are supplemental.

    Exam tipCounting a supplemental subtest as core produces the tempting 4.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) 3 verbal, 4 performanceCounts a supplemental PRI subtest (Figure Weights or Picture Completion) as core; the PRI core is three.
    • (c) 4 verbal, 3 performanceCounts Comprehension, a supplemental VCI subtest, as core; the VCI core is three.
    • (d) 2 verbal, 4 performanceUndercounts the VCI and overcounts the PRI; each index has three core subtests.

  18. An IQ test does NOT provide which of the following? (RML 2021)

    • aHigh test retest reliability
    • bGood predictor of behaviour
    • cHigh internal consistency
    • dGood validity
    ✓ Answer: (b) Good predictor of behaviour
    Psychometric strengths and limits of IQ tests
    The concept

    Why this oneIQ tests predict school achievement well, but how a person behaves in general depends on personality, motivation and circumstance.

    Their strengthsIQ tests do have high test-retest reliability, high internal consistency and good validity.

    Why the other options are not the answer
    • (a) High test retest reliabilityTrue: IQ scores are stable on retesting, so IQ tests have high test-retest reliability.

  33. Impact of psychiatric illness on one's cognitive and intellectual abilities can be estimated on the basis of: (IHBAS 2020)

    The concept

    How it worksWechsler compared 'hold' subtests (Vocabulary, Information, Picture Completion, Object Assembly) with 'don't hold' subtests (Digit Span, Similarities, Digit Symbol, Block Design).

  1. Cronbach's alpha (Lee Cronbach, 1951) measures internal consistency reliability: not validity of any kind, not test-retest and not inter-rater reliability. asked 10 times
  2. The Spearman-Brown prophecy formula (Spearman and Brown, 1910) steps a split-half correlation up to full length, 2r / (1 + r), so .60 becomes .75; in general form kr / [1 + (k - 1)r] it shows that adding parallel items raises reliability. asked 9 times
  3. Test-retest reliability correlates the same test given to the same people on two occasions (the coefficient of stability); it suits stable traits such as intelligence and personality, not changing states such as current mood. asked 9 times
  4. Criterion-related validity has two types: concurrent (test and criterion measured at about the same time, such as a new intelligence test given with an established one) and predictive (criterion measured later, such as entrance scores and later grades). asked 9 times
  5. The Thematic Apperception Test was developed by Christiana Morgan and Henry Murray (1935, Harvard Psychological Clinic), rests on Murray's need-press theory, and is a projective construction technique in which the person tells stories about ambiguous pictures of social scenes. asked 9 times
  6. The three classic MMPI validity scales are L (Lie: naive denial of common minor faults), F (Infrequency: random responding, faking bad or severe pathology; T above 100 suggests random responding or gross exaggeration) and K (Correction: subtle defensiveness; a fraction of K is added to scales 1, 4, 7, 8 and 9). asked 8 times
  7. KR-20 (Kuder and Richardson, 1937) is for dichotomously scored items of varying difficulty; KR-21 assumes equal difficulty and gives a lower estimate when difficulties differ; alpha equals KR-20 when every item is scored 0 or 1. asked 7 times
  8. On the normal curve z = 0 is the 50th percentile, z = +1 the 84th (−1 the 16th), z = +1.5 about the 93rd and z = +2 about the 98th; so IQ 100, 85 and 130 (SD 15) are the 50th, 16th and about 98th percentiles, and IQ 136 on an SD-16 scale (z = 2.25) has about 98.8 per cent below it. asked 7 times
  9. The WAIS-IV (2008; ages 16:0 to 90:11) yields a Full Scale IQ and four index scores, Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory and Processing Speed, from 10 core subtests: 3 verbal (Similarities, Vocabulary, Information), 3 perceptual (Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, Visual Puzzles), 2 working memory (Digit Span, Arithmetic) and 2 processing speed (Symbol Search, Coding). asked 7 times
  10. The 16PF is Raymond B. Cattell's (1949; fifth edition 1993, 185 items): 16 primary source traits found by factor analysis and five global factors, scored in stens; it measures personality traits, not intelligence. asked 7 times
  11. Reliability means consistency, dependability and freedom from measurement error (all of these): a reliable measure gives stable results on different occasions. asked 6 times
  12. SEM = SD × √(1 - r), which estimates the range within which a person's true score probably falls: with SD 15 and r = .91 the SEM is 4.5 (±1 SEM 68 percent, ±1.96 SEM 95 percent). asked 6 times
  13. The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (Sparrow, Balla and Cicchetti, 1984; Vineland-3, 2016) measure adaptive functioning in daily life (communication, daily living skills, socialisation, motor skills), not IQ, personality or psychopathology. asked 6 times
  14. The MMPI (Hathaway and McKinley, 1943; 550 items) is an objective personality inventory that assesses personality and psychopathology for psychiatric diagnosis, covering mood, physical concerns and social attitudes; it is not projective, an intelligence test or an aptitude battery. asked 6 times
  15. Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli to reveal unconscious processes; their main advantage is that they avoid self-report biases and are hard to fake. asked 6 times
  16. John Exner's Comprehensive System (1974) is the standard Rorschach scoring system: it codes location, determinants, form quality and content, gives no IQ, and there is no 'Murray system' of Rorschach scoring. asked 6 times
  17. The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (David Grant and Esta Berg, 1948) is the most widely used test of executive function: it measures set-shifting and cognitive flexibility through perseverative errors and is most sensitive to dorsolateral prefrontal cortex dysfunction. asked 6 times
  18. A test can be reliable without being valid but cannot be valid without being reliable: reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. asked 5 times
  19. Wechsler scaled scores have a mean of 10 and an SD of 3 (range 1 to 19): 10 is the 50th percentile, 7 is 1 SD below (about the 16th, low average), 13 is 1 SD above and 4 is 2 SD below. asked 5 times
  20. The Rorschach (Hermann Rorschach, Psychodiagnostik, 1921) has 10 symmetrical inkblot cards: 5 achromatic (I, IV, V, VI, VII), 2 black and red (II, III) and 3 multicoloured (VIII, IX, X), so five achromatic and five chromatic. asked 5 times
  21. The Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test (Lauretta Bender, 1938; nine figures adapted from Wertheimer, 16 in Bender-Gestalt II, 2003) assesses visual-motor integration and screens for organic brain damage and developmental immaturity; it is not a personality test. asked 5 times
  22. The Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery (Halstead 1947; Reitan) is a fixed battery for comprehensive neuropsychological assessment (Category Test, Tactual Performance, Seashore Rhythm, Speech-Sounds Perception, Finger Tapping, Trail Making); an Impairment Index of 0.5 or more suggests brain damage, and the Rorschach is not part of it. asked 5 times
  23. The BDI-II (Aaron Beck, 1996) is a self-report inventory of 21 items rated 0 to 3 for the past two weeks: 0 to 13 minimal, 14 to 19 mild, 20 to 28 moderate, 29 to 63 severe. asked 5 times
  24. Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN), the proportion of people with the condition whom the test correctly identifies; specificity = TN / (TN + FP), the proportion without it whom the test correctly clears. asked 4 times
  25. Construct validity (Cronbach and Meehl, 1955) is the degree to which a test measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure; convergent validity is a high correlation with measures of the same construct and discriminant validity a low correlation with measures of unrelated constructs. asked 4 times
  26. T scores (McCall, 1922) have a mean of 50 and an SD of 10 (T = 50 + 10z) and are used on the MMPI and NEO-PI-R; T 70 is 2 SD above the mean (original MMPI cut-off) and T 65 is 1.5 SD above (MMPI-2 cut-off). asked 4 times

  28. Stanines run from 1 to 9 (mean 5, SD about 2) in the proportions 4, 7, 12, 17, 20, 17, 12, 7, 4 per cent; stanine 5 holds 20 per cent, the 40th to 60th percentile (mean ± 0.25 SD), and is a normalised, non-linear transformation. asked 4 times
