  1. Who is credited with elaborating the "social representation theory"? (RML 2022)

    • aSerge Moscovici
    • bMichael A. Hogg
    • cRoy F. Baumeister
    • dMark E. Leary
    ✓ Answer: (a) Serge Moscovici
    Serge Moscovici, social representations theory (1961)
    The concept

    The founding studyMoscovici launched social representations theory in 1961 by studying how psychoanalysis became French common sense.

    How it worksA new idea becomes common sense through anchoring (tying it to familiar categories) and objectification (turning it into a concrete image).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Michael A. HoggKnown for work on social identity and his uncertainty-identity theory, not social representations.
    • (c) Roy F. BaumeisterWith Mark R. Leary, proposed the need to belong (1995), a theory of motivation, not of shared common sense.
    • (d) Mark E. LearyProposed the sociometer theory of self-esteem and, with Baumeister, the need to belong (1995); the theorist is Mark R. Leary.

  2. Which one refers to the behaviour that an individual engages in while enacting a role? (RML 2020)

    • aRole taking
    • bRole playing
    • cPlaying at a role
    • dRole expectation
    ✓ Answer: (b) Role playing
    Role theory: role enactment
    The concept

    What it isRole playing (also called role enactment) is the actual behaviour of the person performing a role.

    Why this oneThe stem asks for the behaviour shown while enacting a role: the doing, not the thinking or the expecting.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Role takingMead's mental act of seeing a situation from another person's viewpoint; it is thinking, not behaving.
    • (c) Playing at a roleA young child's make-believe, pretending at a role rather than actually performing one.
    • (d) Role expectationWhat other people expect of the role occupant, not what the occupant actually does.

  4. A tendency to act in a way that is consistent with one's own belief, expectation, or cognition about an event or behavior, thereby increasing the likelihood that the event or behavior will occur, is termed as: (IHBAS 2020)

    • aSelf-fulfilling prophecy
    • bConfirmation bias
    • cMotivational reaction
    • dBase-rate fallacy
    ✓ Answer: (a) Self-fulfilling prophecy
    Merton, self-fulfilling prophecy (1948)
    The concept

    What it isA self-fulfilling prophecy is acting on an expectation in ways that make it come true.

    Who named itMerton coined the term in 1948. The belief changes behaviour, and the behaviour changes the outcome.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Confirmation biasOnly seeks evidence that fits a belief; it changes nothing in the world, so the event is not made more likely.
    • (c) Motivational reactionNot a recognised term in psychology; a made-up distractor.
    • (d) Base-rate fallacyIgnoring how common something is (its base rate) when judging likelihood.

  5. What is the halo effect? (IHBAS 2024)

    • aThe tendency to judge others based on their group membership
    • bThe influence of a positive trait on the perception of other traits
    • cThe tendency to overlook negative behaviors in individuals
    • dThe effect of initial impressions on subsequent judgments
    ✓ Answer: (b) The influence of a positive trait on the perception of other traits
    Thorndike, halo effect (1920)
    The concept

    What it isIn the halo effect, one salient positive trait or an overall impression colours ratings of unrelated traits.

    Who named itThorndike named it in 1920.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) The tendency to judge others based on their group membershipStereotyping: judging a person by the group they belong to.
    • (c) The tendency to overlook negative behaviors in individualsA consequence of a halo, not its definition; the halo is one trait spreading to others.
    • (d) The effect of initial impressions on subsequent judgmentsThe primacy effect (or anchoring), where early information weighs most.

  6. A teacher watches two students interact for less than a minute and correctly infers who is the class leader. This example best illustrates: (IHBAS 2025)

    • aSocial facilitation
    • bSelf-fulfilling prophecy
    • cThin slicing
    • dFundamental attribution error
    ✓ Answer: (c) Thin slicing
    Ambady and Rosenthal, thin slices of behaviour
    The concept

    What it isThin slicing is making accurate judgements from very brief samples of behaviour.

    The evidenceAmbady and Rosenthal showed that silent clips of 30 seconds or less yield accurate judgements.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Social facilitationBetter performance of well-learned tasks in front of others; it is about performing, not judging.
    • (b) Self-fulfilling prophecyThe teacher's expectation did not create the leadership; the teacher only perceived it.
    • (d) Fundamental attribution errorOver-explaining behaviour by disposition; here the brief judgement was accurate, not an error.

  7. Arrange the process of communication in sequence (RML 2020)

    • acoding, decoding, recipient, channel
    • bchannel, encoding, recipient, decoding
    • cchannel, decoding, recipient, encoding
    • dencoding, channel, recipient, decoding
    ✓ Answer: (d) encoding, channel, recipient, decoding
    Sender-receiver model of communication
    The concept

    SendingThe sender first encodes the idea into symbols, and the message travels through a channel.

    ReceivingIt reaches the recipient, who then decodes it. So: encoding, channel, recipient, decoding.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) coding, decoding, recipient, channelPuts decoding before the message is sent through the channel or received.
    • (b) channel, encoding, recipient, decodingSends the message through the channel before it is encoded.
    • (c) channel, decoding, recipient, encodingStarts with the channel and decodes before the recipient gets it, leaving encoding last.

  8. The tendency of people to attribute their own behavior to external causes and other’s behavior to internal causes is referred to as : (IHBAS 2024)

    • aSelf-serving bias
    • bFundamental attribution error
    • cActor-Observer effect
    • dStereotyping
    ✓ Answer: (c) Actor-Observer effect
    Jones and Nisbett, actor-observer effect (1971)
    The concept

    What it isAs actors we explain our own behaviour by the situation; as observers we explain others' by their dispositions.

    Who found itJones and Nisbett described it in 1971.

    Exam tipThe stem contrasts self and others; only this effect covers both sides.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Self-serving biasConcerns our own successes and failures, not the self-versus-other split in explaining behaviour.
    • (b) Fundamental attribution errorConcerns only other people's behaviour; it has no self-versus-other contrast.
    • (d) StereotypingA generalised belief about a group, not an attribution pattern.

  9. The "just-world hypothesis" explains victim-blaming as a result of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aPeople's inherent sadism and desire to see others suffer.
    • bThe need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable, and fair.
    • cA fundamental error in attributing behavior to dispositional factors.
    • dThe process of social comparison with a less fortunate other.
    ✓ Answer: (b) The need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable, and fair
    Melvin Lerner, just-world hypothesis
    The concept

    The beliefMelvin Lerner held that people need to believe the world is orderly, predictable and fair.

    Why victims get blamedAn innocent person's suffering threatens this belief, so observers restore it by deciding the victim deserved it.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) People's inherent sadism and desire to see others suffer.Observers are distressed by the suffering, not sadistic; blaming defends their belief.
    • (c) A fundamental error in attributing behavior to dispositional factors.The dispositional blame is the result of the belief, not its cause.
    • (d) The process of social comparison with a less fortunate other.Downward social comparison is a different process, not a defence of a belief in fairness.

  10. Arvind is a college student who has strong negative feelings about smoking cigarettes. His grandfather died of lung cancer and he believes smoking is disgusting. One night at a party, Arvind's friends all go outside for a cigarette and he decides to smoke as well. What will he most likely experience as a result? (RML 2021)

    • aJustification of efforts
    • bIncreased drive to smoke
    • cCognitive Dissonance
    • dDrive Reduction
    ✓ Answer: (c) Cognitive dissonance
    Festinger, cognitive dissonance theory
    The concept

    What it isCognitive dissonance is Festinger's aversive tension felt when actions clash with beliefs.

    Why hereArvind acts against a strong, important attitude (smoking is disgusting), so he will feel this tension.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Justification of effortsA different paradigm, effort justification: valuing what cost us effort; nothing here involved effort.
    • (b) Increased drive to smokeNothing in the story suggests a stronger urge to smoke; he dislikes smoking.
    • (d) Drive ReductionHull's motivation theory, not a reaction to acting against an attitude.

  11. In which of the following scenarios would it be most difficult to predict behaviour from attitudes? (RML 2021)

    • aWhen the attitude has a great deal of personal relevance for the individual
    • bWhen the attitude is formed by indirect experience
    • cWhen the individual has a lot of knowledge about the attitude object
    • dWhen the attitude is formed by the individual's direct experience
    ✓ Answer: (b) When the attitude is formed by indirect experience
    Regan and Fazio (1977), attitude-behaviour link
    The concept

    Why this oneHearsay-based attitudes, formed by indirect experience, are weak and less accessible, so they predict behaviour poorly.

    The evidenceRegan and Fazio (1977) showed that direct experience strengthens the attitude-behaviour link.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) When the attitude has a great deal of personal relevance for the individualHigh personal relevance strengthens the attitude-behaviour link, so prediction is easier.
    • (c) When the individual has a lot of knowledge about the attitude objectMore knowledge of the object strengthens the link, so prediction is easier.
    • (d) When the attitude is formed by the individual's direct experienceDirect experience makes the attitude strong (Regan and Fazio, 1977), so prediction is easiest.

    Why the other options are wrong

  14. Low-prejudiced people engage in: (IHBAS 2020)

    • bControlled information processing resulting in refuting stereotype
    • cControlled information processing resulting in involuntary stereotyping
    • dAutomatic information processing resulting in voluntary stereotyping
    ✓ Answer: (b) Controlled information processing resulting in refuting stereotype
    Devine (1989), automatic and controlled stereotyping
    The concept

    Stage 1: automaticDevine (1989) found stereotypes are activated automatically in high- and low-prejudiced people alike.

    Stage 2: controlledLow-prejudiced people then use controlled processing to inhibit the stereotype.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Automatic information processing resulting in involuntary stereotypingThe first stage, common to all; low-prejudiced people go on to override it.
    • (c) Controlled information processing resulting in involuntary stereotypingPairs the processes wrongly: controlled processing is what inhibits stereotyping, not what produces it.
    • (d) Automatic information processing resulting in voluntary stereotypingPairs the processes wrongly: automatic processing gives involuntary, not voluntary, stereotyping.

  22. The bystander effect, where the presence of others inhibits helping, is primarily explained by two factors: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aSocial loafing and deindividuation
    • bDiffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance
    • cSocial comparison and normative influence
    • dGroup polarization and groupthink
    ✓ Answer: (b) Diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance
    Latané and Darley, bystander effect
    The concept

    Diffusion of responsibilityLatané and Darley's first mechanism: each bystander assumes someone else will act.

    Pluralistic ignoranceTheir second: each bystander reads the others' calm as a sign that nothing is wrong.

    Key noteThe other pairs are group phenomena of other kinds (Sections 3.6, 3.7 and 3.10).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Social loafing and deindividuationSocial loafing (slacking in a group effort) and deindividuation (losing self-awareness in a crowd) do not explain failing to help.
    • (c) Social comparison and normative influenceSocial comparison (judging ourselves against others) and normative influence (conforming to be liked) are broader processes, not the two named mechanisms.
    • (d) Group polarization and groupthinkGroup polarisation (discussion pushing views to extremes) and groupthink (cohesive groups suppressing dissent) concern group decisions, not helping.

  29. Tim would like to participate in his high school's musical. He has been taking vocal lessons for years and is an excellent singer, but does not have much experience as a dancer. The director gives him a part where he has to sing and dance. Based on the theory of social facilitation, how will the presence of an audience affect Tim's performance? (RML 2021)

    • aHis dancing will improve, but his singing will be impaired
    • bBoth his singing and dancing will improve
    • cHis singing will improve, but his dancing will be impaired
    • dBoth his singing and dancing will be impaired
    ✓ Answer: (c) His singing will improve, but his dancing will be impaired
    Zajonc, drive theory of social facilitation
    The concept

    How it worksBy Zajonc's drive theory, an audience raises arousal, which strengthens the dominant response.

    Applied to TimThe dominant response is correct on a well-learned task (singing) and often wrong on an unpractised one (dancing).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) His dancing will improve, but his singing will be impairedReverses the prediction: arousal helps the well-learned task (singing) and hurts the unpractised one (dancing).
    • (b) Both his singing and dancing will improveIgnores that the effect depends on how well learned the task is; his dancing is unpractised.
    • (d) Both his singing and dancing will be impairedIgnores that the effect depends on how well learned the task is; his singing is well practised.

The facts this chapter's questions ask most, most-asked first. Last read before the exam.

  1. The fundamental attribution error (named by Lee Ross, 1977), also called the correspondence bias, is the tendency to overestimate dispositional and underestimate situational causes of other people's behaviour. asked 6 times
  2. Leon Festinger proposed cognitive dissonance theory (1957): holding inconsistent cognitions, or acting against one's attitude, produces aversive tension that people reduce, usually by changing the attitude when the behaviour cannot be undone. asked 6 times
  3. Diffusion of responsibility is the main explanation of the bystander effect: responsibility spreads across all the bystanders, so each feels less personally obliged and assumes someone else will act (Latané and Darley). asked 6 times
  4. Social loafing, also called the Ringelmann effect, is reduced effort when individual efforts are pooled (Latané, Williams and Harkins 1979); it falls when each contribution is identifiable and evaluated, so a teacher should grade both the group and each member. Its collectivist reversal is social striving (Earley). asked 6 times
  5. Social identity theory (Henri Tajfel and John Turner, 1979): a large part of our self-concept and self-esteem comes from the groups we belong to, and prejudice arises from categorisation into in-groups and out-groups and the need for positive distinctiveness, not from competition for resources. asked 5 times
  6. Solomon Asch (1951, 1956): among about seven confederates unanimously giving an obviously wrong line judgement, participants conformed on about 37 percent of critical trials and about three quarters conformed at least once; this is normative influence towards an incorrect majority, not obedience. asked 5 times
  7. Pluralistic ignorance: in an ambiguous emergency each bystander uses the calm behaviour of the others to decide whether help is needed, and mistakenly concludes that it is not (the smoke-filled room). asked 5 times
  8. Bruce Tuckman (1965) ordered group development as forming, storming (conflict over leadership and the group's definition), norming (cohesion; purpose and strategy clarified) and performing; adjourning was added with Mary Ann Jensen in 1977. Proximity is not a stage. asked 5 times
  9. Social facilitation began with Norman Triplett's 1898 fishing-reel study; Zajonc's drive theory (1965) says the presence of others raises arousal and strengthens the dominant response, improving easy, well-learned tasks and impairing new or difficult ones. asked 4 times
  10. The halo effect (Edward Thorndike, 1920): an overall impression or one positive trait influences ratings of a person's other, unrelated traits; the negative version is the horns effect. It is not primacy, stereotyping, self-serving bias or false consensus. asked 3 times
  11. Normative social influence rests on the desire to be liked and accepted; informational social influence on the desire to be correct (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955). Sherif's autokinetic study shows informational influence and the formation of group norms. asked 3 times
  12. The bystander effect: the more bystanders present, the less likely any one is to help and the slower the help; people help more when alone (seizure study: 85 percent alone versus 31 percent with four others). asked 3 times
  13. A sociogram, the tool of Jacob Moreno's sociometry (1934), maps choices and rejections to show social distance and patterns of choice in a group (stars, isolates, cliques); it captures implicit and explicit interaction patterns and does not measure IQ, personality or economic status. asked 3 times
  14. Groupthink (Irving Janis 1972) is a mode of thinking in highly cohesive groups where the drive for unanimity overrides realistic appraisal, discouraging dissent and producing poor decisions; a devil's advocate or critical subgroup and an impartial leader reduce it. asked 3 times
  15. Kurt Lewin (1890 to 1947), father of modern social psychology: in B = f(P, E) the life space is the person plus the psychological environment, and he founded action research (1946) and group dynamics. asked 2 times
  16. The self-fulfilling prophecy was named and formalised by the sociologist Robert K. Merton (1948): a false definition of a situation evokes behaviour that makes it come true. It is not confirmation bias or the base-rate fallacy. asked 2 times
  17. Melvin Lerner's just-world hypothesis explains victim blaming by the need to believe that the world is orderly, predictable and fair. asked 2 times
  18. In the elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo), systematic processing with careful attention to argument quality is the central route; reliance on mental shortcuts and cues is the peripheral route. There is no emotional or situational route. asked 2 times
  19. Reactance (Jack Brehm, 1966) is the motivation to restore a threatened freedom, often by wanting the forbidden behaviour more. asked 2 times
  20. The glass ceiling is an invisible barrier that stops qualified women and minorities from rising to top positions; the term was popularised by a 1986 Wall Street Journal article. asked 2 times
  21. Intergroup hostility falls with cooperation: at Robbers Cave (Sherif, 1954) only superordinate goals ended it, and Allport's contact hypothesis (1954) requires equal status, common goals, cooperation and support of authorities, law or custom. asked 2 times
  22. The propinquity effect: the more we see and interact with a person, the more likely he or she becomes our friend or partner (Festinger, Schachter and Back 1950); its mechanism is Robert Zajonc's mere exposure effect (1968), in which repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking for it. asked 2 times
  23. Conformity is changing to match a group with no direct request; compliance is changing in response to a direct request; obedience is following a command from an authority. asked 2 times
  24. In Milgram's 1963 baseline study, 26 of 40 men (65 percent) obeyed to the maximum 450 volts, and all went to 300 volts. asked 2 times
  25. Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) demonstrates the power of social roles and situational forces; planned for two weeks, it was stopped after six days. asked 2 times
  26. Cialdini's six compliance principles are reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity (not reward); the foot-in-the-door (Freedman and Fraser, 1966) starts with a small request, then a larger one. asked 2 times
  27. The two classic explanations of the bystander effect are diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance, not social loafing, deindividuation, group polarisation or groupthink. asked 2 times
  28. Social case work was founded by Mary Ellen Richmond (Social Diagnosis, 1917); its process is intake and study, assessment (social diagnosis), intervention, evaluation, termination and follow-up, and its techniques include interviewing, observation, counselling, home visits, collateral contacts and referral, but not content analysis or lobbying. asked 2 times
  29. Grace Coyle established group work as a method within social work; formed groups are convened for an identified purpose with a trained leader and an agency auspice, whereas natural groups come together spontaneously. asked 2 times
  30. In Lawrence Shulman's terms the gatekeeper blocks or diverts the group from painful topics; Trecker's principles include democratic group self-determination, flexible functional organisation and progressive programme experiences. asked 2 times
