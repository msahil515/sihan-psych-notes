  1. Which term refers to the biological unfolding of genetic potential? (IHBAS 2025)

    • aMaturation
    • bSocialization
    • cAccommodation
    • dConditioning
    ✓ Answer: (a) Maturation
    Arnold Gesell, maturation
    The concept

    What it isMaturation is change that unfolds on a genetic timetable, largely independent of practice.

    Who to nameArnold Gesell is the theorist linked with maturation: the biological side of development, as opposed to change that depends on experience.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) SocializationSocialisation is learning the norms and roles of one's society, so it depends on experience, not a genetic timetable.
    • (c) AccommodationAccommodation is Piaget's adjustment of a scheme to fit new experience, so it depends on experience.
    • (d) ConditioningConditioning is learning by association, so it depends on experience, not genetic unfolding.

  2. The fact that people can use later experiences to compensate for earlier deficits is a contradiction of which element of earlier developmental psychology? (IHBAS 2020)

    • aCritical periods
    • bContinuity
    • cSensitive periods
    • dStability
    ✓ Answer: (a) Critical periods
    Clarke and Clarke (1976); English and Romanian Adoptees study
    The concept

    What it isA strict critical period is a window in which an experience must happen; if it is missed, the loss is irreversible.

    The evidenceRecovery through later experience (Clarke and Clarke, 1976; the English and Romanian Adoptees study) shows early deficits can be made up, which contradicts a strict critical period.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) ContinuityContinuity asks whether development is gradual or stage-like, a different question from whether early deficits can be reversed.
    • (c) Sensitive periodsSensitive periods allow late recovery by definition, so compensation fits them rather than contradicting them.
    • (d) StabilityStability asks whether individual differences persist over time, a different question from reversibility.

  3. In the case of human development, the strongest evidence for the critical period hypothesis is found in: (RML 2024)

    • aImprinting
    • bLanguage acquisition
    • cEmbryonic development
    • dDevelopment of vocational interests
    ✓ Answer (as keyed): (a) Imprinting
    Lorenz, imprinting
    The concept

    Why this oneImprinting (Lorenz) is the textbook critical period: a young bird follows the first moving object it sees in a short window after hatching.

    Key noteImprinting is animal evidence. Within human development the clearest critical periods are in embryonic development, where teratogen timing decides which organ is damaged, so (c) is the stronger human answer; the paper keys (a).

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Language acquisitionLanguage shows a sensitive period, not a strict critical period, so it is weaker evidence.
    • (c) Embryonic developmentThe clearest human critical periods: teratogen timing decides which organ is damaged; arguably the better answer for human development, but not the key.
    • (d) Development of vocational interestsVocational interests show no critical period at all.

  4. If a blue eye allele (D) is dominant and a green eye allele (d) is recessive, what is the phenotype of a heterozygous (Dd) child? (Real PYQ 2025)

    • aGreen eyes
    • bBlue eyes
    • cBlue-green eyes
    • dCannot be determined
    ✓ Answer: (b) Blue eyes
    Mendelian dominant-recessive inheritance
    The concept

    How it worksIn dominant-recessive inheritance a heterozygote (Dd) carries one dominant and one recessive allele and shows the dominant allele's trait.

    Apply itD (blue) is dominant, so a Dd child has blue eyes.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Green eyesGreen is recessive, so it needs the dd genotype; a Dd child does not show it.
    • (c) Blue-green eyesA blend would need incomplete dominance, but here dominance is complete.
    • (d) Cannot be determinedThe phenotype can be determined because dominance is stated in the question.

  5. During embryonic development, the nervous system begins forming at the : (IHBAS 2025)

    • aZygote stage
    • bMorula stage
    • cGastrula stage
    • dBlastocyst stage
    ✓ Answer: (c) Gastrula stage
    Gastrulation, week 3 of embryonic development
    The concept

    What happensAt gastrulation, in week 3, the three germ layers form: ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm.

    Nervous systemThe neural plate arises from ectoderm and folds into the neural tube by about week 4.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Zygote stageThe single fertilised cell, the earliest stage, with no germ layers yet.
    • (b) Morula stageA solid ball of cells formed by early cell division; an earlier stage with no germ layers yet.
    • (d) Blastocyst stageA hollow ball of cells that implants in the uterus; still earlier than gastrulation, with no germ layers yet.

  6. A child with hyperphagia, obesity, short stature, and hypotonia is found to have a deletion on chromosome 15 (15q11-q13). This syndrome is : (IHBAS 2025)

    • aFragile X syndrome
    • bTurner syndrome
    • cPrader-Willi syndrome
    • dKlinefelter syndrome
    ✓ Answer: (c) Prader-Willi syndrome
    Paternal 15q11-q13 deletion (genomic imprinting)
    The concept

    What causes itLoss of the paternal 15q11-q13 region gives Prader-Willi syndrome: hypotonia, hyperphagia, obesity and short stature.

    Key noteLoss of the maternal copy of the same region gives Angelman syndrome, so which parent's copy is missing decides the syndrome.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Fragile X syndromeAn FMR1 disorder on the X chromosome, not a chromosome 15 deletion.
    • (b) Turner syndrome45,X, one X chromosome missing in a girl; short stature overlaps, but there is no 15q deletion or hyperphagia.
    • (d) Klinefelter syndrome47,XXY, an extra X chromosome in a male, who is usually tall; not a 15q deletion.

  7. Human motor development is governed by certain basic principles. Which of the following is Not a basic principle of motor development? (RML 2021)

    • aMaturation of the motor system proceeds from gross motor to fine motor behaviours.
    • bMotor development is from cephalo-to-caudal or from head to feet.
    • cMotor development proceeds from the distal to the proximal or from the extremities to the central part of the body.
    • dMotor development plays a major role in social and behavioural expectations.
    ✓ Answer: (c) Distal to proximal
    Proximodistal principle of motor development
    The concept

    The principleThe proximodistal principle says motor control runs from the centre of the body to the extremities: trunk and shoulders before fingers.

    Why this oneOption (c) says distal to proximal, which reverses the real direction, so it is not a principle.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Maturation of the motor system proceeds from gross motor to fine motor behaviours.True: the genuine gross to fine principle; large-muscle control such as sitting comes before fine finger skills.
    • (b) Motor development is from cephalo-to-caudal or from head to feet.True: the genuine cephalocaudal principle; head control comes before control of the legs and feet.
    • (d) Motor development plays a major role in social and behavioural expectations.True: a genuine principle; motor development shapes the social role of the child and what others expect of them.

  8. What is the principal factor that changes the affordances available to the toddler, compared with the infant? (RML 2022)

    • aIndependent locomotion
    • bStarting to speak
    • cThe attachment relationship
    • dNew kinds of caretaking
    ✓ Answer: (a) Independent locomotion
    Gibson, affordances (ecological approach to perception)
    The concept

    What it isAffordances are the action possibilities the environment offers a perceiver, so they depend on what that perceiver can do.

    Why locomotionThey change most when the child's own action abilities change: crawling and walking make toys reachable and drops dangerous.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) Starting to speakSpeech changes the child's social world, not what the surroundings afford action.
    • (c) The attachment relationshipAttachment changes the child's social world, not the action possibilities in the surroundings.
    • (d) New kinds of caretakingCaretaking changes the social world around the child, not what the environment affords the child's own actions.

  9. The process of synaptic pruning is most closely associated with : (RML 2023)

    • aMemory consolidation
    • bNeuronal development and plasticity
    • cNeurotransmitter release
    • dLong-term potentiation
    ✓ Answer: (b) Neuronal development and plasticity
    Synaptic overproduction and pruning in brain development
    The concept

    What happensThe young brain overproduces synapses, then prunes the little-used ones.

    Why this onePruning fits circuits to experience, which is the developmental sculpting of circuits: neuronal development and plasticity.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Memory consolidationMemory consolidation is how new memories become stable, working at existing synapses, not the developmental sculpting of circuits.
    • (c) Neurotransmitter releaseNeurotransmitter release is how signals cross existing synapses, not the removal of unused ones.
    • (d) Long-term potentiationLong-term potentiation strengthens existing synapses with repeated use; a memory mechanism, not developmental pruning.

  10. Which is not attained in Piaget's concrete operational stage of cognitive development? (JEMAS 2025)

    • aReduced egocentrism
    • bDeferred imitation
    • cHypothetico deductive reasoning
    • dTransitive inference
    ✓ Answer: (c) Hypothetico deductive reasoning
    Piaget, formal operational stage (11 or 12 onward)
    The concept

    Why this oneHypothetico-deductive reasoning, forming hypotheses and testing them systematically, is the hallmark of Piaget's formal operational stage, from 11 or 12 onward.

    SoIt is not yet attained in the concrete operational stage, which comes before it.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Reduced egocentrismTrue gain: reduced egocentrism is a concrete operational achievement.
    • (b) Deferred imitationAttained earlier, at 18 to 24 months in the sensorimotor stage, so it is present rather than missing in concrete operations.
    • (d) Transitive inferenceTrue gain: transitive inference (if A is taller than B and B taller than C, A is taller than C) is a concrete operational achievement.

  11. A 5-year-old child is asked to sort a deck of cards. The cards can be sorted by color (red/black) or by suit (hearts, spades, etc.). The child successfully sorts by color but cannot then re-sort the same deck by suit. This inflexibility is an example of: (Real PYQ 2024)

    • aEgocentrism
    • bAnimism
    • cCentration
    • dTransductive reasoning
    ✓ Answer: (c) Centration
    Piaget, preoperational stage
    The concept

    What it isCentration is focusing on one salient feature and ignoring the others, typical of the preoperational child.

    In this caseThe child centres on colour and ignores suit, so the same deck cannot be re-sorted.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) EgocentrismEgocentrism is failure to take another's perspective; no other person's view is involved here.
    • (b) AnimismAnimism is giving life to objects, which has nothing to do with sorting.
    • (d) Transductive reasoningTransductive reasoning runs from particular to particular, an error in reasoning, not inflexibility in sorting.

  12. Formal operational reasoning involves all the following EXCEPT: (RML 2022)

    • ahypothetical reasoning
    • bcounterfactual reasoning
    • cdialectical reasoning
    • dmetacognitive reasoning
    ✓ Answer: (c) Dialectical reasoning
    Postformal thought: Riegel (1973), Basseches (1984)
    The concept

    What it isDialectical reasoning, integrating opposing views, is postformal thought, a level beyond Piaget's final stage.

    Who said soRiegel (1973) and Basseches (1984) described it, so it is not part of formal operations.

    Why the other options are not the answer
    • (a) hypothetical reasoningTrue: hypothetical reasoning, thinking about possibilities, belongs to Piaget's formal operational stage.
    • (b) counterfactual reasoningTrue: counterfactual reasoning, reasoning about what is contrary to fact, belongs to Piaget's formal operational stage.
    • (d) metacognitive reasoningTrue: metacognitive reasoning, thinking about one's own thinking, belongs to Piaget's formal operational stage.

  13. Vygotsky's concept of the 'zone of proximal development' (ZPD) refers to: (RML 2025)

    • aThe difference between what a learner can do independently and what they can achieve with guidance.
    • bA measure of a person's fixed learning capacity across cognitive domains.
    • cA developmental stage characterised by logical reasoning capability.
    • dThe optimal period during which specific cognitive skills must develop.
    ✓ Answer: (a) The difference between what a learner can do independently and what they can achieve with guidance
    Vygotsky, zone of proximal development
    The concept

    What it isThe ZPD runs from the actual developmental level (independent problem solving) to the level reached with a more knowledgeable other.

    Key noteIt is not fixed: it moves as skills are internalised.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (b) A measure of a person's fixed learning capacity across cognitive domains.A fixed capacity is the opposite of the ZPD, which moves as skills are internalised.
    • (c) A developmental stage characterised by logical reasoning capability.Sounds like Piaget's stages of logical operations; the ZPD is a gap between two levels of performance, not a stage.
    • (d) The optimal period during which specific cognitive skills must develop.Describes a critical or optimal period, a time window; the ZPD is a gap between two levels, not a period.

  14. What is the primary difference between Piaget and Vygotsky's theories of language development? (RML 2021)

    • aPiaget argued thought depends on language while Vygotsky argued language depends on thought
    • bVygotsky argued thought depends on language while Piaget argued language depends on thought
    • cVygotsky was concerned with early language development while Piaget was concerned with later language development.
    • dNone of the above
    ✓ Answer: (b) Vygotsky argued thought depends on language while Piaget argued language depends on thought
    Vygotsky versus Piaget on thought and language
    The concept

    Piaget's viewFor Piaget language expresses concepts already built through action, so language depends on thought.

    Vygotsky's viewFor Vygotsky thought and speech have separate roots that merge at about 2, after which language shapes thought.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) Piaget argued thought depends on language while Vygotsky argued language depends on thoughtSwaps the names: Piaget made language depend on thought, and Vygotsky had language shape thought.
    • (c) Vygotsky was concerned with early language development while Piaget was concerned with later language development.False: both studied the speech of young children, egocentric speech for Piaget and private speech for Vygotsky.
    • (d) None of the aboveFails because option (b) states the difference correctly.

  15. In the 'teleological stance', toddlers can understand: (RML 2022)

    • aContingencies
    • bIntentions in action
    • cPrior Intentions
    • dMental states such as belief and desire
    ✓ Answer: (b) Intentions in action
    Gergely and Csibra, teleological stance
    The concept

    What it isIn Gergely and Csibra's teleological stance, infants near the end of the first year read an action as efficient and goal-directed.

    Why this oneSo they grasp the goal visible in an ongoing act, the intention in action, without yet representing mental states.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) ContingenciesMere contingencies are what these infants have already moved beyond; the teleological stance reads goals.
    • (c) Prior IntentionsPrior intentions are plans formed before acting, which infants do not yet represent.
    • (d) Mental states such as belief and desireThese come later: desire psychology at about 2 and belief-desire psychology at 3 to 4.

  16. Which of the following is a key characteristic of the "slow-to-warm-up" temperament category in the Thomas and Chess model? (Real PYQ 2024)

    The concept

    What it isThe slow-to-warm-up child withdraws from new things at first, but mildly, and adapts gradually.

    The modelThomas and Chess described easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up children; roughly 35 percent fitted no single type.

    Why the other options are wrong

  18. According to experts on socioemotional development like Campos (2009) and Kagan (2010), self-conscious emotions appear at which age? (RML 2022)

  21. Which theory of attachment is currently considered the predominantly accepted theory by developmental psychologists? (IHBAS 2020)

    • aPsychoanalytic
    • bLearning
    • cCognitive-developmental
    • dEthological
    ✓ Answer: (d) Ethological
    Bowlby, ethological theory of attachment
    The concept

    What it saysBowlby's ethological theory treats attachment as an evolved behavioural system that keeps the infant near a protector.

    Why it wonFeeding-based theories were undermined by Harlow's contact comfort findings: infant monkeys clung to a soft cloth mother rather than a wire one that fed them.

    Why the other options are wrong
    • (a) PsychoanalyticFreud's view rests on feeding, which Harlow's contact comfort findings undermined.
    • (b) LearningSecondary drive ('cupboard love') theory: the mother is loved because she feeds; Harlow's findings undermined it.
    • (c) Cognitive-developmentalExplains only the timing of attachment, not attachment itself.
