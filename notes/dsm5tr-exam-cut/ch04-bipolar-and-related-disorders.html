DSM-5 pulled the bipolar disorders out of the old "Mood Disorders" chapter and placed them between the psychotic disorders and the depressive disorders, to signal that they bridge the two in symptoms, family history and genetics. Everything here is built from three blocks: the manic episode, the hypomanic episode, and the major depressive episode.

The three episode definitions, learn these once

Manic Episode

  • A. A distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood AND abnormally and persistently increased goal-directed activity or energy, lasting at least 1 week and present most of the day, nearly every day (or any duration if hospitalisation is necessary). The addition of "increased activity or energy" to Criterion A is a DSM-5 change from DSM-IV, which required mood alone.
  • B. During the mood disturbance, 3 or more of the following (4 if the mood is only irritable), to a significant degree and representing a noticeable change from usual behaviour: inflated self-esteem or grandiosity; decreased need for sleep (feels rested after 3 hours, which is not the same as insomnia); more talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking; flight of ideas or subjective racing thoughts; distractibility; increase in goal-directed activity (social, occupational, sexual) or psychomotor agitation; excessive involvement in activities with a high potential for painful consequences (buying sprees, sexual indiscretion, foolish business investments). The mnemonic is DIG FAST: Distractibility, Indiscretion, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness.
  • C. The disturbance is severe enough to cause marked impairment in social or occupational functioning, or to necessitate hospitalisation, or there are psychotic features. Any one of these three makes it mania rather than hypomania.
  • D. Not attributable to a substance or another medical condition. Note the DSM-5 rule: a full manic episode that emerges during antidepressant treatment or ECT and persists at a fully syndromal level beyond the physiological effect of that treatment counts as a manic episode, and therefore as bipolar I. DSM-IV excluded it.
Specifiers These attach to bipolar I when the current or most recent episode is manic. Severity has its own definitions for mania in DSM-5-TR: mild, minimum symptom criteria are met; moderate, a very significant increase in activity or impairment in judgement; severe, almost continual supervision is needed to prevent physical harm to self or others. Psychosis is recorded as mood-congruent (grandiosity, invulnerability, or suspicion that others doubt the person's abilities) or mood-incongruent psychotic features. With mixed features: full manic criteria plus at least 3 depressive symptoms on most days (dysphoria, loss of interest, psychomotor retardation, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, thoughts of death or suicide); if full mania and full depression are present at the same time, the diagnosis is manic episode with mixed features. Also with anxious distress, with catatonia, with peripartum onset, and in partial or in full remission (full: no significant symptoms for 2 months). With rapid cycling and with seasonal pattern also apply, describing the pattern of episodes over time. Melancholic and atypical features apply only to depressive episodes.
Course A manic episode usually begins abruptly, with symptoms escalating over a few days, sometimes after a psychosocial stressor or sleep loss. Untreated, an episode lasts about 3 months (Kaplan and Sadock), which is why treatment is not withdrawn before then; manic episodes are generally briefer and end more abruptly than major depressive episodes. DSM-5 notes that about 60 percent of manic episodes occur immediately before a major depressive episode, so mania often ends by switching into depression. Insight is usually poor during the episode, and people often resist treatment.
DSM-5 change Criterion A now requires increased activity or energy alongside the elevated, expansive or irritable mood; DSM-IV required the mood change alone. The DSM-IV mixed episode (full mania and full depression together) was dropped, along with the old exclusion that symptoms must not meet mixed-episode criteria, and replaced by the with mixed features specifier. A fully syndromal manic episode that emerges during antidepressant treatment or ECT and persists beyond the treatment's physiological effect now counts towards bipolar I, where DSM-IV excluded it. DSM-5-TR (2022): the episode criteria are unchanged, but mania-specific severity definitions (taken from DSM-IV) were added, because the general mild-to-severe definitions made no sense for mania, which by definition causes marked impairment.

Hypomanic Episode

  • Same Criterion A mood plus energy change, but lasting at least 4 consecutive days.
  • Same list of 7 symptoms, same 3 or 4 threshold.
  • The episode is associated with an unequivocal change in functioning that is uncharacteristic of the person when not symptomatic, and the change is observable by others.
  • The episode is not severe enough to cause marked impairment, does not require hospitalisation, and there are no psychotic features. If any of those three appear, it is by definition mania.
Specifiers These attach when the current or most recent episode of bipolar I or II is hypomanic. In bipolar I, a hypomanic episode takes no severity rating and no psychotic-features specifier (psychosis would make it mania); only in partial remission, in full remission or unspecified is added. Specifiers that can attach to the episode: with mixed features (full hypomanic criteria plus at least 3 depressive symptoms on most days), with anxious distress and with peripartum onset. With rapid cycling and with seasonal pattern describe the pattern of episodes over time rather than a single episode.
Course Onset is usually rapid, often within a day or two, and hypomanic episodes are generally briefer and more abrupt in onset than major depressive episodes. A hypomanic episode is often immediately preceded or followed by a major depressive episode. Because hypomania feels good and causes no marked impairment, people rarely seek help for it and present instead when depressed; it often comes to light only on direct questioning or from family and friends, which is why bipolar II is so often missed.
DSM-5 change Criterion A gained the same requirement of increased activity or energy. The 4-day minimum (as in DSM-IV) was kept; hypomanias of only 2 to 3 days alongside major depressive episodes, or hypomanias with too few symptoms, are placed under other specified bipolar and related disorder. A full hypomanic episode that emerges during antidepressant treatment and persists beyond its physiological effect now counts, with the caution that one or two symptoms (irritability, edginess or agitation after an antidepressant) are not enough. With mixed features can now be applied to hypomania. DSM-5-TR (2022): no change to the criteria that exams test; text and codes updated.

Major Depressive Episode

  • 5 or more of 9 symptoms during the same 2-week period, representing a change from previous functioning, and at least one must be (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest or pleasure.
  • The nine: (1) depressed mood most of the day nearly every day (in children and adolescents, can be irritable mood); (2) markedly diminished interest or pleasure in all or almost all activities; (3) significant weight loss or gain (more than 5 percent of body weight in a month) or change in appetite (in children, failure to make expected weight gain); (4) insomnia or hypersomnia; (5) psychomotor agitation or retardation, observable by others, not merely subjective; (6) fatigue or loss of energy; (7) feelings of worthlessness or excessive or inappropriate guilt, which may be delusional; (8) diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness; (9) recurrent thoughts of death, recurrent suicidal ideation without a specific plan, a suicide attempt, or a specific plan. Mnemonic SIG E CAPS plus mood: Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicide.
Specifiers These attach to major depressive disorder or to the depressive episode of bipolar I or II. Severity mild, moderate or severe; with psychotic features, mood-congruent (inadequacy, guilt, disease, death, nihilism, deserved punishment) or mood-incongruent. With melancholic features: loss of pleasure or lack of reactivity, plus 3 of six (distinct quality of mood, worse in the morning, waking at least 2 hours early, marked psychomotor change, significant anorexia or weight loss, excessive guilt). With atypical features: mood reactivity plus 2 of four (weight gain or increased appetite, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, long-standing rejection sensitivity), and not given if melancholic or catatonic features are present in the same episode. With mixed features: at least 3 manic or hypomanic symptoms. Also anxious distress, catatonia, peripartum onset and remission.
Course An episode usually develops over days to weeks, often after a prodrome of anxiety and milder depressive symptoms. Untreated, it typically lasts 6 to 13 months; treated episodes average about 3 months, and stopping antidepressants before 3 months almost always brings the symptoms back (Kaplan and Sadock). DSM-5 notes that recovery begins within 3 months for about two in five people and within 1 year for about four in five, and that the more recent the onset, the likelier near-term recovery. Most episodes end in full remission with return to previous functioning; a minority leave residual symptoms or become chronic.
DSM-5 change The DSM-IV bereavement exclusion was removed; a note now asks the clinician to consider whether a major depressive episode is present in addition to the normal response to a significant loss. Hopeless was added to the examples of subjective depressed mood in Criterion A1. The DSM-IV requirement that the symptoms not meet criteria for a mixed episode was dropped with the mixed episode itself, replaced by the with mixed features specifier. DSM-5-TR (2022): no change to the episode criteria; it added ICD-10-CM codes to record current or past suicidal behaviour and non-suicidal self-injury.

Bipolar I Disorder F31.x

In plain language. One manic episode, ever, in a lifetime. That is the entire requirement. Depression is common but is not needed for the diagnosis.
  • Criteria have been met for at least one manic episode. The manic episode may have been preceded by and may be followed by hypomanic or major depressive episodes.
  • The occurrence of the manic and major depressive episode(s) is not better explained by schizoaffective disorder, schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, delusional disorder, or other specified or unspecified schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
Specifiers (these apply across bipolar and depressive disorders): with anxious distress (mild to severe, based on 2 to 5 symptoms); with mixed features (DSM-5 replaced the DSM-IV "mixed episode", which needed full criteria for both mania and depression simultaneously, with a much more usable specifier requiring 3 symptoms of the opposite pole); with rapid cycling (4 or more mood episodes in 12 months); with melancholic features; with atypical features (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, interpersonal rejection sensitivity); with mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic features; with catatonia; with peripartum onset (during pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum, which is a much narrower window than the popular usage of "postpartum depression"); with seasonal pattern. Also specify current or most recent episode (manic, hypomanic, depressed, unspecified), severity, psychotic features, and remission status.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.6 percent; roughly equal in men and women, though rapid cycling is commoner in women. Mean age of onset about 18. Lifetime suicide risk is roughly 15 times the general population, and bipolar disorder may account for a quarter of all completed suicides. Mania lasting 1 week, hypomania 4 days: those two numbers are asked constantly.
Course A first manic episode usually comes in late adolescence or early adulthood but can occur at any age; manic symptoms first appearing in late mid-life or later should prompt a search for a medical or substance cause (for example frontotemporal neurocognitive disorder). The course is recurrent: more than 90 percent of people with a single manic episode go on to have further mood episodes. Most return to full function between episodes, but about 30 percent have severe impairment in work role function. Kaplan and Sadock list as poor prognostic signs poor premorbid occupational status, alcohol dependence, psychotic features, depressive features (especially between episodes) and male sex; good signs are short manic episodes, later age at onset, few suicidal thoughts and few coexisting psychiatric or medical problems.
Comorbidity Extremely high. Around three-quarters have a comorbid anxiety disorder (panic disorder, social anxiety, specific phobia); more than half have an alcohol use disorder; ADHD, oppositional defiant and conduct disorder in younger patients; borderline and antisocial personality disorders; migraine; metabolic syndrome.
Differential Bipolar II (never a full manic episode). Major depressive disorder (the trap: a person presenting depressed whose past hypomania has never been asked about; always screen). Generalised anxiety, panic, substance use. ADHD (chronic, not episodic, and no decreased need for sleep, and onset before 12). Borderline personality disorder, where mood shifts last hours and are reactive to interpersonal events, versus bipolar where episodes last days to weeks and are autonomous. Schizoaffective and schizophrenia. Substance-induced bipolar and related disorder (stimulants, steroids). Bipolar due to another medical condition (hyperthyroidism, Cushing's, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury).
DSM-5 change DSM-5 moved bipolar disorders out of the single DSM-IV Mood Disorders chapter into their own chapter, Bipolar and Related Disorders, placed between the schizophrenia spectrum and depressive chapters as a bridge between the two. The manic-episode changes carry through: increased activity or energy in Criterion A; the mixed episode replaced by the with mixed features specifier, so 'most recent episode mixed' no longer exists; and persistent antidepressant- or ECT-emergent mania counting as bipolar I. With anxious distress was a new specifier. DSM-5-TR (2022): Criterion B was reworded back towards DSM-IV: at least one manic episode must not be better explained by schizoaffective disorder and must not be superimposed on schizophrenia, schizophreniform, delusional or other psychotic disorder (mania superimposed on these goes to other specified bipolar and related disorder). Mania-specific severity definitions were added, and unspecified mood disorder was restored.

Bipolar II Disorder F31.81

In plain language. At least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, and never a full manic episode. It is not a milder illness, and DSM-5-TR says so explicitly, because the depressive burden is heavier and lasts longer.
  • A. Criteria met for at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode.
  • B. There has never been a manic episode.
  • C. Not better explained by a schizophrenia spectrum disorder.
  • D. The symptoms of depression, or the unpredictability caused by frequent alternation between periods of depression and hypomania, cause clinically significant distress or impairment. Note that the impairment must come from the depression or the alternation, because if the hypomania itself caused marked impairment it would be mania.
Specifiers Specify the current or most recent episode: hypomanic or depressed. Specify if: with anxious distress; with mixed features; with rapid cycling (4 or more mood episodes in 12 months, separated by at least 2 months of partial or full remission or by a switch to the opposite pole); with mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic features (these can attach only to the depressive episode, since psychosis during an elevated episode would make it mania); with catatonia; with peripartum onset; with seasonal pattern. Course: in partial or in full remission when full criteria for a mood episode are not currently met; severity (mild, moderate, severe) when they are. Bipolar II has a single code (F31.81), so all of these are written out rather than coded.
Numbers 12-month prevalence about 0.8 percent. Mean age at onset in the mid twenties, slightly later than bipolar I. People with bipolar II spend more time depressed than people with bipolar I: about half of all weeks in long-term follow-up, against about a third. About 5 to 15 percent go on to have a manic episode, at which point the diagnosis changes to bipolar I permanently.
Course It usually begins with a depressive episode, and several depressive episodes often come before the first hypomania is recognised; about 12 percent of people first diagnosed with major depressive disorder are later rediagnosed with bipolar II. The number of lifetime episodes tends to be higher than in major depressive disorder or bipolar I. Most people return to full function between episodes, but at least 15 percent keep some inter-episode dysfunction and about 20 percent pass straight into another episode without recovering in between. About one-third report a lifetime suicide attempt.
Comorbidity About 60 percent have three or more co-occurring disorders. Anxiety disorders (about 75 percent), substance use disorders (about 37 percent), eating disorders, especially binge-eating disorder, and personality disorders.
Differential Major depressive disorder, the commonest misdiagnosis, because patients present in the depressed phase and do not report hypomania as a problem. Cyclothymic disorder (never a full major depressive episode and never a full hypomanic episode meeting all criteria). Borderline personality disorder. ADHD. Substance-induced states.
DSM-5 change Moved with bipolar I into the new Bipolar and Related Disorders chapter. The hypomanic episode now requires increased activity or energy; a hypomanic episode emerging during antidepressant treatment and persisting beyond the drug's physiological effect counts; and with mixed features can be applied to the hypomanic or the depressive episode. Hypomanias of only 2 to 3 days with depressive episodes go to other specified bipolar and related disorder. The DSM-5 text also stated that bipolar II is not a milder form of bipolar I, because of the time spent depressed and the instability of mood. DSM-5-TR (2022): Criterion C was reworded back towards DSM-IV: at least one hypomanic and at least one major depressive episode must not be better explained by schizoaffective disorder and must not be superimposed on schizophrenia, schizophreniform, delusional or other psychotic disorder.

Cyclothymic Disorder F34.0

In plain language. Two years of bobbing up and down, never quite reaching a full hypomanic or a full depressive episode, and never getting a clear two-month stretch of feeling normal.
  • A. For at least 2 years (1 year in children and adolescents), numerous periods with hypomanic symptoms that do not meet criteria for a hypomanic episode and numerous periods with depressive symptoms that do not meet criteria for a major depressive episode.
  • B. During that 2-year period, the hypomanic and depressive periods have been present for at least half the time, and the person has not been without the symptoms for more than 2 months at a time.
  • C. Criteria for a major depressive, manic or hypomanic episode have never been met.
  • D. Not better explained by a schizophrenia spectrum disorder. E. Not substance or medical. F. Clinically significant distress or impairment. Specify with anxious distress.
Specifiers The only specifier is with anxious distress (2 or more of: feeling keyed up or tense, unusually restless, difficulty concentrating because of worry, fear that something awful may happen, feeling of losing control). There is no severity rating and no remission specifier, and the episode-based specifiers (mixed features, rapid cycling, psychotic features, peripartum onset, seasonal pattern) cannot apply, because cyclothymia by definition contains no full mood episodes.
Numbers Lifetime prevalence 0.4 to 1 percent, equal in men and women, though women present for treatment more. Usual onset in adolescence or early adult life. Risk of progressing to bipolar I or II is roughly 15 to 50 percent.
Course Onset is usually insidious, in adolescence or early adult life, and the course is persistent; it is sometimes regarded as a temperamental predisposition to the other bipolar disorders. Among children with cyclothymic symptoms, mean age at onset is about 6.5 years. When fluctuating hypomanic and depressive symptoms begin late in adult life, bipolar or depressive disorder due to another medical condition (for example multiple sclerosis) must be ruled out first. If a full hypomanic, manic or major depressive episode later occurs, the cyclothymic diagnosis is dropped and replaced by the appropriate disorder.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders and sleep disorders. In children, ADHD is a frequent comorbidity.
Differential Bipolar II (full episodes have been met). Persistent depressive disorder (only depressive symptoms, no hypomanic periods). Borderline personality disorder, again, where the instability is interpersonally triggered. Rapid cycling bipolar. Substance-induced mood lability.
DSM-5 change Moved into the new Bipolar and Related Disorders chapter. DSM-5 tightened the criteria: the hypomanic and depressive periods must be present for at least half the time, and Criterion C now says a major depressive, manic or hypomanic episode has never been met. DSM-IV excluded only major depressive, manic and mixed episodes, and only during the first 2 years, so bipolar I or II could be diagnosed alongside cyclothymia if an episode appeared later; in DSM-5 the cyclothymia diagnosis is dropped instead. With anxious distress became available. DSM-5-TR (2022): no change to the criteria that exams test; text and codes updated.

Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar and Related Disorder, and Bipolar Due to Another Medical Condition

  • A prominent and persistent disturbance in mood, elevated or irritable with or without depressed mood, that developed during or soon after substance intoxication or withdrawal or exposure to a medication capable of producing it, or that is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition.
  • Common substances: amphetamines and other stimulants, cocaine, phencyclidine, steroids. Common medical causes: Cushing's syndrome, hyperthyroidism, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury.
  • Remember the DSM-5 rule again: if a fully syndromal manic episode persists beyond the physiological effect of the antidepressant or ECT that triggered it, it is counted as a manic episode and the person has bipolar I, not a substance-induced disorder.
Specifiers Substance/medication-induced bipolar and related disorder: specify with onset during intoxication or with onset during withdrawal; the code also records the substance and whether a mild or a moderate-to-severe use disorder coexists. Bipolar and related disorder due to another medical condition: specify with manic features (full criteria for a manic or hypomanic episode not met), with manic- or hypomanic-like episode (full criteria met except Criterion D for a manic episode or Criterion F for a hypomanic episode), or with mixed features (depressive symptoms are also present but do not predominate).
Course Onset follows exposure closely. With stimulants a manic or hypomanic state can appear within minutes to an hour of use and usually resolves over 1 to 2 days; with phencyclidine the picture may begin as a delirium with affective features within hours to a few days; with corticosteroids and some immunosuppressants, mania usually follows several days of use, and higher doses carry more risk. Symptoms should settle once the substance is stopped; persistence for about a month beyond intoxication or withdrawal points to a primary bipolar disorder. The medical form usually begins acutely or subacutely within the first weeks or month of the medical condition, though it can appear with a later relapse or worsening. A first manic episode in later life should raise suspicion of a medical or substance cause.
Comorbidity Substance use disorders themselves, plus a primary bipolar disorder in a substantial proportion, since stimulant use is elevated in bipolar disorder. For the medical form, the endocrine or neurological disease and the depression that commonly follows it.
Differential Primary bipolar I or II disorder in someone who also uses substances, settled by whether mood symptoms predate use and whether they persist about a month beyond acute intoxication or withdrawal. Delirium. Substance-induced psychotic disorder where psychosis rather than mood predominates. Personality disorders with affective instability.
DSM-5 change DSM-IV had single categories, substance-induced mood disorder and mood disorder due to a general medical condition, with specifiers such as 'with manic features', 'with depressive features' and 'with mixed features'. DSM-5 split them by polarity, so the elevated-mood forms now sit in the Bipolar and Related Disorders chapter and the depressive forms in Depressive Disorders, and 'general medical condition' became another medical condition. DSM-5 also added the rule that a persistent, fully syndromal antidepressant- or ECT-emergent mania counts as bipolar I, not as substance-induced. DSM-5-TR (2022): Criterion A of the substance/medication-induced form was reworded to match the medical-condition form, dropping the confusing 'with or without depressed mood' wording, and the timing criterion now also covers symptoms arising after withdrawal from a medication; the diagnosis itself is unchanged.
